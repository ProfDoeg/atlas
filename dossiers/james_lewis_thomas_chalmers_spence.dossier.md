# James Lewis Thomas Chalmers Spence: Research Dossier

**Principal published name:** Lewis Spence  
**Life:** 25 November 1874–3 March 1955  
**Prepared:** 20 August 2026  
**Scope:** Biographical, familial, professional, political, intellectual, bibliographical, controversial, and reception history

## Research Note and Evidentiary Conventions

This dossier reconstructs the life of the Scottish journalist, poet, mythographer, folklorist, occult writer, and nationalist organizer who normally published as **Lewis Spence**. It is not about the later Scottish footballer of the same name.

The documentary base is uneven. Spence left a political archive in the National Library of Scotland, but no comparably accessible comprehensive personal archive or modern scholarly biography was located. His friend Charles Richard Cammell supplied the fullest published personal portrait, in *Heart of Scotland* (1956), but that account is affectionate, retrospective, and sometimes hagiographic. Modern reference works and family-history reconstructions often repeat one another. His books are much better documented than his domestic life.

The following labels are used where necessary:

- **Documented:** supported by an archival catalogue, contemporary publication, official election record, title page, or multiple independent sources.
- **Reported:** stated by a named source but not independently corroborated here.
- **Disputed:** credible sources give materially different versions.
- **Unverified:** widely repeated, but no satisfactory underlying record was located.
- **Interpretation:** an inference made by a historian, critic, or this dossier from documented material.
- **Myth or legend:** a story significant to reputation or influence but not established as literal history.

Dates in the publication lists are dates of the earliest edition located or the strongest reconstructable date. Reprints, American issues, retitlings, and modern print-on-demand extracts have generated extensive catalog noise; uncertain dates are marked rather than harmonized silently.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Spence was the eldest of four children of **James Edward Kendall Spence** (born 8 July 1848 in Edinburgh; lost in February 1893) and **Barbara Charlotte Chalmers** (1849–1920).

His father is variously described in local accounts as an insurance agent, property developer, amateur ornithologist, breeder of prize birds, and Sergeant Major in the 1st Forfarshire Royal Regiment of Artillery Volunteers. These roles are not mutually exclusive. In February 1893 James Edward Kendall Spence sailed from Liverpool aboard the White Star Line cargo steamer **SS *Naronic***, apparently accompanying valuable poultry or pigeons intended for American exhibitions or sale. The ship departed on 11 February, disappeared in the North Atlantic with all 74 persons aboard, and was never found. The official inquiry could establish no cause. Lewis was eighteen, not fourteen as one later local account calculates.

The loss was a major family rupture: there was no body, known wreck site, or conclusive explanation. It belongs both to Spence’s factual biography and to the atmosphere of maritime disappearance that later readers have retrospectively associated with his interest in lost civilizations. No evidence has been found that Spence himself made such a causal claim.

Barbara Charlotte Chalmers survived her husband by twenty-seven years. A reliable detailed record of her education, employment, religious life, or role in Lewis’s intellectual formation was not located.

### Grandparents and claimed notable relations

Spence’s paternal grandfather was **Professor James Spence** (1812–1882), an eminent Edinburgh surgeon and professor of surgery who served as a surgeon to Queen Victoria in Scotland. The anatomical “tail of Spence,” an extension of breast tissue into the axilla, bears his name. He married **Margaret Fair** (1825–1905), described in genealogical sources as a daughter of Thomas Fair of Buenos Aires. This placed Lewis within a professionally eminent Edinburgh family, although no evidence was found that he inherited substantial wealth from it.

Local and occult-biographical sources describe Lewis as related through his mother to the Church of Scotland leader **Thomas Chalmers** (1780–1847) and to **James Chalmers** (1782–1853), associated with an early adhesive-postage proposal. The precise lines of descent or collateral relationship were not produced in the sources consulted. The claims should therefore be treated as reported family tradition, not a completed genealogy.

### Siblings

Spence was reportedly the eldest of four children. The best-documented sibling is **Kendall Chalmers Spence**, who performed as **Kendall Chalmers** and became an actor-manager. A complete, independently verified sibling list was not located.

### Marriage and children

On **25 November 1899**, his twenty-fifth birthday, Spence married **Helen Stewart Bruce** (1877–1942), daughter of Edinburgh cabinetmaker George Bruce. The marriage produced four children: one son, generally called **Toby**, and three daughters, **Rhoda**, **Claire**, and **Madge**. Genealogical sites expand some of these names, but their unsourced forms are not treated as certain here.

Cammell recalled that the daughters successively assisted their father as secretaries. The University of Edinburgh archive records a 14 January 1965 letter from **Rhoda Spence** concerning copyright, evidence that at least one daughter continued to administer aspects of his literary estate after his death. No evidence of another spouse, publicly acknowledged partner, or extramarital relationship was located.

Helen died in **1942**. Her cause of death was not established in the consulted material. She was later buried with Lewis in Dean Cemetery.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1874–1893

Spence’s childhood was rooted in the Broughty Ferry–Monifieth area on the Firth of Tay, then in Forfarshire. He attended the **Collegiate School, Broughty Ferry**, and later **Higher Ongar School** in Essex. The move to an English boarding school exposed him early to life outside Scotland, but no surviving school memoir, report, or named teacher was located.

The family’s social position combined professional prestige, commercial activity, military-volunteer service, and a serious interest in birds. The disappearance of his father aboard the *Naronic* in 1893 occurred when Lewis was approaching adulthood and higher education. Later writers sometimes turn the event into a foreshadowing of Atlantis. That symbolism is retrospective; the documented event is the loss itself.

## Education and Formation, c. 1893–1899

Spence attended the **University of Edinburgh**. Sources disagree about the exact credential:

- The National Library of Scotland’s concise biography says only that he was educated at the University of Edinburgh before entering journalism.
- Juliette Wood’s scholarly article in *Béaloideas* states that he qualified as a dentist.
- Some secondary occult biographies claim that he took an M.A., trained in dentistry, and later received an honorary D.Litt.

No university matriculation or graduation record confirming an M.A., dental qualification, or honorary D.Litt. was located in the accessible material. The strongest cautious conclusion is that he undertook professional dental training at Edinburgh and may have qualified, but did not build a known dental career. The honorary doctorate should remain **unverified** unless a University of Edinburgh conferral record is produced.

Secondary biographies say that he became active in the **Scottish Home Rule Association** while a student. This is plausible in light of his later career, but the consulted political archive begins in the 1920s and does not independently document the student episode.

His formative reading is easier to infer from later publications than from personal papers. It included comparative mythology, nineteenth-century ethnology and folklore, European occult encyclopedias, diffusionist anthropology, antiquarian Scottish literature, and the Atlantis theory of Ignatius Donnelly. These traditions gave him a large synthetic vocabulary but also transmitted racial typologies, weakly controlled analogies, and geological models later abandoned by professional scholarship.

## Early Career, 1899–1909

### *The Scotsman* and *The Edinburgh Magazine*

In **1899**, the year of his marriage, Spence joined *The Scotsman* as a sub-editor or copy editor. Sources variously place the end of this employment in 1904 or 1906. The discrepancy probably reflects a transition during which he also edited **The Edinburgh Magazine** in **1904–1905**.

Editorial work trained him to assemble large quantities of information rapidly, write for non-specialists, and impose order on heterogeneous sources. Those habits became the method of his encyclopedias and myth compendia. No complete index of his unsigned newspaper work has been reconstructed; normal sub-editorial labor was often anonymous.

### First book and move to London

His first major book, ***The Mysteries of Britain: Secret Rites and Traditions of Ancient Britain Restored***, appeared in **1905**. It attempted to recover a prehistoric British religious inheritance and proposed migrations into Britain from north-west Africa, connecting early inhabitants with Berbers and Basques. The work established recurring Spence themes: cultural diffusion, survival of archaic ritual, national origins, and reconstruction from scattered comparative clues.

In **1906** he moved to London and joined **The British Weekly**, the influential liberal Nonconformist newspaper associated with Sir William Robertson Nicoll. He worked there until **1909**, usually described as an editor or sub-editor. There is no evidence that Spence became a Nonconformist political theologian; the employment is a professional association, not proof of doctrinal allegiance.

During and immediately after this period he began his sustained work on Mesoamerican tradition: ***The Mythologies of Ancient Mexico and Peru*** (1907) and ***The Popol Vuh: The Mythic and Heroic Sagas of the Kichés of Central America*** (1908).

## Chronological Life History

### 1874–1898: Forfarshire childhood, English schooling, Edinburgh training

Born in the Broughty Ferry–Monifieth area on 25 November 1874, Spence grew up as the eldest child of James Edward Kendall Spence and Barbara Charlotte Chalmers. He was educated locally, at Higher Ongar in Essex, and at Edinburgh University. His father’s disappearance with the *Naronic* in 1893 deprived the family of its male head and left an unresolved maritime death. Spence trained in dentistry according to the strongest scholarly account, but turned to journalism.

### 1899–1905: Marriage, newspaper work, and first authorship

Spence married Helen Bruce on 25 November 1899 and became a *Scotsman* sub-editor. Four children followed over the marriage, though only partial dates are recoverable from the consulted sources. His editorship of *The Edinburgh Magazine* in 1904–1905 marked an early attempt to control a publication rather than merely serve on a newspaper staff. In 1905 *The Mysteries of Britain* announced his interest in prehistory, ritual survival, and national tradition.

### 1906–1909: London and Mesoamerican popularization

The London years at *The British Weekly* widened his publishing contacts. His 1907 and 1908 books introduced British general readers to Mexican, Peruvian, Maya, and K’iche’ narrative materials.

The 1908 *Popol Vuh* requires precise description. It was not a new direct translation from K’iche’. Spence synthesized and abridged earlier European editions and translations, especially the nineteenth-century work associated with Charles Étienne Brasseur de Bourbourg. Its historical importance lies in English-language popularization. Modern advertisements that call Spence “the translator” flatten the mediation and can mislead readers about his linguistic role.

### 1909–1919: Return to Edinburgh and rapid mythographical production

Spence returned to Edinburgh in **1909** and made freelance writing his principal occupation. He produced dictionaries, cultural surveys, and illustrated myth collections at a rapid pace. Subjects included Mexico and Peru, North American Indigenous traditions, Egypt, Babylonia and Assyria, Brittany, the Rhine, and Spain.

His method was compilation and literary retelling rather than fieldwork. No evidence was found that he conducted ethnographic research among Maya, K’iche’, Indigenous North American, Egyptian, Babylonian, or Breton communities. He worked from printed sources, translations, museum-era syntheses, and the comparative assumptions of his time.

Contemporary scholarly reviews identified both strengths and weaknesses. Reviewers acknowledged readability and the ability to marshal a large narrative corpus. The Assyriologist Theophile J. Meek, reviewing *Myths and Legends of Babylonia and Assyria* in 1917, found many inaccuracies. A 1918 *Biblical World* review judged Spence’s preparation inadequate for the subject. These assessments do not show that every retelling was false; they document that specialists did not accept him as a controlling authority in Assyriology.

During the First World War, no military service, government intelligence role, imprisonment, or conscientious-objection proceeding involving Spence was located. He was forty at the outbreak of war. His precise wartime activities beyond authorship remain underdocumented.

### 1920–1923: Occult encyclopedia, mythology theory, poetry, and nationalism

***An Encyclopaedia of Occultism*** appeared in **1920**. It assembled roughly 2,500 entries on magic, ceremonial traditions, occult personalities, spiritualism, psychic phenomena, demonology, and mysticism. It became his most durable reference work, though later occult research has superseded many entries and some claims reproduce older legend as history.

***An Introduction to Mythology*** (1921) set out his comparative approach. Spence treated myth as evidence of historical migration, ritual development, and cultural memory. His method stood between literary folklore, nineteenth-century comparative religion, and diffusionist speculation; it was not the same as later structuralist, functionalist, archaeological, or philological practice.

The poetry collection ***The Phoenix*** (1923) placed him in the broader Scottish literary revival. The Scottish Poetry Library identifies his later volumes *Plumes of Time* and *Weirds and Vanities*, followed by *Collected Poems*. His Scots was consciously archaizing and drew on the vocabulary and cadence of the medieval and early modern makars.

In the early 1920s Spence joined or worked with the **Scots National League**. He moved from home-rule sympathy to organized nationalist activism just as a fragmented movement was trying to create durable institutions.

### 1922: “The English Peril in Scotland”

In March **1922**, Spence published “The English Peril in Scotland” in the *Standard*. Historian Richard J. Finlay reproduces passages showing a racialized and xenophobic form of anti-English nationalism. Spence characterized some English migrants as “surplus undesirables” and invoked supposedly distinctive Saxon mannerisms. The article is documented, not an accusation inferred merely from party membership.

This rhetoric coexisted with Spence’s support for the monarchy, the British Empire or Commonwealth, and constitutional organization. It therefore does not fit a simple republican-versus-unionist axis. Nor does it by itself prove membership in a fascist organization. The record supports a more exact formulation: he articulated ethnic-cultural nationalism containing explicit anti-English stereotypes and racial categories, while pursuing a predominantly constitutional Scottish state project.

### 1924–1927: Atlantis and the Scottish National Movement

***The Problem of Atlantis*** (1924), ***Atlantis in America*** (1925), and ***The History of Atlantis*** (usually dated 1926 or 1927) made Spence a leading twentieth-century popular advocate of historical Atlantis. Drawing on Plato, Donnelly, folklore, diffusionist comparisons, and obsolete geological possibilities, he argued that an Atlantic land or civilization helped transmit culture to Europe and the Americas.

The *Geographical Journal* reviewer of *The Problem of Atlantis* admired the collection of evidence but concluded that Spence believed more than the evidence warranted. That judgment captures the central evidentiary problem: recurring motifs, pyramidal architecture, flood stories, and lexical resemblance do not independently demonstrate contact or a vanished Atlantic continent. Modern plate tectonics and ocean-floor geology are incompatible with the large recently sunken continental models on which much lost-world literature depended.

Politically, Spence became a leader of the **Scottish National Movement**. Sources date its foundation variously to 1924 and 1926; the National Library of Scotland papers begin with organizational correspondence in 1925–1926. The safest reconstruction is that it coalesced in the mid-1920s as an Edinburgh-based body and that Spence served as founder or principal organizer and president. It advocated a Scottish parliament and national self-government, often conceived within the Empire rather than as immediate republican separation.

His pamphlet ***Freedom for Scotland: The Case for Scottish Self-Government*** is normally dated **1927**, although some catalogues give 1926. It presented economic, institutional, and cultural arguments for restoring Scottish government. A modern scholarly edition in *Scotland and the Scots, 1707–2007: A Reader* reproduces the text.

His nationalist poetry collections ***Plumes of Time*** (1926) and ***Weirds and Vanities*** (1927) belong to the same phase. Literature and politics were not separate compartments: archaic Scots, Celtic prehistory, sovereignty, and cultural survival reinforced one another in his public identity.

### 1928–1929: National Party of Scotland and parliamentary candidacy

On **23 June 1928**, the Scottish National Movement, Scots National League, Glasgow University Scottish Nationalist Association, and other elements joined to form the **National Party of Scotland** (NPS), an ancestor of the modern Scottish National Party. Spence became vice-chairman or vice-president and wrote the pamphlet ***The National Party of Scotland*** (1928).

In the **Midlothian and Peebles Northern by-election** of **29 January 1929**, Spence became the party’s first Westminster parliamentary candidate. He received **842 votes (4.5 percent)**, finished fourth, and lost his **£150 deposit**. Labour’s Andrew Clarke won; the other principal candidates were Unionist John Colville and Liberal David Edwin Keir.

Contemporary *Scotsman* headlines preserve the tone of the campaign: “Mr Spence’s Campaign” (8 January), “‘You Are a Communist’: Heckler and Mr Spence” (18 January), and “Scotland Decaying: Mr Spence’s Fears” (25 January). The result was an electoral failure but an institutional milestone: it tested a distinct nationalist label before the NPS had a large organization or established electorate.

### 1930–1933: Factional retreat, Cammell friendship, Poseidon Press, and Lemuria

Spence’s relations with other nationalists deteriorated. His royalism, imperial framework, conservative cultural instincts, and style of leadership conflicted with younger or more radical figures, including circles associated with **Hugh MacDiarmid**. Cammell later said Spence resigned the NPS vice-presidency after censure over his defense of the monarchy. Other summaries place his effective departure around 1932. He appears to have retained nationalist commitments while withdrawing from a leading party role.

Spence told or wrote that he personally preferred more forceful means—Finlay quotes a reference to a rifle—but recognized that most Scots would not accept violence and did not organize an armed campaign. No evidence was found that he belonged to a paramilitary group, plotted political violence, or faced prosecution.

In spring **1931**, he met the younger poet and writer **Charles Richard Cammell**, who became a close friend, collaborator, and later memorialist. Cammell’s portrait describes a tall, increasingly stout, carefully dressed man whose conventional professional appearance concealed an intense engagement with occult subjects. He remembered Spence’s library as containing thousands of books, some rare and valuable, and described a disciplined working routine: writing approximately 10 a.m. to 1 p.m., planning books by chapters, and organizing references on cards. He credited Spence with reading French, Italian, Spanish, Latin, and German. These are firsthand recollections, not examination records.

Spence and Cammell founded **Poseidon Press** and began ***The Atlantis Quarterly*** in **June 1932**. The journal called itself a publication devoted to Atlantean and occult studies. Five issues appeared through 1933. The International Association for the Preservation of Spiritualist and Occult Periodicals has digitized the run. The enterprise was intellectually focused and small-scale; no company accounts or evidence of significant profits were located.

***The Problem of Lemuria*** appeared in 1932 or 1933. Spence proposed a lost Pacific land and a fair-complexioned culture-bearing people. A 1933 *Geographical Journal* review criticized the absence of a proper bibliography, casual citations, and the setting aside of biological and geological objections. Lemuria had begun as a nineteenth-century zoological hypothesis before plate tectonics and was transformed by Theosophy into occult racial history; Spence attempted to make the case historical and comparative, but did not supply evidence accepted by modern earth science or archaeology.

His fiction collection ***The Archer in the Arras and Other Tales of Mystery*** also appeared in 1932. It converted antiquarian and occult motifs into supernatural stories rather than presenting them as documentary claims.

### 1934–1939: Historical, literary, and Druidic work

In the later 1930s Spence continued writing while remaining outside the center of electoral politics. ***Boadicea: Warrior Queen of the Britons*** (1937) offered a popular historical biography of Boudica. His attraction to Boudica joined antiquarian reconstruction with national-resistance symbolism.

The first-edition history of his Druid book is confused. Modern references often date ***The History and Origins of Druidism*** to **1949**, while some bibliographies attach **1938** to an earlier form or edition. Without a securely inspected 1938 title page, 1949 should be used for the standard published book and 1938 retained as a catalog possibility requiring verification.

### 1940–1945: War, Nazi occult narrative, widowhood, and late syntheses

***The Occult Causes of the Present War*** (1940) interpreted National Socialism through a history of pagan, magical, Satanic, racial, and secret-society currents. It belongs to an early wartime literature that explained Nazism as occult eruption. The Nazi regime did make extensive use of racial myth, ritualized politics, symbols, and selective Germanic prehistory; individual Nazis had esoteric interests. That documentary core does not establish Spence’s larger demonological chain of causation. Historians of “Nazi occultism” treat such wartime books as evidence for the making of the legend as well as for contemporary attempts to interpret the regime.

In 1941 Cammell became editor of the spiritualist periodical ***Light*** and published work by Spence alongside Montague Summers, Victor Neuburg, and Dion Fortune. This places Spence within a British occult and psychical-research publishing network, though it does not prove agreement among all contributors.

Helen Spence died in **1942**. In the same year Lewis issued ***Will Europe Follow Atlantis?***, using the destroyed-continent story as a warning about civilizational collapse. ***The Occult Sciences in Atlantis*** followed in **1943**, reconstructing an imagined Atlantean esoteric culture. ***The Outlines of Mythology*** (1944) and ***The Religion of Ancient Mexico*** (1945) condensed earlier interests for general readers.

The first publication date of ***The Magic Arts in Celtic Britain*** is variously given as 1945 and 1949. The work gathered charms, divination, second sight, fairy belief, witchcraft, and ritual survival into a national framework. As with his other folklore books, it combines recorded tradition with speculative historical continuity.

### 1946–1953: British folklore, pension, last books, and collected poetry

Spence’s late books narrowed toward Britain and Scotland: ***British Fairy Origins*** (1946), ***The Minor Traditions of British Mythology*** (1948), ***The Fairy Tradition in Britain*** (1948), ***The History and Origins of Druidism*** (standard edition 1949), ***Second Sight: Its History and Origins*** (1951), and ***Scottish Ghosts and Goblins*** (1952).

In **1951** he received a **Civil List pension** for services to literature. This was an honor and income supplement, not evidence of large wealth. No amount was found in the consulted sources.

***Collected Poems*** appeared in Edinburgh in **1953**, two years before his death. It consolidated his claim to be read as a Scottish poet rather than only an occult compiler. Later literary assessments have been selective: the Scottish Poetry Library preserves his place in the Scottish Renaissance, but his poetry remains less widely read than the occult and mythology books.

### 1954–1955: Final year and death

No detailed medical narrative of Spence’s final illness was located. He died at 34 Howard Place, Edinburgh, on **3 March 1955**. Cammell dated his “Lament for Lewis Spence” to that day; *The Scotsman* published it on 7 March. Spence was buried in Dean Cemetery with Helen.

- Other periodical venues included the *Hibbert Journal*, *Glasgow Herald*, *The Times*, *Folklore*, *The Open Court*, *Scots Independent*, *Standard*, and other literary or occult publications. A complete article index has not been published.

### Political institutions

- **Scottish Home Rule Association**: student involvement reported but not independently verified here.
- **Scots National League**: member and activist in the early 1920s.
- **Scottish National Movement**: principal founder/leader in the mid-1920s.
- **National Party of Scotland**: founding participant and vice-chairman/vice-president from 1928; parliamentary candidate in 1929; leadership role ended after internal disputes.

### Learned and esoteric associations

- **Royal Anthropological Institute**: Fellow; frequently printed as F.R.A.I.
- **Scottish Anthropological and Folklore Society**: reported vice-president.
- **Ancient Druid Order**: honorary Presider. The precise dates and practical duties remain unclear.
- An unnamed occult order of “international range” was alleged by Cammell. Because he did not name the order or provide documents, the initiation cannot be independently tested.

### Finances, property, and business structure

The surviving public record is thin. Spence supported a household principally through journalism and an unusually large flow of commercial books. Cammell thought he looked prosperous and described an important private library, but appearance and books do not establish net worth. Poseidon Press was a real publishing project, yet no incorporation papers, partnership deed, balance sheet, creditor file, or bankruptcy was located. The 1951 Civil List pension suggests recognized literary service and possibly a need for support; its amount was not established.

No verified estate valuation, probate inventory, investment portfolio, landholding, patent, industrial technology, or controlling interest in a substantial company was found. No patent or invention is known. His “technology” was editorial: card-indexed research, serial production of reference books, and reuse of a large library across related subjects.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Scottish nationalism

Spence’s politics developed from home-rule activism into organized nationalism. His preferred end-state was not consistently the later model of a republic outside all British institutions. His speeches and the recollections around him indicate support for a restored Scottish state or parliament under the Crown and within an imperial or commonwealth structure.

Three strands should be kept distinct:

1. **Institution-building:** He helped move Scottish nationalism from small leagues toward the NPS and stood in its first Westminster contest.
2. **Cultural restoration:** He treated Scots language, Celtic and pre-Celtic antiquity, folklore, and poetry as materials of national recovery.
3. **Ethnic rhetoric:** He sometimes described English migration and Scottish decline in racialized, hostile terms. This is documented in 1922 and should not be erased by later constitutional respectability.

Later commentators have variously called his politics conservative, romantic, racialist, “tartan Tory,” proto-fascist, or anti-fascist. None of these labels alone reconstructs the record. He was royalist and empire-oriented; he used racial-national language; he quarreled with left-nationalists; he wrote an anti-Nazi wartime book; and no evidence located places him in a fascist party or collaborationist organization.

### Mythology and comparative religion

Spence treated myths as layered historical documents. He assumed that similarities across continents could disclose migration, lost centers of civilization, or common ritual origins. This was attractive to general readers and allowed broad synthesis, but it repeatedly converted analogy into genealogy without sufficient controls.

His Mesoamerican work helped make K’iche’, Maya, Mexican, and Peruvian narratives accessible to English-speaking audiences at a time when such material was difficult to obtain. It also filtered those traditions through European editions, evolutionary anthropology, and a compiler who was not demonstrably fluent in the relevant Indigenous languages.

### Atlantis, Lemuria, and diffusionism

Spence distinguished himself from purely Theosophical lost-world writers by presenting tables of parallels, archaeological reports, folklore, and geological claims as an empirical case. Nevertheless, the method retained the underlying problem of diffusionism: similar human solutions and narrative patterns were treated as signs of a single vanished source.

His Atlantis was more than a place. It became a mechanism connecting Cro-Magnon peoples, fair-complexioned culture bearers, megaliths, Egypt, the Americas, esoteric wisdom, and catastrophe. These racial and civilizational hierarchies were common in early twentieth-century popular anthropology but are not accepted frameworks in contemporary archaeology or genetics.

### Occultism and psychical subjects

Spence’s encyclopedic occultism ranged from ceremonial magic and demonology to spiritualism, second sight, and folklore. Cammell’s testimony portrays him as a practicing initiate; a competing modern local account sees him primarily as a scholarly writer and honorary Druid. The documentary record establishes sustained participation in occult publishing and Druidic office, not the inner content of private ritual practice.

### Poetry and literary Scots

Spence’s poetry linked personal antiquarianism with the Scottish Renaissance. He used Scots in a consciously learned, sometimes sixteenth-century register. This put him near—but not always politically aligned with—Hugh MacDiarmid, Marion Angus, Violet Jacob, and other revival writers. His later reputation as poet is modest but not invented: four lifetime collections are catalogued by the Scottish Poetry Library.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Helen Stewart Bruce Spence

Wife from 1899 until her death in 1942 and mother of his four children. The documentary record says little about her independent life. Her long participation in a household dependent on freelance production was materially important even where formal authorship was Spence’s.

### Rhoda, Claire, Madge, and Toby Spence

The daughters reportedly served at different times as secretaries; Rhoda later dealt with copyright. Toby is named in family accounts but remains otherwise obscure in the sources consulted.

### Charles Richard Cammell

Friend from 1931, Poseidon Press partner, *Atlantis Quarterly* co-editor, occult-network intermediary, and posthumous memorialist. Cammell is indispensable but not neutral. His claim that Spence belonged to an unnamed international occult order and his “Last of the Bards” elegy amplified the initiated-sage image.

### Hugh MacDiarmid and nationalist rivals

MacDiarmid belonged to the literary and political world in which Spence operated, but their ideological temperaments diverged. The NPS brought together constitutionalists, cultural revivalists, socialists, royalists, and more radical nationalists; conflict over policy and leadership helped push Spence from office. “Enemy” is too strong for the available evidence, but **political and cultural rival** is justified.

### John MacCormick, James Valentine, Roland E. Muirhead, and William Gillies

These organizers formed part of the coalition around Spence that converted separate home-rule and nationalist societies into the NPS. **John MacCormick** and **James Valentine**, working through the Glasgow University Scottish Nationalist Association, pressed the older organizations toward amalgamation. **Roland E. Muirhead** represented the Scottish Home Rule and organized-nationalist current and became a senior NPS figure. **William Gillies** was associated with the Scots National League. Their cooperation with Spence was institutional rather than evidence of uniform ideology: the new party deliberately contained independence and home-rule factions.

### R. B. Cunninghame Graham and Compton Mackenzie

The writer and former parliamentarian **Robert Bontine Cunninghame Graham** presided over the unification meeting and gave the movement an elder-statesman figure; the novelist **Compton Mackenzie** was another prominent literary nationalist in the NPS world. Spence belonged to the same attempt to turn cultural prestige into a political constituency. No close private friendship with either man was established from the sources consulted.

### Sir William Robertson Nicoll and *The British Weekly*

Spence’s London employer connected him to a major Nonconformist publishing network. Direct mentorship is possible but not documented in the sources consulted; employment should not be inflated into discipleship.

### Ignatius Donnelly

Donnelly’s ***Atlantis: The Antediluvian World*** was a principal intellectual predecessor. There was no personal meeting—Donnelly died in 1901 before Spence’s Atlantis phase—but the conceptual debt is clear.

### Montague Summers, Victor Neuburg, and Dion Fortune

These occult writers appeared with Spence in *Light* under Cammell in 1941. This documents a shared publication network, not necessarily friendship, collaboration, or doctrinal agreement.

### Robert E. Howard

No meeting or correspondence between Spence and the American pulp writer Robert E. Howard has been established. Jeffrey Shanks argues from textual and geographical parallels that Spence, together with the Theosophical writer William Scott-Elliot, helped shape Howard’s lost-world geography, Cro-Magnon Atlanteans, Kull stories, and Hyborian Age. This is a scholarly influence argument, not a documented personal relationship.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Racialized and anti-English political writing — documented

Spence’s 1922 “English Peril” article used collective biological and character stereotypes about English migrants. Later nationalist historiography sometimes minimizes this as ordinary interwar rhetoric or treats it as decisive proof of fascism. The primary fact is narrower and firmer: the article exists and contains explicitly racialized hostility. The wider label remains interpretive.

### Political violence — rhetoric documented; organization not found

Finlay reports Spence’s statement that he would personally prefer a rifle while acknowledging that the Scottish public would reject such means. No evidence located shows weapons procurement, a violent cell, conspiracy, assault, sedition charge, or imprisonment. The difference between provocative preference and operational violence is material.

### Fascism and Nazism — disputed characterization

Some modern discussions associate the ethnic nationalism, racial typologies, and occult themes of writers like Spence with fascist currents. Other researchers emphasize his constitutional activity and 1940 attack on Nazism. The evidence consulted supports neither exonerating silence nor an undocumented party label:

- **Documented:** racial and anti-English rhetoric; racialized prehistory; monarchy and Empire; conflict with left-nationalists; anti-Nazi wartime polemic.
- **Not established:** membership in the British Union of Fascists, a Nazi organization, or an intelligence-linked far-right network.
- **Open interpretive question:** how his cultural racialism should be situated within the spectrum of interwar European nationalisms.

### Scholarly reliability — sustained criticism, not fraud finding

Specialist reviewers criticized inaccuracies, inadequate training, weak citation, and conclusions exceeding evidence. This is a controversy over method and competence. No court, university tribunal, or professional body was found to have ruled that Spence fabricated evidence or committed scholarly fraud. Much of his problem arose from uncritical synthesis of sources and obsolete paradigms rather than demonstrable invention of documents.

### Representation of Indigenous traditions

Spence’s books popularized Mesoamerican and North American traditions but were produced without documented community collaboration or fieldwork. He often spoke of large civilizational or racial types and freely rewrote translated narratives for a British readership. Modern readers should distinguish preservation-through-publication from authoritative Indigenous voice. No evidence was found that the communities concerned authorized him as representative or translator.

### “Translation” of the *Popol Vuh* — misleading later attribution

Spence did not produce a direct K’iche’-to-English critical translation. His 1908 volume was an adaptation and popular synthesis based on prior European work. Calling it simply “Spence’s translation” is a bibliographical exaggeration that obscures both the K’iche’ text and the intermediaries.

### Criminal, civil, and financial record — none located

No arrest, criminal charge, conviction, imprisonment, civil lawsuit, fraud proceeding, corruption investigation, money-laundering allegation, sexual-abuse allegation, bankruptcy, intelligence affiliation, or regulatory sanction involving Spence was found in the sources searched. This is a negative research result, not proof that no private dispute ever occurred.

## Health, Habits, and Personal Manner

Cammell described Spence as physically tall and well built, later corpulent, immaculately dressed, and outwardly conventional. He portrayed him as disciplined, domestic, moderate in alcohol, and a user of tobacco. No evidence of alcoholism or drug addiction was found.

Cammell also described anxiety or hypochondriacal concern about illness. This is a friend’s observation, not a clinical diagnosis. No psychiatric hospitalization, diagnosed psychological disorder, suicide attempt, major accident, or chronic disease was identified. The cause of death remains unknown in the accessible record.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. Atlantis as a historical mother-civilization

**Story:** A substantial Atlantic land or civilization existed in late prehistory, influenced Europe, North Africa, and the Americas, and was destroyed by catastrophe; myths and cultural parallels preserve its memory.

**Appearance and circulation:** Spence developed the case from *The Problem of Atlantis* (1924) through *Atlantis in America*, *The History of Atlantis*, *The Atlantis Quarterly*, *Will Europe Follow Atlantis?*, and *The Occult Sciences in Atlantis*. He inherited important elements from Ignatius Donnelly and circulated them through commercial books and an occult periodical.

**Evidence for:** Plato’s story; widespread flood traditions; genuine trans-oceanic questions; formal similarities among myths and monuments; then-current uncertainty about ocean geology.

**Evidence against:** No accepted archaeological layer, inscriptional chain, genetic history, or oceanographic evidence demonstrates Spence’s Atlantic civilization. Modern plate tectonics and mapping of the Atlantic floor exclude the required recently submerged continent. Similarity does not by itself demonstrate contact.

**Reputational effect:** Atlantis made Spence internationally recognizable and kept his books in print, while moving him outside academic archaeology. It also influenced fantasy world-building and modern esoteric histories.

### 2. Lemuria and fair-complexioned Pacific culture bearers

**Story:** A lost Pacific land supported an advanced people whose traditions survive in Asia, Oceania, and the Americas.

**Appearance and circulation:** *The Problem of Lemuria* (1932/1933), in conversation with earlier zoological hypotheses and Theosophical occult history.

**Evidence for:** The nineteenth-century distribution puzzle that originally prompted the name “Lemuria,” plus Spence’s cultural comparisons.

**Evidence against:** Plate tectonics accounts for continental relationships without a recently drowned Pacific continent. Contemporary reviewers already noted Spence’s avoidance of contrary biological and geological evidence.

**Reputational effect:** The book linked him to Theosophical lost-race narratives even though he tried to distinguish his evidentiary method from revelation-based occult history.

### 3. Spence as direct translator or Indigenous authority

**Story:** Spence translated the *Popol Vuh* and transmitted Maya/K’iche’ sacred knowledge directly.

**Appearance and circulation:** Modern reprints, book descriptions, and brief biographies often use “translated by” or “Mayan scholar” without qualification.

**Evidence for:** He produced one of the early accessible English popularizations and engaged closely with printed Mesoamerican sources.

**Evidence against:** The book declares its dependence on previous European editions; no evidence establishes direct translation from K’iche’ or field training in a K’iche’ community.

**Reputational effect:** The shorthand expanded his authority while obscuring the text’s colonial editorial chain.

### 4. Secret initiate and Druid elder

**Story:** Spence was not merely an occult historian but a high initiate possessing secret knowledge, and a Druid leader in a lineage of ancient authority.

**Appearance and circulation:** Cammell’s *Heart of Scotland* (1956) says Spence was initiated in an unnamed international order; modern Druid writers emphasize his office as honorary Presider of the Ancient Druid Order.

**Evidence for:** Long occult engagement, personal testimony from a close friend, Druidic office, Poseidon Press, *Atlantis Quarterly*, and *Light* network.

**Evidence against or limiting:** The order is unnamed and no charter, membership roll, ritual record, or correspondence was produced. “Honorary Presider” can be a literary distinction rather than evidence of controlling ritual authority.

**Reputational effect:** The story turned a methodical freelance compiler into an esoteric sage and helped later Druid and occult communities claim him as a predecessor.

### 5. The father lost at sea as occult destiny

**Story:** The unexplained loss of Spence’s father in the Atlantic prefigured or psychically caused Lewis’s obsession with Atlantis.

**Appearance and circulation:** This is mainly a retrospective biographical association, encouraged by the dramatic *Naronic* disappearance.

**Evidence for:** The loss is factual, traumatic, Atlantic, and unresolved.

**Evidence against:** No statement from Spence linking the event to his Atlantis theory was located; the Atlantis books began three decades later and have identifiable literary sources.

**Reputational effect:** It gives his intellectual career a fated narrative shape that the documents do not establish.

### 6. Honorary doctorate

**Story:** The University of Edinburgh awarded Spence an honorary D.Litt.

**Appearance and circulation:** Repeated in some occult and local biographies.

**Evidence for:** Persistent secondary repetition and his recognized literary career.

**Evidence against or limiting:** The accessible University of Edinburgh and National Library descriptions consulted do not confirm it; no conferral date or citation was located.

**Reputational effect:** It upgrades a freelance popular writer into a university-honored scholar. Until an official roll is produced, it remains unverified.

### 7. “Last of the Bards”

**Story:** Spence was the terminal representative of an ancient Scottish bardic and Druidic wisdom.

**Appearance and circulation:** Cammell’s “Lament for Lewis Spence,” written on 3 March and published in *The Scotsman* on 7 March 1955, imagines him drinking from the Druids’ fountain and departing for the Island of the Blest.

**Evidence:** It is demonstrably a poetic memorial, not a literal institutional genealogy.

**Reputational effect:** The elegy crystallized Spence’s self-presentation as poet, nationalist antiquary, and occult custodian.

### 8. Hundreds of separate Spence books

**Story:** Online catalogues imply that Spence wrote 300–400 distinct books.

**Appearance and circulation:** Open Library, Goodreads, print-on-demand catalogues, and marketplace metadata aggregate editions, translations, alternate titles, chapter extracts, and posthumous compilations.

**Evidence against:** Contemporary biographical summaries say more than forty books, which matches the number of reconstructable lifetime book-length works. Modern titles such as *Celtic Spells and Charms*, *The Atlantis of Plato*, *The Evidence for Lemuria from Myth and Magic*, and *The Satanic Element in Nazism* are often extracts or retitlings, not newly discovered lifetime books.

**Reputational effect:** Inflated counts make him appear even more prolific and conceal the recycling structure of the posthumous occult market.

## Books and Major Works About Lewis Spence

No full modern cradle-to-grave scholarly biography was located. The following works are therefore especially important, despite differing scope and reliability.

### Biographical and memorial sources

- **Charles Richard Cammell, *Heart of Scotland* (London: Robert Hale, 1956).** Contains the fullest firsthand portrait of Spence: appearance, home, library, work routine, family secretarial help, political disappointment, Poseidon Press, and alleged occult initiation. ## Death, Burial, and Estate

Spence died in Edinburgh on 3 March 1955, reportedly at his Howard Place home, aged eighty. His cause of death is not specified in the accessible sources used here. He was buried with Helen in Dean Cemetery.

No probate calendar entry or reliable estate value was located. Rhoda Spence’s later copyright correspondence shows that literary rights remained a family concern. The subsequent public-domain status of much of his corpus encouraged widespread reprinting, extraction, and metadata duplication.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Scottish nationalism

Spence is an institutional ancestor of the Scottish National Party through the Scottish National Movement and National Party of Scotland. His 1929 candidacy is important less for its 842 votes than for establishing a separate nationalist electoral presence. Later party memory tends to retain the organizational contribution and downplay his royalism, imperial model, occult interests, and racialized anti-English writing. Historical reconstruction requires all four.

### Folklore and mythology

His myth books remained accessible gateways for general readers. They preserve a record of what early twentieth-century British publishers considered the world’s mythic canons, but not a neutral transcript of those traditions. Their continuing value is literary, historiographical, and bibliographical; factual claims require checking against modern language-specific scholarship and community knowledge.

### Occultism and modern Druidry

*An Encyclopaedia of Occultism* remains frequently reprinted and quoted. Spence’s Ancient Druid Order office, Celtic books, and Cammell’s initiate portrait made him useful to modern Druid and pagan lineages. Gerald Gardner and later occult writers drew on the broad reference culture to which Spence contributed. Citation or thematic borrowing does not prove direct organizational descent.

### Lost-world fiction and popular culture

Spence’s maps, racial migrations, and catastrophic continents entered the imaginative reservoir of twentieth-century fantasy. The most developed scholarly case concerns Robert E. Howard. More diffuse influence appears in pulp lost-world stories, alternative archaeology, occult publishing, and online Atlantis culture.

### Scholarly reinterpretation

Modern scholarship tends to treat Spence not as a discoverer of Atlantis or a primary authority on Indigenous traditions, but as a revealing intermediary among journalism, folklore, popular anthropology, nationalism, occultism, and commercial publishing. His work records the permeability of those fields before academic specialization hardened. It also exposes how apparently neutral myth compilation could carry racial hierarchies and nationalist projects.

### Reputation split

His posthumous identity divides into several partially disconnected figures:

- the early Scottish nationalist organizer;
- the poet of archaizing Scots;
- the popularizer of world mythology;
- the encyclopedist of occultism;
- the Atlantis and Lemuria theorist;
- the alleged initiate and Druid elder;
- the public-domain “author” of hundreds of duplicated or extracted titles.

All derive from the same career, but no single one is a sufficient biography.

## Atlas Connections

### Plato

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Spence repeatedly wrote about Plato as the earliest literary authority for Atlantis, most substantially in *The Problem of Atlantis* (1924), *The History of Atlantis* (1926/1927), and later Atlantean books. He also discussed Plato’s conception of the afterlife in *An Introduction to Mythology* (1921). No personal connection was possible; this was sustained critical and interpretive engagement with Plato’s writings. [Spence, *An Introduction to Mythology*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45048/45048-h/45048-h.htm), [Encyclopedia.com, “Atlantis”](https://www.encyclopedia.com/literature-and-arts/classical-literature-mythology-and-folklore/folklore-and-mythology/atlantis)

### Ignatius Loyola Donnelly

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Spence treated Donnelly’s *Atlantis: The Antediluvian World* (1882) as a foundational modern Atlantis argument, adopting and revising its diffusionist model in his own Atlantis books beginning in 1924. Donnelly died in 1901, so no meeting or correspondence is established; the crossing is literary and intellectual influence. [Lewis Spence biography](https://www.encyclopedia.com/science/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/spence-james-lewis-thomas-chalmers-1874-1955), [Encyclopedia.com, “Atlantis”](https://www.encyclopedia.com/literature-and-arts/classical-literature-mythology-and-folklore/folklore-and-mythology/atlantis)

### Helena Petrovna Blavatsky

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Spence discussed Blavatsky’s Theosophical treatment of Lemuria and lost races while attempting to distinguish his own comparative and allegedly empirical case from revelation-based Theosophy in *The Problem of Lemuria* (1932/1933). This was engagement with her published doctrine, not evidence of personal contact; Blavatsky died in 1891. [Contemporary discussion of Spence’s *Problem of Lemuria*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/geographical-journal/article/abs/problem-of-lemuria-the-sunken-continent-of-the-pacific-by-lewis-spence/), [discussion linking Spence’s Lemuria survey with Blavatsky](https://journals.jcu.edu.au/linq/article/download/978/935)

### Rudolf Steiner

- **[T1-] Reported fact:** Spence’s survey of Lemurian theories included the occult lost-continent tradition associated with Steiner as well as Blavatsky. No correspondence, meeting, or shared organization between Spence and Steiner has been documented. [Discussion of Spence’s Lemuria sources](https://journals.jcu.edu.au/linq/article/download/978/935)

### Friedrich Max Müller

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** In *An Introduction to Mythology* (1921), Spence devoted sustained attention to Müller’s philological or “solar-myth” school, summarized his career and theory that myth could arise through a “disease of language,” and assessed later anthropological objections to that method. [Spence, *An Introduction to Mythology*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45048/45048-h/45048-h.htm)

### Homer

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Spence discussed Homer and the disputed authorship, formation, religious significance, and mythological contents of the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* at length in *An Introduction to Mythology* (1921), treating the poems as principal written sources for Greek mythology. [Spence, *An Introduction to Mythology*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45048/45048-h/45048-h.htm)

### Hesiod

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Spence wrote about Hesiod’s accounts of Pandora, Elysium, Cronus, and the Greek gods in *An Introduction to Mythology* (1921), comparing the Hesiodic poems with Homer as sources that fixed the character of Greek divinities. [Spence, *An Introduction to Mythology*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45048/45048-h/45048-h.htm)

### Brothers Grimm

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Spence engaged with Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm’s *Deutsche Mythologie*, *Deutsche Sagen*, and *Kinder- und Hausmärchen* when tracing the development of folklore and mythological scholarship in *An Introduction to Mythology* (1921). The connection is bibliographical; the brothers died before Spence’s birth. [Spence, *An Introduction to Mythology*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45048/45048-h/45048-h.htm)

### Saint Augustine of Hippo

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Spence cited and discussed Augustine’s treatment of pagan myth while surveying ancient and Christian interpretations of mythology in *An Introduction to Mythology* (1921). [Spence, *An Introduction to Mythology*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45048/45048-h/45048-h.htm)

### Miguel de Cervantes Saavedra

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** In *Legends and Romances of Spain* (1920), Spence discussed Cervantes, quoted his judgment of the romance *Palmerin of England*, and devoted a section to *Don Quixote* and Cervantes’s literary development. [Spence, *Legends and Romances of Spain*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/38530/38530-h/38530-h.htm)

### Hernán Cortés

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Spence repeatedly wrote about Cortés in *The Myths of Mexico & Peru* (1913), including the landing at Veracruz, the march against Tenochtitlan, the conquest’s violence, the episode of Cortés’s horse at Petén Itzá, and the reported identification of the Spaniard with the returning Quetzalcoatl. Spence also listed an English edition of Cortés’s letters among his sources. [Spence, *The Myths of Mexico & Peru*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/53080/53080-h/53080-h.htm)

### Motecuhzoma Xocoyotzin (Moctezuma II)

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Spence narrated episodes from Moctezuma II’s reign in *The Myths of Mexico & Peru* (1913), including the reception of Cortés, the Tlalhuicole story, and the legend that Moctezuma’s sister Papantzin returned from death to warn him of the Spanish conquest. Spence presented the latter explicitly as a traditional legend rather than independently verified history. [Spence, *The Myths of Mexico & Peru*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/53080/53080-h/53080-h.htm)

### Francisco Pizarro González

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Spence wrote about Pizarro and the conquest of Peru in *The Myths of Mexico & Peru* (1913), describing the conquistador critically and placing the invasion within his historical introduction to Inca religion and mythology. [Spence, *The Myths of Mexico & Peru*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/53080/53080-h/53080-h.htm)

### El Inca Garcilaso de la Vega

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Spence used Garcilaso’s *Royal Commentaries of the Incas* as a principal written source for the Peruvian portions of *The Myths of Mexico & Peru* (1913), praised his narrative as an essential account of the Inca past, and listed the 1609 work in his bibliography. [Spence, *The Myths of Mexico & Peru*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/53080/53080-h/53080-h.htm)

### Nebuchadnezzar II

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Spence supplied a discrete historical treatment of Nebuchadnezzar II’s reign, campaigns, building activity, capture of Jerusalem, and later legendary reputation in *Myths and Legends of Babylonia and Assyria* (1916). Some chronology in that popular account was already vulnerable to contemporary specialist criticism. [Spence, *Myths and Legends of Babylonia and Assyria*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/45137/pg45137-images.html)

### Herodotus of Halicarnassus

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Spence repeatedly evaluated Herodotus as a source in *Myths and Legends of Babylonia and Assyria* (1916), comparing his measurements and description of Babylon and the temple of Bel with archaeological findings and noting where the ancient account appeared exaggerated or partly corroborated. [Spence, *Myths and Legends of Babylonia and Assyria*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/45137/pg45137-images.html)

### Ctesias of Cnidus

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** In *Myths and Legends of Babylonia and Assyria* (1916), Spence discussed Ctesias alongside Herodotus as an ancient authority for Babylon, testing the dimensions attributed to the city against the excavated topography. [Spence, *Myths and Legends of Babylonia and Assyria*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/45137/pg45137-images.html)

### Thomas the Apostle

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Spence recorded traditions associating Thomas the Apostle with pre-Columbian America in *The Myths of Mexico & Peru* (1913): colonial chroniclers identified the expected culture hero with Saint Thomas, while related traditions connected him with Maya and Andean crosses. Spence reported these as missionary-era identifications and legends, not demonstrated apostolic travel. [Spence, *The Myths of Mexico & Peru*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/53080/53080-h/53080-h.htm)

### Dion Fortune

- **[T4] Documented fact:** In 1941 Spence and Fortune occupied the same British occult–spiritualist publishing milieu: Charles Richard Cammell, then editor of *Light*, published contributions from both writers. This establishes a shared periodical setting, but no meeting, correspondence, friendship, or direct response by either writer to the other has been located. [Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids, “Notes on Lewis Spence”](https://druidry.org/resources/notes-on-lewis-spence-1874-1955)

No stronger T5 personal meeting, T9 object-transmission, or subject-to-counterpart T2 prophecy connection was located among the roster. Mere contemporaneity, shared occult interests, or appearance in the same broad intellectual tradition has therefore not been counted.
