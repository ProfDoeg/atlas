# Thomas Aquinas: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Reconstructable life history, writings, networks, controversies, legends, cult, and reception  
**Evidentiary caution:** Thomas left almost no autobiographical writing. The principal narrative sources—the canonization depositions of 1319 and 1321, William of Tocco’s *Ystoria sancti Thome de Aquino*, Bernard Gui’s life, Peter Calo, and Tolomeo of Lucca—were written or collected decades after his death and for institutional or canonization purposes. They preserve testimony from acquaintances but also hagiographic patterning. Modern chronology below follows the critical syntheses of Jean-Pierre Torrell, Pasquale Porro, James A. Weisheipl, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP), with unresolved points marked.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Latin name:** *Thomas de Aquino*; in medieval manuscripts also *Thomas Aquinas* (“Thomas of Aquino”).
- **Italian:** Tommaso d’Aquino; **French:** Thomas d’Aquin; **Spanish:** Tomás de Aquino.
- **Religious status:** Dominican friar (*Ordo Praedicatorum*, Order of Preachers), priest, theologian, university master.
- **Later titles:** Saint Thomas Aquinas; *Doctor Angelicus* (“Angelic Doctor”); *Doctor Communis* (“Common/Universal Doctor”); sometimes *Doctor Eucharisticus*. These are posthumous honorifics, not names used in ordinary life.
- **Nickname/story:** *bos mutus* or “dumb/silent ox,” supposedly used by fellow students because of his size and taciturnity; Albert the Great’s prophecy that the ox’s “bellowing” would fill the world belongs to the early biographical tradition, not a contemporary transcript.
- **Birth:** probably 1224/25, conventionally 1225. No exact day survives. The strongest modern location is the Aquino family castle at **Roccasecca**, then in the Kingdom of Sicily, between Rome and Naples. “Aquino” identifies the family/territory rather than necessarily the town of birth. A later Calabrian claim for Belcastro is local tradition, not the critical consensus.
- **Death:** 7 March 1274, Cistercian abbey of Fossanova, Papal States, while travelling toward the Second Council of Lyon.
- **Age:** approximately 48 or 49.
- **Burial and principal relics:** first Fossanova; principal bones transferred to the Dominican church of the Jacobins, Toulouse, in 1369; moved to Saint-Sernin during the French Revolution and returned to the Jacobins in 1974. Competing skull claims survive at Toulouse and Priverno.
- **Canonization:** 18 July 1323 by Pope John XXII at Avignon, after inquiries in Naples (1319) and Fossanova (1321).
- **Doctor of the Church:** proclaimed by Pius V in 1567.
- **Patronage:** Leo XIII made him patron of Catholic schools, colleges, and universities in 1880; feast now normally 28 January in the Roman calendar.

## Source Base and Its Limits

The earliest dossiers are not neutral biographies. William of Tocco, a Dominican who had heard Thomas teach late in life, gathered material for canonization beginning in 1317. The first formal inquiry heard approximately forty-two depositions in Naples in 1319; the second, at Fossanova in November 1321, heard more than one hundred witnesses. Their testimony is valuable for remembered routines, journeys, death, physical appearance, and the early cult, but the legal objective was proof of sanctity and miracles. Bernard Gui and Peter Calo used overlapping Dominican materials. Tolomeo of Lucca knew Thomas and supplies some independent chronology. The SEP rightly notes that the authenticity of many colorful stories cannot now be determined.

For writings and dating, the continuing **Leonine Edition** (*Sancti Thomae de Aquino Opera omnia iussu impensaque Leonis XIII P.M. edita*) is the critical standard. Corpus Thomisticum provides electronic Leonine texts, ancient work catalogues, life sources, and bibliographic tools. The Oxford chronological catalogue limits itself to works whose authenticity is not seriously disputed.

## Family and Ancestry

Thomas belonged to the lesser/high military nobility of the northern Kingdom of Sicily. His family’s lands lay in a strategic frontier zone repeatedly contested by emperor and pope.

- **Father:** Landulf (Landolfo), lord/knight of Roccasecca, connected with the counts of Aquino and serving in the political-military world of Emperor Frederick II. Later genealogical claims sometimes inflate him into a “count”; the precise title varies in retellings.
- **Mother:** Theodora, usually identified with the noble Teate/Caracciolo-Rossi milieu. Exact ancestry is less securely documented than popular pedigrees imply.
- **Paternal relative:** Sinibald, abbot of Monte Cassino, traditionally called Thomas’s uncle. The family apparently imagined a prestigious Benedictine future for its youngest son, perhaps ultimately the abbacy.
- **Siblings:** the standard reconstruction gives at least eight older siblings. Brothers commonly named **Aimo/Aimone**, **Rinaldo/Raynald**, and **Landulf**; sisters **Marotta** (later a Benedictine abbess), **Theodora**, **Maria**, and **Adelasia/another uncertainly named sister**. Medieval records and modern genealogies do not produce a perfectly stable list.
- **Rinaldo:** associated with Frederick II’s service and reportedly executed in 1246 after involvement in conspiracy against the emperor. Details vary.
- **Marotta:** entered Benedictine life and became an abbess. Hagiography later made deceased siblings figures in Thomas’s visions.
- **Marriage/partners/children:** none. As a Dominican cleric Thomas professed celibacy. No credible evidence identifies a sexual partner or child.

### Family politics and money

The family possessed castles, land, armed retainers, and court connections. Thomas therefore grew up materially privileged. His entry into the mendicant Dominicans threatened the family’s dynastic strategy: unlike an abbacy at wealthy Monte Cassino, Dominican profession offered no inheritable office and placed him in a recently founded order dependent on corporate poverty. The family’s seizure and confinement of him should be read both as coercion and as a dispute over lineage, patronage, and clerical career capital.

Thomas personally left no known business enterprise, investment portfolio, patent, or separately managed estate. His productive infrastructure was institutional: Dominican housing, university chairs, papal and royal commissions, scribes, secretaries, parchment, libraries, and translation networks.

## Childhood and Early Life, c. 1224/25–1244

### Roccasecca and Monte Cassino

At roughly age five Thomas was placed at the Benedictine abbey of Monte Cassino as an oblate or pupil, probably around 1230. There he learned Latin literacy, chant, scripture, and elementary liberal arts. The often-repeated anecdote that the child repeatedly asked “What is God?” is hagiographic characterization rather than a documented verbatim utterance.

Monte Cassino sat inside the struggle between Frederick II and Pope Gregory IX. In 1239 Frederick expelled or dispersed monks he regarded as politically unreliable. Thomas consequently left the abbey and went to **Naples**, a major intellectual and political turning point.

### University of Naples, c. 1239–1244

Frederick II’s University of Naples was a state-founded institution independent of papal Paris. Thomas studied the arts—grammar, logic, natural philosophy, and the newly available Aristotle. Later sources name **Peter of Ireland** as a teacher in logic and natural philosophy and **Master Martin** in grammar. The curriculum exposed him to Aristotelian texts and Arabic commentarial traditions earlier and more directly than a monastic education would have.

At Naples he encountered the Dominicans at San Domenico Maggiore. In spring 1244, probably in April, he received their habit. The decisive influences were the order’s combination of common life, preaching, formal study, and mobility.

## Detention by His Family, 1244–1245

Dominican superiors tried to move the novice toward Rome and Paris, perhaps partly to remove him from family pressure. His brothers intercepted him near Aquapendente or on the road north and returned him to family custody. He was held for roughly a year, probably at Monte San Giovanni and/or Roccasecca. “Kidnapping” accurately describes the coercive seizure in modern language, but no medieval criminal prosecution followed; kin exercised practical power beyond anything a modern court would permit.

During confinement his Dominican habit was reportedly taken from him, family members pressed him to abandon the order, and sisters were sent to persuade him. The sources say he instead taught or converted them to his choice. The famous temptation episode—brothers introducing a prostitute or sexually available woman, Thomas driving her away with a burning brand, marking a cross, and angels girding him with perpetual chastity—appears in the canonization/hagiographic tradition. Its evidentiary status is **late sworn story shaped as chastity miracle**, not independently established event.

The resolution was face-saving. According to the tradition, his mother allowed or arranged a covert escape through a window rather than publicly concede. By 1245 he had rejoined the Dominicans. Pope Innocent IV’s supposed offer to let him become abbot of Monte Cassino while retaining the Dominican habit is reported in later sources; its precise documentary basis and timing are uncertain.

## Education and Formation: Paris and Cologne, 1245–1252

Thomas went to the Dominican convent of Saint-Jacques in Paris, the order’s university base. There he studied under **Albert the Great (Albertus Magnus)**. When Albert was sent to organize the Dominican *studium generale* at Cologne in 1248, Thomas accompanied him and remained until about 1252.

Albert was Thomas’s most consequential teacher: encyclopedic natural philosophy, Aristotle, Arabic commentators, Dionysian/Neoplatonic theology, and the conviction that pagan philosophy could be critically appropriated. Thomas was not merely an “Aristotelian”: Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, Boethius, Proclus, Avicenna, Averroes, Maimonides, John Damascene, patristic exegesis, canon law, and scripture are pervasive sources.

At Cologne Thomas may have served as *magister studentium* and began biblical exposition. Ordination to the priesthood is usually placed around 1250–51, perhaps by Archbishop Conrad of Hochstaden, but the exact date and officiant are not securely recorded.

The “dumb ox” story locates his fellow students’ mockery here or in Paris and Albert’s recognition of his ability after a disputation. It captures remembered traits—large build, silence, intellectual intensity—but its polished prophecy marks it as literary memory.

## Early Career and First Paris Regency, 1252–1259

Thomas returned to Paris in 1252 to complete the Dominican theological cursus:

- **1252–54:** bachelor charged with biblical lectures (*baccalaureus biblicus*).
- **1254–56:** bachelor lecturing on Peter Lombard’s *Sentences*; the resulting *Scriptum super libros Sententiarum* is his first vast synthesis.
- **1256:** licensed/master in theology after institutional conflict.
- **1256–59:** regent master in theology at Paris.

### Mendicant–secular controversy

Secular masters led by **William of Saint-Amour** opposed Dominican and Franciscan access to university chairs, questioned mendicant privileges, and attacked the friars as signs of apocalyptic disorder. Teaching was disrupted and promotion delayed. Thomas answered in *Contra impugnantes Dei cultum et religionem* (1256), defending religious poverty, preaching, and mendicant teaching. Pope Alexander IV condemned William’s *De periculis novissimorum temporum* and ordered admission of the friars. Thomas’s mastership was ultimately recognized in 1256/57.

This was an institutional and political victory, not a private lawsuit. It linked university governance, papal jurisdiction, secular clerical livelihoods, and the economic model of mendicancy.

### Teaching and first mature works

His inaugural lecture is associated with *Rigans montes de superioribus suis*. He produced *De ente et essentia*, *De principiis naturae*, *De veritate*, quodlibetal disputations, biblical lectures, and the beginnings of a distinctive synthesis of metaphysics, cognition, ethics, and sacred doctrine.

## Italy, 1259–1268

The Dominican system rotated regent masters. Thomas returned to the Roman province after 1259 and worked at several sites; older biographies sometimes turn this into a continuous “papal court” appointment, but modern scholarship is more cautious.

### Naples/Anagni and Orvieto, c. 1259–1265

The provincial chapter made him general preacher in 1260. He was associated with Anagni and then, from 1261, Orvieto, where Pope Urban IV’s curia resided. He taught friars and completed much of the *Summa contra Gentiles*.

Urban IV commissioned or received:

- *Contra errores Graecorum*, assembled from a defective Latin florilegium of Greek fathers to aid Latin–Greek theological controversy;
- the *Catena aurea*, a chained patristic commentary on the four Gospels;
- probably the office and Mass texts for the feast of **Corpus Christi** after the feast’s universal extension in 1264.

Authorship of individual Eucharistic hymns—*Pange lingua* (including *Tantum ergo*), *Sacris solemniis* (including *Panis angelicus*), *Verbum supernum* (including *O salutaris hostia*), and *Lauda Sion*—is traditional and strongly embedded in liturgical reception, but textual scholars distinguish the well-supported office as a whole from questions about every component’s authorship and redaction.

### Rome (Santa Sabina), 1265–1267

The Dominican chapter directed Thomas to establish a *studium* at Rome. At Santa Sabina he designed a curriculum beyond ordinary provincial instruction. He began the *Summa theologiae*, explicitly intended for beginners frustrated by the disorder and repetition of existing manuals. This was educational technology in the medieval sense: a carefully engineered question–article system for training judgment.

### Viterbo, 1267–1268

Thomas operated near Clement IV’s court at Viterbo. **William of Moerbeke**, Dominican translator from Greek, belonged to this intellectual environment. Older scholarship claimed William translated Aristotle specifically at Thomas’s request; contact is highly plausible and revised Greek–Latin translations materially enabled Thomas’s commentaries, but a coordinated papal “translation project” commissioned for him is not proven.

**Reginald of Piperno** became Thomas’s *socius*, confessor, secretary, travel companion, and reportator. Thomas dictated rapidly to multiple scribes; witnesses later marveled that he could dictate on several subjects in sequence or simultaneously. This is partly hagiographic amplification, but surviving autographs and reportation practices establish an unusually organized writing workshop.

## Second Paris Regency, 1268/69–1272

Thomas returned to a volatile Paris. Three overlapping struggles dominated:

1. renewed hostility toward mendicant orders;
2. Franciscan–Dominican disputes over creation, illumination, plurality of forms, and poverty;
3. conflict over Latin “Averroists,” especially **Siger of Brabant**, who defended controversial readings of Aristotle.

Thomas rejected the thesis that all humans share one possible intellect in *De unitate intellectus contra Averroistas* (1270), addressing opponents with unusual sharpness. In *De aeternitate mundi contra murmurantes* he argued that reason cannot demonstrate that a created world must have a temporal beginning: eternity and creation are conceptually compatible, although revelation teaches a beginning. This placed him between conservative theologians and radical Aristotelians.

He continued the *Summa*, disputed questions on evil and virtues, quodlibets, commentaries on Aristotle, Job, John, and Paul, and polemics over religious life. Dominican allies included **John of Vercelli**; Franciscan interlocutors and opponents included **John Peckham**. Thomas and Bonaventure were later paired as harmonious masters, but surviving evidence for intimate friendship is thin; they were eminent contemporaries in different orders and intellectual traditions.

## Naples and Final Creative Period, 1272–1273

The Roman provincial chapter in Florence authorized a new Dominican *studium generale* and allowed Thomas to choose its site, staff, and curriculum. He selected Naples, taught there from late 1272, and continued Part III of the *Summa*. The Angevin government under **Charles I of Anjou** paid an annual subsidy—often given as one ounce of gold per month or a comparable teaching stipend—to support his university teaching. Exact accounting and whether payment went personally or institutionally require caution; it demonstrates royal support, not ownership of a commercial enterprise.

He preached vernacular Lenten sermons in Naples in 1273 on the Creed, Lord’s Prayer, Hail Mary, Ten Commandments, and commandments of love. What survives is largely reportation by listeners or disciples rather than autograph publication.

## The Cessation of Writing, December 1273

On 6 December 1273, feast of St Nicholas, after Mass in the chapel of St Nicholas at San Domenico Maggiore, Thomas underwent an experience that altered his behavior. Reginald urged him to resume dictation. In the early-life tradition Thomas eventually said that he could write no more because all he had written seemed like straw compared with what had been revealed to him.

**Documented core:** he stopped composing, leaving the *Summa theologiae* at III, question 90, and close associates attributed the cessation to an extraordinary experience.  
**Unknown:** neurological episode, exhaustion, depression, mystical ecstasy, stroke, or deliberate spiritual silence cannot be diagnosed from the sources.  
**Later embellishments:** a crucifix speaking “You have written well of me, Thomas; what reward will you have?” and his answer “Nothing but you, Lord” (*Non nisi te, Domine*); levitation; Marian apparitions; and detailed visions. These circulated through Dominican hagiography and devotional art.

The later **Supplementum** to the *Summa* was compiled by disciples, probably in Reginald’s circle, from Thomas’s earlier commentary on Book IV of Lombard’s *Sentences*. It must not be treated as the continuation Thomas himself wrote.

## Final Journey, Illness, and Death, 1274

Pope Gregory X summoned Thomas to the Second Council of Lyon, where Latin–Greek reunion was central and *Contra errores Graecorum* was relevant. He left Naples on foot or by mule with Reginald. He visited his sister Theodora at Maenza and became seriously ill. The traditional account has him transferred to nearby Fossanova because, as a religious, he wished to die in a monastery. He reportedly expounded the Song of Songs to the Cistercians; no secure text survives.

He died on the morning of 7 March 1274 and was buried at Fossanova. Reginald received his confession and preached the funeral sermon according to later testimony.

### Competing causes of death

- **Contemporaneous/early account:** sudden or progressive illness during the journey; no precise diagnosis.
- **Trauma hypothesis:** a Latin source reports that he struck his head on an overhanging tree branch. A 2024 *World Neurosurgery* historical-forensic paper proposed chronic subdural hematoma. The authors examined historical descriptions and visually considered a reputed skull; examination was inconclusive and rival skull relics prevent secure osteological identification. This is a **plausible modern hypothesis, not a confirmed diagnosis**.
- **Poisoning by Charles of Anjou:** Dante’s *Purgatorio* XX.67–69 says Charles “sent Thomas back to heaven,” interpreted by early commentators as poisoning through a physician. The story reflects anti-Angevin political memory and was repeated by later chroniclers. No contemporary medical, judicial, or eyewitness evidence supports it; early companion testimony does not allege foul play. It remains historically important as hostile political legend.
- **Ecstatic death:** devotional versions make death the bodily consequence of mystical union. This is theological interpretation, not medical evidence.

## Religious, Philosophical, Political, and Intellectual Development

### Method

Thomas distinguished truths accessible to natural reason from mysteries known only by revelation while holding that genuine truth cannot contradict truth. Sacred doctrine was a *scientia* proceeding from principles known by God and the blessed. The *Summa* article’s objections are **not Thomas’s views**: his position normally appears in the *respondeo* and replies.

### Aristotle and other sources

Thomas’s project integrated Aristotle’s logic, metaphysics, psychology, ethics, and natural philosophy into Christian theology while rejecting eternity of the world as revealed doctrine, a single shared intellect, astrological determinism, and limits on divine freedom. He called Aristotle “the Philosopher,” Averroes “the Commentator,” and drew heavily on Avicenna’s essence/existence distinction. Albertus Magnus was the living mediator; William of Moerbeke’s translations reduced dependence on Arabic–Latin versions.

### Metaphysics and natural theology

- act and potency; matter and form; substance and accident;
- real distinction between essence and existence in creatures;
- God as subsistent being itself (*ipsum esse subsistens*), simple, immutable, perfect, good, intelligent, and free;
- the “five ways” as demonstrations from motion/change, efficient causality, contingency, gradation, and governance—not five scientific experiments and not Thomas’s complete doctrine of God;
- analogy rather than univocal or purely equivocal predication of God.

### Human person and knowledge

The human being is a single substance composed of body and rational soul; the soul is the substantial form of the body and survives death but is naturally oriented to embodied life. Knowledge begins in sensation and is abstracted by intellect. Against Averroism each person possesses an individual intellectual principle.

### Ethics and law

Human action aims at happiness, fully realized only in the beatific vision. Virtues include acquired cardinal virtues and infused theological virtues. The account of law distinguishes eternal, natural, human, and divine law. Natural law’s first precept is that good is to be done and pursued and evil avoided. Prudence connects general principles to circumstances.

### Politics

In *De regno* and the *Summa*, government exists for the common good. Kingship can be efficient but readily degenerates into tyranny; mixed government and lawful constraints appear elsewhere in his corpus. He allowed resistance to tyranny through public authority but generally opposed private assassination because of disorder. Modern labels such as liberal, socialist, capitalist, or theocrat are anachronistic.

### Economics

Thomas discussed commutative justice, fraud in sales, “just price,” ownership, almsgiving, and usury. Private property could be lawful for administration and peace, while use should remain ordered to common need; in extreme necessity a person may take what is required for survival. He condemned usury as charging for the use of money understood as consumed in use, while allowing compensation for independently incurred loss in some later-developed applications. He did not formulate a modern price-control program or economic system.

### Sacraments and Eucharist

His account of transubstantiation uses substance/accident language to explain the Eucharistic change. The Corpus Christi office made his theology continuously audible in Catholic worship.

## Important Relationships and Networks

- **Albert the Great:** principal teacher and intellectual model; defended Thomas after death and is the direct link to the queued Albertus Magnus dossier.
- **Reginald of Piperno:** closest documented companion, secretary, confessor, reportator, and witness; critical to the preservation and posthumous completion of works.
- **William of Moerbeke:** Greek translator and fellow Dominican; intellectual contact likely, individual commissions by Thomas sometimes overstated.
- **Urban IV and Clement IV:** papal patrons/commissioners; no proof Thomas formally held the later office “Master of the Sacred Palace.”
- **Gregory X:** summoned him to Lyon.
- **John of Vercelli:** Dominican master general and institutional superior.
- **William of Saint-Amour:** opponent in the mendicant controversy.
- **Siger of Brabant:** target/interlocutor in the unity-of-intellect controversy; not simply an enemy in a personal sense.
- **John Peckham:** Franciscan critic in Paris; later condemned Thomistic propositions at Oxford.
- **Bonaventure:** eminent Franciscan contemporary, frequently paired in later Catholic memory and in Dante; personal intimacy is not established.
- **Charles I of Anjou:** ruler and financial patron of the Naples chair; later cast by Dantean tradition as Thomas’s murderer.
- **Pope John XXI / Stephen Tempier:** their investigation and condemnation formed the immediate posthumous controversy, though Thomas was not named as the target.
- **Dante Alighieri:** born while Thomas was alive but not a known acquaintance; made Thomas a major speaker in *Paradiso* X–XIII and transmitted the poisoning story in *Purgatorio*.
- **Giordano Bruno:** a later Dominican reader/rebel in an intellectual world structured by scholastic Aristotelianism; he criticized Aristotelian and scholastic frameworks. Claims of a direct personal lineage are impossible and doctrinal dependence must be shown text by text.

## Controversies, Coercion, and Doctrinally Difficult Material

### Family detention

Thomas was the victim, not perpetrator, of prolonged extrajudicial family confinement. No surviving prosecution or damages action is known.

### Defense of executing persistent heretics

In *Summa theologiae* II–II, q.11, a.3, Thomas argued that convicted, relapsed/persistent heretics could justly be excommunicated by the Church and delivered to secular power for death, analogizing doctrinal corruption to counterfeiting. He also required admonition and opportunities for repentance. This is **Thomas’s stated normative position**, not merely an objection, and it provided later theological support for coercive religious regimes. There is no evidence that Thomas personally investigated, sentenced, tortured, or executed anyone.

### Capital punishment and political violence

In *Summa contra Gentiles* III.146 and related texts he permitted public authority to kill dangerous wrongdoers for the common good, using an organism/member analogy. He did not give private persons a general license to kill. Just-war analysis required legitimate authority, just cause, and right intention.

### Jews and non-Christians

Thomas treated Judaism as religious error within a supersessionist Christian framework and accepted legal disabilities typical of Latin Christendom. Yet he opposed forcibly baptizing Jewish or other unbelieving children against parental will, on natural-justice and parental-authority grounds (*ST* II–II, q.10, a.12; III, q.68, a.10). He allowed rulers to tolerate unbelieving rites to avoid greater evils and preserve goods, while treating heretics—baptized persons judged to have repudiated the faith—more harshly. These positions combine limited toleration with a fundamentally unequal confessional order.

### Women and sex

Thomas inherited Aristotle’s reproductive biology and repeated that, at the level of particular natural generation, woman is a “misbegotten” or “deficient male”; in his own reply he simultaneously held that woman is intended in the order of universal nature and necessary to species and social life. He assigned male governance within household/church hierarchies and denied priestly ordination to women. Quoting only the initial objection in *ST* I, q.92 can misattribute argumentative voices, but the hierarchy and defective embryology are genuinely present in his considered system.

### Slavery

He held that slavery is not part of nature’s first intention and arose after sin, but could belong to the *ius gentium* and be justified by utility and governance. He did not advocate universal abolition. His sacramental and moral claims about spiritual equality did not dissolve medieval legal servitude.

### Sexuality

Within medieval natural-law theology Thomas classified non-procreative sexual acts, including same-sex acts, as contrary to nature and gravely sinful. This is documentary doctrine, not evidence about his own sexuality. The chastity temptation story is hagiography, not biographical proof of orientation or psychology.

### *Contra errores Graecorum*

Thomas reasoned from a Latin compilation containing mistranslations and inauthentic/poorly transmitted patristic materials. The work is important to Latin–Greek polemic but cannot be treated as a reliable direct digest of Greek fathers. Its limits reflect the textual infrastructure available to him.

### Condemnations of 1277 and Oxford

On 7 March 1277, exactly three years after Thomas’s death, Paris bishop **Étienne Tempier** condemned 219 propositions under penalty of excommunication. The act named no authors. Some propositions overlap or conflict with Thomistic theses, especially divine knowledge, individuation, and angelic/intellectual issues, but modern scholarship rejects the simple claim that “twenty propositions by Thomas were formally condemned” as though the syllabus named him. Siger, Boethius of Dacia, Arabic philosophy, and anonymous arts teaching were major targets.

At Oxford on 18 March 1277, Dominican archbishop **Robert Kilwardby** condemned propositions including the unity of substantial form, associated with Thomas. His Franciscan successor John Peckham renewed opposition in 1284. The Dominican Order defended Thomas; the Paris censure was later qualified/withdrawn insofar as it touched him after his 1323 canonization. These conflicts show that “official Catholic philosopher” was a later outcome, not Thomas’s uncontested contemporary status.

### No evidence for modern financial/criminal categories

No credible record links Thomas to fraud, bribery, money laundering, intelligence services, sexual abuse, addiction, or commercial corruption. Such categories are mostly inapplicable to the surviving thirteenth-century record. Political entanglement existed through family, papal, imperial, Dominican, university, and Angevin networks.

## Works by Thomas Aquinas

Dates are approximate and frequently overlap because texts were revised, dictated, or reported. Titles are conventional Latin titles; Thomas may not have assigned every title. “Authentic” follows modern critical catalogues rather than mere medieval attribution.

### Major theological syntheses

1. *Scriptum super libros Sententiarum magistri Petri Lombardi* (c. 1252–56), commentary on Peter Lombard’s *Sentences*; authentic.
2. *Summa contra Gentiles* / *Liber de veritate catholicae fidei contra errores infidelium* (c. 1259–65); authentic. The popular idea that it was a missionary handbook specifically for Spanish Dominicans converting Muslims is disputed and lacks decisive evidence.
3. *Summa theologiae* (c. 1265–6 Dec. 1273); authentic, unfinished at III, q.90. The *Supplementum* is posthumous compilation from the *Sentences* commentary.
4. *Compendium theologiae ad fratrem Reginaldum* (c. 1265–73); authentic, unfinished.

### Disputed questions

- *Quaestiones disputatae de veritate* (1256–59), twenty-nine questions, broader than the title suggests.
- *De potentia* (c. 1265–66).
- *De anima* (c. 1265–66).
- *De spiritualibus creaturis* (c. 1267–68).
- *De malo* (c. 1266–70).
- *De virtutibus in communi*, *De caritate*, *De correctione fraterna*, *De spe*, *De virtutibus cardinalibus* (mainly second Paris regency, c. 1269–72).
- Twelve sets of *Quaestiones de quodlibet* (c. 1256–59 and 1269–72), public disputations on questions proposed by the audience.

### Biblical commentaries and lectures

- *Expositio super Isaiam ad litteram*; *Super Ieremiam*; *Super Threnos* (early; some textual status/dating debated).
- *Expositio super Iob ad litteram* (c. 1261–65).
- *Catena aurea in quattuor Evangelia* (c. 1262–67), patristic chains on Matthew, Mark, Luke, John.
- *Lectura super Matthaeum* (reportatio; incomplete textual transmission).
- *Lectura super Ioannem* (reported and corrected tradition).
- *Expositio et lectura super epistolas Pauli apostoli* (Pauline lectures, various dates/redactions).
- *Postilla super Psalmos* / commentary on Psalms 1–54 (late, incomplete; reportatio).
- Lectures/commentaries on the Song of Songs are reported at his death, but no authentic text is securely preserved.

### Commentaries on Aristotle

- *Sententia libri De anima*.
- *Sententia libri De sensu et sensato* (with related *De memoria* material).
- *Sententia libri Ethicorum*.
- *Sententia libri Politicorum* (unfinished; later completed by Peter of Auvergne).
- *Sententia libri Metaphysicae*.
- *Sententia super Physicam*.
- *Sententia super Meteora* (unfinished; continuation not Thomas’s).
- *Expositio libri Peryermenias* (*De interpretatione*; unfinished, continuation by Thomas of Sutton or another hand).
- *Expositio libri Posteriorum analyticorum*.
- *Sententia super De caelo et mundo* (unfinished; continuation by Peter of Auvergne).
- *Sententia super libros De generatione et corruptione* (unfinished; continuation by Thomas of Sutton/another).

These are close expositions but include clarifying *digressiones*. A paraphrase of Aristotle is not automatically Thomas’s endorsed position.

### Commentaries on Neoplatonic and Boethian texts

- *Super Boetium De Trinitate* (c. 1257–59), incomplete.
- *Expositio libri Boetii De hebdomadibus*.
- *Super librum Dionysii De divinis nominibus*.
- *Super librum De causis expositio* (c. 1272), recognizes the *Liber de causis* as derived from Proclus rather than Aristotle.

### Philosophical and theological opuscula

- *De ente et essentia* (c. 1252–56).
- *De principiis naturae* (c. 1252–56).
- *De mixtione elementorum*; *De motu cordis*; *De operationibus occultis naturae*.
- *De substantiis separatis* (unfinished, late).
- *De aeternitate mundi contra murmurantes* (c. 1270–71).
- *De unitate intellectus contra Averroistas* (1270).
- *De regno ad regem Cypri* (c. 1265–67), unfinished; later continuation is by another author, commonly Ptolemy/Tolomeo of Lucca.
- *De sortibus*; *De iudiciis astrorum* (letter on astrological judgments); *De forma absolutionis*; *De secreto* and other short determinations/catalogued letters where titles and dating vary.
- *De rationibus fidei ad cantorem Antiochenum*.
- *Contra errores Graecorum* (c. 1263–64).
- *Contra doctrinam retrahentium a religione* (1270–71).
- *Contra impugnantes Dei cultum et religionem* (1256).
- *De perfectione spiritualis vitae* (1269–70).
- *Contra pestiferam doctrinam retrahentium homines a religionis ingressu*.
- *Responsio de articulis XLII*; *Responsio de articulis XXXVI*; *Responsio de articulis VI*; *Responsio ad magistrum Ioannem de Vercellis de articulis CVIII*—expert responses whose modern titles/counts vary by catalogue.
- *Epistola ad ducissam Brabantiae* (advice on governance, Jews, taxation, and offices; transmission/redaction problems require critical editions).
- *Epistola ad Bernardum abbatem Casinensem*; *De emptione et venditione ad tempus* and other responsa.

### Sermons, prayers, and liturgy

- University sermons preserved under themes including *Rigans montes*, *Hic est liber mandatorum Dei*, *Puer Iesus*, *Attendite a falsis*, and others; authenticity varies sermon by sermon and the Leonine sermon edition has revised the corpus.
- Collationes/reportations on the Apostles’ Creed, Lord’s Prayer, Hail Mary, Ten Commandments, and two commandments of charity (Naples, 1273); often mediated by reportatores.
- Office for Corpus Christi, *Sapientia aedificavit sibi domum* tradition, antiphons, responsories, readings, and prayers. The hymns *Pange lingua*, *Sacris solemniis*, *Verbum supernum*, and sequence *Lauda Sion* are traditionally attributed; precise textual authorship should be stated with appropriate caution.
- Prayers such as *Adoro te devote* are traditionally attributed; critical confidence is not uniform across all devotional pieces published under his name.

### Autographs, dictation, and reportation

Thomas wrote some drafts in the difficult hand called *littera inintelligibilis* but produced much through dictation. A *reportatio* is a student/secretary’s record of lectures, sometimes revised by the master; it is neither modern ghostwriting nor necessarily verbatim autograph. Reginald and other reportatores are therefore co-producers of textual transmission without being co-authors of Thomas’s doctrines in the modern sense.

### Uncertain or spurious attributions

- **Uncertain in modern catalogues:** *De quattuor oppositis*; *Officium de festo S. Augustini*; some sermons, prayers, and minor responsa.
- **Spurious:** *Aurora consurgens* (alchemical/Jungian text), *De lapide philosophico*, *Thesaurus alchemiae*, *De sacramento altaris* in some transmitted forms, *De moribus divinis*, an *Expositio in Canticum canticorum*, many later prayers and sermons, and continuations appended to unfinished Aristotelian commentaries.
- **Posthumous compilation:** *Supplementum* to the *Summa theologiae*; based on authentic earlier material but not composed as such by Thomas.

### Editions and publication history

Thomas wrote before print. “Original publication date” therefore means composition/circulation in manuscript. The first large printed collected edition appeared at Rome in 1570 (the Piana edition commissioned under Pius V). Leo XIII established the Leonine critical edition in 1879; volumes began in 1882 and continue. Corpus Thomisticum supplies searchable Latin texts and bibliographic data. The *Index Thomisticus*, initiated by Roberto Busa, converted the corpus into a machine-readable concordance and became foundational to digital humanities.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### 1274–1323: disputed master to saint

Thomas died respected but controversial. The 1277 Paris and Oxford condemnations damaged or constrained parts of his teaching. Dominicans institutionalized study of his work and defended the unity of substantial form and other theses. Canonization in 1323 dramatically altered the balance: critique of his doctrine now risked appearing to challenge a saint, though philosophical disputes continued.

### Dante

In *Paradiso* X–XIII Thomas leads a circle of wise spirits, praises Francis of Assisi, and introduces Bonaventure’s reciprocal praise of Dominic. Dante’s Thomas is a poetic authority of ordered wisdom, not a documentary portrait. In *Purgatorio* XX Dante also helped perpetuate the Charles-of-Anjou poisoning rumor. The *Comedy* is deeply compatible with Thomistic ethical and cosmological structures, but calling it simply “the Summa in verse” suppresses other sources and Dante’s departures.

### Canonical and university authority

- 1323: canonized.
- 1369: relic translation to Toulouse strengthened Dominican international cult.
- 1567: Pius V declared him Doctor of the Church and sponsored the Piana works edition.
- 1879: Leo XIII’s *Aeterni Patris* made Thomas central to Catholic philosophical renewal.
- 1880: patron of Catholic schools and universities.
- 1917 and 1983 canon-law traditions required theological formation to attend particularly to Thomas, though the degree of exclusivity changed.

“Neo-Thomism” was never one school. Major strands include Leonine historical scholarship, strict-observance/commentarial Thomism (Cajetan, John of St Thomas), existential Thomism (Gilson, Maritain), transcendental Thomism (Maréchal, Rahner, Lonergan), River Forest/Aristotelian Thomism, analytic Thomism, natural-law jurisprudence, virtue ethics, and ressourcement readings.

### Critics and reinterpretations

- Scotists and nominalists rejected core metaphysical and epistemological theses.
- Reformation theologians criticized scholastic method while sometimes retaining natural-law and Aristotelian categories.
- Early modern philosophers displaced scholastic physics and metaphysics; Giordano Bruno’s anti-Aristotelian cosmology belongs to this contested afterlife, not a simple Thomas–Bruno dialogue.
- Feminist, Jewish, queer, disability, postcolonial, and political-theological scholarship scrutinizes Thomas’s hierarchies, supersessionism, sexual ethics, slavery accommodation, and punitive doctrines while sometimes redeploying participation, embodiment, virtue, and common-good concepts.
- Historians distinguish the thirteenth-century Thomas from “Thomism,” from textbook neo-scholasticism, and from papal uses of his authority.

### Science, logic, and digital humanities

Thomas’s Aristotelian physics was superseded, but his analyses of causation, explanation, mind, action, law, virtue, and language remain active in philosophy. Roberto Busa’s *Index Thomisticus*—lemmatizing the corpus with punched-card and computer assistance—made Aquinas central to the origin story of digital humanities.

## Compact Chronology

- **1224/25:** born, probably at Roccasecca in the Kingdom of Sicily.
- **c. 1230:** placed at Monte Cassino.
- **1239:** leaves after imperial disruption; studies at University of Naples.
- **Spring 1244:** joins Dominicans at Naples.
- **1244–45:** seized and confined by family; eventually rejoins order.
- **1245–48:** studies at Paris under Albert the Great.
- **1248–52:** follows Albert to Cologne; early teaching/exegesis; priestly ordination probably c. 1250–51.
- **1252–56:** bachelor at Paris; lectures on scripture and Lombard’s *Sentences*.
- **1256:** master of theology amid mendicant–secular conflict; writes *Contra impugnantes*.
- **1256–59:** first Paris regency; *De veritate* and early opuscula.
- **1259:** leaves Paris for Italy.
- **1260:** named general preacher in Roman province.
- **1261–65:** Orvieto; completes much of *Summa contra Gentiles*; papal commissions.
- **1263/64:** *Contra errores Graecorum* and *Catena aurea* work.
- **1264:** universal feast of Corpus Christi; office traditionally attributed to Thomas.
- **1265–67:** Santa Sabina, Rome; begins *Summa theologiae*.
- **1267–68:** Viterbo; contact with curial and translation networks.
- **1268/69–72:** second Paris regency; combats Latin Averroism and opponents of mendicants; prolific commentaries/disputations.
- **1270:** Paris condemns thirteen Averroist propositions; Thomas writes *De unitate intellectus*.
- **1272:** establishes Dominican *studium generale* at Naples.
- **1273:** Naples preaching cycle; continues Part III of *Summa*.
- **6 Dec. 1273:** transformative experience; ceases writing.
- **Early 1274:** departs for Second Council of Lyon; falls ill after journey through Maenza.
- **7 Mar. 1274:** dies at Fossanova.
- **7 Mar. 1277:** Tempier condemns 219 propositions at Paris; some conflict with Thomistic positions.
- **18 Mar. 1277:** Kilwardby’s Oxford condemnation strikes theses associated with Thomas.
- **1319:** first canonization inquiry, Naples.
- **1321:** second inquiry, Fossanova.
- **18 Jul. 1323:** canonized by John XXII at Avignon.
- **1369:** principal relics transferred to Toulouse.
- **1567:** declared Doctor of the Church by Pius V; Piana edition follows in 1570.
- **1879:** Leo XIII issues *Aeterni Patris* and establishes the Leonine revival/edition project.
- **1880:** named patron of Catholic schools and universities.
- **1923:** Pius XI issues *Studiorum Ducem* for the canonization centenary.
- **1940s–70s:** Roberto Busa develops *Index Thomisticus*.
- **1969:** Roman-calendar feast moved from 7 March to 28 January.
- **1974:** relics returned from Saint-Sernin to the Jacobins, Toulouse; 700th death anniversary.
- **2023:** 700th canonization anniversary; new Toulouse reliquary and renewed relic tours.
- **2024:** historical-forensic paper proposes chronic subdural hematoma, explicitly unconfirmed.
- **2024–25:** 750th death-anniversary observances and international relic exhibitions.

## Research Conclusions and Unresolved Questions

1. The skeleton of Thomas’s career is unusually secure; exact birth date, some Italian postings, ordination details, and fine dating of texts remain uncertain.
2. Family confinement is historically credible; its temptation miracle cannot be verified independently.
3. Albert’s mentorship and Reginald’s collaboration are fundamental. William of Moerbeke’s exact commission relationship is frequently overstated.
4. The December 1273 cessation is well attested as behavior; its content and medical/psychological meaning are unrecoverable.
5. Natural illness and a recent chronic-subdural-hematoma hypothesis are compatible with available evidence; poisoning by Charles of Anjou is a politically potent but unsupported legend.
6. Thomas’s authorization of coercion against convicted heretics, capital punishment, patriarchal hierarchy, sexual prohibitions, and legal slavery must be recorded alongside his limits on forced baptism, tyranny, property, war, and private violence.
7. Posthumous “Thomism” is plural and sometimes projects later ecclesiastical uniformity back onto a thinker contested in his own universities.
