## Works by John Brown

### Buildings, workshops, and material production

- **1825–1835:** *John Brown’s Richmond tannery*. A commercial tannery built and operated by Brown in Richmond Township, Pennsylvania; fire destroyed its wooden structure in 1907, leaving archaeological foundations. Its ordinary output—leather rather than individually titled objects—survives only as an unenumerated body of work.[1]

- **1835–1839:** *John Brown’s Franklin Mills tannery*. A second tanning works established beside the Cuyahoga River in what is now Kent, Ohio. The tannery itself was eventually demolished, and no catalogue of its leather production is known.[2]

- **1849–1859:** *North Elba farm*. A family farm and antislavery settlement project within Gerrit Smith’s “Timbuctoo” experiment. Brown made the farm as a place of livelihood and mutual aid, but the surviving farmhouse was principally built in 1855 by his son-in-law Henry Thompson; it therefore resists attribution to Brown as an architectural work.[3]

- **1857–1859:** *John Brown pikes*. A commissioned arsenal of approximately 950 pole weapons intended for people liberated through Brown’s projected campaign. Brown specified a form derived from a Bowie knife, but Connecticut forgemaster Charles Blair manufactured the objects; they are Brown’s procurement and design project, not his handiwork.[4]

### Essays and autobiographical writing

- **After mid-1848; unfinished:** *Sambo Mistakes* (usually printed as *Sambo’s Mistakes*). An unfinished satirical and admonitory essay written in the assumed voice of a Black speaker. Brown’s manuscript survives, but the traditional claim that it appeared in *The Ram’s Horn* cannot be verified from the newspaper’s surviving issues, and its exact date and authorial title remain unsettled.[5]

- **1857:** *The Childhood of John Brown*. An autobiographical account composed as a letter for twelve-year-old Henry L. Stearns. Because it was elicited for a child and written in the third person, it is simultaneously correspondence, memoir, and moral instruction rather than a conventional autobiography.[6]

- **April 1857:** *Old Brown’s Farewell*. A short address to emblematic objects and places of American liberty, composed after disappointing fundraising in New England. Brown reportedly intended publication but was persuaded to withhold it, so it remained an unrealized public manifesto.[7]

- **January 1859:** *Old Brown’s Parallels*. A signed newspaper letter contrasting the unpunished Marais des Cygnes massacre with the official pursuit of Brown after his Missouri liberation expedition. Its “parallel” structure turns reportage into an indictment of the state’s unequal valuation of lives.[8]

### Protocols, constitutions, and manifestos

- **15 January 1851:** *Words of Advice: Branch of the United States League of Gileadites*. A protocol for collective resistance to the Fugitive Slave Act, adopted by the Springfield organization “as written and recommended by John Brown.” It is also a manifesto because its instructions were intended to produce organized action, not merely describe principles.[9]

- **February–May 1858:** *Provisional Constitution and Ordinances for the People of the United States*. A constitution for the interracial polity Brown hoped to establish among liberated people and antislavery fighters. Brown called it his own contrivance, but Richard J. Hinton later credited John Henry Kagi with smoothing its language and legal form; the Chatham convention discussed and adopted it on 8 May, making it both Brown’s obra and a collectively ratified instrument.[10]

- **May 1858:** *Journal of the Chatham Convention*. A record of the convention that adopted Brown’s constitution and elected provisional officers. It belongs to Brown’s organizational project, but it is not securely an individually authored Brown text and should not be collapsed into the constitution itself.[11]

- **May 1858:** *Form letter of the Chatham Convention committee*. A circular connected with the convention’s organization. Its survival among Brown’s papers establishes it as part of the project, but committee authorship prevents confident attribution to Brown alone.[11]

- **12 July 1858:** *Articles of Agreement for Shubel Morgan’s Company*. A nine-article camp protocol regulating discipline, labor, captured property, prisoners, weapons, and obedience. “Shubel Morgan” was Brown’s alias, but the surviving text is an agreement signed by members and is therefore a collective rule rather than an exclusively authored declaration.[12]

- **4 July 1859:** *A Declaration of Liberty by the Representatives of the Slave Population of the United States of America*. A manifesto modeled structurally on the Declaration of Independence and intended to speak in the name of enslaved people. The surviving scroll is in Owen Brown’s handwriting, probably acting as his father’s amanuensis; intellectual attribution to John Brown is traditional and strongly supported, but sole physical authorship is not.[13]

### Speeches and performed statements

- **18 February 1857:** *Speech before a committee of the Massachusetts legislature*. An appeal for aid to antislavery forces in Kansas, surviving in Brown’s one-leaf autograph manuscript. It is an oral performance preserved as a text rather than a separately authored book or pamphlet.[11]

- **18 October 1859:** *Statements after capture at Harpers Ferry*. Brown’s answers to questioning by Governor Henry A. Wise, Senator James M. Mason, Representative Clement Vallandigham, and others. Reporters produced the surviving versions, so they are documented speech-events whose exact wording is partly mediated by transcription.[14]

- **2 November 1859:** *Address to the Virginia court*. Brown’s statement before sentence was imposed at Charles Town. It functioned as courtroom allocution, moral defense, and public manifesto; contemporary newspaper reports and the trial record transmit slightly differing texts.[14]

- **1859:** *Testimonies of Captain John Brown at Harper’s Ferry*. A title applied to published compilations of Brown’s post-capture remarks rather than to a single manuscript work made under that title. It therefore resists treatment as an independent obra: the creation is editorial, while Brown supplied the speech-events.[15]

### Correspondence and records

- **1820s–1859:** *Surviving correspondence*. Brown made hundreds of family, business, political, fundraising, and prison letters, but no complete scholarly edition of his extant correspondence exists. Franklin B. Sanborn’s *Life and Letters* is the foundational collected edition but is selective; the Kansas and West Virginia archival collections preserve additional originals and copies, including three business letterbooks from the Perkins & Brown wool partnership. Treating this open, dispersed corpus as one body of work avoids inventing a false definitive list.[15][16][17]

- **1840s–1850s:** *Perkins & Brown business records*. Letterbooks, accounts, contracts, and memoranda created through Brown’s wool-brokerage partnership with Simon Perkins Jr. These are transactional records and collaborative business instruments, not literary “works,” though they document a substantial body of writing and commercial organization.[16]

- **1850s:** *Memorandum book*. A working notebook preserved within the archival Brown papers. It is an evolving tool rather than a finished text and therefore resists both a fixed title and the status of a completed work.[17]

- **1859:** *Harpers Ferry planning papers*. Lists, correspondence, draft governmental documents, accounts, and operational notes found at or connected with the Kennedy Farm. They manifest Brown’s project but cannot all be assigned to him: his sons, Kagi, and other participants also copied, drafted, or maintained documents.[11][14]

- **October–December 1859:** *Charlestown prison letters*. A distinct late corpus addressed to family members, supporters, opponents, clergy, and public figures. Many were consciously written for circulation, making the group at once private correspondence, religious testimony, political advocacy, and preparation of Brown’s public memory; surviving editions remain selective rather than complete.[15][16]

- **2 December 1859:** *Last words*. A brief autograph note handed over on the morning of Brown’s execution. Though untitled by Brown, it acts as a prophetic manifesto; “Last Words” is an editorial description, not a secure authorial title.[14]

### Publishing and organizational projects

- **1848:** *Henry Highland Garnet’s Address to the Slaves of the United States of America, Brown-financed edition*. Brown financed the printing of Garnet’s address, reportedly producing or underwriting two thousand copies. The text is Garnet’s obra, not Brown’s; Brown’s made thing was the edition and its intended circulation.[18]

- **1851:** *United States League of Gileadites, Springfield branch*. An interracial self-defense organization formed by Brown with members of Springfield’s Black community to resist slave catchers. It is a collective institution made through a protocol and meeting, not an individually owned “work,” and evidence for sustained activity after its founding is limited.[9]

- **Mid-1840s–1859; unfinished:** *Subterranean Pass-Way*. Brown’s evolving project for an armed chain of mountain strongholds and escape routes through which enslaved people could liberate themselves and be defended. No single definitive plan survives, so its obra is the intention, organization, and preparations rather than a finished document.[4][10]

- **1856:** *Pottawatomie operation*. Brown helped plan and led the party responsible for killing five proslavery settlers in Kansas. It was a made political action, not a text or conventional artwork; the extent of Brown’s direct participation in each killing has been disputed, although his leadership and responsibility for the operation are broadly accepted.[19]

- **December 1858–March 1859:** *Missouri liberation expedition*. An armed project directed by Brown that removed eleven enslaved people from Missouri and escorted them toward freedom in Canada. Brown transformed the operation into public argument through *Old Brown’s Parallels*; whether to call the expedition rescue, raid, or theft depends upon the legal and political standpoint being described.[8][19]

- **16–18 October 1859; unfinished:** *Harpers Ferry project*. Brown’s attempt to seize the federal armory, gather an interracial liberation force, and extend resistance into the slaveholding South. It combined constitutional drafting, military organization, weapons, disguises, aliases, fundraising, performance, and direct action: in that limited sense it is a political Gesamtkunstwerk, although calling a lethal historical event a “work” should remain deliberately uncomfortable.[10][14]
