# George Herbert Walker Bush: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs the documented life of George Herbert Walker Bush chronologically. It distinguishes among:

- **Documented fact:** supported by official records, archival holdings, contemporaneous documents, or multiple reliable sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a credible secondary source but not independently verified here from the underlying record.
- **Allegation:** an identified person or institution asserted it, without a judicial finding establishing it.
- **Investigative finding:** conclusion of an official or professionally conducted investigation.
- **Disputed interpretation:** the underlying events are documented, but their meaning, legality, responsibility, or motivation remains contested.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulating story for which reliable substantiation is absent or materially deficient.

No exhaustive biography of a modern president can reproduce every journey, speech, meeting, financial instrument, or administrative decision. The archival collections, oral histories, public papers, correspondence, and scholarly literature identified below provide routes for deeper investigation.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** George Herbert Walker Bush  
**Common forms:** George H. W. Bush; George Bush; George Herbert Walker Bush  
**Family nickname:** “Poppy,” used from childhood and within his family and social circle  
**Retrospective identifiers:** “Bush 41,” “41,” and “Bush Senior,” principally to distinguish him from his son George W. Bush  
**Military designation:** Lieutenant (junior grade), United States Naval Reserve  
**Political affiliation:** Republican Party

**Birth:** June 12, 1924, Milton, Massachusetts  
**Death:** November 30, 2018, Houston, Texas, aged ninety-four  
**Burial:** December 6, 2018, George H. W. Bush Presidential Library grounds, College Station, Texas, beside Barbara Bush and their daughter Pauline Robinson “Robin” Bush

- United States ambassador to the United Nations, March 1, 1971–January 18, 1973

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and social background

Bush was the second son of **Prescott Sheldon Bush** and **Dorothy Wear Walker Bush**.

Prescott Bush was a Yale graduate, investment banker, partner at Brown Brothers Harriman, and Republican United States senator from Connecticut from 1952 to 1963. Dorothy was the daughter of businessman and sportsman **George Herbert Walker**, after whom George H. W. Bush was named. The Walkers and Bushes belonged to an affluent northeastern Protestant business and political milieu with connections to banking, railroads, manufacturing, elite schools, country clubs, and Republican politics.

The family’s wealth gave Bush access to Phillips Academy, Yale, and influential commercial networks. Family culture simultaneously emphasized competition, emotional restraint, manners, loyalty, athletics, and public service. Bush later represented his departure for Texas as an attempt to establish an identity outside his father’s immediate institutional world, although family contacts materially assisted his oil career.

### Siblings

- Prescott Sheldon Bush Jr. (1922–2010)
- Nancy Walker Bush Ellis (1926–2021)
- Jonathan James Bush (1931–2021)
- William Henry Trotter “Bucky” Bush (1938–2018)

### Marriage and children

Bush met **Barbara Pierce** at a Christmas-season dance in Greenwich, Connecticut, in December 1941. They became engaged during the war and married on January 6, 1945, in Rye, New York. Their marriage lasted seventy-three years, until Barbara’s death on April 17, 2018.

Their children were:

1. **George Walker Bush**, born July 6, 1946; governor of Texas, 1995–2000; forty-third president, 2001–2009.
2. **Pauline Robinson “Robin” Bush**, born December 20, 1949; died October 11, 1953, from leukemia.
3. **John Ellis “Jeb” Bush**, born February 11, 1953; governor of Florida, 1999–2007; presidential candidate in 2016.
4. **Neil Mallon Bush**, born January 22, 1955; businessman.
5. **Marvin Pierce Bush**, born October 22, 1956; businessman.
6. **Dorothy Walker “Doro” Bush Koch**, born August 18, 1959; author and philanthropist.

Robin’s illness and death were formative family traumas. Barbara later recalled that grief contributed to premature whitening of her hair. George immersed himself heavily in work, a reaction that family accounts have variously described as coping, emotional reserve, and avoidance.

### Ancestry controversies

Claims about Bush’s ancestry have sometimes been folded into broader “dynasty” narratives. The securely documented point is that he descended from interconnected New England and Midwestern mercantile families and from the Walker banking-industrial network.

A separate controversy concerns **Prescott Bush’s prewar business connections**, particularly Union Banking Corporation and firms associated with German industrialist Fritz Thyssen. The United States government vested Union Banking Corporation property under the Trading with the Enemy Act in 1942. This is a documented episode in Prescott Bush’s business history. It does **not**, by itself, establish that George H. W. Bush participated in Nazi-linked business, was responsible for his father’s transactions, or inherited a clandestine political commitment from them. Attempts to transfer Prescott Bush’s legal and moral responsibility automatically to his son are genealogical-political argument, not evidence of George H. W. Bush’s personal conduct.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1924–1942

Bush was born in Milton, Massachusetts, and raised principally in Greenwich, Connecticut. The family also spent time at Walker’s Point, its summer property in Kennebunkport, Maine, which remained one of Bush’s most important residences throughout his life.

He attended Greenwich Country Day School and, beginning in 1937, Phillips Academy in Andover, Massachusetts. At Andover he was involved in student government and athletics, captained baseball and soccer teams, and became senior-class president. Biographical accounts describe him as socially adept, competitive, dutiful, and strongly formed by the school’s ethic of service.

The attack on Pearl Harbor occurred during his senior year. Bush decided to enter naval aviation rather than proceed immediately to college. He graduated on his eighteenth birthday, June 12, 1942, and enlisted that day.

---

## Naval Service and the Second World War, 1942–1945

Bush trained in naval aviation at Chapel Hill, North Carolina; Minneapolis, Minnesota; and Corpus Christi, Texas. He received his wings and commission in June 1943 and was among the youngest United States naval aviators at that time.

Assigned to Torpedo Squadron 51 aboard the aircraft carrier **USS San Jacinto**, he flew the Grumman TBF/TBM Avenger. His aircraft carried the name **Barbara** in honor of Barbara Pierce. The commonly cited total is fifty-eight combat missions.

### Chichijima shootdown

On September 2, 1944, Bush piloted an Avenger during an attack on Japanese installations at Chichijima in the Bonin Islands. Antiaircraft fire struck the aircraft. Bush completed or continued his attack run, then flew offshore and bailed out. The submarine **USS Finback** rescued him after he had spent several hours in a life raft.

His crewmates, Lieutenant junior grade **William G. White** and Radioman Second Class **John Delaney**, did not survive. Accounts differ about whether one or both men successfully left the aircraft; no dependable evidence establishes that either survived the descent. Bush carried enduring grief and questions about why he survived.

Bush received the Distinguished Flying Cross, three Air Medals, and the Presidential Unit Citation associated with the *San Jacinto*. He returned to duty and was honorably discharged in September 1945.

### Chichijima cannibalism story

**Documented fact:** Japanese personnel executed and cannibalized several captured American airmen from raids on Chichijima. The facts emerged in war-crimes proceedings and were later popularized by James Bradley’s *Flyboys*.

**Frequently repeated dramatic formulation:** Bush “narrowly escaped being eaten.” This is broadly true in the counterfactual sense that other downed aviators from the operation who were captured suffered that fate. It should not be transformed into a claim that Bush personally witnessed the killings or knew of them during the war.

## Education and Formation, 1945–1948

After discharge, Bush entered Yale University. The postwar accelerated program allowed him to graduate in 1948 with a Bachelor of Arts in economics.

At Yale he:

- Played first base for the varsity baseball team and became captain.
- Participated in two College World Series.
- Met Babe Ruth during a ceremonial presentation shortly before Ruth’s death.
- Joined Delta Kappa Epsilon.
- Was selected for the secret senior society **Skull and Bones**.
- Graduated Phi Beta Kappa.

Yale reinforced networks that later appeared in his political, intelligence, and business careers. Evidence of elite networking is abundant; claims that Skull and Bones functioned as a hidden command structure controlling his presidency are not substantiated.

---

## Oil Business and Geographical Movement, 1948–1966

### Texas and California

After Yale, George and Barbara Bush moved to Odessa, Texas. Bush worked as an equipment clerk and salesman for **Dresser Industries**, whose leadership included family acquaintance **Neil Mallon**. The family lived for a period in California and then returned to Midland, Texas.

Their early Texas circumstances were modest relative to Bush’s family background, though not disconnected from family capital and contacts. Bush’s image as entirely “self-made” therefore requires qualification: he worked intensively and accepted entry-level assignments, but his access to investors and Dresser reflected inherited social capital.

### Bush-Overbey and Zapata

In 1951 Bush and **John Overbey** established the Bush-Overbey Oil Development Company. In 1953 Bush, Hugh Liedtke, Bill Liedtke, Overbey, and investors formed **Zapata Petroleum Corporation**. Zapata successfully participated in oil exploration, and Bush became a millionaire while still comparatively young.

In 1954 he became president of **Zapata Off-Shore Company**, an offshore drilling subsidiary. The family moved to Houston by the late 1950s. Zapata rigs operated in the Gulf of Mexico and abroad, including the Caribbean, Persian Gulf, and other international locations.

Bush disposed of most of his operating interest as he moved into politics, although the precise evolution of holdings is complex. Later presidential assets were placed in a blind trust. A 1991 public-paper entry reported 1990 income including $245,911 from the Bushes’ blind trust and noted that net royalties from *Looking Forward* were donated to charity.

### Zapata records and intelligence mythology

Some early Zapata securities records were reportedly destroyed under routine Securities and Exchange Commission retention procedures. That documentary gap, the company’s offshore operations, the CIA’s later use of the word **Zapata** for the Bay of Pigs invasion plan, and Bush’s subsequent intelligence career became components of conspiracy narratives.

No consulted primary record establishes that Bush named, financed, or operationally supported the Bay of Pigs invasion, supplied CIA vessels, or used Zapata platforms for that operation. The State Department’s official history identifies “Zapata” as the CIA plan’s codename but does not connect it to Bush’s company.

## Entry into Politics, 1962–1970

Bush served as chairman of the Harris County Republican Party and participated in building a competitive Republican organization in a state then dominated by Democrats.

### 1964 Senate campaign

He won the Republican nomination for the United States Senate but lost to Democratic incumbent **Ralph Yarborough**. Bush’s campaign opposed the Civil Rights Act of 1964 on constitutional and federal-power grounds. This position later became a recurrent subject of criticism, particularly because Bush subsequently supported major civil-rights measures as a congressman.

### House of Representatives

Bush won election in 1966 from a newly configured Houston-area district and was reelected in 1968.

His House work included:

- Service on the House Ways and Means Committee, an unusually important assignment for a freshman.
- Support for the Fair Housing Act of 1968.
- Support for ending the military draft and creating an all-volunteer force.
- Work on fiscal, trade, family-planning, and population questions.
- A comparatively moderate Republican voting profile by later party standards.

Bush supported family-planning programs and was sometimes nicknamed “Rubbers” by congressional critics. During his later national campaigns he moved toward an anti-abortion position, a shift opponents described as political and supporters described as a changed conviction.

### 1970 Senate defeat

At President Richard Nixon’s encouragement, Bush gave up his House seat to run again for the Senate. He defeated conservative Robert Morris in the Republican primary but lost the general election to **Lloyd Bentsen**. The defeat ended his elected legislative career until the presidency.

---

## Diplomatic and Party Appointments, 1971–1975

### United Nations ambassador

Nixon appointed Bush United States ambassador to the United Nations. He served from 1971 to January 1973. His tenure included the People’s Republic of China’s admission and the Republic of China/Taiwan’s loss of the Chinese seat.

Bush opposed expelling Taiwan while supporting forms of representation for Beijing; the United States position failed. His performance nevertheless strengthened his reputation as a loyal, personable diplomat.

### Republican National Committee chairman and Watergate

Bush became RNC chairman in January 1973 as Watergate engulfed the Nixon administration. He publicly defended Nixon and the party while privately expressing concern. After the Supreme Court compelled release of Oval Office recordings and Nixon’s position became untenable, Bush joined other Republican leaders in concluding that resignation was necessary.

**Documented fact:** Bush was not charged with participating in the Watergate burglary or cover-up.

**Political criticism:** As party chairman, he defended Nixon longer than later narratives of principled distance sometimes imply. His defenders emphasize institutional loyalty and the incomplete information available to him; critics emphasize political calculation.

President Gerald Ford replaced him at the RNC and sent him to Beijing.

### United States Liaison Office in China

Because Washington and Beijing lacked full diplomatic relations, Bush served as chief of the United States Liaison Office rather than ambassador. He and Barbara arrived in 1974 and traveled around Beijing by bicycle, an image later central to his China recollections.

He met senior Chinese officials, studied the country’s developing relationship with the United States, and sent extensive diary reports. The tenure lasted approximately fourteen months. Bush hoped for the vice presidency under Ford but was passed over in favor of Nelson Rockefeller; he also appeared on Ford’s later vice-presidential shortlists.

---

## Director of Central Intelligence, 1976–1977

Ford appointed Bush director of Central Intelligence during a crisis in the CIA’s legitimacy following the Church Committee, Pike Committee, Rockefeller Commission, and disclosures of domestic surveillance, assassination plotting, and other abuses.

Bush’s mandate was institutional restoration. He cultivated relationships with Congress, defended intelligence officers, reorganized some management structures, and attempted to repair morale while accommodating increased oversight.

His tenure included:

- Continuing controversy over Soviet military estimates.
- The “Team B” competitive analysis exercise, whose participants generally advanced more threatening estimates of Soviet intentions and capabilities than CIA analysts.
- Transition from decades of comparatively insulated covert power toward permanent legislative oversight.
- Intelligence involvement with foreign authoritarian governments, including that of Manuel Noriega, whose relationship with United States agencies predated and continued beyond Bush’s directorship.

There is no established evidence that Bush committed a criminal offense as CIA director. Critics argue that Team B politicized intelligence and strengthened later neoconservative threat assessments. Defenders maintain that it tested institutional assumptions.

President Jimmy Carter replaced Bush in January 1977. Bush returned to Houston, taught or lectured intermittently, joined corporate boards, worked in banking and business circles, and prepared a presidential campaign.

---

## Presidential Campaign of 1980

Bush entered the Republican primaries as an experienced establishment candidate. He called Ronald Reagan’s supply-side program “voodoo economics,” won the Iowa caucuses, and briefly appeared capable of stopping Reagan. Reagan defeated him in New Hampshire and consolidated the nomination.

At the Republican convention, Reagan selected Bush as his running mate after negotiations about an unprecedented arrangement with Gerald Ford collapsed. Bush thereafter supported the Reagan program, including economic policies he had criticized.

The Reagan-Bush ticket defeated Jimmy Carter and Walter Mondale in 1980 and won reelection overwhelmingly in 1984.

---

## Vice Presidency, 1981–1989

Bush was an unusually active vice president for his era while remaining publicly deferential to Reagan.

### Functions and travel

He chaired or participated in bodies concerned with deregulation, crisis management, national security, counterterrorism, narcotics, and space policy. He traveled extensively as a diplomatic representative, attended state funerals, met allied and authoritarian leaders, and built relationships later useful in his presidency.

After Reagan was shot on March 30, 1981, Bush returned from Texas but refused theatrically presidential arrangements; he said the president alone landed on the White House South Lawn. Reagan formally transferred power to Bush for several hours during surgery on July 13, 1985, making Bush acting president under the Twenty-Fifth Amendment.

### Ideological adaptation

Bush moved from northeastern Republican moderation toward Reagan’s conservative coalition. He adopted a firmer anti-abortion position, supported tax reduction, military buildup, anticommunist foreign policy, and conservative judicial appointments. He retained a comparatively pragmatic style and close ties to foreign-policy professionals including James Baker and Brent Scowcroft.

### Iran-Contra

The Reagan administration secretly sold arms to Iran while seeking hostage releases and supported the Nicaraguan Contras despite statutory restrictions. Proceeds from arms sales were diverted to the Contra enterprise. Bush attended meetings and received briefings relating to the Iran initiative.

Bush repeatedly said he had been “out of the loop.” His diary and later evidence showed that he knew more about the arms-for-hostages dimension than this formulation suggested. Independent Counsel Lawrence Walsh’s final report devoted a chapter to Bush and stated that a complete picture remained unresolved because Bush declined a final interview after late evidence emerged.

**What is established:**

- Bush attended relevant policy meetings.
- He knew of the Iran arms initiative and expressed reservations in private records.
- There is no court judgment convicting Bush of an Iran-Contra crime.
- He was never indicted.
- His December 1992 pardons of Caspar Weinberger and five others ended or precluded proceedings that could have produced further evidence.

**Walsh’s investigative position:** Bush’s late disclosure of a relevant diary and the pardons were part of a pattern that obstructed full historical and judicial accountability. Walsh said the Weinberger pardon, before trial, foreclosed a proceeding at which Bush might have testified.

**Bush’s position:** The pardoned men had served patriotically, had not profited, had suffered disproportionately, and were caught in disputes better understood as policy conflicts than criminal misconduct.

**Status:** Documented controversy and unresolved historical question; not a conviction or adjudicated finding that Bush joined a criminal conspiracy.

## Election of 1988

Bush defeated Bob Dole and other Republican primary opponents and selected Indiana senator **Dan Quayle** as his running mate. At the Republican convention he promised: **“Read my lips: no new taxes.”**

He defeated Massachusetts governor **Michael Dukakis**, winning approximately 53.4 percent of the popular vote and 426 electoral votes.

### Campaign themes and attack advertising

Bush’s campaign, managed by **Lee Atwater**, portrayed Dukakis as weak on crime, defense, patriotism, and environmental management. Roger Ailes advised on media strategy.

The independent National Security Political Action Committee broadcast the notorious “Weekend Passes” advertisement featuring William Horton, a Black prisoner who committed rape and assault while absent under Massachusetts’s furlough program. The official Bush campaign broadcast the related “Revolving Door” advertisement without displaying Horton’s photograph, while Bush and his campaign repeatedly raised the case.

**Documented fact:** The most notorious Horton-image advertisement was formally independent of the campaign.

**Documented political connection:** Atwater and the Bush campaign made the furlough issue central, and Bush personally invoked Horton.

**Disputed interpretation:** Critics describe the campaign as racialized fear politics or a dog whistle; campaign defenders argued that the subject was violent crime and executive responsibility. The racial imagery, selective personalization, and political coordination surrounding the issue remain central to assessments of Bush’s electoral methods.

## Presidency, 1989–1993

### Administration and governing network

Key figures included:

- James A. Baker III, secretary of state and later White House chief of staff
- Brent Scowcroft, national security adviser
- Dick Cheney, secretary of defense
- Colin Powell, chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff
- Nicholas Brady, treasury secretary
- Richard Thornburgh and William Barr, attorneys general
- John Sununu, White House chief of staff until 1991
- Dan Quayle, vice president
- Robert Gates, deputy national security adviser and later director of Central Intelligence

Bush described his desired public culture as a “kinder, gentler nation” and promoted “a thousand points of light,” meaning voluntary civic organizations. His governing style favored private personal diplomacy, handwritten correspondence, coalition building, incremental bargaining, and experienced advisers.

## Domestic Policy

### Budget, recession, and “no new taxes”

Bush inherited structural federal deficits. In 1990 he negotiated a bipartisan budget agreement containing spending restraints and tax increases. This violated his 1988 pledge and damaged his standing with conservative Republicans. The agreement contributed to later deficit reduction, but the immediate political result was a conservative revolt led in part by Newt Gingrich and enduring distrust among the Republican base.

The economy entered recession in 1990–1991, followed by a slow and uneven recovery. Unemployment and perceptions that Bush was inattentive to domestic distress contributed to his defeat.

### Americans with Disabilities Act

Bush signed the **Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990**, prohibiting disability discrimination in employment, public accommodations, transportation, and other areas. It is one of the major civil-rights statutes of the twentieth century.

### Clean Air Act Amendments

He signed the **Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990**, creating, among other mechanisms, the sulfur-dioxide cap-and-trade program used to reduce acid-rain pollution. The legislation combined environmental regulation with market mechanisms.

### Immigration

Bush signed the Immigration Act of 1990, expanding legal immigration categories and revising employment- and family-based systems.

### Civil Rights Act of 1991

After vetoing an earlier civil-rights bill that he described as encouraging quotas, Bush signed the Civil Rights Act of 1991. The episode illustrated both his resistance to some remedies for employment discrimination and his willingness to accept revised bipartisan legislation.

### Education and voluntarism

The administration developed the “America 2000” education strategy and promoted Points of Light volunteer initiatives. Many proposals were not enacted in the form initially envisioned.

### Supreme Court appointments

Bush appointed:

- **David Souter**, who became associated with the Court’s more moderate-to-liberal bloc.
- **Clarence Thomas**, whose confirmation hearings included Anita Hill’s allegations that Thomas had sexually harassed her. Bush stood behind Thomas, who denied Hill’s claims and was confirmed 52–48.

Bush was not accused of participating in the alleged conduct. His selection of Thomas and support throughout the hearings remain politically contested.

### AIDS

The administration continued federal AIDS programs and Bush signed the Ryan White Comprehensive AIDS Resources Emergency Act. Activists nevertheless accused the administration of inadequate urgency, funding, visibility, and leadership. ACT UP protests targeted Bush’s White House. The record combines significant statutory action with continued criticism from affected communities.

### Firearms

The administration temporarily halted and then permanently restricted importation of certain semiautomatic rifles. This later contributed to conflict with absolutist gun-rights organizations.

## Foreign Policy

### End of the Cold War

Bush entered office while Soviet power was rapidly changing. He initially conducted a policy review that critics called a “pause.” Thereafter he worked closely with **Mikhail Gorbachev**, Eduard Shevardnadze, Helmut Kohl, François Mitterrand, Margaret Thatcher, and others.

Major developments included:

- Malta summit with Gorbachev, December 1989.
- Reunification of Germany in 1990 through the Two Plus Four diplomatic process.
- Conventional Armed Forces in Europe Treaty.
- START I, signed in 1991.
- Management of the failed August 1991 Soviet coup.
- Recognition of post-Soviet states and negotiation of START II with Boris Yeltsin, signed in January 1993.

Bush deliberately avoided public triumphalism as communist governments collapsed. Supporters credit this restraint with reducing Soviet humiliation and helping prevent violent escalation. Critics argue that United States policy offered insufficient economic assistance after the Soviet collapse and prioritized stability over democratic transformation.

### Tiananmen Square

After the Chinese government’s June 1989 military suppression of demonstrations, Bush publicly condemned the violence and imposed some sanctions. He also secretly sent Brent Scowcroft and Lawrence Eagleburger to Beijing to preserve strategic relations.

**Documented fact:** The secret mission occurred.

**Disputed interpretation:** Defenders regarded it as necessary statecraft; critics saw it as undercutting public condemnation and insulating Chinese leaders from consequences. Bush’s earlier Beijing post and personal relationships shaped his approach.

### Panama and Manuel Noriega

On December 20, 1989, Bush ordered **Operation Just Cause**, the invasion of Panama. The stated purposes included protecting United States citizens, defending treaty rights, restoring the elected government, and arresting General **Manuel Noriega** on drug-trafficking charges.

Noriega surrendered in January 1990 and was taken to the United States, where he was convicted.

**Documented complication:** Noriega had maintained relationships with United States intelligence agencies over many years, including periods before and during Bush’s CIA leadership. Knowledge of Noriega’s trafficking and abuses accumulated while Washington continued to use him.

**Casualties:** United States figures and independent estimates differed substantially. The Pentagon reported several hundred Panamanian deaths; human-rights organizations and Panamanian sources argued that civilian deaths were higher. Exact totals remain unresolved.

**Legal and human-rights controversy:** Human Rights Watch/Americas Watch found failures to observe proportionality and minimize civilian harm in some operations. The United Nations General Assembly characterized the intervention as a violation of international law. The United States defended it as lawful self-defense, treaty protection, and support for democracy.

### Persian Gulf crisis and war

Iraq invaded Kuwait on August 2, 1990. Bush organized a broad international coalition, obtained a series of United Nations Security Council resolutions, deployed forces to Saudi Arabia in Operation Desert Shield, and secured congressional authorization for force.

Operation Desert Storm began January 17, 1991. Coalition ground operations began February 24; Bush suspended offensive operations after one hundred hours. Kuwait was liberated, but **Saddam Hussein** remained in power.

Successes commonly attributed to the administration include the large coalition, United Nations framework, limited stated objective, rapid battlefield outcome, and avoidance of an occupation of Baghdad.

Controversies include:

- Large-scale destruction of Iraqi military and civilian infrastructure.
- Bombing of facilities whose loss damaged water, electricity, sanitation, and civilian health.
- Civilian deaths, including the Amiriyah shelter bombing.
- Attacks on retreating Iraqi forces and mixed military-civilian traffic on the “Highway of Death.” Human Rights Watch concluded that some attacks appeared indiscriminate.
- Bush’s calls encouraging Iraqis to overthrow Saddam, followed by the coalition’s decision not to intervene directly when the regime crushed Shiite and Kurdish uprisings.
- Postwar sanctions whose humanitarian consequences became increasingly severe during the 1990s.

The decision not to occupy Baghdad was consistent with the United Nations mandate and Bush’s stated goal of liberating Kuwait. Later advocates of the 2003 invasion sometimes treated it as unfinished business; Bush and Scowcroft argued that occupying Iraq would have fractured the coalition and produced indefinite occupation.

### Arab-Israeli diplomacy

The administration convened the 1991 Madrid Conference, bringing Israel, Arab states, and Palestinian representatives into direct negotiations. Bush clashed with Israeli prime minister **Yitzhak Shamir** over loan guarantees and settlement activity.

### Somalia

In December 1992 Bush authorized United States participation in **Operation Restore Hope**, a United Nations-backed humanitarian intervention intended to secure relief delivery during famine and civil war. The operation continued under Bill Clinton and expanded into a politically and militarily more ambitious mission.

### Yugoslavia

The administration supported diplomacy, sanctions, and humanitarian measures but declined major military intervention as Yugoslavia disintegrated. Critics argued that this caution allowed atrocities to expand; officials feared an open-ended ground war without a workable settlement.

### NAFTA

Bush negotiated and signed the North American Free Trade Agreement in December 1992. Congress approved it and Clinton signed implementing legislation in 1993. Supporters associated NAFTA with integrated trade and export growth; critics associated it with job displacement, weakened bargaining power, and corporate mobility. Responsibility belongs to both administrations and Congress, not Bush alone.

---

## 1992 Election and Departure from Office

Bush’s standing reached extraordinary levels after the Gulf War but declined with recession, the tax reversal, conservative dissatisfaction, and the perception that he lacked a compelling domestic program.

Republican challenger **Patrick Buchanan** exposed dissatisfaction within the party. Independent candidate **Ross Perot** emphasized deficits and political dysfunction. Democrat **Bill Clinton** won with approximately 43 percent of the popular vote and 370 electoral votes; Bush received about 37.5 percent and Perot about 18.9 percent.

Bush’s gracious concession letter to Clinton later became a prominent symbol of peaceful transfer and personal restraint. This posthumous symbolism sometimes obscures the severity of the 1988 campaign and the substantial attacks used in 1992.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, Property, and Financial Interests

### Business entities

- Dresser Industries: employee after Yale; entry facilitated by family acquaintance Neil Mallon.
- Bush-Overbey Oil Development Company: co-founder.
- Zapata Petroleum Corporation: co-founder.
- Zapata Off-Shore Company: president.
- Various postgovernment corporate boards and advisory relationships before the 1980 campaign.

### Principal residences and properties

Bush lived at different times in:

- Milton, Massachusetts
- Greenwich, Connecticut
- Andover, Massachusetts
- Naval training and deployment sites
- New Haven, Connecticut
- Odessa and Midland, Texas
- Ventura-area California during early oil assignments
- Houston, Texas
- Washington, D.C.
- New York during the United Nations appointment
- Beijing, China
- The vice-presidential residence at Number One Observatory Circle
- The White House
- Walker’s Point, Kennebunkport, Maine

The Bush family compound at Walker’s Point originated in the Walker family and became central to Bush family identity, diplomacy, recreation, and media imagery.

### Wealth and financial structure

Bush accumulated substantial wealth through oil and investment activity but was not among the wealthiest modern presidents. Exact lifetime net-worth estimates differ and often rely on incomplete asset valuations. During the presidency, assets were placed in a blind trust. Public tax information reported investment income, charitable deductions, salary, and book income.

No court finding established that Bush used the presidency to enrich Zapata or committed securities fraud. Broad claims that every later Bush-family business benefited directly from his official decisions usually combine distinct people, firms, and decades without demonstrating a specific transaction.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

Bush’s worldview combined:

- Episcopal religious observance and a vocabulary of duty, providence, service, and personal decency.
- Northeastern establishment Republicanism.
- Texas business conservatism.
- Cold War internationalism.
- Institutional loyalty to the intelligence, diplomatic, and military establishments.
- Pragmatism in negotiating legislation and international settlements.
- Increasing accommodation to the Republican Party’s anti-abortion and Reaganite conservative wings.

He was generally less ideological than Reagan and less interested in comprehensive political doctrine. His private diaries and letters show stronger emotion, irritation, competitiveness, and self-doubt than his controlled public persona.

The “vision thing,” a phrase attributed to him during the 1988 campaign, became shorthand for the criticism that he lacked ideological or programmatic coherence. Supporters instead regard restraint, coalition maintenance, and attention to personal relationships as the core of his statecraft.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Barbara Bush

Spouse, political partner, family organizer, and major public figure. Their correspondence records affection, separation during war, grief, ambition, and domestic tension. Barbara cultivated a plainspoken persona distinct from the family’s wealth and elite background.

### James Baker

Friend, campaign manager, adviser, White House chief of staff, treasury secretary under Reagan, and secretary of state under Bush. Baker was Bush’s most important political operator and diplomatic collaborator.

### Brent Scowcroft

National security adviser and co-author. Their partnership emphasized orderly process, confidential diplomacy, and balance-of-power reasoning.

### Ronald Reagan

Primary rival in 1980, then president and political patron. Bush subordinated his independent profile as vice president and inherited Reagan’s coalition, but differed in temperament, rhetoric, fiscal flexibility, and some foreign-policy judgments.

### Bill Clinton

Opponent in 1992. In later life the two developed a close friendship through tsunami relief, Hurricane Katrina fundraising, public appearances, and family contact. Barbara Bush jokingly called Clinton another son.

### Mikhail Gorbachev

Negotiating partner during the end of the Cold War. Bush sought to avoid humiliating him while pursuing German reunification and arms reduction.

### Helmut Kohl

Close partner in German reunification. Kohl and Bush maintained a long friendship.

### George W. Bush and Jeb Bush

Bush was father, adviser, fundraiser, symbol, and occasional political complication to both sons. George W.’s presidency prompted continual comparison, especially on Iraq. The elder Bush generally avoided public disagreement while associates sometimes conveyed unease over George W.’s more ideological advisers.

### Intelligence and national-security network

Important figures included William Casey, Robert Gates, Dick Cheney, Colin Powell, Don Gregg, Oliver North, Caspar Weinberger, George Shultz, and Lawrence Eagleburger. Their relationships intersected in the Reagan administration, Iran-Contra, and later national-security policy, but their roles and degrees of knowledge differed materially.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Unresolved Questions

## Iran-Contra pardons

On December 24, 1992, Bush pardoned former defense secretary Caspar Weinberger and five other Iran-Contra defendants or convicted figures: Elliott Abrams, Duane Clarridge, Clair George, Alan Fiers, and Robert McFarlane.

**Documented effect:** The pardons terminated Weinberger’s pending prosecution and eliminated further criminal exposure for the others.

**Walsh’s accusation:** The pardons completed a cover-up and protected Bush from evidence likely to emerge at Weinberger’s trial.

**Bush’s defense:** The prosecutions represented criminalization of policy disputes and excessive punishment of public servants.

**Legal status:** The pardon power was constitutionally exercised and never invalidated. Bush was not charged with obstruction in connection with the pardons. Whether they constituted an abuse of power remains a historical and constitutional judgment, not a court finding.

## Sexual-touching allegations

In 2017, eight women publicly alleged that Bush touched or grabbed their buttocks during photographs or encounters occurring from 1992 through 2016. Identified accusers included Heather Lind, Jordana Grolnick, Roslyn Corrigan, and Megan Elizabeth Lewis; others spoke publicly or anonymously through news organizations.

Bush’s spokesman did not categorically deny that touching occurred. The office stated that Bush, using a wheelchair in later years, sometimes told a joke and “patted women’s rears” in what he intended as humor, apologized to anyone offended, and denied an intention to cause distress.

Important evidentiary distinctions:

- The allegations did not produce criminal charges, trial findings, or convictions.
- Some accusations concerned periods before Bush used a wheelchair, weakening the physical-position explanation as a complete account.
- The spokesman’s statement was a partial acknowledgment of a recurring practice, not an admission to every accuser’s exact account.
- A separate, more extreme civil allegation by Margie Schoedinger produced no adjudicated finding substantiating her claims and should not be treated as established fact.

## Willie Horton and racial politics

The furlough incident was real, but the selective concentration on Horton, imagery of a Black offender, and repeated association with Dukakis were criticized as racial manipulation. Bush’s campaign denied racist motivation. The distinction between the independent Horton-image advertisement and the official campaign’s related messaging is important but does not separate the campaign from the overall strategy.

## Panama: legality, prior relationship, and casualties

The invasion removed an indicted dictator and enabled the elected government to take office. It also followed years in which United States agencies had treated Noriega as a useful intelligence relationship despite criminality and repression. Civilian casualties remain disputed, and human-rights investigations criticized aspects of the operation.

## Gulf War conduct

No international tribunal convicted Bush of war crimes. Human-rights organizations nevertheless documented potentially indiscriminate attacks and severe civilian infrastructure damage. Claims that the entire Gulf War was simply a criminal oil seizure are unsupported reductions; claims that the campaign was nearly bloodless are likewise inconsistent with the evidence.

### Nayirah testimony

Before the war, a young Kuwaiti witness identified as “Nayirah” told a congressional human-rights caucus that Iraqi troops removed babies from incubators. She was later revealed as the daughter of Kuwait’s ambassador to the United States, and her appearance had been assisted by the public-relations firm Hill & Knowlton. The most dramatic incubator story was not reliably substantiated.

There is no demonstrated evidence that Bush personally devised the testimony. His administration repeated atrocity reports useful in building support for war, however, without adequately established verification.

## AIDS policy

Bush signed major legislation but faced sustained activist criticism for insufficient funding, urgency, and public engagement. Assertions that he did “nothing” are factually inaccurate; assertions that his policy satisfied the needs identified by affected communities disregard the contemporary record.

## Clarence Thomas nomination

Bush called Thomas the most qualified available candidate, a judgment widely disputed because of Thomas’s limited judicial experience and the political importance of replacing Thurgood Marshall. Anita Hill’s accusations were against Thomas, not Bush, but Bush’s selection and unwavering support tied his administration to the confirmation controversy.

## Tax pledge

Bush’s unequivocal “no new taxes” promise conflicted with the 1990 agreement he signed. Whether the reversal was responsible fiscal compromise or a breach of political trust is interpretive; the contradiction itself is documented.

## Family influence and “dynasty”

Bush’s father, sons, brothers, and extended relations occupied political, financial, and nonprofit roles. This supports describing the Bushes as a political dynasty. It does not establish collective legal responsibility for every family member’s action.

Neil Bush’s role in the failed Silverado Savings and Loan became a campaign and family controversy. Federal regulators accused Neil of conflicts and breaches of fiduciary duty; he settled a civil action without admitting wrongdoing. George H. W. Bush was not charged in the Silverado matter.

## October Surprise theory

The theory alleges that Reagan-Bush campaign representatives secretly negotiated with Iran in 1980 to delay release of American hostages until after the election. Some versions place Bush at covert meetings in Paris.

Congressional investigations found insufficient credible evidence to establish the alleged conspiracy. Witness accounts, travel claims, and documentary anomalies continued to sustain the story. No charge or court finding established Bush’s participation. The theory remains an unresolved allegation in popular literature but lacks the evidentiary foundation required to state it as fact.

## “New World Order”

Bush used “new world order” in 1990–1991 to describe a post-Cold War system of collective security and United Nations-backed resistance to aggression. Conspiracy movements detached the phrase from that context and treated it as an admission of a secret plan for world government.

The speeches are documented; the supposed admission of membership in a hidden totalitarian directorate is not.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Secret CIA service before 1976

**Story:** Bush was a covert CIA officer from the 1950s, using Zapata as a front.

**Origins and circulation:** The theory grew from Bush’s oil operations, a CIA memorandum referring to a different or disputed “George Bush,” Zapata/Bay of Pigs name overlap, and later investigative and assassination literature.

**Evidence for:** Bush had international oil contacts, belonged to elite networks, and later became CIA director. An FBI memorandum from 1963 referred to briefing “Mr. George Bush of the Central Intelligence Agency.”

**Evidence against:** The CIA identified the referenced employee as another George Bush; no personnel file, payroll record, operational document, or corroborated testimony establishes George H. W. Bush as a CIA officer before 1976. Circumstantial compatibility is not proof.

**Status:** Allegation or theory, unverified.

### JFK assassination involvement

**Story:** Bush helped organize, finance, conceal, or oversee John F. Kennedy’s assassination; related versions place him in Dallas or Tyler on November 22, 1963.

**Evidence cited by proponents:** An FBI record of Bush reporting a potential threat by a Houston Republican; disputed recollections of his location; the CIA “George Bush” memorandum; Zapata associations; photographs said to resemble him.

**Evidence against:** No official assassination investigation identified Bush as a participant. Photographic identifications are speculative. Reporting a threat to the FBI is not evidence of organizing the assassination. No authenticated operational record places him in the plot.

**Reputational effect:** The story became a durable component of anti-CIA and anti-Bush conspiracy culture and has been amplified by internet communities.

**Status:** Conspiracy theory; unsupported by reliable evidence.

### Bay of Pigs vessels named *Houston* and *Barbara*

**Story:** Bush supplied CIA invasion ships named after his home city and wife.

**Assessment:** Vessels and codenames associated with the operation have been retroactively linked to Bush. No consulted official history establishes his ownership or operational involvement. Name coincidence and later biography carry the argument.

**Status:** Rumor or myth.

### Skull and Bones as hidden government

**Story:** Bush’s Yale society membership proved that he served a transgenerational occult or financial government.

**Documented core:** He was a Skull and Bones member, as were several relatives and associates.

**Unsupported expansion:** Membership alone does not establish that the society dictated policy, controlled the CIA, or ordered wars.

**Status:** Documented association surrounded by unsupported mythology.

### “Wimp” versus warrior-patriarch mythology

Newsweek’s 1987 “Fighting the ‘Wimp Factor’” cover crystallized doubts about Bush’s toughness. His campaign countered through his wartime record, aggressive campaigning, and imagery of national strength. Later hagiography sometimes reversed the caricature into an uncomplicated portrait of martial courage and patrician virtue. Both images simplify a record combining physical courage, political caution, harsh campaign tactics, diplomatic restraint, and willingness to use force.

### Grocery scanner story

A 1992 press account suggested Bush was amazed by an ordinary supermarket scanner, reinforcing an image of elite detachment. The event involved a trade-convention demonstration of newer scanner technology, and the original pool reporting was more qualified than the later legend. The episode mattered politically even though its popular form was misleading.

## Later Life, 1993–2018

Bush returned to Houston and Kennebunkport. The George Bush Presidential Library and Museum opened at Texas A&M University in College Station in 1997.

He undertook paid speeches, charitable work, correspondence, memoir projects, and ceremonial diplomacy. His friendship with former opponent Bill Clinton became especially visible after they were appointed to lead fundraising following the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami. They also worked on Hurricane Katrina relief.

Bush undertook parachute jumps to mark his seventy-fifth, eightieth, eighty-fifth, and ninetieth birthdays, consciously returning to the defining experience of his wartime bailout.

He supported George W. Bush’s presidential campaigns but generally respected the separation between their administrations. George W. Bush later published *41: A Portrait of My Father*.

### Health

Bush was diagnosed with Graves’ disease while president, as was Barbara Bush. In later life he experienced vascular parkinsonism, impaired mobility, and recurrent hospitalizations involving pneumonia, respiratory problems, and infections. He increasingly used a wheelchair or motorized scooter.

Barbara Bush died on April 17, 2018. George was hospitalized the following day with a bloodstream infection but recovered sufficiently to return home and later travel to Maine.

He died at his Houston home on November 30, 2018. His final hours included family calls, including one with George W. Bush. Contemporary accounts did not identify one simple acute cause publicly; his death followed prolonged vascular parkinsonism, declining mobility, infections, and general frailty.

His body lay in state in the United States Capitol. A state funeral was held at Washington National Cathedral on December 5, followed by a service at St. Martin’s Episcopal Church in Houston. A funeral train carried his remains to College Station for burial.

## Works by George H. W. Bush

### Books and edited writings

1. **Looking Forward: An Autobiography** (1987). Published under Bush’s name with journalist and political adviser **Victor Gold**. It is a campaign-era autobiography and should be treated as collaboratively written rather than an entirely solitary literary work.

2. **A World Transformed** (1998), with **Brent Scowcroft**. A co-authored account of foreign policy during the collapse of the Soviet bloc, German reunification, and the Gulf War. It is policy memoir, not a comprehensive autobiography.

3. **All the Best, George Bush: My Life in Letters and Other Writings** (1999; later expanded editions). An edited collection of genuine letters, diary extracts, memoranda, and writings. Editorial selection and framing distinguish it from an unmediated archive.

4. **The China Diary of George H. W. Bush: The Making of a Global President** (2008), edited by **Jeffrey A. Engel**. Bush’s contemporaneous 1974–1975 diary, published decades later with scholarly editing and annotation.

5. **Speaking of Freedom: The Collected Speeches** (2009). An edited selection of speeches delivered under Bush’s name. Presidential speeches ordinarily involve professional speechwriters and staff revision; delivery and authorization are genuine, but sole compositional authorship should not be assumed.

### Official documentary corpus

- *Public Papers of the Presidents of the United States: George Bush*, annual volumes for 1989–1993.
- Presidential executive orders, proclamations, signing statements, veto messages, news conferences, radio addresses, letters, nomination statements, and diplomatic remarks.
- Vice-presidential records, memoranda, schedules, and national-security documents.
- Congressional speeches and statements, 1967–1971.
- United Nations statements, 1971–1973.
- Republican National Committee correspondence and statements, 1973–1974.
- CIA speeches, testimony, correspondence, and administrative records, 1976–1977.
- Personal correspondence and diaries held at the George H. W. Bush Presidential Library.

These official texts were often drafted collectively. They are attributable to Bush as official statements but not necessarily products of unaided personal composition.

## Posthumous Reputation and Historical Reinterpretation

Bush’s reputation has risen in many elite and popular assessments since his 1992 defeat. Common reasons include:

- Comparison with later presidents.
- His acceptance of electoral defeat.
- German reunification and management of the Soviet collapse.
- The limited objective of the Gulf War.
- Bipartisan legislation such as the ADA and Clean Air Act amendments.
- Friendship with Bill Clinton.
- Personal correspondence emphasizing family loyalty and civility.

Countervailing interpretations emphasize:

- The racial politics of the 1988 campaign.
- Iran-Contra pardons.
- Civilian suffering in Panama and Iraq.
- Support for authoritarian partners when strategically useful.
- Tiananmen back-channel diplomacy.
- Slow or inadequate responses to AIDS and economic distress.
- Movement toward anti-abortion politics and conservative judicial transformation.
- Elite family networks inconsistent with the self-made Texas image.

A commemorative “last gentleman” narrative became especially prominent after his death. It reflects genuine elements of his interpersonal behavior and transition conduct but can sanitize hard-edged campaigning, covert-policy controversies, and military decisions. Conversely, conspiracy portrayals that reduce his entire career to secret CIA control generally substitute association and coincidence for documentary proof.

## Atlas Connections

Only roster members with a documented personal, familial, political, or institutional intersection are included. Mere contemporaneity is excluded.

### George Herbert Walker Bush

- **Nature:** Subject of this dossier and roster member.
- **Dates/places:** 1924–2018; United States and extensive international service.
- **Label:** Documented fact.

### Prescott Sheldon Bush

- **Nature:** Father; banker, political model, and United States senator. His Yale, financial, and Republican networks formed part of George H. W. Bush’s early environment.
- **Dates/places:** Family relationship, 1924–1972; Connecticut, New York, Maine, and Washington.
- **Label:** Documented fact.

### George Walker Bush

- **Nature:** Eldest son; political heir, Texas governor, and forty-third president. George H. W. Bush advised and campaigned for him but held no formal office in his administration.
- **Dates/places:** 1946–2018 family relationship; Texas, Washington, Maine, and international family appearances.
- **Label:** Documented fact.

### John Fitzgerald Kennedy

- **Nature:** Bush was a Republican activist during Kennedy’s presidency and entered national politics in the decade following Kennedy’s assassination. No documented personal political collaboration has been established.
- **Contested narrative:** Conspiracy literature alleges Bush participated in or knew about Kennedy’s assassination, drawing on disputed identity records, Zapata associations, and uncertain location claims.
- **Label:** The historical coexistence is documented; assassination connection is an unsupported allegation or theory.

### Manuel Antonio Noriega Moreno

- **Nature:** Panamanian military ruler who had longstanding relationships with United States intelligence agencies. The Bush administration invaded Panama in December 1989, captured Noriega, and transferred him for United States prosecution.
- **Dates/places:** Intelligence-policy relationship predating 1989; direct presidential antagonism, Panama, 1989–1990.
- **Label:** Documented fact; the extent and content of any personal Bush-Noriega interaction during Bush’s CIA tenure require further archival specification.

### Saddam Hussein al-Tikriti

- **Nature:** Iraqi president whose invasion of Kuwait produced the United States-led coalition war authorized by Bush. No direct personal meeting is established here.
- **Dates/places:** Adversarial head-of-government relationship, principally 1990–1991; Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, Washington, and United Nations diplomacy.
- **Label:** Documented fact.

### Oliver Laurence North

- **Nature:** National Security Council staff member central to Iran-Contra while Bush was vice president. North’s operations and later prosecution became part of the inquiry into what senior officials, including Bush, knew.
- **Dates/places:** Reagan administration, principally Washington, 1981–1986; investigations thereafter.
- **Label:** Documented institutional and political association; Bush’s complete knowledge remains unresolved.

### William Joseph Casey

- **Nature:** Reagan campaign manager and later director of Central Intelligence while Bush was vice president; major figure in disputed accounts of the 1980 October Surprise and Iran-Contra.
- **Dates/places:** National Republican campaign and Reagan administration, 1980–1987.
- **Label:** Documented political association; covert-election conspiracy claims remain allegations.

### William Jefferson Clinton

- **Nature:** Defeated Bush in the 1992 presidential election; later became his friend and philanthropic collaborator on tsunami and hurricane relief.
- **Dates/places:** Electoral rivalry, 1992; collaboration and friendship principally 2005–2018, United States and international relief work.
- **Label:** Documented fact.

### Hillary Diane Rodham Clinton

- **Nature:** First lady during the administration succeeding Bush; encountered Bush through presidential-family, ceremonial, and bipartisan public networks. No independent close relationship comparable to Bush’s friendship with Bill Clinton is established.
- **Dates/places:** Principally Washington and later ceremonial events.
- **Label:** Reported/documented public association, limited in significance.

### Benjamin Netanyahu

- **Nature:** Israeli diplomat and political figure within the United States-Israel relationship during Bush’s presidency. Bush’s principal documented policy conflict was with Prime Minister Yitzhak Shamir; Netanyahu served as deputy foreign minister and participated in the Madrid-era diplomatic environment.
- **Dates/places:** 1989–1992, Israel, Washington, and Madrid-process diplomacy.
- **Label:** Documented institutional intersection; no major personal bond established here.

### Fidel Alejandro Castro Ruz

- **Nature:** Leader of Cuba throughout Bush’s political and intelligence career; object of United States containment and embargo policy. Bush supported anti-Castro policy but no personal meeting is established.
- **Dates/places:** Political antagonism, especially during Bush’s CIA and presidential service.
- **Label:** Documented policy relationship, not a documented personal relationship.

### David Rockefeller

- **Nature:** Member of the northeastern financial and foreign-policy establishment in which the Bush and Walker families operated. Both participated in national and international policy networks.
- **Qualification:** Shared institutional milieu alone is insufficient to establish a specific decisive patron-client relationship.
- **Label:** Reported social/institutional association; stronger claims require transaction-specific evidence.

### George Washington

- **Nature:** Bush invoked Washington symbolically and took the presidential oath in 1989 using the Bible associated with Washington’s first inauguration.
- **Dates/places:** January 20, 1989, United States Capitol.
- **Label:** Documented symbolic connection, not a personal historical relationship.

## Compact Chronology

- **1924, June 12:** Born in Milton, Massachusetts.
- **1930s:** Raised in Greenwich, Connecticut; summers at Walker’s Point, Maine.
- **1937–1942:** Attends Phillips Academy, Andover.
- **1941:** Meets Barbara Pierce.
- **1942, June 12:** Graduates from Andover and enlists in the Navy.
- **1943:** Receives naval aviator’s wings.
- **1944, September 2:** Shot down near Chichijima; rescued by USS *Finback*.
- **1945, January 6:** Marries Barbara Pierce.
- **1945:** Leaves naval service and enters Yale.
- **1946:** George W. Bush born.
- **1948:** Graduates from Yale; moves to Texas.
- **1949:** Robin Bush born.
- **1951:** Helps establish Bush-Overbey.
- **1953:** Robin dies; Jeb Bush born; Zapata Petroleum formed.
- **1954:** Becomes president of Zapata Off-Shore.
- **Late 1950s:** Family relocates to Houston.
- **1964:** Loses United States Senate race to Ralph Yarborough.
- **1966:** Elected to the House.
- **1968:** Reelected; supports Fair Housing Act.
- **1970:** Loses Senate race to Lloyd Bentsen.
- **1971–1973:** United Nations ambassador.
- **1973–1974:** Republican National Committee chairman during Watergate.
- **1974–1975:** Chief of United States Liaison Office in Beijing.
- **1976–1977:** Director of Central Intelligence.
- **1980:** Runs for president; becomes Reagan’s running mate.
- **1981–1989:** Vice president.
- **1985, July 13:** Serves briefly as acting president.
- **1986:** Iran-Contra becomes public.
- **1988:** Wins presidency after campaign against Michael Dukakis.
- **1989, January 20:** Inaugurated as forty-first president.
- **1989, June:** Tiananmen crackdown and United States sanctions/back-channel diplomacy.
- **1989, December:** Malta summit; invasion of Panama.
- **1990:** Signs ADA, Clean Air Act amendments, immigration legislation, and tax-containing budget agreement.
- **1990–1991:** Organizes coalition after Iraqi invasion of Kuwait.
- **1991, January–February:** Gulf War.
- **1991:** START I; Madrid Peace Conference; Clarence Thomas confirmed.
- **1992:** Signs NAFTA; defeated by Bill Clinton.
- **1992, December 24:** Pardons six Iran-Contra figures.
- **1993, January:** Signs START II with Boris Yeltsin; leaves office January 20.
- **1997:** Presidential library opens at Texas A&M.
- **1998–1999:** Publishes major foreign-policy memoir and letter collection.
- **2005 onward:** Relief collaboration and friendship with Bill Clinton.
- **2008:** *China Diary* published.
- **2011:** Receives Presidential Medal of Freedom.
- **2012:** HBO documentary *41* released.
- **2014:** Makes ninetieth-birthday parachute jump.
- **2017:** Multiple women publicly accuse him of inappropriate touching; spokesman acknowledges occasional rear-patting intended as humor and apologizes.
- **2018, April 17:** Barbara Bush dies.
- **2018, November 30:** Dies in Houston.
- **2018, December 6:** Buried at College Station after state and church funerals.
