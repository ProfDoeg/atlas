# Ahmad al-Majriti: Research Dossier

## Scope and Identification Note

**Finding:** No securely attested medieval person is known simply as **“Ahmad al-Majriti.”** In the documentary record relevant to the *Rutbat al-ḥakīm* and *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm*, **Aḥmad** is principally a patronymic: the historical astronomer’s name was **Maslama ibn Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī**, “Maslama, son of Aḥmad, the man from Madrid.”

Over centuries of manuscript cataloguing, translation, abbreviation, and modern metadata creation, that patronymic sometimes became detached from *Maslama* and circulated as if it were the author’s personal name. “Ahmad al-Majriti” is therefore best reconstructed as a **bibliographical identity produced by conflation**, not as a separately documented individual.

Two historical men stand behind the corpus:

1. **Maslama ibn Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī**, astronomer and mathematician, associated with Madrid and Córdoba, died in 398 AH/1007–08 CE.
2. **Abū al-Qāsim Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī**, Cordoban traditionist, traveller, esoteric practitioner, and—according to the now-dominant scholarly identification—author of the *Rutbat al-ḥakīm* and *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm*. He lived approximately 293–353 AH/905–06–964 CE.

This dossier consequently distinguishes:

- the **name-object** “Ahmad al-Majriti”;
- the **traditional attribution** to Maslama ibn Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī;
- the **probable historical author**, Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī;
- the later Latin author-persona **Picatrix**.

### Evidentiary conclusion

**Documented fact:** Medieval and modern catalogues assign *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm* and *Rutbat al-ḥakīm* to “Maslama ibn Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī.”

**Documented fact:** The internal dates supplied by the works place the *Rutba* in 339–342 AH/950–953 and the *Ghāya* in 343–348 AH/954–959.

**Chronological objection:** If Maslama ibn Aḥmad was born around 950 and died in 1007–08, he could not credibly have written the *Rutba* beginning in 950.

**Current scholarly finding:** Maribel Fierro’s 1996 identification of the author as Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī was strengthened by Godefroid de Callataÿ, Sébastien Moureau, Mourad Kacimi, and manuscript evidence published after 2013. It is the strongest available identification, although an autograph or contemporary colophon conclusively naming the author has not surfaced.

## Family and Ancestry

### Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī

His transmitted genealogy is:

**Maslama ibn Qāsim ibn Ibrāhīm ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ḥātim.**

The nisbas **al-Qurṭubī** and **al-Andalusī** associate him with Córdoba and al-Andalus. Some modern authority records add **al-Zayyāt**, which ordinarily denotes an oil merchant or dealer, but the evidence that Maslama himself practiced that occupation is insufficient to reconstruct an actual business.

No reliable surviving source identifies:

- his mother;
- the occupations or social position of his parents;
- siblings;
- spouse or partner;
- children;
- inherited property;
- a securely traceable lineage after his death.

### Maslama ibn Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī

His father was named **Aḥmad**. Longer genealogies differ among late sources and catalogues, sometimes inserting Qāsim, ʿAbd Allāh, ʿUmar, or Waḍḍāḥ. These variants may themselves reflect contamination between the two Maslamas.

The nisba **al-Majrīṭī** associates him or his family with Majrīṭ, medieval Madrid. Early biographical tradition places his mature professional life at Córdoba.

### The alleged daughter “Fāṭima al-Majrīṭiyya”

**Modern legend / unsupported claim:** Popular biographies sometimes state that Maslama al-Majrīṭī had a daughter named Fāṭima of Madrid who became an astronomer and collaborated with him.

The claim is absent from the principal medieval biographical notices. Its earliest trace identified in modern discussions is a twentieth-century encyclopedia entry. No manuscript, contemporaneous chronicle, astronomical work, teacher-student chain, or independent medieval biography presently establishes her existence.

The story has nevertheless become part of modern commemorative narratives about women in Islamic science. It should not be used as evidence for the family of either Maslama without a newly identified primary source.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Maslama ibn Qāsim

He was reportedly born in **293 AH/905–06 CE**, probably in Córdoba. Later notices say that he heard or collected a large body of hadith in Córdoba before undertaking his eastern journey.

No source consulted supplies recoverable information about:

- childhood household;
- elementary education;
- economic circumstances;
- childhood illnesses;
- siblings;
- marriage;
- youthful employment.

His birth year is partly reconstructed from the report that he died in 353 AH at sixty years of age.

### Maslama ibn Aḥmad

Traditional modern accounts place his birth around **338 AH/950 CE in Majrīṭ**, with a subsequent move to Córdoba. The securely recoverable medieval record is much fuller concerning his later scholarly standing than his childhood.

Claims that he travelled throughout the Islamic East appear to result at least partly from the later fusion of his biography with Maslama ibn Qāsim’s well-documented *riḥla*. De Callataÿ and Moureau specifically identify this as one of the biographical elements transferred between the two men.

## Education and Formation

## Maslama ibn Qāsim: Cordoba and the Eastern *Riḥla*

He began as a collector and transmitter of hadith. Named teachers in the transmitted biographical tradition include:

- Muḥammad ibn ʿUmar ibn Lubāba;
- Aḥmad ibn Khālid;
- Aḥmad ibn Mūsā al-Tammār at Qayrawān;
- ʿAbd Allāh ibn Muḥammad ibn Fiṭṭīs at Qayrawān;
- Ṣāliḥ ibn Aḥmad ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-ʿIjlī al-Kūfī at Tripoli;
- Aḥmad ibn Muḥammad ibn Khalaf in Crete;
- Muḥammad ibn Zabbān in Egypt;
- Abū Jaʿfar al-Ṭaḥāwī in Egypt;
- al-Dībīlī at Mecca;
- ʿAlī ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Mubashshir at Wāsiṭ;
- Abū Rawq al-Hazzānī at Basra;
- Abū Bakr ibn Ziyād al-Naysābūrī at Baghdad.

Other authority databases name al-Ḥusayn ibn Ismāʿīl al-Maḥāmilī, Aḥmad ibn Muḥammad al-ʿAnazī, Muḥammad ibn Abān al-Balkhī, and ʿAlī ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Makhzūmī.

Before 320 AH/932–33, he left al-Andalus. His itinerary is reported as including:

- Qayrawān;
- Tripoli;
- Alexandria;
- Crete;
- Egypt/Fusṭāṭ;
- al-Qulzum;
- Jeddah;
- Mecca;
- possibly Medina;
- Basra;
- Wāsiṭ;
- Baghdad;
- al-Madāʾin;
- Ramla and Jerusalem;
- Sīrāf;
- Yemen;
- Syria.

The full sequence and duration at every stop cannot be fixed. He returned to al-Andalus shortly after 325 AH/936–37 according to de Callataÿ and Moureau’s reconstruction.

### Intellectual acquisitions

**Scholarly reconstruction:** During or through this eastern journey Maslama obtained:

- hadith and biographical knowledge;
- writings connected with Sahl al-Tustarī;
- writings attributed to Dhū al-Nūn al-Miṣrī;
- material associated with the *Rasāʾil Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ*;
- alchemical and talismanic materials circulating in Egypt, Iraq, Syria, and the eastern Mediterranean.

The claim that he personally introduced the complete *Rasāʾil Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ* into al-Andalus is strong but inferential. It rests on the timing of his journey, manuscript notices, and extensive textual dependence of the *Rutba* and *Ghāya* upon the *Rasāʾil*.

## Maslama ibn Aḥmad: Scientific Formation

The surviving notices describe Maslama ibn Aḥmad as a leading Andalusi mathematician and astronomer. His precise teachers are not securely named.

His formation is visible through his works:

- engagement with al-Khwārizmī’s astronomical tables;
- recalculation of astronomical parameters for Córdoba;
- work with the Hijri calendar;
- use of al-Battānī;
- work connected with Ptolemy’s *Planisphaerium*;
- arithmetic and inheritance calculations;
- training of a substantial group of mathematicians and instrument makers.

## Early Career

### Maslama ibn Qāsim

Upon returning to Córdoba, Maslama became known as a prolific transmitter. Later scholars described him as bringing back “much knowledge.” He taught and transmitted hadith to a circle of students.

A politically important reported pupil was **ʿAbd Allāh**, son of the Umayyad caliph ʿAbd al-Raḥmān III. ʿAbd Allāh was accused of conspiring against his father and was executed in 339 AH/950–51. No evidence establishes that Maslama participated in the conspiracy. The teacher-student relationship places him near a politically exposed courtly network but does not prove political complicity.

### Maslama ibn Aḥmad

He became active at Córdoba during the later Umayyad caliphate, especially in the intellectual world associated with al-Ḥakam II and his successors. Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa describes him as the outstanding teacher of a generation of Andalusi scientific scholars.

There is no evidence of a formal salaried court office, directorship, company, or commercial partnership in the modern sense.

## Chronological Life History

## c. 905–06: Birth of the probable author

Maslama ibn Qāsim was born in 293 AH, apparently in Córdoba. His ancestry beyond the names preserved in his genealogy is unknown.

## Before 932–33: Education in Córdoba

He studied hadith and related religious disciplines in Córdoba. His later lost biographical work reportedly concentrated on transmitters omitted from al-Bukhārī’s historical compilation, indicating training in *rijāl* criticism and scholarly biography.

## Early–mid 930s: Journey east

He travelled through North Africa, Egypt, the Hijaz, Iraq, the Persian Gulf, Yemen, Syria, and possibly Crete. The journey brought him into contact with numerous hadith scholars and intellectual currents unavailable in Córdoba.

### Jerusalem lion story

**Legend reported in biographical literature:** Yaḥyā ibn al-Haytham allegedly related that Maslama once slept inside Jerusalem’s sacred precinct after its doors had been shut and awoke to find a lion beside him.

The surviving database excerpt breaks off before the story’s conclusion. It belongs to a genre of saintly or marvel-filled biography and cannot be treated as zoological or eyewitness fact. Its preservation demonstrates that Maslama’s eastern journey acquired a hagiographic dimension.

## Shortly after 936: Return to al-Andalus

De Callataÿ and Moureau place his return shortly after 325 AH/936. He brought books, traditions, and possibly the *Rasāʾil Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ*.

## 950–953: Composition of the *Rutbat al-ḥakīm*

The work’s internal chronology places its composition between 339 and 342 AH.

The title may be translated as:

- *The Rank of the Sage*;
- *The Degree of the Sage*;
- *The Step of the Sage*.

It presents alchemy as one of the two culminating sciences of philosophy. It treats substances, metals, laboratory operations, coded terminology, and earlier authorities. Zosimos of Panopolis is especially important.

The *Rutba* describes itself in relation to the *Rasāʾil*. Later readers interpreted ambiguous statements as claiming that its author had composed the *Rasāʾil* themselves. This helped create the false image of a three- or four-work “Maslama corpus.”

## 950–51: Court connection and political danger

Maslama reportedly taught ʿAbd Allāh, a son of ʿAbd al-Raḥmān III. The prince was executed after being accused of conspiracy.

**Documented association; no documented complicity:** Nothing in the evidence consulted shows that Maslama was arrested, tried, imprisoned, exiled, or punished in connection with the prince.

## 954–959: Composition of the *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm*

The author states that he began the work after completing the *Rutba* and finished it in 348 AH/959.

Its full title is usually transmitted as:

**غاية الحكيم وأحق النتيجتين بالتقديم**  
*Ghāyat al-ḥakīm wa-aḥaqq al-natījatayn bi-l-taqdīm*

Translations vary:

- *The Aim of the Sage and the More Worthy of the Two Results to Be Advanced*;
- *The Goal of the Wise*;
- *The Aim of the Sage*.

The “two results” are alchemy and magic. The author ranks magic or astral-talismanic science as the higher culmination.

The work is a compilation rather than a wholly original system. It combines:

- Aristotelian and Neoplatonic cosmology;
- astrology;
- planetary images and talismans;
- lunar mansions;
- magical timing;
- incenses and suffumigations;
- stones, plants, animals, and bodily materials;
- prayers and invocations to planetary spiritual beings;
- Hermetic materials;
- material ascribed to Plato, Aristotle, Hermes, Hippocrates/Buqrāṭīs, al-Kindī, Jābir ibn Ḥayyān, the Nabataeans, Indians, and others;
- extensive verbal dependence on the *Rasāʾil Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ*.

David Pingree demonstrated that many sources are identifiable compilations, translations, and pseudepigrapha rather than direct access to the named ancient authors.

## 964: Blindness and death

Later biographical accounts say that Maslama became blind after returning to al-Andalus. The date and cause of blindness are unknown.

Ibn al-Faraḍī’s notice places his death on a **Monday, with eight nights remaining in Jumādā I, 353 AH**, at the age of sixty. Exact conversion to the Julian calendar is uncertain because medieval lunar dates depend on month observation; it falls in 964 CE.

The place of death is generally treated as Córdoba, but the cited notice does not establish a street, residence, burial place, or tomb.

No cause of death is recorded.

## The Parallel Life: Maslama ibn Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī

## c. 950: Traditional birth at Majrīṭ

Modern reference works commonly give 338 AH/950 CE and Madrid. The early evidence does not securely establish an exact day or month.

## Later tenth century: Career in Córdoba

He worked in mathematics and astronomy and assembled an influential teaching circle. His adaptation of al-Khwārizmī’s *Zīj* converted chronological elements from the Persian era to the Islamic calendar and adjusted geographic parameters for Córdoba.

He also worked on or transmitted material connected with Ptolemy’s *Planisphaerium* and al-Battānī’s planetary equations.

## Students and school

Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa names:

- Ibn al-Samḥ;
- Ibn al-Ṣaffār;
- al-Zahrāwī;
- al-Kirmānī;
- Ibn Khaldūn—not the fourteenth-century historian, but an earlier scholar bearing the name.

Claims that the famous surgeon Abū al-Qāsim al-Zahrāwī was formally his student require caution because names and scholarly circles may have been simplified in later transmission. The broad existence of a major scientific school around al-Majrīṭī is well supported.

## 1004–08: Death

The most common date is 398 AH/1007–08 CE. Other transmitted dates include 395 and 397 AH. He died shortly before or near the outbreak of the Cordoban civil war.

The cause of death, burial place, family situation, and estate are unknown.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

## Religious identity

Maslama ibn Qāsim operated publicly as a collector and transmitter of hadith. His intellectual profile, however, was controversial.

Contemporaneous or near-contemporaneous critics accused him of:

- weakness as a transmitter;
- lying or transmitting unreliable material;
- deficient judgment;
- *tashbīh*, language interpreted as anthropomorphism;
- charms, incantations, and *nīranjāt* or magical operations.

Counter-statements survive:

- Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad ibn Yaḥyā ibn Mufarrij reportedly said that Maslama “was not a liar,” although he judged him weak-minded.
- Ibn Ḥazm said that some Andalusis were hostile to him and sometimes called him a liar.
- Ibn Ḥajar called him a man of high standing and reported that the accusation of anthropomorphism came from enemies.

These are evaluations within hadith criticism and sectarian controversy, not judicial findings.

## Bāṭinism

Fierro interpreted Maslama’s environment as Andalusi **bāṭinism**: an emphasis on the inner or hidden meanings of revelation and the cosmos. The term does not by itself establish formal membership in an Ismaili organization.

The *Rutba* and *Ghāya* share with the *Rasāʾil Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ*:

- graded initiation;
- concealment of knowledge;
- cosmic hierarchy;
- number symbolism;
- correspondences between microcosm and macrocosm;
- the notion that philosophical sciences culminate in transformative knowledge.

**Scholarly interpretation, not proven affiliation:** Maslama may have absorbed Ismaili-adjacent, Ṣūfī, Hermetic, or broadly esoteric materials during his eastern travels. No membership register or confession of institutional Ismailism survives.

## Magic and monotheism

The *Ghāya* repeatedly invokes God but also instructs the operator to address planetary spirits, burn substances appropriate to them, fashion images, and seek concrete effects.

Later readers divided over whether this represented:

- natural magic founded on occult properties;
- astral religion;
- philosophical theurgy;
- demonic magic;
- idolatry or *shirk*;
- a technical synthesis whose compiler did not endorse every source quoted.

The work’s compilatory structure prevents every ritual statement from being treated as the author’s personal confession.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Maslama ibn Qāsim

- **ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān III:** Reported pupil; executed for alleged conspiracy in 950–51. No evidence connects Maslama to the plot.
- **Abū Jaʿfar al-Ṭaḥāwī:** Teacher or hadith source encountered in Egypt.
- **Numerous eastern hadith masters:** Documentary teacher-transmitter relationships preserved in biographical literature.
- **Sahl al-Tustarī and Dhū al-Nūn al-Miṣrī:** Intellectual transmission through texts; no personal meeting was possible because both died before or near Maslama’s youth.
- **Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ:** Strong textual relationship and probable transmission of their writings; personal membership or meetings are unproven.
- **Zosimos of Panopolis:** Ancient textual authority treated as a master in alchemy; not a personal relationship.
- **Ibn al-Faraḍī:** Later Cordoban biographer and critic, not a known personal associate.
- **Ibn Ḥazm:** Later evaluator who defended him against some hostile accusations.

### Maslama ibn Aḥmad

- **al-Ḥakam II:** Possible patronage or courtly connection; documentary details are limited.
- **Ibn al-Samḥ, Ibn al-Ṣaffār, al-Kirmānī, al-Zahrāwī, and an earlier Ibn Khaldūn:** Named as leading students in the later biographical tradition.
- **al-Khwārizmī, al-Battānī, Ptolemy:** Textual and scientific predecessors, not contemporaries.

### Posthumous agents of the name

- **Alfonso X of Castile:** Ordered the Arabic *Ghāya* translated into Castilian in 1256; this was the decisive transfer into Latin Europe.
- **Yehudā ben Moshe:** Tentatively proposed by Pingree as the Castilian translator; attribution remains conjectural.
- **Aegidius de Tebaldis of Parma:** Tentatively proposed as responsible for the Latin version; uncertain.
- **Ibn Khaldūn:** Assigned the alchemical and magical pair to Maslama al-Majrīṭī, helping canonize the mistaken attribution.
- **Aydamir al-Jildakī:** Often identified as the earliest known writer explicitly assigning the occult corpus to al-Majrīṭī.
- **Wilhelm Printz:** Identified the Arabic *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm* as the source of the Latin *Picatrix* in the early twentieth century.
- **Aby Warburg, Hellmut Ritter, Martin Plessner, David Pingree, Frances Yates, Maribel Fierro, Godefroid de Callataÿ, Sébastien Moureau, Mourad Kacimi, and Wilferd Madelung:** Principal modern agents in rediscovery, editing, interpretation, and reassignment.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

## Authorship controversy

### Traditional claim

Maslama ibn Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī wrote the *Rutbat al-ḥakīm* and *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm*.

**Status:** Traditional attribution, now generally rejected or heavily qualified.

### Evidence against

- The works’ internal dates are incompatible with the usual birth chronology of Maslama ibn Aḥmad.
- Early biographies of the astronomer mention mathematical and astronomical works but not the *Rutba* or *Ghāya*.
- Biographical details in relevant manuscript annotations fit Maslama ibn Qāsim better.
- Maslama ibn Qāsim’s eastern journey and reported involvement with charms fit the corpus’s source base.
- The works’ use of the *Rasāʾil* corresponds with evidence that ibn Qāsim transmitted that material westward.

### Evidence preserving uncertainty

- No autograph survives.
- Many manuscripts explicitly name Maslama ibn Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī.
- Some modern scholars and Arabic publications continue to defend the traditional attribution, citing Ibn Khaldūn, Ḥājjī Khalīfa, and manuscript title pages.
- Much of the decisive reconstruction depends on later annotations and comparative textual reasoning.
- A fully exhaustive stemmatic study of every *Ghāya* and *Rutba* witness remains difficult.

### Current assessment

**Best-supported scholarly identification:** Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī.

**Residual uncertainty:** The possibility of compilation, revision, multiple redactions, or later reattribution cannot be eliminated.

## Accusations against Maslama ibn Qāsim

### False or unreliable hadith transmission

**Allegation:** Some Andalusis accused him of lying.

**Counter-evidence:** A named critic reportedly denied that he was a liar but described him as intellectually weak. Ibn Ḥazm attributed at least some attacks to hostility.

**Status:** Contested scholarly accusation; no criminal adjudication.

### Anthropomorphism

**Allegation:** He was said to have made objectionable statements involving *tashbīh*, likening God to created things.

**Counter-evidence:** Ibn Ḥajar stated that only an enemy attributed anthropomorphism to him.

**Status:** Sectarian or theological accusation, unresolved.

### Magic, charms, and talismans

Ibn al-Faraḍī described him as a practitioner or possessor of charms and *nīranjāt*. This description supports, but does not prove, his authorship of the magical corpus.

**Status:** Near-contemporary reported characterization.

### Political conspiracy

His pupil ʿAbd Allāh was executed for allegedly plotting against the caliph.

**Status regarding Maslama:** No accusation against Maslama has been documented. Any claim that he belonged to the conspiracy is speculation.

## Ritual violence and objectionable recipes

The *Ghāya/Picatrix* contains operations involving animal substances, blood, suffumigations, sacrifices, poisons, repellent materials, erotic coercion, hostility, separation, destruction, and domination.

**Documented textual content:** Such recipes exist within the transmitted work.

**Unresolved question:** Whether the compiler performed them, believed all of them effective, or included them as antiquarian source material cannot be established.

## Modern adversarial search findings

Dedicated searches combining the name and both historical candidates with “controversy,” “scandal,” “allegations,” “lawsuit,” “fraud,” “accusations,” “conspiracy,” and comparable Arabic terms produced:

- no modern lawsuit;
- no financial-fraud investigation;
- no corruption record;
- no money-laundering claim;
- no criminal indictment or conviction;
- no intelligence-service connection;
- no credible modern scandal involving a living person by this identity.

The significant negative record is medieval and intellectual: contested hadith reliability, accusations of anthropomorphism, esoteric practice, dangerous ritual instructions, and confused authorship.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## “Ahmad al-Majriti” as an independent man

**Story:** Ahmad al-Majriti was a medieval occultist who wrote *Picatrix*.

**Appearance and circulation:** Modern metadata, abbreviated citations, machine-generated biographies, book listings, and name truncations.

**Evidence:** No independent biographical notice separates such a man from Maslama ibn Aḥmad.

**Assessment:** Bibliographical phantom created when “ibn Aḥmad” was detached from Maslama.

**Reputational effect:** It multiplied author identities and obscured the two actual Maslamas.

## The great scientist-magician

**Story:** A single Madrid-born genius mastered mathematics, astronomy, alchemy, occult philosophy, talismans, hadith, theology, and the *Rasāʾil*, then created a school encompassing them all.

**Origin:** Medieval conflation, firmly visible by the time of Ibn Khaldūn and reinforced by manuscript notices.

**Evidence against:** The chronology and disciplinary biographies separate the astronomer from the travelling traditionist-esotericist.

**Effect:** Produced the durable “al-Majrīṭī” of encyclopedias—scientist, alchemist, magician, and philosopher in one life.

## Author of the *Rasāʾil Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ*

**Story:** Maslama composed the entire encyclopedia of the Brethren of Purity, followed by the *Rutba* and *Ghāya* as advanced stages.

**Evidence:** Ambiguous language in the *Rutba* and later Andalusi annotations.

**Evidence against:** The *Rasāʾil* is a layered, collective or pseudonymous corpus with an eastern formation history.

**Assessment:** False as literal authorship; important as evidence for Maslama ibn Qāsim’s role in transmission.

## Fāṭima al-Majrīṭiyya

**Story:** Al-Majrīṭī’s daughter was an accomplished astronomer who worked beside him.

**Evidence:** Modern retellings beginning no earlier than the twentieth century.

**Evidence against:** Absence from the medieval biographical and astronomical record.

**Status:** Modern commemorative legend.

## Conservation of mass experiment

**Story:** Al-Majrīṭī demonstrated conservation of mass by heating mercury or another substance in a sealed vessel and observing no loss of weight.

**Problem:** The story is commonly attached to the *Rutba* and therefore to the wrong Maslama. Modern formulations often impose a later scientific concept on a medieval alchemical operation.

**Assessment:** A textual experiment may underlie the claim, but attribution to Maslama ibn Aḥmad and characterization as discovery of the modern law of conservation of mass are not secure.

## “Picatrix” as the author’s real name

Explanations have included:

- corruption of an Arabic personal name;
- a transformation of *Maslama*;
- derivation from Spanish or Latin *picare/picator/picatrix*;
- corruption of **Buqrāṭīs/Biqrāṭīs**, an Arabic form related to Hippocrates;
- the name of a sage cited inside the text.

No derivation commands universal agreement. The Latin work turned its obscure title into an author-persona, allowing “Picatrix says…” to circulate as if Picatrix were a historical magician.

## A wholly pagan or “Sabean” author

The prominence of planetary prayer and material attributed to Harran encouraged theories that the author was secretly Sabean, pagan, or an anonymous Harranian.

**Evidence:** Strong Harranian and astral-religious material exists in the compilation.

**Evidence against:** The work is framed in Islamic monotheistic language, contains multiple source layers, and fits an Andalusi compiler with access to eastern materials.

**Status:** Theory, not established biography.

## Deliberate forgery

It is sometimes implied that one of the Maslamas falsely used the other’s name.

No evidence establishes an intentional medieval impersonation. Accretion through copyists, title pages, ambiguous references, and later harmonization provides a sufficient explanation.

## Works by or Attributed to the Person

## A. Probable works of Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī

### 1. *Rutbat al-ḥakīm*

**Arabic:** رتبة الحكيم  
**Extended title:** *Rutbat al-ḥakīm wa-madkhal al-taʿlīm* in some witnesses.  
**Date:** 339–342 AH/950–953 CE.  
**Subject:** Alchemy, laboratory practice, philosophical initiation, substances, metals, coded names.  
**Authorship:** Traditionally assigned to Maslama ibn Aḥmad; now strongly assigned to Maslama ibn Qāsim.  
**Survival:** Numerous Arabic manuscripts.  
**Modern edition:** Wilferd Madelung, Arabic text with English introduction, *The Book of the Rank of the Sage*, Corpus Alchemicum Arabicum IV, 2016/2017.  
**Status:** Genuine attribution to ibn Qāsim is probable, not autograph-proven.

### 2. *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm wa-aḥaqq al-natījatayn bi-l-taqdīm*

**Arabic:** غاية الحكيم وأحق النتيجتين بالتقديم  
**Date:** 343–348 AH/954–959 CE.  
**Subject:** Astral magic, astrology, talismans, cosmology, planetary spirits, magical compounds.  
**Later title:** *Picatrix*.  
**Authorship:** Probable ibn Qāsim; traditionally ibn Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī.  
**Structure:** Four books.  
**Arabic edition:** Hellmut Ritter, 1933.  
**German translation:** Hellmut Ritter and Martin Plessner, 1962.  
**Latin critical edition:** David Pingree, 1986.  
**Major modern translations:** French by Béatrice Bakhouche, Frédéric Fauquier, and Brigitte Pérez-Jean, 2003; English from Latin by Dan Attrell and David Porreca, 2019; multiple modern esoteric translations exist, of varying reliability.

### 3. Biographical work on hadith transmitters

Ibn Ḥajar reports that Maslama compiled a one-volume history of transmitters whom al-Bukhārī had omitted. Its exact title and survival status are uncertain.

**Status:** Reported lost work.

### 4. Hadith transmissions

Hadith databases preserve a small corpus transmitted under his name. These are not an authored “book” in the modern sense; they survive through later isnāds and compilations.

## B. Works associated with Maslama ibn Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī

### 1. Adaptation of al-Khwārizmī’s *Zīj*

Known through the Arabic and Latin astronomical tradition. It adjusted chronological and geographic data for al-Andalus and Córdoba.

**Status:** Secure or substantially secure.

### 2. *Kitāb al-Muʿāmalāt*

Translated as *Book of Transactions* or *Applied Mathematics*. It likely treated practical arithmetic.

**Status:** Named in medieval biography; survival uncertain.

### 3. *Ikhtiṣār taʿdīl al-kawākib min zīj al-Battānī*

“Summary of the planetary equations from al-Battānī’s tables.”

**Status:** Named in medieval biography.

### 4. Work on Ptolemy’s *Planisphaerium*

The Arabic/Latin transmission associates al-Majrīṭī with the work. Whether every element is original, revised, or transmitted through his school requires manuscript-level differentiation.

### 5. Treatise on inheritance calculations

Reported by later scholarship as his only clearly religious-law-related mathematical work.

### 6. Astrolabe treatises, *Thimār al-ʿadad*, *Kitāb al-aḥjār*, *Rawḍat al-ḥadāʾiq*

These appear in later bibliographies with varying titles.

**Status:** Mixed, uncertain, or misattributed; they should not be placed in his secure corpus without manuscript verification.

## C. Works falsely or dubiously attributed to Maslama al-Majrīṭī

- *Rutbat al-ḥakīm* — probable work of ibn Qāsim.
- *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm/Picatrix* — probable work of ibn Qāsim.
- *Rasāʾil Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ* — not his composition.
- *al-Risāla al-jāmiʿa* — later accretion to the false “Maslama trilogy.”
- Some alchemical and astrological works catalogued under “Maslama ibn Aḥmad” may derive from either the occult corpus or later pseudepigraphy.

## Transmission and Publication History

### Arabic circulation

The *Ghāya* circulated widely in Arabic manuscripts, frequently under Maslama ibn Aḥmad’s name. A manuscript dated 4 Shaʿbān 653 AH/15 September 1255, described by Christie’s, demonstrates circulation immediately before the Alfonsine translation.

The *Rutba* also circulated extensively. De Callataÿ and Moureau’s work identified a large manuscript tradition, including biographical annotations that preserve—and help diagnose—the conflation.

### 1256–58: Castilian and Latin translation

The Latin prologue says Alfonso X ordered the book translated from Arabic into Spanish in 1256. The dates supplied in several calendar systems do not agree perfectly, producing a range of 1256–58 in scholarship.

The complete Castilian version is lost. A fragment or related Alfonsine material survives in Vatican manuscript Reg. lat. 1283. The Latin was made from the Castilian rather than directly from Arabic.

### Fifteenth–seventeenth centuries

The earliest known explicit European reference is Johannes Hartlieb’s *Buch aller verbotenen Künste* of 1456. The earliest securely dated Latin manuscript was copied in 1458–59.

Latin copies circulated among physicians, astrologers, magicians, and collectors. A British Library manuscript passed through the hands of Simon Forman, Richard Napier, Elias Ashmole, and William Lilly.

### Twentieth-century rediscovery

- Aby Warburg searched for the *Picatrix* manuscript tradition by 1910.
- Wilhelm Printz identified the Arabic *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm* as the source of the Latin text around 1914.
- Hellmut Ritter edited the Arabic text in 1933.
- Ritter and Martin Plessner published a German translation in 1962.
- David Pingree published the critical Latin text in 1986.
- Maribel Fierro reassigned authorship to Maslama ibn Qāsim in 1996.
- Manuscript research by Kacimi, de Callataÿ, and Moureau strengthened that attribution.
- Wilferd Madelung edited the *Rutba* in 2016/17.

## Death, Later Life, and Present Status

### Maslama ibn Qāsim

He became blind at an unspecified date. He died in 353 AH/964, reportedly aged sixty. No cause of death or burial place is known.

### Maslama ibn Aḥmad

He died in or near 398 AH/1007–08. No cause of death or burial site is securely documented.

### Ahmad al-Majriti

As a detached name, he never had a separately documented birth or death. His “life” is bibliographical:

- formed from the patronymic *ibn Aḥmad*;
- attached to the prestige of al-Majrīṭī;
- propagated through manuscript titles and catalogues;
- translated into the *Picatrix* author-persona;
- challenged by modern philology;
- still present in library metadata, editions, commercial listings, websites, and popular histories in 2026.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

The composite al-Majrīṭī became one of the most consequential authorial identities in the history of learned magic.

### Arabic-Islamic reception

The *Rutba* and *Ghāya* were treated as the paired culmination of philosophy: terrestrial spirits through alchemy and celestial spirits through magic. Ibn Khaldūn discussed both while criticizing obscurity, magical practice, and occult speculation.

The corpus contributed to later discussions of:

- talismans;
- planetary spirituality;
- alchemy;
- hidden properties;
- letter magic;
- the limits of legitimate natural science.

### Latin-European reception

Through Alfonso X’s translation project, the *Ghāya* became *Picatrix*. It supplied Renaissance readers with a large, systematic account of astral magic.

Its influence or documented use is associated with:

- Marsilio Ficino;
- Giovanni Pico della Mirandola;
- Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa;
- Johannes Trithemius;
- Paracelsus;
- Giordano Bruno;
- Tommaso Campanella;
- later astrologers and ritual magicians.

The degree of direct dependence varies. Agrippa explicitly used medieval conjurational authorities including *Picatrix*. For Ficino, Pico, Bruno, and Campanella, scholarship ranges from identifiable textual borrowing to broader participation in the same astromagical tradition.

### Modern scholarly reputation

The corpus became central to twentieth-century study of:

- Renaissance Hermeticism;
- the relationship between magic and early science;
- Arabic-to-Latin translation;
- Andalusi intellectual history;
- the *Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ*;
- pseudepigraphy and unstable authorship.

Aby Warburg’s interest helped restore *Picatrix* to scholarly visibility. Frances Yates made it part of the intellectual background to Renaissance magic, although later scholars revised aspects of her broad “Hermetic tradition” model. Pingree reconstructed its sources and Latin tradition. Fierro’s authorship argument transformed the biographical question.

### Popular occult revival

Modern occult publishers market *Picatrix* as:

- an ancient grimoire;
- a manual of planetary magic;
- a recovered Arabic book of forbidden knowledge;
- a source for practical talisman making.

Such editions sometimes preserve obsolete attribution to “Maslama al-Majriti,” “Pseudo-Majriti,” or “Picatrix” and often omit the distinction between the two Maslamas.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted record are included.

### Maslama ibn Ahmad al-Majriti

**Documented fact — identity overlap:** The requested name is derived from the patronymic and nisba of **Maslama ibn Aḥmad al-Majrīṭī**. His scientific biography was fused with the occult corpus.

### Maslama ibn Qasim al-Qurtubi

**Strong scholarly identification:** He is the probable author behind the *Rutbat al-ḥakīm* and *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm*. This is the dossier’s most important atlas crossing: Ahmad al-Majriti is the travelling misnomer; Maslama ibn Qāsim is the probable human author.

### Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi

**Documented textual connection:** The *Ghāya* uses or attributes astrological, magical, and philosophical material to al-Kindī. This is influence through texts, not a meeting.

### Aristotle

**Documented textual connection:** Aristotelian and pseudo-Aristotelian cosmology, natural philosophy, and occult-property traditions form part of the compiled intellectual framework.

### Plato

**Documented textual connection:** Plato appears as an ancient magical and philosophical authority, often through pseudepigraphic or late antique traditions rather than securely Platonic works.

### Abraham

**Mythic/textual connection:** Abraham appears within the ancient-sage and prophetic universe of astrological and magical literature. No historical interaction is possible.

### King Solomon

**Mythic/textual connection:** Solomonic authority belongs to the legendary background of magical texts and spirit control. This is a literary-symbolic association.

### Maimonides

**Reported textual reception:** Maimonides knew and condemned forms of astral magic related to the literature represented by *Picatrix*. Direct use of this exact work must be established passage by passage; there was no personal connection.

### Ramon Llull

**Reported intellectual environment:** Llull lived within the later western Mediterranean world in which Arabic astral, combinatorial, and occult sciences circulated. No direct personal or textual dependence on the author was established in the sources consulted.

### Albertus Magnus

**Reported reception context:** Albertus participated in the medieval Latin discussion of astrology, talismans, and occult properties into which *Picatrix* entered. A specific direct citation was not established here.

### Giovanni Pico della Mirandola

**Documented/reported textual reception:** Modern scholarship examines Pico’s reading of *Picatrix* and its place in his critique and transformation of astral magic.

### Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa von Nettesheim

**Documented textual connection:** Agrippa’s works refer to and use the medieval conjurational and astromagical tradition represented explicitly by *Picatrix*.

### Johannes Trithemius

**Reported textual reception:** Scholarship on Renaissance readers of *Picatrix* includes Trithemius. The relationship is posthumous textual use.

### Paracelsus

**Reported textual reception:** Included among Renaissance readers or inheritors of the *Picatrix* tradition; the exact degree of direct borrowing remains a specialist question.

### Giordano Bruno

**Documented/reported intellectual influence:** *Picatrix* formed part of the astral-magical literature relevant to Bruno’s images, cosmology, and magical theory. Frances Yates made this relationship central to her interpretation, portions of which later scholarship revised.

### Tommaso Campanella

**Documented/reported reception:** Campanella belongs to the later Renaissance and early modern line of planetary and natural magic in which *Picatrix* was read.

### Frances Amelia Yates

**Documented scholarly connection:** Yates studied *Picatrix* at the British Museum and integrated it into her reconstruction of Renaissance Hermetic and magical traditions.

### Aby Moritz Warburg

**Documented scholarly connection:** Warburg sought manuscripts of *Picatrix* by 1910 and helped make astral imagery and magical transmission central subjects of the institute bearing his name.

### Ibn Arabi

**Reported contextual connection only:** Both belong to Andalusi histories of esotericism, but no personal relationship was possible and no direct dependence was established in the consulted evidence.

### Ziryab

**No supported connection:** Both are associated with Córdoba in different periods, but no documentary personal, institutional, or textual link was found.

### Maimonides, Roger Bacon, John Dee, Giordano Bruno, and Isaac Newton as a generalized “occult-science chain”

Only some links in this common narrative are documented. Shared interest in astrology, alchemy, or magic does not itself prove transmission. No unsupported direct chain has been added.

### Remaining roster

No documentary meeting, correspondence, family relationship, patronage, rivalry, or demonstrable textual influence was identified with the other listed atlas figures. Given the pseudo-authorial nature of “Ahmad al-Majriti,” absence of personal crossings is expected.
