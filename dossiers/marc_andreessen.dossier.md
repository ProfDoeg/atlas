# Marc Andreessen — Research Dossier

**Subject:** Marc Lowell Andreessen  
**Date of dossier:** 19 August 2026  
**Status:** Living  
**Scope:** Birth through 19 August 2026  
**Method:** Documentary chronology assembled from primary and near-primary material where available (oral history, university archives, SEC filings, court opinions, White House and Defense Department records, company statements and Andreessen's own writings), supplemented by contemporaneous and retrospective reporting. Claims made in litigation or political controversy are identified by procedural status rather than treated as findings.

## Evidence and Status Conventions

This dossier uses the following distinctions:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a primary record, contemporaneous corporate/government document, court opinion, or multiple strong sources.
- **Self-report / recollection:** a statement made by Andreessen or a close associate, which may be accurate but is not independently proved merely by being remembered.
- **Allegation:** a claim made by a litigant, critic, activist investor, journalist, or political opponent.
- **Pleading-stage ruling:** a court has held that allegations are sufficiently pleaded to proceed, not that they are true.
- **Settlement:** litigation ended by agreement; unless specifically stated otherwise, this is not an admission or adjudication of liability.
- **Reported investigation:** reputable reporting says an authority is examining conduct; this is not a charge, indictment, or finding.
- **Myth / legend / shorthand:** a story or symbolic construction that has circulated around the subject, whether wholly false, partly true, or broadly interpretive.
- **Unresolved:** available sources do not establish a definitive answer.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and siblings

Andreessen was born in Iowa and raised in **New Lisbon, Wisconsin**, a small rural community. The most detailed published reconstruction of his family background is Tad Friend’s 2015 *New Yorker* profile, which was based on extensive access to Andreessen and people close to him.

His father, **Lowell Andreessen**, worked as a sales manager for **Pioneer Hi-Bred International**, the seed company. His mother, identified in sources as **Pat** or **Patricia Andreessen**, worked in customer service at **Lands’ End**. Retrospective profiles also identify a younger brother, **Jeff Andreessen**.

Andreessen has been unusually reluctant to discuss his nuclear family. Friend reported that a close friend had never had a substantive conversation with him about his parents or brother and remembered Andreessen summarizing the relationship as mutually distant. That should be treated as a reported family estrangement, not as evidence for any specific abuse or event. Andreessen himself described his childhood world in terms of material austerity, rural isolation, and a self-denying Midwestern culture. He recalled relatives using an outhouse, a party-line telephone, wood chopping when his father stopped paying for gas heat during a tight period, and limited access to bookstores and cultural institutions.

These memories became part of Andreessen’s own explanation of his technological worldview. He has linked growing up far from stores, information, and institutions to his later enthusiasm for Amazon, the Internet, and technologies that eliminate geographical limits on access. His wife Laura later offered a related family interpretation: Netscape, in her telling, grew partly out of his childhood experience of being unable to access knowledge in a small town.

The family history beyond his parents and brother is only fragmentarily documented in the sources reviewed. Andreessen has supplied cultural descriptions of Scandinavian and Midwestern farm life, but this dossier found no authoritative public genealogy establishing a complete line of ancestry.

### Marriage: Laura Arrillaga-Andreessen

Andreessen met **Laura Arrillaga** in 2005 at a New Year’s Eve dinner hosted by a leading investor in eHarmony. The account in *The New Yorker*, based on both spouses, says they talked for roughly six and a half hours. Andreessen sent her seventeen emails the next day. Before their second date he reportedly delivered a lengthy, structured argument for becoming an exclusive couple. They married approximately nine months after meeting, in **2006**.

Laura Arrillaga-Andreessen is a philanthropist, educator and author associated for years with Stanford. She is the daughter of **John Arrillaga**, the Silicon Valley real-estate developer and billionaire who, with Richard Peery, became a major developer of office property in Silicon Valley. The Arrillaga family therefore joined Andreessen not only to a philanthropic network but to an older layer of Silicon Valley wealth built through land and commercial property rather than software.

A 2015 profile described Andreessen as having found a “replacement family” in Laura and her father. That phrase is a journalist’s interpretive description supported by comments from Laura and by Andreessen’s evident closeness to John Arrillaga; it should not be elevated into a formal statement by Andreessen about severing his birth family.

### Child

In **March 2015**, Marc and Laura announced the birth of a son, **John**, named for Laura’s father. The child was carried by a gestational surrogate. The couple has kept most details of their son’s life private. Andreessen has occasionally discussed introducing him to technology and AI, but this dossier does not reconstruct a private minor’s life beyond facts already made public by the parents.

### Philanthropic and estate structures

SEC filings from the Opsware and Facebook periods document entities including an **Andreessen 1996 Living Trust** and an **Andreessen 1996 Charitable Remainder Trust / Unitrust** holding securities. Their appearance in public filings shows that trust structures have long been part of Andreessen’s estate and charitable-financial arrangements. The filings do not by themselves establish the full terms, beneficiaries, tax treatment, or current status of those trusts.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Cedar Falls to New Lisbon

Marc Andreessen was born in **Cedar Falls, Iowa**, on 9 July 1971, and grew up in **New Lisbon, Wisconsin**. Sources differ on exactly when the family moved, and no primary relocation record was located. The stable documentary point is that his formative childhood and schooling were in New Lisbon.

New Lisbon was a small community whose high school culture, according to later reporting, revolved heavily around athletics. Andreessen was tall but not athletically oriented. Former teachers and classmates remembered a bright student interested in mathematics, computers, English, history, and large questions rather than school sports.

### First programming: competing versions

There are at least two commonly repeated stories about when Andreessen began programming:

1. University of Illinois and prize biographies say he **taught himself programming at about age nine**.
2. Andreessen told *The New Yorker* that in **1983, at age twelve**, he used a Radio Shack **TRS-80** in the school library and wrote a calculator program for mathematics homework.

A third retrospective account, David Sheff’s profile, says an operation and hospital recovery gave him time to study a programming book. These stories are not necessarily mutually exclusive: learning BASIC at nine, a hospital episode, and writing a useful school program at twelve could all have happened. But the exact “first moment” is not securely established by a contemporaneous source.

### Barbara Blackstone

An unusually personal later episode links Andreessen back to New Lisbon. **Barbara Blackstone**, a New Lisbon High School teacher, disappeared on 9 July 1987 and was later found dead; the homicide remained unsolved. In **2009**, when investigators reopened or renewed attention to the case, Andreessen, a former student, offered a **$25,000 reward** for information leading to a conviction. Local accounts described him as regarding Blackstone as one of his most important or best teachers. The reward is a documented example of an enduring tie to his otherwise rarely discussed hometown. Nothing in the sources suggests Andreessen had any connection to the crime beyond having been her student and later funding the reward.

### Formative themes in his own recollection

Across later interviews Andreessen repeatedly returned to a small set of childhood contrasts:

- remoteness versus access;
- local scarcity versus digital abundance;
- blue-collar or farm-town life versus Silicon Valley’s culture of starting companies;
- consuming science-fiction futures versus trying to build them;
- practical software versus abstract theory.

He recalled watching *Knight Rider*, *Star Trek*, and other technological fantasy and later reading these not merely as entertainment but as predictions that engineering could eventually approximate. These recollections became part of an autobiographical mythology of “escape into the future,” but they are also consistent with his subsequent preference for applied programming, mass distribution, and scale.

## Education and Formation

### University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign

Andreessen studied **computer science at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign (UIUC)**. His degree is variously catalogued in institutional sources as **B.S. ’94**, while biographies and interviews state that he completed his degree in **December 1993**. The apparent discrepancy is best understood as a completion/calendar-versus-alumni-class-year difference; no evidence was found that he lacked the degree.

In a 1995 Smithsonian oral history, Andreessen said one of his first university jobs was in a research lab at the university’s **physics research facility**. There he encountered high-end computing and the already-functioning Internet. He emphasized that he was more interested in **applied programming—making things people would use—than in theory**.

### IBM, Austin

Andreessen participated in an IBM co-op program in **Austin, Texas**, for parts of **1990 and 1991**. He recalled IBM’s effort to compete in the 3D workstation market. Secondary technical profiles place him in an AIX graphics environment involving X Window System and 3D APIs. The Smithsonian oral history confirms the Austin co-op and 3D-workstation context but is less granular about individual code contributions.

The IBM period was a first significant migration outside the Midwest and a first exposure to a large technology corporation. Andreessen returned to Illinois and was drawn to the National Center for Supercomputing Applications partly because of its graphics and computing work.

### NCSA

By approximately **1992**, Andreessen was working at the **National Center for Supercomputing Applications (NCSA)**. In his own retrospective telling, NCSA was an unusually fertile environment: federally supported computing infrastructure, high-bandwidth networking, scientists and programmers across levels, and enough institutional slack to experiment.

He was an hourly programmer; later profiles give his wage as **$6.85 per hour**. He said the existing Internet was powerful but unfriendly to non-specialists. Gopher, email, FTP, and early Web software existed, and Tim Berners-Lee’s World Wide Web protocols and HTML were already in circulation. Andreessen’s insight was not the invention of the Internet or Web, but the opportunity to combine:

- the Web’s global hypertext model,
- desktop graphical interfaces,
- inline multimedia,
- a cross-platform client,
- rapid Internet distribution,
- and aggressive user-feedback iteration.

### Eric Bina and Mosaic

Andreessen’s crucial technical collaborator was **Eric J. Bina**, a programmer at NCSA. In the Smithsonian oral history Andreessen described the initial team simply as himself and “another gentleman,” but elsewhere the collaborator is unambiguously Bina. They spent roughly three months producing the first version in C. As the project grew, many other NCSA developers contributed ports, support, documentation, and later versions.

NCSA Mosaic’s release history is often reduced to one date, but the documentary record shows a staged rollout:

- Illinois’s Distributed Museum gives **23 January 1993** for an initial release/announcement.
- Andreessen in 1995 remembered introducing Mosaic around **March 1993**.
- Contemporary histories describe a Unix/X version early in 1993 and Macintosh/Windows ports later that year.
- The University Archives guide gives **November 1993**, apparently referring to the broader public/multiplatform release preserved in its records.

A single “birthday” therefore obscures a development process. By late 1993 Mosaic was the decisive popular graphical Web browser.

### What Mosaic did and did not invent

Mosaic was **not the first Web browser**, and Andreessen did **not invent the World Wide Web**. Tim Berners-Lee and colleagues at CERN created the Web’s core architecture and earlier browser/editor software. Mosaic built on those standards.

What Mosaic did unusually well was integrate graphical presentation and Web navigation into a product ordinary desktop users could understand. Inline images, a relatively minimalist interface, familiar back/forward navigation, broad protocol support, cross-platform versions, and fast iteration made it vastly more usable than most predecessors for a mass audience. Contemporary and archival sources consistently identify Mosaic as one of the first and the most consequential early graphical browsers.

### Intellectual and organizational lessons from NCSA

Two experiences from NCSA reappear throughout Andreessen’s later career:

1. **Distribution as product strategy.** He saw Internet distribution and free/low-friction access as a way to create a user base faster than traditional boxed-software sales.
2. **Institutional frustration.** As commercial interest in Mosaic grew, NCSA and the University of Illinois sought licensing revenue and the project expanded from a loose programming effort into a politically and commercially important institutional asset. Andreessen later described the environment as increasingly stressful and “political.”

The university owned the NCSA code. Andreessen and the later Netscape team did **not** simply take Mosaic’s code to California. In the 1995 Smithsonian interview he explicitly said they chose to rewrite the browser from scratch because they did not want the university code and because a commercial product needed different performance, security and cross-platform architecture.

### Technical publication

Andreessen and Bina published:

**Marc Andreessen and Eric Bina, “NCSA Mosaic: A Global Hypermedia System,” *Internet Research*, vol. 4, no. 1 (1994), pp. 7–17.**

This is the key formal technical paper under his name from the Mosaic period.

## Early Career

### Move to California, 1993–1994

Andreessen visited Silicon Valley in **October 1993**, liked the density of technology companies, completed his degree, and moved west. He took a job at **Enterprise Integration Technologies (EIT)** in Palo Alto. In his Smithsonian oral history he said he lasted roughly **three months**.

This move from Illinois to California was both geographic and conceptual. Andreessen later contrasted the Midwest, where he felt company formation was culturally alien, with Silicon Valley, where starting a company was an expected path.

### Meeting Jim Clark

In early 1994 Andreessen met **James H. “Jim” Clark**, the founder of Silicon Graphics, who was leaving SGI and looking for a new software opportunity. Clark contacted Andreessen after hearing about Mosaic. Their first discussions reportedly ranged across interactive television and networked entertainment. They converged on building a commercial Internet company around a new browser.

Clark was the older founder, financier and organizational patron Andreessen had not had at NCSA. For Andreessen, Clark became a bridge into venture capital, executive recruitment, and high-speed company formation.

### Recruiting the Mosaic team

Andreessen and Clark went back to Illinois and recruited a group of NCSA programmers, including several people who had worked on Mosaic. Andreessen later recalled a core team of roughly **six or seven**. They moved to California and began writing a browser from scratch during the spring and summer of **1994**.

The company was initially **Mosaic Communications Corporation**. Because the University of Illinois/NCSA had rights and commercial interests associated with the Mosaic name, the company changed its name to **Netscape Communications Corporation**. The browser became **Netscape Navigator**.

## Chronological Life History

### 1971–1989: Iowa birth, Wisconsin childhood

- **1971:** born Cedar Falls, Iowa.
- Childhood and adolescence in New Lisbon, Wisconsin.
- Learns programming in childhood; exact first age varies among sources.
- Attends New Lisbon schools; develops interests in computing and reading, and later describes rural information scarcity as formative.
- **1987:** teacher Barbara Blackstone is murdered; decades later Andreessen funds a reward in the unsolved case.
- Late 1980s: graduates high school and enters UIUC.

### 1989–1993: Illinois, IBM, NCSA, Mosaic

At Illinois, Andreessen studies computer science, works in university research computing and completes IBM co-op periods in Austin in 1990 and 1991. Around 1992 he joins NCSA.

Late 1992 into 1993, Andreessen and Eric Bina construct Mosaic. The browser spreads rapidly through Internet distribution. Andreessen’s importance lies partly in product instinct: he pushes for a browser ordinary people can use and for multimedia to be embedded directly into documents.

Commercial inquiries begin arriving. NCSA’s loose research culture gives way to licensing, administrative attention, and a larger team. Andreessen finishes his degree in December 1993 and leaves.

### 1994: Netscape formation

After a short stay at EIT, Andreessen meets Jim Clark. They recruit former NCSA colleagues and form Mosaic Communications, soon renamed Netscape.

The new code base is written from scratch. The team focuses on speed over modem connections, cross-platform support, a minimalist graphical interface, and security. Netscape’s business model combines broad/free browser distribution with commercial licensing and server products.

Netscape Navigator appears publicly in **October 1994** in early form; commercial product shipment follows by December. Adoption is explosive.

### 1995: Netscape IPO and sudden celebrity

Netscape enters 1995 with extraordinary browser market share and becomes the corporate emblem of the commercial Web.

Andreessen, still in his early twenties, holds technical/product leadership roles. SEC records later summarize his Netscape service as co-founder with positions including **Chief Technology Officer** and **Executive Vice President of Products**. Jim Barksdale, recruited as chief executive, gives the fast-growing company experienced managerial leadership.

On **9 August 1995**, Netscape went public. Five million shares were offered at **$28**. The stock opened far above the offer price, traded as high as roughly $75, and closed at $58.25. Depending on the share count and price point used, press accounts put Netscape’s first-day value in the rough **$2–3 billion** range.

The offering became a historical symbol beyond the company itself. It demonstrated that an Internet company with a short operating history could command enormous public-market enthusiasm, and “the Netscape moment” later became shorthand for the opening of the dot-com boom.

Andreessen’s personal Netscape equity made him wealthy while still 24. *Time* later put him on a throne, barefoot, with a crown-like visual treatment—one of the defining images of the young Internet mogul.

### 1995–1998: Browser wars and Microsoft

Netscape’s greatest strategic conflict was with **Microsoft**.

Microsoft initially underestimated the Web and then moved aggressively to make Internet Explorer a core part of Windows. Netscape saw the browser as a potential platform capable of making the operating system less important; Microsoft saw that possibility as a threat.

A **21 June 1995** meeting between Microsoft and Netscape became central evidence in later U.S. antitrust proceedings. Netscape witnesses said Microsoft effectively proposed a division of browser markets or pressured Netscape not to compete in key Windows browser functionality. Microsoft disputed this characterization. The later federal antitrust case established broader findings that Microsoft perceived Netscape as a platform threat and undertook exclusionary conduct to protect the Windows monopoly. The legal record should not be simplified into “a court proved every version of Netscape’s account of the June meeting,” but Microsoft’s campaign against browser threats became an important part of the government case.

Microsoft bundled Internet Explorer with Windows and used OEM, ISP, and distribution agreements to expand its browser presence. Netscape also made strategic mistakes of its own. It expanded quickly, struggled with product complexity, shifted toward enterprise software, and suffered heavy losses as competition intensified. Later accounts by Andreessen and former executives acknowledge that Netscape was not merely an innocent technological victim of Microsoft; internal execution and strategy mattered.

By early 1998 Netscape’s once-dominant browser share was falling sharply. The company announced a major loss and restructuring. It also released browser source code, the beginning of the Mozilla lineage that eventually produced Firefox.

### 1998–1999: Sale to AOL and AOL CTO

On **24 November 1998**, AOL announced that it would acquire Netscape in a stock transaction initially valued at about **$4.2 billion**. Because AOL’s stock price rose before the deal closed, retrospective accounts sometimes put the transaction’s closing value much higher—around $10 billion. Both figures circulate because they refer to different valuation dates.

The deal closed in **March 1999**. Andreessen became **Chief Technology Officer of America Online** in 1999, reporting into Steve Case’s organization. Contemporary reporting said he bought a house near **Dulles, Virginia**, while retaining a California residence, expecting a bicoastal life.

He resigned from AOL in **September 1999**. The role was brief. Andreessen had moved from programmer-founder to a senior title inside the era’s largest online-service company, but did not remain a big-company executive.

### 1999–2002: Loudcloud

Andreessen’s next company emerged from discussions with his former Netscape colleague **Ben Horowitz**, along with **Tim Howes** and **In Sik Rhee**. The founding story is repeatedly located at Andreessen’s Palo Alto house in 1999.

**Loudcloud** aimed to run Internet infrastructure for other companies: hosting, operations, servers, and automated management. It was an early version of what would later be called cloud infrastructure or managed cloud services, although it predated Amazon Web Services and today’s cloud architecture.

The timing was brutal. Loudcloud raised large sums and expanded into the dot-com peak, then watched much of its startup customer base collapse. It completed an IPO in **March 2001**, during the market crash rather than at the boom’s height.

The company burned cash, cut staff, and confronted the possibility of failure. In 2002 it sold its managed-services/hosting business to **Electronic Data Systems (EDS)** for approximately **$63.5–65 million** and kept its internally developed automation software.

### 2002–2007: Opsware

After selling the hosting operation, the company renamed itself **Opsware**. Rather than operating customers’ servers, it sold software that automated data-center management.

This pivot became one of Andreessen and Horowitz’s central entrepreneurial legends: Loudcloud’s original business model nearly failed, but the internal software built to survive became the actual product.

Andreessen served as co-founder and **chairman**; Horowitz was CEO. Opsware eventually developed into a substantial enterprise-software company.

On **23 July 2007**, Hewlett-Packard announced an agreement to acquire Opsware for **$14.25 per share**, valuing the company at more than **$1.6 billion**; HP’s subsequent filings describe an aggregate purchase price of roughly **$1.7 billion**. The acquisition closed in September 2007.

A 2007 Opsware filing provides unusually concrete evidence of Andreessen’s equity structure. It lists approximately:

- **6,458,785 shares** through the Andreessen 1996 Living Trust;
- **1,438,000 shares** through a 1996 charitable remainder trust;
- **1,821,375 options** exercisable within the specified disclosure window.

These holdings document a major source of Andreessen’s wealth, though they do not alone establish the exact after-tax proceeds he ultimately realized.

### 2003 onward: angel investing

Andreessen and Horowitz began angel investing while Opsware was still active. Andreessen later said they invested roughly **$10 million across about fifty companies**, separately and together. Among the best-known early investments or relationships were Facebook, Twitter, and LinkedIn.

This activity shifted Andreessen’s economic model from a single-company founder to an owner of a portfolio of technology bets.

### 2004–2011: Ning

Andreessen co-founded **Ning** with **Gina Bianchini** in 2004; the service launched in 2005. Ning allowed users and organizations to create customized social networks, an idea shaped by the Web 2.0 period’s enthusiasm for user-generated communities and configurable platforms.

Andreessen was a co-founder/chairman and technical figure; Bianchini served as CEO for much of the company’s early history.

Ning attracted a large number of networks but struggled with monetization and the cost of free service. In **2010**, under new CEO Jason Rosenthal, it abandoned its free model, laid off a substantial fraction of employees, and shifted toward paid subscriptions.

In **2011**, Glam Media acquired Ning in a transaction widely reported at roughly **$150 million**, principally in stock. The sale made Ning neither a total failure nor the transformative social platform once imagined. It is a useful counterexample to the myth that Andreessen’s major projects invariably became category winners.

### 2006: marriage; Facebook relationship deepens

Andreessen married Laura Arrillaga in 2006.

That year he also became an important outside adviser to **Mark Zuckerberg** during Yahoo’s roughly **$1 billion** acquisition offer for Facebook. In Andreessen’s later telling, most people around Zuckerberg wanted him to accept; Andreessen repeatedly told him not to sell. Zuckerberg did not. The episode helped form a durable mentor/director relationship.

### 2008–2018: major public-company boards

Andreessen joined the **Facebook board in June 2008**. Meta’s 2026 proxy still lists him as a director, making the relationship one of the longest in the company’s governance history.

He joined **eBay’s board in September 2008**, served through October 2014, and became embroiled in the later Carl Icahn/Skype conflict dispute.

Other public-company directorships included:

- **Hewlett-Packard:** 2009–2015;
- **Hewlett Packard Enterprise:** 2015–2018;
- **Blue Coat Systems:** earlier service ending in 2005, according to eBay-era filings;
- later **Coinbase Global** and **Samsara**.

Andreessen’s position on boards has often overlapped with a16z investment relationships. That is normal in venture capital but creates recurring corporate-governance questions when his firm owns stakes in entities transacting with companies whose boards he serves on. Public proxies document and disclose such relationships.

### 2009: Andreessen Horowitz

Andreessen and Ben Horowitz founded **Andreessen Horowitz**, widely known as **a16z**, in **2009**. The first fund was approximately **$300 million**.

The firm deliberately rejected the small-partnership model common in venture capital. Andreessen and Horowitz wanted a full-service organization with recruiting, marketing, communications, business development, regulatory/policy, and operational support for founders. They have described the design partly as a reaction against frustrations they experienced as entrepreneurs. Profiles have also compared the model to talent agencies such as Creative Artists Agency: an institution built around a powerful network and service platform rather than only a few investing partners.

The division of labor became characteristic:

- **Ben Horowitz:** operating/management focus, culture, personnel and CEO problems;
- **Marc Andreessen:** broad technology theses, investment framing, long-run strategy and public intellectual role.

The firm’s early and later investments included stakes in companies such as Facebook/Meta, Skype, Airbnb, GitHub, Instagram, Coinbase, Oculus, Slack, Lyft, Pinterest and many others. The exact portfolio is vast and changing. It is important not to attribute every a16z investment personally to Marc Andreessen: different partners lead different deals, and fund ownership is distinct from personal ownership.

### 2009–2014: Skype investment and eBay conflict allegations

While Andreessen served on eBay’s board, eBay decided to sell a controlling interest in Skype.

In **September 2009**, eBay agreed to sell approximately **65% of Skype** to a consortium including Silver Lake, Index Ventures, the Canada Pension Plan Investment Board, and Andreessen Horowitz. The transaction valued Skype at about **$2.75 billion** and delivered roughly $1.9 billion in cash to eBay.

In **2011**, Microsoft agreed to acquire Skype for **$8.5 billion**.

During activist investor **Carl Icahn’s** 2014 campaign against eBay, Icahn accused Andreessen of having conflicts of interest and argued that eBay sold Skype too cheaply to a group that included Andreessen’s new venture firm, only for the buyers to realize a much larger value in the Microsoft sale. Icahn framed this as evidence of failed or disloyal governance.

Andreessen and eBay rejected the accusation of wrongdoing. eBay said conflict procedures had been followed and that independent directors handled relevant decisions. Reuters Breakingviews noted the appearance of conflict while also distinguishing appearance from proof. No court judgment, regulatory finding, indictment, or fraud conviction against Andreessen emerged from the Skype transaction in the sources reviewed.

Andreessen left eBay’s board in **October 2014**, shortly before the PayPal separation.

### 2012–2013: public stature and engineering honors

Andreessen was named to **TIME 100** in 2012.

In **2013**, he shared the **inaugural Queen Elizabeth Prize for Engineering** with Tim Berners-Lee, Vint Cerf, Robert Kahn, and Louis Pouzin. The prize recognized the Internet and Web technologies that transformed communications; Andreessen’s contribution was associated with Mosaic and the popularization of Web browsing.

He was also inducted into the **Internet Hall of Fame** in 2013.

These honors place his historical contribution carefully: not as inventor of the Internet or Web, but as a major engineer of the interface through which the Web became accessible to a mass public.

### 2015: son; mature a16z era

In March 2015 Andreessen and Laura announced the birth of their son John.

A major *New Yorker* profile that year captured Andreessen as a venture capitalist at peak cultural influence. It also recorded the less flattering parts of his managerial reputation from Netscape: impatience, volatility, contemptuous remarks to subordinates, and his own recognition that he had been a poor manager. Andreessen did not deny the basic portrait; he described Netscape as a “one-way door” that made him intolerant of perceived obstacles and admitted that the down cycles affected him more strongly than successes.

This period also solidified the image of Andreessen as an intellectual broadcaster—an investor who used Twitter, essays, interviews and podcasts to construct macro theses about technology, markets and society.

### 2016: India / Free Basics controversy

In February 2016 India’s telecom regulator prohibited discriminatory pricing for data services, effectively blocking Facebook’s **Free Basics** model in its proposed form.

During a Twitter argument over the decision, Andreessen wrote that **“Anti-colonialism has been economically catastrophic for the Indian people for decades.”** The remark was immediately read in India and elsewhere as an American billionaire suggesting colonial rule had been economically desirable.

Andreessen deleted the tweet and apologized “in full and without reservation.” Mark Zuckerberg called the comment deeply upsetting and said it did not represent Facebook.

**Status:** documented statement and documented apology; political/reputational controversy; no allegation of crime.

### 2015–2017: Facebook stock reclassification controversy

Facebook sought to create a new non-voting share class that would allow Zuckerberg to sell or donate large amounts of stock while retaining voting control.

A Facebook board **Special Committee** consisting of Susan Desmond-Hellmann, Marc Andreessen, and Erskine Bowles was established to negotiate the terms. Facebook’s proxy presented the committee as independent/disinterested.

Shareholders sued in Delaware. During discovery, text messages became public showing Andreessen privately communicating with Zuckerberg while the committee was negotiating. The messages showed Andreessen giving Zuckerberg real-time strategic guidance and updating him about the committee. Plaintiffs argued that this compromised the supposedly arm’s-length process.

The texts are documentary evidence of the private communications; the legal conclusion plaintiffs wanted drawn from them—breach of fiduciary duty—was never adjudicated at trial. In **September 2017**, shortly before trial, Zuckerberg and Facebook abandoned the reclassification plan. The underlying suit became moot. Facebook later paid substantial plaintiffs’ attorneys’ fees under Delaware’s corporate-benefit doctrine.

Subsequent Delaware litigation over board costs and demand-futility rules produced important corporate-law decisions but did not result in a trial finding that Andreessen personally committed fraud or a criminal offense.

### 2019: a16z becomes a registered investment adviser

Andreessen Horowitz restructured as a **registered investment adviser (RIA)** in 2019. The change gave the firm broader flexibility to hold assets and pursue strategies—especially crypto—that traditional venture-capital exemptions constrain.

The firm’s registration appears in the SEC’s Investment Adviser Public Disclosure system. The transformation also signaled that a16z no longer resembled a conventional boutique VC partnership.

### 2020: “It’s Time to Build”

During the early COVID-19 pandemic Andreessen published **“It’s Time to Build.”** The essay argued that the United States had failed to build adequate housing, health capacity, education systems, transportation, manufacturing, and other physical/institutional infrastructure. He attributed the failure not to inability but to a collective unwillingness or institutional blockage.

The essay became a concise statement of what later commentators called “buildism” or abundance-oriented technological politics. It also made his 2022 opposition to dense housing in Atherton especially politically salient.

### 2021: Malibu real estate; Coinbase direct listing

#### Malibu

In **2021**, Marc and Laura Andreessen bought a major **Paradise Cove, Malibu** estate for approximately **$177 million**, then a California record for a residential transaction. Reporting later described additional Malibu purchases around **$44.5 million** and **$34 million**, putting their total reported Malibu spending above $250 million.

Residential-price records are not the same thing as liquid net worth, but these purchases are concrete evidence of Andreessen’s wealth and movement of some personal life from Atherton toward Southern California.

#### Coinbase

Andreessen had long been associated with Coinbase through a16z. The firm first invested in Coinbase in **2013** and became one of its major venture backers. Andreessen joined Coinbase’s board before the company’s 2021 direct listing.

Coinbase went public through a **direct listing on 14 April 2021**, without the conventional lockup that prevents insiders from immediately selling.

The direct listing became the subject of the civil derivative litigation **Grabski ex rel. Coinbase Global, Inc. v. Andreessen et al.**, discussed in detail below.

### 2022: Atherton housing dispute

Andreessen’s 2020 call for America to build far more housing collided with a local land-use dispute in **Atherton, California**, one of the wealthiest residential towns in the United States.

In 2022 Marc and Laura submitted a public comment opposing proposals to create multifamily housing capacity in the town. They supported Atherton’s existing low-density pattern—roughly one house per acre in key areas—and expressed concerns about traffic, noise and property values.

The opposition was legal civic participation, not misconduct. Its significance was reputational: critics contrasted the position with Andreessen’s public declaration that America needed to build far more housing. The Atlantic and other outlets made the episode a central example in debates about “NIMBY” politics among affluent technology leaders.

### 2023: AI and explicit techno-optimism

#### “Why AI Will Save the World”

In June 2023 Andreessen published **“Why AI Will Save the World.”** He rejected catastrophic AI narratives, argued that AI would augment human intelligence, increase productivity, improve medicine and education, and make creative and professional expertise widely available.

He also framed AI development geopolitically, arguing that slowing U.S. development could advantage China and that open, rapid innovation was strategically important.

#### “The Techno-Optimist Manifesto”

On **16 October 2023**, Andreessen published **“The Techno-Optimist Manifesto.”**

The document is more explicit ideologically than his earlier business essays. It advocates:

- technology as a primary engine of human progress;
- markets as generative and positive-sum;
- rapid economic growth;
- abundant energy;
- AI acceleration rather than deceleration;
- the fusion of technology and markets into what he adopts from **Nick Land** as the **“techno-capital machine”**;
- “accelerationism” in the sense of consciously propelling technological development.

The manifesto supplies a long list of “Patron Saints of Techno-Optimism,” ranging from Ada Lovelace, Adam Smith, Friedrich Hayek and Milton Friedman to artists, engineers, futurists and more politically controversial figures. Critics particularly noted his inclusion or invocation of figures associated with futurism, accelerationism and reactionary intellectual currents, including Nick Land and, in the broader list, Filippo Tommaso Marinetti.

Andreessen himself does not label the manifesto fascist and explicitly frames it as a technological/economic creed rather than a conventional political party platform. The presence of intellectual figures with extremist or fascist associations is nevertheless a real feature of the text’s genealogy, not an invented association. Whether the manifesto should itself be classified as “fascist,” “neoreactionary,” “libertarian,” “accelerationist,” or “techno-capitalist” is an interpretive argument rather than a documentary fact. “Accelerationist” is the clearest self-description because the manifesto explicitly says, “We believe in accelerationism.”

### 2024: Little Tech and support for Donald Trump

On **5 July 2024**, Andreessen and Horowitz published **“The Little Tech Agenda.”** They defined “Little Tech” as startups in opposition to entrenched Big Tech incumbents and argued that government policy had become the greatest threat to startups. The document advocated:

- lighter regulation for startups;
- crypto-friendly policy;
- resistance to restrictive AI regulation;
- opposition to a proposed tax on unrealized capital gains;
- high-skilled immigration;
- nuclear power;
- industrial reshoring through automation;
- rebuilding the military-industrial base around AI and autonomous technology;
- U.S. technological competition with China and regulatory competition with Europe.

The essay asserted that a16z would support politicians based on their policies toward startups rather than party identity.

On **16 July 2024**, Andreessen and Horowitz used their podcast to announce support for **Donald Trump** over Joe Biden in the presidential race, arguing that Trump’s policy positions were more favorable to startups, crypto, AI and venture capital.

Andreessen had not always been a Republican-aligned donor. Retrospective accounts and his own discussions indicate support for multiple Democratic presidential candidates in earlier decades, including Bill Clinton, Al Gore, Barack Obama and Hillary Clinton, while he supported Mitt Romney in 2012 and Carly Fiorina during part of the 2016 Republican primary. His 2024 move therefore represented a sharp political realignment rather than an unbroken partisan history.

Campaign-finance reporting documented **millions of dollars** in Andreessen contributions to Trump-aligned and Republican committees in 2024. It also documented major contributions to crypto-focused political committees such as **Fairshake**, whose spending supported pro-crypto candidates in both parties. Because campaign-finance totals change depending on whether one counts Andreessen personally, his spouse, a16z entities, joint committees, or related PAC networks, any single headline total should be read with its accounting definition attached.

### 2024–2025: Trump transition and DOGE orbit

After Trump’s 2024 election victory, Andreessen became involved in the transition and the technology-policy network around the incoming administration.

Reporting by *The Washington Post*, *Financial Times*, and *Business Insider* described him as:

- spending extensive time at Mar-a-Lago during the transition;
- advising on technology, AI, crypto, economic and personnel questions;
- interviewing or helping identify candidates for senior government positions;
- participating in discussions connected to Elon Musk’s government-efficiency project.

Andreessen later called himself an **“unpaid volunteer”** in relation to DOGE-oriented work. Reporting does not establish that he held a salaried or Senate-confirmed executive-branch office. A White House official told *The Washington Post* in 2025 that he was **not an official adviser to the president** in the context of a private AI group chat. The correct description is therefore influential informal adviser/participant rather than formal DOGE chief or Cabinet officer.

### 2025: university/DEI messages and Meta privacy trial settlement

#### Private messages about universities and NSF

In July 2025 *The Washington Post* published excerpts from private WhatsApp messages it had reviewed. In messages sent in May, Andreessen attacked Stanford and MIT as political lobbying operations, argued universities had discriminated against Trump voters through DEI and immigration preferences, and wrote that universities would “pay the price.” He called for the National Science Foundation to receive the **“bureaucratic death penalty”** and be rebuilt.

He also referred to a personal dispute involving Laura Arrillaga-Andreessen and Stanford, alleging that Stanford had forced her out of a philanthropy center she helped create and saying the episode would cost the university billions in future donations. Stanford did not substantively confirm his account in the article; Laura did not comment.

The messages are documented journalistic evidence in the sense that the Post reviewed screenshots and spoke with chat participants. They were private comments, not government orders. The White House said participants in the chat were acting in personal capacities and that Andreessen was not an official presidential adviser.

The episode demonstrates a further shift from his 2024 “Little Tech” document, which explicitly praised American higher education and high-skilled immigration, toward a more hostile 2025 rhetoric about universities, DEI and parts of the immigration system.

#### Meta privacy derivative litigation

A shareholder derivative case arising from Facebook/Meta privacy failures and the Cambridge Analytica scandal targeted Zuckerberg and multiple current and former directors/officers, including Andreessen. Plaintiffs sought billions and alleged that board members failed to ensure compliance with privacy obligations, particularly the 2012 FTC consent order.

The Delaware trial began in **July 2025**. Defendants denied wrongdoing.

The parties settled almost immediately after trial began. Later-disclosed terms included approximately **$190 million** in payment to Meta funded through directors-and-officers insurance plus governance reforms. Defendants did **not** admit wrongdoing. Because the case settled, there was no trial judgment finding that Andreessen had breached fiduciary duties.

### 2025: Atherton property sale

Andreessen’s long-associated Atherton residence, purchased in 2007 for about **$16.6 million**, was listed years later and sold in **August 2025** for approximately **$27.2 million**, according to Bay Area reporting.

This sale, together with enormous Malibu purchases and reports of greater time in Florida and Washington, suggests a geographic diversification of his life away from the classic Sand Hill Road/Atherton base. The sources reviewed do **not** establish a single definitive current legal domicile, and it would be unwarranted to state that he has permanently abandoned California solely from property reporting.

### 2026: federal advisory roles, political spending, growth of a16z, and legal scrutiny

#### a16z scale

Andreessen Horowitz states that as of **30 April 2026** it manages **more than $100 billion** across multiple funds.

On **9 January 2026**, the firm announced more than **$15 billion** in newly raised funds:

- American Dynamism: **$1.176 billion**
- Apps: **$1.7 billion**
- Bio + Health: **$700 million**
- Infrastructure: **$1.7 billion**
- Growth: **$6.75 billion**
- Other venture strategies: **$3 billion**

The firm claimed this represented more than 18% of all U.S. venture-capital dollars allocated in 2025. That percentage is the firm’s own framing.

The scale of a16z makes Andreessen no longer merely a venture capitalist making individual company bets. He is co-founder of an asset-management and political-policy institution with a major communications arm, regulatory apparatus, defense portfolio, crypto operation, and network of former government officials and operators.

#### Coinbase derivative case: January 2026 ruling

On **30 January 2026**, Delaware Chancellor **Kathaleen St. J. McCormick** denied Coinbase’s Special Litigation Committee’s motion to terminate the derivative action **Grabski ex rel. Coinbase Global, Inc. v. Marc Andreessen et al.**

The underlying suit alleges that directors and officers sold approximately **$2.9 billion** of Coinbase stock during the 14 April 2021 direct listing while possessing material nonpublic information, avoiding approximately **$1.09 billion** in losses before later negative disclosures. Court opinions identify a16z-linked sales associated with Andreessen of more than **$118.7 million** and call the sale the firm’s largest exit at that point.

In **February 2024**, the court had already denied defendants’ motion to dismiss. At the pleading stage the court found the plaintiff had stated a sufficiently pleaded claim; it did **not** find insider trading actually occurred.

Coinbase then created a two-member Special Litigation Committee that investigated and recommended terminating the action. In 2026 the court refused to terminate because of material factual questions about one SLC member, **Gokul Rajaram**, and his professional/economic ties to Andreessen and a16z. The opinion expressly did not accuse Rajaram of bad faith; the issue was whether his relationships created an unacceptable risk of bias for independence purposes.

**Status as of 19 August 2026:** ongoing civil derivative litigation in the reviewed record; no final judgment establishing that Andreessen engaged in insider trading; no criminal charge identified.

#### PCAST appointment

On **25 March 2026**, President Trump appointed Andreessen to the **President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology (PCAST)**. The White House identified David Sacks and Michael Kratsios as co-chairs and named Andreessen alongside figures including Sergey Brin, Safra Catz, Michael Dell, Larry Ellison, Jensen Huang, Lisa Su and Mark Zuckerberg.

PCAST is an advisory body; membership is a formal federal appointment but not a Cabinet post.

#### Defense Policy Board

On **29 June 2026**, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth appointed Andreessen to the **Defense Policy Board**, an advisory committee providing strategic advice to senior Defense Department officials.

This appointment is material because a16z has increasingly invested in defense and national-security technology through its **American Dynamism** practice. Portfolio companies include businesses involved in autonomous systems, drones, defense manufacturing, surveillance, ships and aerospace. The overlap creates an identifiable potential conflict-of-interest question: Andreessen now advises the Pentagon while his firm owns stakes in companies whose value can depend on federal defense policy and procurement.

The *Guardian* in August 2026 drew additional attention to co-investments between a16z and **1789 Capital**, where Donald Trump Jr. is a partner, in defense/space businesses. The article framed this as an ethics concern. No source reviewed establishes that Andreessen used his Defense Policy Board position to direct a contract to a16z or committed a procurement crime. The documentary facts are the appointment, the investments/co-investments, and the financial-policy overlap; improper influence remains an allegation or concern unless evidence of an act emerges.

#### Political spending, 2026 cycle

By mid-2026 Andreessen, Horowitz, their families and a16z had become among the largest technology-linked political spenders in the United States.

*The Washington Post*’s donor tracker in July 2026 grouped **Ben Horowitz & Marc Andreessen** at approximately **$91.3 million** in “large donations,” with major sums to the AI-focused **Leading the Future**, the crypto-focused **Fairshake**, and Trump-aligned **MAGA Inc.** That figure is a grouped tracker total, not a statement that Marc personally wrote checks totaling exactly $91.3 million.

Separate reporting in *The Guardian* placed Marc and Laura Arrillaga-Andreessen’s current-cycle contributions above **$43 million** under its accounting method and described an even larger combined spending network involving the Horowitzes and a16z.

The political spending is overwhelmingly technology-policy focused but increasingly Republican-aligned. Fairshake and similar industry committees also support pro-crypto candidates across party lines, so “Republican megadonor” and “bipartisan industry-policy spender” can both describe different portions of the record.

#### Reported Justice Department investigation of a16z

On **17 August 2026**, Reuters reported, citing Bloomberg’s sources, that the U.S. Department of Justice was investigating **Andreessen Horowitz** over whether firm partners improperly served on boards of competing AI/data companies.

The companies named included **Databricks** and **Fivetran**. Ben Horowitz sits on Databricks’ board; a16z partner **Martin Casado** sits on Fivetran’s board and had also served on the board of dbt Labs, which Fivetran acquired.

The reported issue is consistent with federal antitrust law’s restrictions on certain **interlocking directorates** among competing corporations.

**Crucial status distinctions:**

- Reuters reported an **investigation**, not a prosecution.
- The Justice Department declined comment to Reuters.
- a16z did not immediately respond to Reuters’ request for comment in that report.
- The report did not identify Marc Andreessen as the director simultaneously sitting on the named competing-company boards.
- No criminal charge, civil complaint, consent decree, or finding was reported by 19 August 2026.
- Such an investigation can end without an enforcement action.

## Political / Religious / Intellectual / Artistic Development

### Religion

No reliable source reviewed establishes a formal current religious affiliation for Andreessen. He discusses history, philosophy, political economy and technological “belief” in quasi-religious language, but calling him an adherent of a specific faith would be speculation.

### From applied programmer to technology theorist

Andreessen’s earliest clearly documented philosophy is practical rather than political: software should be **useful, easy, fast to distribute, and broadly accessible**. His NCSA oral history repeatedly contrasts applied programming with theory.

Netscape added a second principle: **platform control matters**. The browser could become a layer above the operating system, which made it a strategic threat to Microsoft and taught Andreessen about winner-take-all software markets.

Loudcloud/Opsware added a third: **company survival and timing matter as much as technical correctness**. He later concluded Loudcloud had been years too early and acknowledged that technological trajectories can be right while companies fail because markets arrive at the wrong time.

Venture capital converted these lessons into portfolio theory: because technological outcomes follow a power-law distribution, an investor can be wrong frequently and still succeed spectacularly if a few bets become enormous.

### “Software is eating the world,” 2011

Andreessen’s *Wall Street Journal* essay **“Why Software Is Eating the World”** argued that software companies were not a small technology sector but were absorbing and transforming industries across the economy.

The phrase became one of the most influential venture-capital slogans of the 2010s and a16z later placed “Software Is Eating the World” at the center of its institutional branding.

### 2020 buildism

“It’s Time to Build” moved from digital software to the physical and institutional world. Andreessen argued the U.S. had underbuilt housing, transportation, medicine, manufacturing and public capacity. The essay anticipated later “abundance” discourse across ideological lines but was sharper in blaming institutions for stagnation.

### Crypto

a16z became one of the largest venture investors and political advocates in cryptocurrency. Andreessen’s crypto position combines:

- belief in software-based financial innovation;
- hostility to regulation-by-enforcement;
- concern that U.S. restrictions will push technology offshore;
- a financial interest through firm investments and tokens.

The last point is important: a16z’s own disclosures explicitly state that in crypto investments it acts in its own financial interest, not necessarily for other token holders. Policy advocacy and economic self-interest therefore coexist openly.

### AI

Andreessen’s AI politics are strongly anti-restriction. He regards AI as a general-purpose intelligence amplifier, opposes “doomer” arguments for slowing development, and frames the U.S.-China AI race as a national-security issue.

His 2023 writing goes beyond optimism into a moral claim: preventing beneficial AI can itself cost lives. That argument is central to his rejection of precautionary AI regulation.

### Techno-optimism and accelerationism

The 2023 manifesto gives Andreessen’s most systematic ideology:

- technological progress as morally good;
- capitalism/markets as creative rather than exploitative;
- material abundance and energy expansion;
- a preference for growth over redistribution-centered zero-sum politics;
- explicit accelerationism;
- strong skepticism toward institutions and intellectual traditions he sees as dedicated to risk-aversion or stagnation.

He borrows “techno-capital machine” from Nick Land, an important fact in the genealogy of the text. Land’s later political work is associated with neoreaction and accelerationism. Andreessen’s use does not by itself establish that he accepts all of Land’s racial or anti-democratic politics.

### Shift in partisan alignment

Andreessen’s voting/support history is ideologically mixed. He has described supporting Democratic presidential candidates across much of the 1990s and 2000s, Romney in 2012, and Hillary Clinton in 2016 after an initial preference for Carly Fiorina. In 2016 he opposed Trump in part over immigration.

By 2024 he supported Trump. His stated reasons centered on:

- crypto regulation;
- AI regulation;
- antitrust;
- taxation of unrealized gains;
- startup formation;
- national competition in technology.

By 2025–26 his rhetoric broadened into culture-war and institutional themes: DEI, universities, immigration selection, the National Science Foundation, and a more explicit alliance with Trump administration policy networks.

### National-security turn

a16z’s **American Dynamism** strategy and Andreessen’s public arguments increasingly connect venture capital to:

- weapons and autonomous systems;
- reindustrialization;
- manufacturing;
- aerospace;
- U.S.-China competition;
- government procurement;
- border and public-safety technology.

The 2026 PCAST and Defense Policy Board appointments formalize a relationship between his private technological worldview and federal advisory institutions.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### 1. Microsoft browser war and antitrust case

**Andreessen’s legal status:** competitor/witness-side figure, not defendant.

**Documented facts:** Microsoft saw Netscape as a platform threat. Internet Explorer was integrated and bundled into Windows, and Microsoft entered distribution agreements designed to increase browser share. Federal antitrust findings concluded Microsoft engaged in anticompetitive conduct to maintain its operating-system monopoly.

**Disputed historical point:** what exactly Microsoft proposed at the June 1995 meeting with Netscape. Netscape described an improper market-division or coercive proposal; Microsoft disputed that framing.

**Outcome for Andreessen:** Netscape lost browser dominance but was acquired by AOL. No criminal or civil finding against Andreessen arose from this episode.

### 2. eBay / Skype transaction and Carl Icahn allegations

**Status:** public conflict-of-interest allegation; no finding of fraud against Andreessen located.

**Underlying transaction:** eBay sold control of Skype in 2009 to a consortium including Andreessen Horowitz while Andreessen sat on eBay’s board. Skype was then sold to Microsoft at a much higher valuation in 2011.

**Icahn claim:** the sale deprived eBay shareholders of value and Andreessen’s dual role was improper.

**Response:** Andreessen/eBay denied wrongdoing and said conflict procedures and independent review were used.

**What is established:** the dual economic relationship and transactions are factual.  
**What is not established:** that Andreessen secretly stole Skype, committed fraud, or breached fiduciary duty in a court-adjudicated sense.

### 3. Facebook stock reclassification / secret coaching of Zuckerberg

**Status:** documented communications; civil fiduciary allegations; plan abandoned before trial; no final liability judgment.

**Documented facts:** Andreessen served on a board special committee negotiating with Zuckerberg. Unsealed texts showed he privately coached or updated Zuckerberg about negotiations.

**Plaintiff theory:** the committee was not genuinely independent and Andreessen breached fiduciary duties.

**Outcome:** Facebook abandoned the reclassification shortly before trial. Litigation became moot; substantial plaintiffs’ fees were paid. Later Delaware decisions concerned derivative procedure and corporate costs.

**No criminal allegation:** the episode was a corporate-governance dispute.

### 4. India / colonialism comment

**Status:** documented public statement and apology.

Andreessen’s statement that anti-colonialism had been economically catastrophic for India triggered strong reaction because it appeared to defend colonialism during a dispute over Facebook Free Basics.

He deleted the comment and apologized. Zuckerberg publicly repudiated it.

**No legal finding or investigation** resulted from the speech itself.

### 5. Atherton housing opposition

**Status:** documented public land-use advocacy; reputational contradiction, not illegality.

Andreessen publicly called for substantially more housing construction in 2020, then in 2022 he and his wife opposed multifamily zoning in Atherton and supported maintaining extremely low density.

The contradiction is documentary. Whether it reflects hypocrisy, legitimate local preference, property-value defense, traffic concern or some mixture is interpretive.

### 6. Meta privacy / Cambridge Analytica derivative case

**Status:** civil shareholder allegation; trial begun; settlement; no admission.

Plaintiffs alleged Meta directors/officers failed to protect privacy and comply with obligations, exposing the company to a $5 billion FTC penalty and other harm. Andreessen was among the directors targeted.

A Delaware trial opened in July 2025 and was quickly settled. Later terms included about $190 million in D&O-insurance-funded payment to Meta and governance measures. Defendants denied wrongdoing.

**No court finding that Andreessen personally caused Cambridge Analytica’s conduct or violated criminal law.**

### 7. Coinbase direct-listing derivative litigation

**Status:** ongoing civil litigation as of the dossier date; pleading-stage claims sustained; no final liability judgment.

**Allegation:** directors and executives possessed material nonpublic information when selling in Coinbase’s April 2021 direct listing and avoided approximately $1.09 billion in losses.

**Andreessen/a16z-linked sale:** Delaware opinions and the complaint place the amount above $118.7 million.

**2024 ruling:** motion to dismiss denied; the court held claims sufficiently pleaded.

**2026 ruling:** Special Litigation Committee motion to terminate denied, principally because of a factual independence problem involving committee member Gokul Rajaram’s relationships with Andreessen/a16z.

**Important distinction:** denying dismissal or termination is not a judicial finding that insider trading occurred.

**Criminal status:** no criminal insider-trading charge against Andreessen located.

### 8. 2025 leaked/private messages on universities, DEI, immigration and NSF

**Status:** reported authenticated screenshots reviewed by *The Washington Post* and corroborated by chat participants; not a legal case.

The messages show Andreessen advocating an aggressive political “counterattack” on universities and dismantling/rebuilding NSF. They also reveal a personal Stanford grievance involving his wife.

**Dispute/limits:** Andreessen did not comment to the Post; Stanford did not validate his account of Laura’s departure; White House said he was not an official adviser in that chat.

### 9. Defense Policy Board / a16z defense investments / Trump-family co-investment questions

**Status:** potential conflict-of-interest concern raised by reporting; no proven procurement misconduct.

**Facts:** Andreessen is a Pentagon advisory-board member; a16z invests in defense contractors; a16z has co-invested with 1789 Capital, where Donald Trump Jr. is a partner, in some companies.

**Allegation/concern:** policy influence could increase the value of private holdings or favor connected companies.

**Evidence missing:** no reviewed source demonstrates Andreessen directed a particular federal contract, took an illegal payment, or was found to violate a federal ethics rule.

### 10. Political contributions and regulatory benefits

Critics argue that the rapid policy reversal in Washington on crypto, AI and technology regulation after 2024 benefited donors including Andreessen and a16z.

The **donations and policy interests are documented**. Some regulatory changes also plainly benefit portfolio sectors.

What is **not** established in the reviewed record is an illegal quid pro quo between a specific Andreessen contribution and a specific official act. Such a claim requires more than temporal proximity and shared interests.

### 11. Justice Department scrutiny of a16z, August 2026

**Subject reported:** potentially unlawful interlocking directorates involving a16z partners and competing AI/data companies, including Databricks and Fivetran.

**Andreessen personally:** not identified in Reuters’ report as the director occupying the specific overlapping seats.

**Current status:** unresolved.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: “Marc Andreessen invented the Internet”

**1. What the story says**  
Popular shorthand sometimes turns Andreessen into “the inventor of the Internet” or “inventor of the Web.”

**2. When and where it appears**  
The confusion grew during the mid-1990s press explosion around Netscape, when Andreessen became the visible young face of the Internet boom. It persists in simplified biographies and promotional descriptions.

**3. Who circulated it**  
Mainstream profiles, television shorthand, technology marketing and later business lore have all contributed. It is usually simplification rather than a claim Andreessen himself originated.

**4. Evidence for or against it**  
The claim is false in the literal sense. The Internet’s packet-networking architecture predates Andreessen by decades; TCP/IP is associated with Vint Cerf, Bob Kahn and many collaborators. Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web’s core architecture at CERN. Andreessen and Bina created Mosaic, an extraordinarily influential browser that made the already-existing Web accessible to a much wider population.

Andreessen himself has explicitly acknowledged the government-funded Internet infrastructure and preexisting Web standards in his Smithsonian oral history.

**5. Reputational effect**  
The myth raised Andreessen from important browser engineer to symbolic “inventor of cyberspace,” helping create the young-genius cult around Netscape. It also irritated earlier Web pioneers and obscured the distributed, institutional history of the Internet.

### Myth 2: “Mosaic was the first Web browser”

**1. Story**  
Mosaic is frequently called the first browser, first graphical browser, or first browser to show images.

**2. Appearance**  
The formulation appears in 1990s and later journalistic profiles, university summaries and award biographies.

**3. Circulators**  
Technology press, educational material and institutional biographies.

**4. Evidence**  
Browsers existed before Mosaic, including Berners-Lee’s original WorldWideWeb/Nexus and other early clients. Even contemporary accounts recognized predecessor browsers. Mosaic’s defensible priority is not absolute firstness but mass usability and popularity. Its inline-image handling, graphical interface, cross-platform ports and distribution made it the browser that introduced enormous numbers of people to the Web.

**5. Reputational effect**  
“First browser” makes for a cleaner heroic story but understates both predecessors and Eric Bina/NCSA’s collaborative role.

### Myth 3: “Andreessen single-handedly created Mosaic”

**1. Story**  
A lone undergraduate genius writes Mosaic and launches the Web.

**2. Appearance**  
Personality-centered profiles and business mythology.

**3. Circulators**  
Media narratives, motivational biographies, simplified company histories.

**4. Evidence**  
Andreessen was a principal originator and product driver, but **Eric Bina** co-wrote the initial browser and NCSA staff contributed ports, systems work and later development. Mosaic also depended on Berners-Lee’s Web, NSF-funded networking, university infrastructure, Unix/X and graphical-interface conventions.

**5. Reputational effect**  
The lone-genius story amplified Andreessen’s brand but erases collaborators and public infrastructure.

### Myth 4: The barefoot “king of the Internet”

**1. Story**  
Andreessen is the young monarch of a new economy: barefoot, crowned or enthroned, richer and more powerful than old industrial executives.

**2. Appearance**  
Most famously in *Time*’s mid-1990s visual treatment after Netscape’s IPO.

**3. Circulators**  
Netscape publicity, magazine culture and dot-com-era business press.

**4. Evidence**  
The photo/image is real; the monarchy is symbolic. Andreessen owned valuable Netscape shares and had genuine technical authority, but Jim Clark, Jim Barksdale, venture investors, Microsoft, telecom firms and capital markets all constrained what Netscape could do.

**5. Reputational effect**  
It established the template for later young technology-founder celebrity: casual dress, youth, technical genius, vast paper wealth, and apparent displacement of older institutions.

### Myth 5: “Netscape’s IPO caused the dot-com boom”

**1. Story**  
9 August 1995 is the Big Bang of the commercial Internet and the single beginning of the dot-com bubble.

**2. Appearance**  
Retrospective business histories frequently call it the Internet’s “Big Bang” or the “Netscape moment.”

**3. Circulators**  
Financial press, Internet-history podcasts, venture-capital lore and later comparisons with AI IPO markets.

**4. Evidence**  
The IPO was genuinely catalytic and symbolically extraordinary. But the commercial Internet was already expanding through ISPs, online services, Web companies, telecom investment and falling hardware costs. Monetary conditions, venture capital, later IPOs and public enthusiasm all contributed to the bubble.

**5. Reputational effect**  
The story makes Andreessen a founding prophet of the entire dot-com economy, reinforcing his authority to pronounce on later technology cycles.

### Myth 6: “Microsoft alone killed Netscape”

**1. Story**  
Netscape was a perfect company destroyed solely by an illegal Microsoft monopoly.

**2. Appearance**  
Browser-war histories and antitrust retellings.

**3. Circulators**  
Microsoft critics, Netscape nostalgists, antitrust commentary.

**4. Evidence**  
The federal antitrust record strongly supports the proposition that Microsoft engaged in exclusionary conduct to protect Windows and targeted Netscape as a platform threat. But Netscape also expanded rapidly, made product and strategic errors, suffered internal turbulence and pivoted toward enterprise markets. Andreessen and other insiders later acknowledged company mistakes.

**5. Reputational effect**  
The pure-victim version preserves Netscape as a technologically flawless martyr. The opposite Microsoft-friendly myth—that Netscape simply failed because its product was bad—also ignores the antitrust findings. The documentary record supports both anticompetitive pressure and internal failure.

### Myth 7: “Andreessen can see the future”

**1. Story**  
Andreessen is a technological oracle whose job is to identify the future decades before others.

**2. Appearance**  
Profiles including *The New Yorker* and *Wired* explicitly built around his predictive reputation; a16z’s brand markets thesis-driven foresight.

**3. Circulators**  
Andreessen himself, the firm’s media operation, entrepreneurs seeking capital, technology press, supporters and critics.

**4. Evidence**  
He has made important successful calls: graphical Web browsing, software’s spread across industries, cloud/infrastructure automation, social networks, mobile, crypto and AI as large investable categories. He has also backed failures, mistimed technologies, and watched highly valued companies collapse. His own venture model assumes most forecasts/investments will not dominate; a few enormous winners determine returns.

**5. Reputational effect**  
The oracle narrative creates deal flow and influence. It is also self-reinforcing: founders want investment from the investor believed able to “see” the future.

### Myth 8: “Techno-Optimist Manifesto proves Andreessen is a fascist”

**1. Story**  
A hostile contemporary reading identifies the manifesto and Andreessen’s rightward politics as fascist or neo-reactionary, pointing to his references to accelerationist thinkers and controversial “patron saints.”

**2. Appearance**  
Critical journalism and political commentary after October 2023, intensified after his Trump support.

**3. Circulators**  
Critics on the political left, some technology-policy writers, and opponents of accelerationism.

**4. Evidence**  
The manifesto does explicitly endorse accelerationism and borrows Nick Land’s phrase “techno-capital machine.” Its reference list includes figures with fascist or reactionary associations alongside liberal, libertarian, socialist, scientific and artistic figures. That establishes an eclectic and sometimes politically extreme intellectual genealogy.

The text does **not** say “I am a fascist,” establish one-party dictatorship as its program, or make every political belief of its cited figures Andreessen’s own. Classifying the whole document as fascist is therefore an interpretation, not a documentary fact. Classifying it as explicitly **accelerationist, pro-market and techno-capitalist** is textually grounded.

**5. Reputational effect**  
The controversy transformed a venture-capital worldview into an object of political-intellectual analysis and made Andreessen a representative figure in debates about Silicon Valley’s movement toward the political right.

### Myth 9: “Andreessen runs DOGE / the Trump administration from behind the scenes”

**1. Story**  
Andreessen is portrayed in some commentary as an unelected technology oligarch exercising covert state power.

**2. Appearance**  
After Trump’s 2024 victory, when Andreessen spent substantial time in transition circles and participated in personnel/policy discussions.

**3. Circulators**  
Critical press, political opponents, social-media narratives about Silicon Valley capture of government.

**4. Evidence**  
There is strong reporting that Andreessen was an influential informal transition adviser, participated in hiring discussions, spent time at Mar-a-Lago, and called himself an unpaid volunteer around DOGE activity. In 2026 he received two genuine federal advisory appointments, PCAST and the Defense Policy Board.

There is **not** evidence in the sources reviewed that he formally led DOGE, held Cabinet authority, or possessed a secret legal office equivalent to executive control.

**5. Reputational effect**  
The narrative reflects a real increase in his political access while often overstating the formal powers of that access.

### Myth 10: “Every a16z position is Marc Andreessen’s personal position or investment”

**1. Story**  
Portfolio companies, political contributions and board decisions attributed to a16z are routinely described as Andreessen’s own holdings or decisions.

**2. Appearance**  
Media shorthand and social-media discussion.

**3. Circulators**  
Supporters, critics, financial press.

**4. Evidence**  
Andreessen is co-founder and a general partner, but a16z is a large registered investment adviser with many partners, funds, LPs and separate legal entities. Deal leadership varies. A fund’s shares are not automatically Andreessen’s personal shares. Conversely, as co-founder he has institutional influence and cannot plausibly be treated as unrelated to the firm’s overall strategy.

**5. Reputational effect**  
The conflation simultaneously exaggerates his direct ownership and accurately reflects the fact that his name/brand is inseparable from the institution.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Andreessen is living, so a truly posthumous reputation does not yet exist. The following are current historical interpretations likely to shape later assessments.

### 1. Mosaic and the mass Web

His least disputable technological legacy is Mosaic’s role in making the Web visually usable and culturally legible to non-specialists. The more accurate formulation is **co-creator/popularizer of a foundational graphical browser**, not inventor of the Web.

### 2. Netscape as commercial rupture

Netscape demonstrated that the browser could be a platform, that free Internet distribution could build enormous markets, and that a young Internet company could reach public markets at extreme speed. Its failure to maintain browser dominance became equally instructive: platform power, distribution and operating-system control can overwhelm an innovative entrant.

### 3. Mozilla/open-source lineage

Netscape’s decision to open its browser source in 1998 helped create the Mozilla project, whose institutional and software descendants include Firefox. Andreessen was not the sole author of Mozilla, but Netscape’s corporate history is part of the lineage.

### 4. Cloud infrastructure before the cloud

Loudcloud was early and nearly disastrous. Opsware’s pivot demonstrated that infrastructure-management software could become a major enterprise category. Andreessen and Horowitz later used this story to build their theory of founder resilience and crisis management.

### 5. Venture-capital institutionalization

a16z changed expectations for what a venture firm could be: a large operating platform, media organization, recruiter, lobby/policy shop and eventually registered adviser managing tens of billions rather than a small partnership of dealmakers.

Whether this is viewed as professionalization or as the concentration of private technological power is part of the continuing debate.

### 6. Venture capital as ideology and media

Andreessen made the investor’s worldview itself into a product. “Software is eating the world,” “It’s Time to Build,” and “The Techno-Optimist Manifesto” are not merely market analysis; they are ideological texts that recruit founders, shape policy and justify capital allocation.

### 7. Tech-right political realignment

His movement from support for Democratic presidential candidates to major Trump backing and federal advisory roles makes him a central case in the political realignment of Silicon Valley in the 2020s.

Future historians will likely examine whether this shift was driven primarily by:
- crypto and AI regulation;
- taxation and antitrust;
- post-2020 culture conflict;
- perceived institutional hostility to technology;
- national-security competition with China;
- business interests;
- or a genuine philosophical evolution.

The current documentary record supports elements of all of these; no single explanation is sufficient.

### 8. Conflicts and governance

The Skype/eBay conflict allegations, Facebook reclassification texts, Coinbase derivative litigation, defense-policy overlap and reported DOJ scrutiny of a16z form a recurring governance theme: Andreessen’s power comes from occupying multiple networks simultaneously—investor, director, adviser, political donor. Those overlapping roles create opportunities for influence and also recurring questions about independence.

Some episodes produced no adjudicated wrongdoing; one current civil case remains unresolved. Their historical significance lies partly in showing the governance problems created when venture firms become institutions with holdings across entire technological ecosystems.

### 9. Mythic figure of the “future”

Andreessen has been represented successively as:
- awkward Midwestern computer prodigy;
- barefoot king of the Internet;
- Microsoft antagonist;
- dot-com survivor;
- cloud pioneer;
- venture oracle;
- techno-optimist;
- accelerationist;
- crypto/AI political power broker;
- Trump-era technology adviser.

Each image contains a piece of the documented life and a layer of exaggeration.

- **9 July:** Marc Lowell Andreessen born in Cedar Falls, Iowa.

- Andreessen becomes a major magazine/television symbol of Internet boom.

- **24 November 1998:** AOL announces Netscape acquisition.

### 1999
- AOL-Netscape deal closes.
- Andreessen serves briefly as AOL CTO.
- Resigns September.
- Co-founds Loudcloud with Ben Horowitz, Tim Howes and In Sik Rhee.

### 2000
- Loudcloud grows aggressively during dot-com peak.

### 2001
- Loudcloud IPO during technology downturn.

### 2002
- Sells hosting operations to EDS.
- Company renamed Opsware; pivots to data-center automation software.

### 2003
- Andreessen and Horowitz begin significant angel investing around this period.

### 2004
- Co-founds Ning with Gina Bianchini.

### 2005
- Meets Laura Arrillaga at New Year’s Eve dinner.
- Ning era develops.
- Several later-granted Ning-related patent applications originate in this period.

### 2006
- Marries Laura Arrillaga.
- Advises Mark Zuckerberg not to accept Yahoo’s roughly $1 billion Facebook offer.

### 2007
- HP announces and completes acquisition of Opsware for roughly $1.6–1.7 billion.
- Public filing documents Andreessen trust-held Opsware shares/options.
- Purchases Atherton estate for about $16.6 million.

### 2008
- June: joins Facebook board.
- September: joins eBay board.

### 2009
- Founds Andreessen Horowitz with Ben Horowitz; first fund about $300 million.
- Joins HP board.
- eBay sells Skype control to consortium including a16z.
- Andreessen offers $25,000 reward in Barbara Blackstone cold case.

### 2010
- Ning eliminates free tier, lays off employees, shifts business model.

### 2011
- Ning sold to Glam Media.
- Microsoft agrees to buy Skype for $8.5 billion.
- Publishes “Why Software Is Eating the World.”

### 2012
- Named to TIME 100.

### 2013
- Shares inaugural Queen Elizabeth Prize for Engineering.
- Internet Hall of Fame induction.
- a16z invests in Coinbase.
- Later-granted Ning-related patents continue to appear.

### 2014
- Carl Icahn publicly attacks Andreessen over Skype/eBay conflicts.
- Andreessen resigns from eBay board in October.

### 2015
- March: son John announced, born via gestational surrogate.
- Major *New Yorker* profile.
- Ends HP board service; transitions through HPE board after corporate split.

### 2016
- February: India anti-colonialism tweet controversy; deletes and apologizes.
- Facebook stock-reclassification special committee and shareholder litigation; private Andreessen-Zuckerberg texts later become public.

### 2017
- Zuckerberg/Facebook abandon stock reclassification shortly before trial.
- Andreessen’s role in special committee remains a governance controversy.

### 2018
- HPE board service ends around this period.
- Cambridge Analytica crisis intensifies Meta/Facebook privacy scrutiny.

### 2019
- a16z becomes a registered investment adviser.

### 2020
- Publishes “It’s Time to Build.”

### 2021
- Buys Malibu Paradise Cove estate for about $177 million.
- **14 April:** Coinbase direct listing; directors/insiders sell shares, later challenged in Delaware derivative litigation.

### 2022
- Andreessen and Laura oppose multifamily-density proposals in Atherton, attracting national criticism in light of his build-housing rhetoric.

### 2023
- April: Coinbase derivative complaint filed.
- June: publishes “Why AI Will Save the World.”
- **16 October:** publishes “The Techno-Optimist Manifesto,” explicitly endorsing accelerationism.

### 2024
- **1 February:** Delaware court denies motion to dismiss Coinbase derivative claims at pleading stage.
- **5 July:** Andreessen and Horowitz publish “The Little Tech Agenda.”
- **16 July:** podcast announces support for Donald Trump based on technology policy.
- Major donations to Trump-aligned, Republican and crypto-focused political committees.
- Trump wins election; Andreessen becomes involved in transition/DOGE-oriented policy and hiring discussions.

### 2025
- Reports describe Andreessen as an informal, unpaid participant in Trump/DOGE transition networks.
- May: sends private WhatsApp messages attacking university DEI/immigration policies and NSF; messages published by *Washington Post* in July.
- July: Meta privacy derivative trial begins and quickly settles; defendants deny wrongdoing.
- August: Atherton estate sold for approximately $27.2 million.
- November: Meta settlement terms later reported at approximately $190 million plus governance changes.

### 2026
- **9 January:** a16z announces more than $15 billion in new funds.
- **30 January:** Delaware court denies Coinbase Special Litigation Committee’s motion to terminate *Grabski v. Andreessen*.
- **25 March:** President Trump appoints Andreessen to PCAST.
- **30 April:** a16z reports more than $100 billion under management as of this date.
- **29 June:** appointed to Defense Policy Board.
- July/August: campaign-finance reporting places Andreessen/Horowitz/a16z network among largest technology political spenders.
- **11 August:** *Guardian* reports on Defense Policy Board and Trump-family-linked 1789 Capital co-investment concerns.
- **17 August:** Reuters reports DOJ investigation of a16z over possible interlocking AI-company directorships.
- **19 August:** Andreessen alive; no announced charge or finding from the a16z DOJ inquiry; Coinbase civil derivative case remains unresolved in reviewed public record.
