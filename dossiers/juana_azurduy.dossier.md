# Juana Azurduy de Padilla: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary cautions

Juana Azurduy’s biography is unusually entangled with patriotic literature, oral tradition, nineteenth-century historical fiction, twentieth-century nationalism, and twenty-first-century feminist and decolonial commemoration. Many familiar details were first recorded decades after the events and cannot be independently verified.

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a surviving contemporary record or directly identifiable archival document.
- **Reported fact:** stated by later historians or institutions, usually with plausible but incompletely accessible documentation.
- **Disputed claim:** competing documentary or historiographical versions exist.
- **Legend or literary tradition:** important to her reception but not established by contemporaneous evidence.
- **Unverified claim:** repeated without an identifiable primary source.

The strongest modern documentary reconstruction consulted is Norberto Benjamín Torres’s *Juana Asurdui de Padilla (1780–1862): la historia detrás de la leyenda*, which reproduces or identifies baptismal, marriage, baptismal, death, property, military, and pension records. Its findings correct several errors still repeated by Argentine and Bolivian government pages.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Documented name at baptism:** Juana Asurdui.

**Documented married name:** Juana Asurdui de Padilla; later records also use Juana Azurduy de Padilla.

**Common modern form:** Juana Azurduy de Padilla.

**Other spellings:** Asurdui, Azurdui, Azurduy, Assurduy. “Azurduy” is the standardized Spanish spelling; “Asurdui” is the spelling found in the baptismal and marriage documentation identified by Torres.

**Frequently but incorrectly given identity:** Juana Azurduy Bermúdez, daughter of Matías Azurduy and Eulalia Bermúdez. Recent documentary research indicates that this was another child bearing a similar name, born or baptized in 1780, whose record became conflated with the guerrilla’s.

**Titles and ranks during life:**

- Commander or captain associated with the insurgent force usually called **Los Leales**.
- **Teniente coronel** of the armies of the United Provinces of the Río de la Plata, formally approved in 1816.
- Commonly called **la Coronela** in later remembrance.

**Posthumous ranks and honorifics:**

- Heroína Nacional of Bolivia.
- General of the Bolivian armed forces, posthumously, 1962.
- Generala of the Argentine Army, posthumously, under Argentine Decree 892/2009.
- Mariscal or Mariscala of Bolivia in modern commemorative usage.
- “Flor del Alto Perú,” popularized particularly by Félix Luna and Ariel Ramírez’s 1969 song.

**Birth:** Probably January 1780, in La Plata—now Sucre—in the Audiencia of Charcas, Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata.

**Baptism:** 26 March 1780, church of San Pedro de Tarabuco.

**Death:** 25 May 1862, Sucre, Bolivia.

**Recorded cause or condition at death:** The parish death entry reportedly says she died “con hinchazón”—with swelling or edema—after receiving Communion. This describes a symptom, not a secure medical diagnosis.

**Burial:** Common grave in Sucre’s cemetery according to the received account. Remains identified as hers were exhumed and transferred to the Casa de la Libertad in 1962, although the certainty obtainable from an exhumation a century after burial in a common-grave environment is necessarily limited.

**Principal occupation:** Landholder, insurgent organizer, mounted guerrilla commander, and officer in the wars against Spanish royal authority in Charcas and the northern Río de la Plata theater.

**Political alignment:** Revolutionary or “patriot” forces supporting separation from Spanish rule. Her operational affiliations passed through the revolutionary networks of Chuquisaca, the Auxiliary Army of Peru/Army of the North, the Padilla insurgency in La Laguna, and Martín Miguel de Güemes’s forces.

## The Birth and Identity Dispute

### The traditional version

For much of the twentieth century, biographies said that Juana was born at Toroca on **12 July 1780**, daughter of **Matías Azurduy** and **Eulalia Bermúdez**. Some variants made the father a wealthy white hacendado and the mother an Indigenous or mestiza woman. Argentina legislated commemorations around 12 July, and Bolivia also institutionalized that date.

The version was reinforced by Joaquín Gantier’s influential biography and repeated by government, educational, journalistic, and popular sources. It remains present on official Argentine pages.

### The documentary correction

**Documented fact:** A baptismal entry preserved in the Archivo y Biblioteca Arquidiocesanos “Monseñor Miguel de los Santos Taborga” records that on **26 March 1780**, Pedro Dávalos administered oil and chrism at San Pedro de Tarabuco to:

> Juana, two months old, mestiza, legitimate daughter of Isidro Asurdui and Juliana Llanos.

The record identifies Rosa Sárate as godmother. Because it says Juana was approximately two months old, the precise birthday is unrecoverable, but birth in late January 1780 is the strongest inference.

**Documented fact:** The marriage record of **19 May 1799** for Manuel Ascencio Padilla and Juana Asurdui identifies her consistently with this parentage. It does not identify her as Juana Bermúdez.

**Disputed claim resolved in favor of the newer reconstruction:** The 12 July record appears to belong to **Juana Azurduy Bermúdez**, daughter of Matías Azurduy and Eulalia Bermúdez, not to Padilla’s wife. The two women were conflated in later historiography.

In 2019–2026, historians in Sucre repeatedly sought correction of official observances. Local authorities have at times suspended or reconsidered 12 July ceremonies, while pre-existing Argentine and Bolivian statutes still preserve the traditional date.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Documented fact:**

- Father: **Isidro Asurdui**.
- Mother: **Juliana Llanos**.

The baptismal record calls Juana a legitimate daughter and categorizes her as **mestiza**.

### Paternal ancestry

Torres’s reconstruction identifies Juana’s paternal grandparents as:

- **Joseph de Asurdui y Otálora**, of Spanish or Basque family background.
- **Paula Valencia**, described in the reconstruction as an Indigenous noblewoman descended from the cacique family of Quila Quila among the Yamparáez.

This ancestry is best labeled **reported from genealogical and parish research**, rather than treated as completely established from the baptismal entry alone.

Claims that the surname descended from a distinguished medieval European house circulated in older genealogical writing. Heather Hennes and Torres both show how genealogical elevation became part of the heroic legend. A Basque linguistic origin for Asurdui/Azurduy is plausible; elaborate claims of noble European descent require more evidence than surname resemblance.

### Siblings and guardians

Later biographies mention a brother named **Blas** who died young and a sister named **Rosalía**. They also describe Juana as orphaned while still a child and subsequently placed with relatives and in a convent.

These details are **reported facts of uncertain documentation**. They should not be confused with what is directly established by the surviving baptismal and marriage records.

### Marriage

**Documented fact:** Juana married **Manuel Ascencio Padilla** at San Miguel de Moromoro on **19 May 1799**.

Padilla was a landholder and later insurgent commander in the La Laguna region. Older biographies sometimes place the wedding in 1802 or 1805 and give Juana’s age as twenty-five. The marriage register makes 1799, when she was approximately nineteen, the strongest date.

### Children

The marriage produced at least six children in the revised documentary reconstruction, though popular biographies usually say five.

Records and later accounts identify:

1. **Manuel** — a son whose death was entered at San Miguel de Moromoro on 24 May 1800. This early Manuel may explain inconsistencies in later five-child lists.
2. **Romualdo** — baptized in La Plata on 7 February 1803.
3. **Mariano** — baptized at San Miguel de Moromoro on 15 May 1806.
4. **Juliana** — died at El Villar on 29 April 1814.
5. **Mercedes** — known from family reconstructions; documentation was not recovered in the sources consulted.
6. **Luisa** — the only child generally reported to have survived into adulthood.

Traditional biographies instead list **Manuel, Mariano, Juliana, Mercedes, and Luisa**, omitting either the infant who died in 1800 or Romualdo and sometimes treating records as if they referred to the same child. The exact reconciliation of the names remains a genealogical problem.

### Deaths of the children

The standard narrative says that the four older children died from disease, hunger, exposure, or malaria during the guerrilla campaigns, after periods of flight and concealment.

- Juliana’s death on 29 April 1814 is supported by a parish death record.
- The documentary basis for every detail surrounding the other deaths is less clear.
- Graphic scenes in which Juana digs their graves herself belong principally to later biographical and literary narration.

### Luisa

Luisa reportedly survived the war, married, and had descendants. A descendant through Luisa participated in later efforts to preserve the family memory. Stories that Luisa was born during battle, that Juana fought immediately after childbirth, or that mother and infant escaped by plunging into a river are discussed below as heroic tradition rather than fully documented fact.

## Childhood and Early Life

Juana grew up between La Plata/Sucre, Tarabuco, Moromoro, and the rural properties associated with her family. Her upbringing placed her in a multilingual, socially mixed region with Spanish-speaking urban elites and Quechua-speaking Indigenous communities.

Later biographies attribute the following formative experiences to her:

- Learning Quechua, and sometimes Aymara, from childhood.
- Riding horses and working in the countryside.
- Accompanying her father on journeys.
- Learning practical knowledge from Indigenous laborers and rural communities.
- Resisting restrictive expectations placed on girls.
- Becoming an expert rider and acquiring familiarity with weapons.

The general picture is plausible given her later operational environment, but many of the childhood anecdotes were recorded long after her death.

### Orphanhood and inheritance

Traditional biographies say that both parents died while she was still young. Her father’s death is sometimes described as murder arising from a romantic or aristocratic rivalry. No primary legal record substantiating that murder was located in the sources consulted.

As a legitimate daughter of a propertied family, Juana inherited interests in rural land. Later events show that she and Padilla possessed or claimed properties, especially **Culcu/Cullcu**. Her childhood was therefore not one of continuous poverty: she came from a landholding household, even though her family’s exact wealth is difficult to quantify.

## Education and Formation

### Convent education

The familiar story says that Juana was placed in the **Convent of Santa Teresa in Chuquisaca**, either for education or in preparation for religious life, but was expelled after rebelling against convent discipline or quarreling with the superior.

This is a **reported tradition**, not securely documented in the materials consulted. Versions differ on her age, the duration of her residence, the nature of the conflict, and whether she ever seriously intended to become a nun.

The story became narratively useful because it contrasted cloistered femininity with the mounted commander she later became. It should not be treated as a verbatim episode without a convent register or contemporary testimony.

### Languages and literacy

Juana probably spoke Spanish and Quechua. Claims of fluency in Aymara are common but less securely tied to her home region and require caution.

Her later petitions and correspondence indicate participation in literate administrative culture, although surviving documents may have been written by clerks or secretaries and signed or marked by her. No complete study of her handwriting or autograph corpus was located.

### Political influences

Biographies often credit:

- Memories of the Indigenous revolts associated with Tomás, Dámaso, and Nicolás Katari.
- The rebellion of Túpac Amaru II and Micaela Bastidas.
- Enlightenment writing, Rousseau, and the French Revolution.
- The intellectual climate of the University of San Francisco Xavier in Chuquisaca.
- Manuel Ascencio Padilla and his educated revolutionary associates.

Direct evidence of Juana reading Rousseau or the *Encyclopédie* has not been established. Such claims usually extrapolate from the intellectual world of Chuquisaca and Padilla’s associations.

Her political conduct nevertheless shows a durable commitment to armed resistance against restored royal authority and to the revolutionary project linking Charcas with the United Provinces.

## Chronological Life History

## 1780–1799: Birth, rural formation, and marriage

- Probably born in La Plata in January 1780.
- Baptized or completed baptismal rites at San Pedro de Tarabuco on 26 March.
- Raised in a mestiza landholding family with ties to rural Chuquisaca.
- Later tradition places her in a convent and portrays her as expelled for insubordination.
- Married Manuel Ascencio Padilla at Moromoro on 19 May 1799.

The marriage joined neighboring family properties and ultimately became a military and political partnership.

## 1800–1808: Family and estate life

The couple’s children began arriving in 1800. Records place them at Moromoro and La Plata, indicating movement between rural estates and the city.

Padilla had connections to Chuquisaca’s educated circles. Accounts portray the couple as increasingly hostile to colonial legal inequalities and the exclusion of American-born or mixed-origin inhabitants from office. Specific private conversations attributed to them are reconstructions, not documented transcripts.

## 1809: Chuquisaca uprising

On **25 May 1809**, a revolt in Chuquisaca displaced the president of the Audiencia, Ramón García de León y Pizarro. The uprising’s immediate motives and its status as a consciously separatist “first cry of liberty” remain subjects of wider historiographical debate.

Juana and Manuel are traditionally said to have supported the revolutionary movement. The exact operations performed by Juana in 1809 are poorly documented. Later nationalist accounts sometimes credit her with conspicuous leadership from the first day; this exceeds the available contemporary record.

After royal authority was re-established, Padilla became involved in resistance networks. The family’s land, mobility, and relationships with rural communities became operational resources.

## 1810–1811: Alliance with the Buenos Aires revolution and reversal after Huaqui

The May Revolution in Buenos Aires produced the first Auxiliary Expedition to Upper Peru. Juan José Castelli’s army entered the region, and Padilla’s group supported the revolutionary cause.

After the patriot defeat at **Huaqui on 20 June 1811**, royalist restoration exposed local insurgents and their families to confiscation and arrest. The Padilla properties were reportedly seized.

A widely repeated account says Juana and her children were captured or confined at a hacienda, after which Padilla organized their rescue and Juana helped overpower the guards. The broad pattern—family persecution, dispossession, and flight—is credible; the cinematic particulars are later narrative.

The couple subsequently operated among Indigenous and mestizo communities in the mountainous countryside.

## 1812–1813: Guerrilla organization and Belgrano’s Army of the North

Under Manuel Belgrano’s command, the Army of the North again advanced into Upper Peru. The Padillas placed their forces at its service.

Their troops formed part of a decentralized insurgent world later called the **“republiquetas.”** That term was popularized by Bartolomé Mitre and should not be mistaken for a uniform contemporary constitutional structure. These were locally supported armed territories led by caudillos who alternated between independent action and cooperation with armies sent from Buenos Aires.

Juana helped recruit, supply, guide, and sometimes command fighters. Later tradition calls her unit **Los Leales** and describes a female corps known as **Las Amazonas**. A women’s mounted contingent is plausible and appears in later military memory, but its fixed organization, membership, and strength are difficult to reconstruct.

Following patriot defeats at **Vilcapugio** and **Ayohuma** in 1813, the Padillas again continued irregular resistance without sustained support from a conventional army.

## 1814: Family catastrophe and continuing resistance

Parish documentation records daughter Juliana’s death at El Villar on **29 April 1814**. Other children reportedly died during the same general period from disease and deprivation.

Later biographies describe Juana moving the children between refuges, leaving them with trusted Indigenous families while campaigning, and personally burying them. These scenes may preserve family memory, but most lack contemporaneous corroboration.

The losses became central to later depictions of Azurduy as simultaneously warrior and sacrificial mother.

## 1815: La Laguna insurgency

The Padillas commanded forces across La Laguna, Tomina, Pomabamba, Tarabuco, Yamparáez, Presto, and neighboring valleys and mountain routes. Their effectiveness depended on:

- Rural intelligence networks.
- Indigenous and mestizo recruitment.
- Familiarity with ravines and passes.
- Captured weapons.
- Rapid mounted attacks.
- Dispersal before royalist retaliation.
- Links to other insurgent commanders.

Accounts associate Juana with actions at Pintatora and other sites. The story that she left a battle to give birth to Luisa and then returned to combat is not securely documented and varies in date and location.

## 1816: Peak military recognition

### El Villar and the captured standard

On or around **3 March 1816**, Juana led a mounted attack near **El Villar**. The force captured a royalist flag or standard and weapons. Later accounts embellish the engagement with precise numbers—often thirty horsemen or thirty women—and with single-combat details.

**Documented core:** Manuel Belgrano wrote from Tucumán on **26 July 1816** commending Azurduy’s conduct and transmitting recognition of her valor. The archival reference is Archivo General de la Nación, Documentos Escritos, Sala X, legajo 23-2-3.

Belgrano recommended her for promotion. The supreme director, **Juan Martín de Pueyrredón**, approved the rank of **teniente coronel**. Modern summaries sometimes incorrectly say that Martín Miguel de Güemes conferred the rank.

### Belgrano’s sword

The story that Belgrano personally gave Juana his sword in admiration is deeply embedded in Argentine remembrance and was repeated by the Argentine presidency in 2009.

Its status is **reported tradition with uncertain documentary basis**. Belgrano’s written commendation is documented; the precise ceremony and provenance of a surviving sword require separate artifact-level proof.

### Death of Manuel Ascencio Padilla

Royalist forces under Francisco Javier de Aguilera intensified operations against the La Laguna insurgency. In September 1816 Padilla was defeated and killed, usually dated **14 September 1816**, in fighting associated with **La Laguna/El Villar**.

His head was severed and publicly displayed by the royalists. Juana is said to have led an attack that recovered it. This recovery is an established part of the later narrative but lacks the same level of accessible contemporary proof as Belgrano’s letter.

Juana was reportedly wounded in this phase of combat, with variants mentioning wounds to the leg and chest.

Padilla’s death damaged the insurgent organization. Some accounts suggest that male commanders resisted accepting Juana as sole successor. The evidence supports loss of command cohesion, but the simple formula “expelled because she was a woman” is a modern interpretation unless tied to a specific order.

## 1817–1821: Northern Argentina and Güemes

After Padilla’s death and further royalist pressure, Juana moved south toward the frontier of the United Provinces, eventually operating under **Martín Miguel de Güemes** in Salta.

Güemes’s forces conducted the defensive irregular war that restricted royalist movement into northern Argentina. Azurduy’s precise command, troop strength, and participation in particular engagements during these years are imperfectly documented.

She reportedly received material support, recognition, or a uniform through Güemes’s command. Statements that Güemes appointed her lieutenant colonel confuse his recognition with Pueyrredón’s formal 1816 approval.

After Güemes was mortally wounded and died in **June 1821**, Juana’s active military role effectively ended. She remained in Salta for a period with her surviving daughter.

## 1822–1825: Poverty, return, and the creation of Bolivia

Juana sought assistance for returning to her homeland. Some accounts say the Salta government provided money or transport after earlier requests were delayed.

The final royalist defeat in Upper Peru and the establishment of Bolivia in 1825 made return possible.

### Meeting with Bolívar and Sucre

**Reported fact, supported by strong historical tradition:** Simón Bolívar and Antonio José de Sucre met Azurduy in Chuquisaca in 1825. Bolívar recognized her service and arranged a pension.

A famous statement attributed to Bolívar says, in substance, that Bolivia should have been called Padilla or Azurduy because the republic owed them so much. The wording appears in later retellings and should not be quoted as an authenticated verbatim remark without a contemporary text.

Claims that Bolívar personally promoted her to colonel on **1 April 1825** are chronologically problematic because Bolívar had not yet arrived in Upper Peru on that date. The documented 1816 lieutenant-colonelcy is firmer than this alleged 1825 promotion.

### Restoration of Culcu

On **11 August 1825**, the new republican authorities ordered the restoration of the hacienda **Culcu/Cullcu** to Juana. Recovery was not necessarily simple: long confiscation, occupation, competing claims, and war damage had diminished the economic value of revolutionary-era property.

She received a state pension generally reported as **sixty pesos**, though sources do not always specify whether this was monthly, annual, or paid regularly. Arrears and bureaucratic interruptions appear to have limited its value.

## 1826–1847: Postwar life

Juana lived principally in and around Chuquisaca/Sucre and Culcu. She attempted to sustain herself and her daughter through land and pension income.

Popular memory often collapses four decades into the statement that she “died forgotten in absolute poverty.” The evidence supports financial difficulty and declining public attention, but she was not continuously without property:

- She recovered Culcu after independence.
- She received a pension.
- She later acquired another hacienda.
- The pension was reduced, rather than simply never paid.

Her circumstances should therefore be described as those of a financially constrained veteran and land claimant, not automatically as literal lifelong destitution.

## 1848–1858: Robledo and reduced pension

**Documented or closely reported fact:** On **24 August 1848**, Juana acquired the hacienda **Robledo**.

This transaction complicates the starkest poverty legend. It does not prove prosperity: land could be debt-burdened, damaged, disputed, or produce little cash. No complete estate inventory, purchase price analysis, or long-term income account was found in the consulted material.

On **24 April 1858**, under President José María Linares’s economy measures affecting military pensions or *montepíos*, her pension was reduced to approximately **thirty pesos**. Some summaries say it was revoked in 1857; Torres’s chronology supports reduction in 1858. No evidence was found that this action specifically targeted her for political or gender reasons.

## 1859–1862: Final years and death

Juana spent her final years in Sucre, associated with a residence in the Coripata area and, in one reconstruction, at what became Avenida España 218.

The boy **Indalecio Sandi** is said to have lived with or been cared for by her and to have been present near the end. Later testimony attributed to Sandi helped shape the account of her last years.

She died on **25 May 1862**, the anniversary of the Chuquisaca and Buenos Aires revolutionary commemorations. Her parish death record states that she died at home, over eighty, widow of Colonel Padilla, after receiving Communion, and suffering “hinchazón.”

The story says Sandi sought military funeral honors but officials were occupied with independence celebrations and refused. A minimal religious burial costing one peso followed, and she was placed in a common grave. The death entry is documentary; the conversation with military officials is later reported testimony.

## Military Organization, Institutions, and Projects

## The Padilla insurgency or Republiqueta de La Laguna

Juana’s central institutional setting was not a modern standing regiment but a regional insurgent system based on:

- The Padilla household and estates.
- Rural Indigenous and mestizo communities.
- Local caudillo relationships.
- Auxiliary armies entering from the Río de la Plata.
- Captured arms, horses, food, and intelligence.
- Temporary command hierarchies adapted to guerrilla warfare.

Its territory covered much of central and eastern Chuquisaca, excluding some regions controlled by other commanders.

### Los Leales

Later institutional histories call Juana captain of the **Batallón Los Leales**. Claims that it reached **10,000 fighters** or that she personally commanded that number should be treated cautiously. Insurgent strength fluctuated, and later writers often combined all locally mobilizable supporters with actual combatants under immediate command.

### Las Amazonas

The name **Las Amazonas** is applied to Indigenous and mestiza women who fought with Azurduy. Women certainly participated in the independence wars as fighters, couriers, suppliers, spies, camp followers, and organizers. The existence of a permanently constituted all-women regiment with standardized strength is less securely demonstrated.

### Claims of thirty-three victories

The statement that Azurduy won **thirty-three battles** is common in governmental and commemorative literature. No audited list of thirty-three engagements attributable to her sole command was found. It is best labeled a commemorative statistic.

## Property, Wealth, and Financial Relationships

### Family wealth

Juana came from a family possessing rural property. She and Padilla were neither landless peasants nor members of the highest peninsular nobility. Their position is best described as **provincial landholding mestizo/criollo society**.

### Confiscation

After revolutionary reversals, royalist authorities confiscated or occupied Padilla family properties. Confiscation was both punitive and economically damaging, depriving the insurgency of a logistical base.

### Culcu/Cullcu

The property was restored by the republican government in August 1825. Recovery did not erase wartime losses, displacement, or deterioration.

### Robledo

Juana acquired Robledo in 1848. The acquisition is a significant corrective to claims that she had no assets whatsoever in later life.

### Pension

- Pension attributed to Bolívar/Sucre’s recognition: usually sixty pesos.
- Later reduced to approximately thirty pesos under José María Linares.
- Payment regularity and real purchasing power remain uncertain.
- “Pension revoked” and “pension reduced” are competing summaries; the dated 1858 reduction is better supported.

### Poverty at death

**Supported in qualified form:** She experienced financial strain and lacked the social and ceremonial treatment her rank might have warranted.

**Overstatement:** That she was entirely propertyless, wholly abandoned by the state from 1825 onward, or reduced continuously to begging is not established by the property and pension record.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political position

Azurduy’s political identity arose through war rather than a surviving theoretical corpus. Her actions show allegiance to:

- Resistance to restored Spanish royal government.
- Cooperation with the revolutionary authorities of the United Provinces.
- Local popular and Indigenous mobilization.
- The republican settlement represented by Bolívar and Sucre after 1825.

There is insufficient evidence to assign her a fully articulated later ideology—liberal, socialist, nationalist, feminist, or indigenist—in the modern senses.

### Indigenous relationships

Her mestiza classification, probable Quechua ability, rural upbringing, and dependence on Indigenous community networks were materially important. Later portrayals range from whitening her into a criolla aristocrat to presenting her as purely Indigenous. The baptismal record supports **mestiza**, while the exact balance of her ancestry remains genealogically reconstructed.

Descriptions of her as leading an Indigenous social revolution independent of the Buenos Aires armies may overstate her autonomy; descriptions of her merely accompanying her husband or obeying Argentine generals understate her operational leadership.

### Religion

She was baptized Catholic, married in the Church, had her children baptized, received Communion before death, and received a Catholic burial rite.

Claims that her image embodies Pachamama, an Andean earth-mother archetype, or an Indigenous sacred warrior belong to later symbolic interpretation. No evidence shows that she personally formulated such a religious identity.

### Gender

Her military activity violated conventional gender expectations, but later writers often made it acceptable by emphasizing:

- Her status as Padilla’s loyal wife.
- Her motherhood and bereavement.
- Exceptional bravery rather than collective female military participation.
- Marian, saintly, or sacrificial imagery.
- The “Amazon” archetype.

Modern feminist recovery emphasizes independent command and women’s public agency. Both older domesticating narratives and modern emancipatory narratives select from a fragmentary record.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Manuel Ascencio Padilla

Husband, political partner, fellow landholder, and insurgent commander. Their partnership was the central organizational unit of the La Laguna resistance until his death in 1816.

### Manuel Belgrano

Commander of the Army of the North. The Padillas cooperated with his army. His 26 July 1816 letter formally praised Juana’s capture of a royalist standard and supported military recognition.

### Juan Martín de Pueyrredón

As supreme director of the United Provinces, he approved her rank of lieutenant colonel following Belgrano’s recommendation. A personal meeting has not been established.

### Martín Miguel de Güemes

Azurduy joined or operated within Güemes’s northern defense system after Padilla’s death. Güemes provided her an institutional home during her exile in the south. Later accounts sometimes transfer Pueyrredón’s promotion decision to Güemes.

### Simón Bolívar

Met or received her in 1825 according to strong historical tradition and supported a pension. The famous “Bolivia should be called Padilla or Azurduy” statement is insufficiently authenticated in its familiar wording.

### Antonio José de Sucre

Associated with Bolívar’s reception and recognition of Azurduy in Chuquisaca. As the central military and political authority of early Bolivia, he formed part of the governmental network through which her pension and property restoration were handled.

### Juan José Castelli

The Padilla movement cooperated with the first Buenos Aires revolutionary army in Upper Peru. A documented personal meeting with Juana was not found.

### Juan Wallparrimachi/Huallparrimachi

Remembered as an Indigenous or mestizo poet and fighter associated with the Padilla forces. Much of his relationship with Juana was literary elaboration, especially in Lindaura Anzoátegui’s historical novels. His precise biography remains obscure.

### Francisco Javier de Aguilera

Royalist commander and principal military enemy in the 1816 destruction of the Padilla insurgent position. His forces defeated and killed Manuel Ascencio Padilla.

### Indalecio Sandi

A boy or young dependent associated with Juana’s final household. Later testimony credited to him is an important but retrospective source for the poverty and burial narrative.

## Controversies, Violence, and Evidentiary Questions

## Participation in political violence

Azurduy was an armed insurgent commander in a civil, imperial, and independence war marked by executions, mutilation, confiscation, reprisals, and attacks on civilians and combatants.

She organized or participated in ambushes, cavalry attacks, seizures of arms, and killings of royalist troops. Later heroic biographies openly depict her killing guards and opponents. The broad fact of lethal military activity is unquestionable; precise personal kill counts and choreographed duels are not documented.

Royalists treated the Padilla forces as rebels rather than lawful soldiers. Padilla’s beheading and the display of his head formed part of retaliatory terror. Insurgent recovery and display narratives must be read within the same violent political culture.

No evidence was found of a criminal prosecution in an independent civil court, a corruption investigation, fraud, money laundering, intelligence-service relationship in the modern sense, or private profiteering by Azurduy.

## The wrong birth date and parentage

This is the most important substantive biographical controversy.

- **Traditional claim:** 12 July 1780, Toroca; parents Matías Azurduy and Eulalia Bermúdez.
- **Documentary evidence:** 26 March baptism of a two-month-old Juana Asurdui, born in La Plata, daughter of Isidro Asurdui and Juliana Llanos; 1799 marriage record links this Juana to Padilla.
- **Assessment:** The traditional biography merged two women with similar names.
- **Continuing effect:** Laws, holidays, official pages, statues, school materials, and international commemorations remain tied to 12 July.

## Rank and promotion

- **Documented:** Belgrano’s commendation and Pueyrredón’s approval of lieutenant-colonel rank in 1816.
- **Disputed or inaccurate:** Claims that Güemes alone awarded the rank.
- **Chronologically doubtful:** A 1 April 1825 personal promotion by Bolívar.
- **Documented posthumously:** Argentine promotion to generala in 2009.

## The sword of Belgrano

- **Story:** Belgrano gave Azurduy his own sword after her valorous action.
- **Evidence:** Belgrano’s written commendation is real; the exact presentation story is not equally well demonstrated in the consulted documentation.
- **Assessment:** Reported tradition, not securely authenticated artifact history.

## Numbers of troops and victories

- “Ten thousand troops,” “thirty-three victories,” and a fixed “Amazonas” battalion recur in official commemoration.
- No complete contemporary muster roll or battle list substantiating those exact totals was located.
- These should be treated as commemorative claims derived from later synthesis.

## Poverty and abandonment

- **Supported:** Financial hardship, reduced pension, inadequate funeral honors, and burial in a common grave.
- **Counterevidence to the absolute version:** Restored land, pension, and purchase of Robledo in 1848.
- **Assessment:** Relative impoverishment and neglect are well supported; total lifelong destitution is an exaggeration.

## Identification of the remains

The state transferred remains identified as hers to the Casa de la Libertad in 1962. A century had elapsed since burial, reportedly in a common grave. No modern forensic identification was described in the consulted sources.

The remains are therefore best described as **officially identified and commemoratively accepted as hers**, not scientifically proven beyond doubt.

## Modern monument controversy

A large bronze monument by Andrés Zerneri, funded by Bolivia for approximately seven million bolivianos—widely reported as about US$1 million—was inaugurated behind Argentina’s Casa Rosada in July 2015 by Evo Morales and Cristina Fernández de Kirchner.

Controversies included:

- Removal and relocation of the pre-existing Christopher Columbus monument donated by the Italian community.
- Litigation and jurisdictional disputes between national and Buenos Aires city authorities.
- Criticism that the substitution instrumentalized history for Kirchnerist and decolonial politics.
- Counterclaims that replacing Columbus with Azurduy corrected a colonial and male-centered commemorative landscape.
- Construction delays and subsequent deterioration.
- A second relocation in 2017 because of the Paseo del Bajo works.

These disputes concerned modern public memory and expenditure; they do not constitute allegations of wrongdoing by the historical Azurduy.

## Dedicated adversarial search, including 2024–2026

Searches combining her name with controversy, scandal, accusations, lawsuits, fraud, corruption, conspiracy, criticism, and equivalent Spanish terms found:

- The identity and birth-date conflation.
- Criticism of embellished heroic biography.
- The Columbus/Azurduy monument dispute.
- Political disputes over her adoption by Bolivian and Argentine governments.
- Debate over whether modern feminist, nationalist, or decolonial narratives project categories onto a sparse record.
- Recent disputes involving institutions or welfare programs named after her, which have no personal connection to the historical Azurduy.

No credible evidence was found that Azurduy engaged in personal financial fraud, corruption, self-enrichment, espionage for a foreign power, or non-war criminal enterprise. Search results alleging misconduct generally concerned namesake institutions and were excluded from her personal record.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## The untamable convent girl

**Story:** Juana entered Santa Teresa intending to become a nun but was expelled after repeatedly defying the mother superior.

**Appearance and circulation:** Later biographies, children’s literature, popular histories, and dramatizations.

**Evidence:** No convent disciplinary record was located.

**Function:** Foreshadows her military rebellion and contrasts institutional femininity with freedom on horseback.

**Status:** Legend or unverified biographical tradition.

## Her father raised her “as a son”

**Story:** After a male child died, Isidro trained Juana in riding, estate management, and masculine pursuits because he needed a male heir.

**Evidence:** Plausible family-memory motif but not established by a contemporary statement.

**Effect:** Older narratives explained her military aptitude as the result of exceptional masculine upbringing; modern narratives use it to illustrate resistance to gender roles.

**Status:** Unverified tradition.

## Murder of her father

**Story:** Isidro was murdered by an unnamed aristocrat, sometimes because of a sexual rivalry.

**Evidence:** No criminal record or contemporaneous account was found.

**Status:** Rumor or biographical legend.

## Amazon commander

**Story:** Juana led an organized corps of women called Las Amazonas, sometimes hundreds strong.

**Evidence:** Women participated in her forces, and later military accounts acknowledge female combatants. Exact structure and numbers remain unproven.

**Effect:** Linked her to the ancient Amazon archetype and to Andean traditions of militant women.

**Status:** Documented female participation; disputed formal organization and scale.

## Fighting while pregnant and giving birth during battle

**Story:** During an attack, Juana withdrew briefly, gave birth to Luisa, and returned to command; another version has her escaping with the newborn through the Río Grande.

**Evidence:** No contemporaneous birth or battle report confirming the sequence was found. Dates and locations vary.

**Effect:** Became perhaps the strongest expression of the combined warrior-mother myth.

**Status:** Heroic legend based possibly on a real campaign-period birth.

## Digging her children’s graves

**Story:** After disease killed four children, Juana personally buried them and resumed fighting.

**Evidence:** Juliana’s death is documented; the precise grave-digging scene is later narrative.

**Effect:** Established bereaved motherhood as the moral price of independence.

**Status:** Literary or family-memory tradition around documented family losses.

## Recovery of Padilla’s severed head

**Story:** Juana led a raid, killed the guard, recovered her husband’s displayed head, and buried it.

**Evidence:** Padilla’s beheading is historically credible and well established in the tradition; the tactical details of recovery are less securely documented.

**Effect:** Presents Juana as avenger, widow, and successor.

**Status:** Reported historical tradition with legendary embellishment.

## Belgrano’s sword

**Story:** Belgrano removed his own sword and presented it to Juana.

**Evidence:** His written praise is documented; the ceremony and sword provenance are not fully established.

**Effect:** Materialized recognition from the canonical Argentine army and helped incorporate a Chuquisaca guerrilla into Argentina’s national pantheon.

**Status:** Reported tradition.

## Bolívar’s “Bolivia should be called Padilla or Azurduy”

**Story:** Bolívar told Sucre that Bolivia should bear the name Padilla or Azurduy because of what the couple had done for independence.

**Evidence:** Repeated in later biographies, speeches, and the Sanjinés film; no authenticated contemporaneous wording was located.

**Effect:** Authorizes Azurduy’s primacy through the voice of the continent’s best-known liberator.

**Status:** Famous but unverified quotation.

## Thirty-three battles and ten thousand soldiers

**Story:** Juana won thirty-three battles and commanded 10,000 troops.

**Evidence:** No contemporaneous consolidated roster or engagement list was found.

**Effect:** Converts dispersed guerrilla service into numerical heroic credentials.

**Status:** Commemorative statistic.

## Death in “absolute poverty and total oblivion”

**Story:** She was wholly abandoned, penniless, and unknown until dying alone.

**Evidence for:** Reduced pension, modest final circumstances, minimal funeral, and common grave.

**Evidence against literal form:** She regained Culcu, received a pension, purchased Robledo in 1848, and was known locally by people who transmitted her story.

**Status:** Substantially based on hardship but rhetorically exaggerated.

## Amazon, saint, Pachamama, and feminist ancestor

Heather Hennes identifies several currents in the evolving legend:

- The European and American Amazon-warrior tradition.
- Armed female saints, especially the Joan of Arc pattern.
- Maternal and Marian sacrifice.
- Association of woman and Bolivian land.
- Pachamama symbolism.
- Modern feminist recovery.

These are not claims about Azurduy’s self-understanding. They are documented interpretations through which later societies made her military transgression culturally intelligible.

## Works by or Attributed to Juana Azurduy

Juana Azurduy was not known as a literary author, theorist, or memoirist. No book, diary, manifesto, or sustained first-person autobiography by her is known.

### Surviving documentary corpus

Her attributable output consists principally of:

- Military communications.
- Petitions to revolutionary or republican authorities.
- Claims for pension, pay, recognition, travel assistance, or restoration of property.
- Administrative and property documents.
- Possible dictated letters.

Documents written by clerks on her behalf should not automatically be treated as autograph compositions. A definitive scholarly edition of all her correspondence was not located.

### Important documents concerning rather than authored by her

- Manuel Belgrano’s commendation, Tucumán, 26 July 1816; Archivo General de la Nación, Sala X, legajo 23-2-3.
- Pueyrredón-era documentation approving the rank of lieutenant colonel.
- Baptismal record, San Pedro de Tarabuco, 26 March 1780.
- Marriage record, San Miguel de Moromoro, 19 May 1799.
- Children’s baptismal and death records.
- Orders restoring Culcu in August 1825.
- Pension and *montepío* records.
- Robledo acquisition record, 24 August 1848.
- Parish death entry, 25 May 1862.

No evidence supports attributing the famous song “Juana Azurduy” to her; it was written by Félix Luna and Ariel Ramírez more than a century after her death.

## Books and Major Works About Juana Azurduy

### Early biographical and commemorative writing

- Samuel Velasco Flor, early biographical account of Juana Azurduy, published in or around **1871**. This is commonly identified as the first extended biography and helped establish the erroneous 8 March 1781 birth tradition in one historiographical strand.
- Joaquín Gantier, *Doña Juana Azurduy de Padilla* (**1946**). Foundational modern biography, influential but now superseded on key identity and birth questions.
- Norberto Benjamín Torres, *Juana Asurdui de Padilla (1780–1862): la historia detrás de la leyenda* (**2018**). Documentary revision reproducing or identifying parish and administrative records.
- Patricia Fernández de Aponte, *Juana Azurduy de Padilla: La Generala*. Sympathetic biographical study produced within Bolivian commemorative historiography.
- Berta Catalina Wexler, *Juana Azurduy Llanos y las mujeres del ejército revolucionario*; and “Juana Azurduy, la flor del Alto Perú: el rol de las mujeres en las guerras por la independencia (1808–1825),” *Todo es Historia*, no. 538 (**May 2012**).
- Pacho O’Donnell, *Juana Azurduy: la teniente coronela*. Popular, partly novelized biography; useful for reception but not a substitute for documentary verification.
- Mercedes P. de Goyeneche and other Argentine “patricias” collections include Azurduy in broader commemorative histories; exact editions vary.

### Historical fiction central to the legend

Lindaura Anzoátegui de Campero’s trilogy helped define the late-nineteenth-century literary Azurduy:

- *Huallparrimachi* (**1892 or 1894**, depending on edition/catalogue).
- *En el año 1815* (**1895**).
- *Manuel Ascencio Padilla*, written as part of the trilogy but published posthumously or much later; one scholarly catalogue gives **1976**.

These are historical novels, not eyewitness sources. They shaped later descriptions of Azurduy, Wallparrimachi, Padilla, and the emotional world of the insurgency.

### Academic studies of memory and representation

- Heather Hennes, “Corrientes culturales en la leyenda de Juana Azurduy de Padilla,” *Cuadernos Americanos* 2, no. 132 (**2010**), pp. 93–115.
- Martín F. Yriart, “Juana Azurduy: de la mujer histórica al mito popular,” Instituto Cervantes.
- Studies of Anzoátegui’s Azurduy, including “Azurduy por Anzoátegui: subjetividad femenina y espacio público en *Huallparrimachi*.”
- “Con serena dignidad: representación de Juana Azurduy de Padilla en la obra *En el año 1815*.”
- “El español no pasará, con mujeres tendrá que pelear: representaciones de Juana Azurduy en la literatura argentina para las infancias,” *Orbis Tertius*.
- Marina Drehmer and Manuela Bergamin Neves, “Monumento à Juana Azurduy: o epicentro de um debate sobre memória e agenciamentos do passado,” *Epígrafe* 13, no. 2, pp. 307–340.
- “Juana Azurduy e os embates da história,” *Discursividades*.
- Research by Bolivian historians Hugo Canedo Gutiérrez, Fernando Suárez Saavedra, Norberto Benjamín Torres, William Lofstrom, Mario Castro, and others on the baptismal and identity problem.

### Broader military histories

Important contextual sources include:

- Bartolomé Mitre, *Historia de Belgrano y de la independencia argentina*.
- Andrés García Camba, *Memorias para la historia de las armas españolas en el Perú*.
- Studies of the Army of the North, the Upper Peruvian insurgencies, and the so-called Republiquetas.
- Archival collections of Manuel Belgrano’s correspondence and the Argentine Archivo General de la Nación.

These works must be read critically: Mitre systematized the “republiquetas” from a later Argentine national perspective, while García Camba wrote from the royalist military side.

## Films, Music, Television, and Archival Material

### Film

**Jorge Sanjinés, director, *Juana Azurduy, guerrillera de la Patria Grande* (Bolivia, 2016).**

The film combines historical reconstruction, popular memory, and Sanjinés’s political cinema. It dramatizes Bolívar’s encounter with Azurduy and repeats the statement that the new nation should have borne her or Padilla’s name. It is a major work of memory, not a primary historical source.

### Unrealized or reported screen project

A biographical project sometimes called *Pimienta roja*, linked in entertainment reporting to Natalia Oreiro, was publicized but did not become the definitive major feature associated with Azurduy. It generated criticism concerning casting and the representation of her mestiza/Indigenous ancestry.

### Television and educational productions

- Argentine educational programming, including Pakapaka’s *La asombrosa excursión de Zamba*, has presented Azurduy to children.
- Argentine and Bolivian public broadcasters have shown the Sanjinés film and anniversary programming.
- Government educational portals maintain portraits, capsule biographies, and teaching materials, many of which retain the superseded 12 July identity.

### Music

**“Juana Azurduy”**, lyrics by **Félix Luna**, music by **Ariel Ramírez**, issued on *Mujeres Argentinas* (**1969**) and famously performed by **Mercedes Sosa**.

The song popularized “flor del Alto Perú” and the image of a captain braver than male commanders. It became one of the principal vehicles of her Argentine popular canonization.

### Archives and museum collections

- Archivo y Biblioteca Arquidiocesanos “Monseñor Miguel de los Santos Taborga,” Sucre: baptismal and parish records.
- Archivo General de la Nación, Argentina: Belgrano correspondence and Army of the North documentation.
- Archivo y Biblioteca Nacionales de Bolivia: state, commemorative, property, and monument collections.
- Casa de la Libertad, Sucre: official repository of the remains attributed to her and associated commemorative objects.
- Sociedad Geográfica y de Historia “Sucre”: local historical and documentary work.
- Biblioteca Esteban Echeverría, Buenos Aires Legislature: Juana Azurduy collection of facsimiles, correspondence, proclamations, and biographical works.

## Death and Burial

**Documented fact:** Juana died in Sucre on 25 May 1862, at more than eighty years of age, widow of Colonel Padilla.

The parish entry states that she:

- Died at home.
- Received Communion.
- Suffered from “hinchazón.”
- Was a resident of the parish.

“Hinchazón” could reflect cardiac, renal, hepatic, infectious, or nutritional disease, but no diagnosis can responsibly be assigned.

The one-peso burial and common-grave story is plausible and supported by later historical reconstruction. Claims that every official consciously chose to dishonor her are harder to prove; the reported explanation was that public authorities were occupied with 25 May festivities.

In 1962, centenary commemorations transformed her burial status:

- Bolivia declared or reaffirmed her as a national heroine.
- She received posthumous military rank.
- Remains officially accepted as hers were moved to a mausoleum in the Casa de la Libertad.
- A century of relative neglect was converted into state cult.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Nineteenth century

Her memory survived through family descendants, local oral history, veterans, early historians, and historical fiction. Samuel Velasco Flor and Lindaura Anzoátegui were important in converting fragmented memory into biography and national romance.

### Twentieth century

Joaquín Gantier’s biography consolidated a heroic life story, including genealogical and birth claims now disputed.

The centenary of her death in 1962 produced:

- Exhumation and ceremonial reburial.
- Bolivian state recognition.
- Military promotion.
- Monument and museum activity.

The 1969 Luna–Ramírez song, especially through Mercedes Sosa, gave Azurduy a transnational popular identity.

### Twenty-first century

Azurduy became a state symbol in both Bolivia and Argentina:

- Bolivia created the **Bono Juana Azurduy**, a maternal and child health conditional-transfer program, by Supreme Decree 0066 in 2009.
- Argentina promoted her posthumously to generala under Decree 892/2009.
- Cristina Fernández de Kirchner presented military insignia at the Casa de la Libertad in 2010.
- Argentina and Bolivia associated 12 July with bilateral fraternity, although the date is now historically disputed.
- A large monument funded by Bolivia was installed in Buenos Aires in 2015 and moved in 2017.
- Schools, military units, airports, provinces, towns, women’s organizations, political groups, streets, and welfare programs bear her name.

### Competing modern appropriations

Her image is used in several partially competing frameworks:

- Argentine national independence.
- Bolivian national independence.
- Pan–Latin American or “Patria Grande” unity.
- Indigenous and mestizo resistance.
- Feminist recovery of women’s military agency.
- State military commemoration.
- Left-nationalist and decolonial symbolism.
- Criticism of politicized rewriting of national pantheons.

These appropriations concern later memory. None alone exhausts the historical person.

## Atlas Connections

Only documented or strongly reported intersections with persons on the supplied atlas roster are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Manuel Belgrano — documented fact

Azurduy and Manuel Ascencio Padilla cooperated with Belgrano’s Army of the North during its Upper Peruvian campaigns. Belgrano’s letter from Tucumán dated **26 July 1816** praised her role in capturing a royalist standard near El Villar and supported her promotion. The personal sword-presentation story is reported but less securely documented.

### Juan Martín de Pueyrredón y O’Doggan — documented administrative connection

As supreme director of the United Provinces, Pueyrredón approved Azurduy’s promotion to **teniente coronel** in 1816 following Belgrano’s recommendation. No personal meeting was established.

### Martín Miguel de Güemes — reported fact

After Manuel Ascencio Padilla’s death and the weakening of the La Laguna insurgency, Azurduy moved south and served within Güemes’s defensive system in Salta, approximately **1817–1821**. Later accounts attribute a uniform and other recognition to Güemes. The claim that he originally conferred her lieutenant-colonelcy conflicts with the Pueyrredón documentation.

### Simón Bolívar — reported fact; quotation disputed

Bolívar reportedly met Azurduy in Chuquisaca in **1825** and supported a pension recognizing her service. The quotation that Bolivia ought to have been called Padilla or Azurduy is famous but was not verified in a contemporary document.

### Antonio José de Sucre — reported fact

Sucre was associated with Bolívar’s 1825 recognition of Azurduy and with the early Bolivian governmental framework that provided her pension and restored property. A direct meeting is strongly reported, although the exact dialogue is not securely recorded.

### Micaela Bastidas Puyucahua and José Gabriel Condorcanqui Noguera — documented regional influence, not personal connection

Azurduy was born during the great Andean uprising led by Túpac Amaru II and Micaela Bastidas, and later historiography places her political formation in the afterlife of those revolts. No meeting was possible or documented: both were executed in 1781, when she was an infant. Their inclusion is therefore limited to documented historical influence on the regional political world, not a personal relationship.

### Manuela Sáenz de Vergara y Aizpuru — no documented personal crossing found

Both became female icons of South American independence and were connected to Bolívar’s political world, but no meeting, correspondence, or collaboration between them was found.

### José de San Martín — no documented personal crossing found

Azurduy’s service formed part of the broader independence struggle in which San Martín was a principal commander, but no reliable source consulted documents a meeting, correspondence, or direct command relationship.

### María Remedios del Valle — no documented personal crossing found

Both were women associated with the Army of the North and later received renewed Argentine commemoration. The consulted material did not establish a meeting or shared unit at the same place and date.

### Remaining atlas roster

No defensible direct connection to the other roster members was found. Shared revolutionary symbolism, later comparison, residence within the same former viceroyalty, or appearance in the same modern commemorative program was not treated as a personal connection.
