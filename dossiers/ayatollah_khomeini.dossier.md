# Ruhollah Mostafavi Musavi Khomeini: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs the life, writings, government, controversies, mythology, and afterlife of Ruhollah Khomeini from published primary documents, contemporary reporting, academic research, institutional records, and human-rights investigations consulted through 2 September 2026.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a primary document, contemporaneous record, or multiple strong sources.
- **Reported fact:** credibly reported but not independently established here from a primary record.
- **Allegation:** a claim by an identified party for which proof remains incomplete.
- **Disputed claim:** competing accounts or genuine evidentiary uncertainty exist.
- **Rumor or myth:** culturally or politically consequential but unproved.
- **Institutional finding:** conclusion of a government, court, international organization, or documented investigation.
- **Inference:** conclusion drawn from documented circumstances, identified as such.

Names and Persian or Arabic titles are transliterated in simplified form unless exact scholarly transliteration is useful.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Sayyed Ruhollah Mostafavi Musavi Khomeini  
**Persian:** سید روح‌الله مصطفوی موسوی خمینی  
**Common forms:** Ruhollah Khomeini; Rouhollah Khomeyni; Ayatollah Khomeini; Imam Khomeini  
**Birth surname:** Mostafavi, assigned under Iran’s surname system; he ordinarily became known by the nisba “Khomeini,” meaning “of Khomein.”  
**Religious lineage:** “Musavi” asserted descent through Musa al-Kazim, the seventh imam of Twelver Shiism; “Sayyed” likewise designated claimed descent from Muhammad.  
**Poetic pen name:** “Hendi” or “Hindi,” referring to the family’s Indian connection.  
**Titles:** Ayatollah; Grand Ayatollah; marjaʿ-e taqlid (“source of emulation”); faqih; Leader of the Revolution; Founder of the Islamic Republic; first Supreme Leader of Iran.  
**“Imam”:** In Iranian revolutionary usage, not a claim that he was one of the Twelve Imams. The designation appeared in a 1970 poem by Neʿmat Azarm, became a revolutionary slogan, and was institutionalized in the 1979 constitution’s preamble.

**Best-supported birth date:** 24 September 1902, corresponding to 20 Jumada II 1320 AH, in Khomein, then in Lorestan and now in Markazi Province, Iran. The date deliberately coincided in commemorative culture with the traditional birthday of Fatima, Muhammad’s daughter.

**Birth-date discrepancy:** His civil identity record has also been reported as giving 17 May 1900. His elder brother Mortaza Pasandideh and the principal detailed biographical reconstruction support 24 September 1902. The reason for the discrepancy is not conclusively established.

**Death:** Shortly before midnight on 3 June 1989 in or near Jamaran, northern Tehran. A frequently reproduced precise time is 22:20 Iran time.

**Cause of death:** Heart failure following surgery for gastrointestinal bleeding associated with stomach cancer. His daughter Zahra Mostafavi reported five heart attacks in the ten days after surgery on 23 May.

**Citizenship and nationality:** Iranian.

**Religion:** Twelver Shiʿa Islam.

**Principal roles:**

- Senior teacher and jurist in Qom and Najaf.
- Leading opponent of Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi from 1962.
- Exile in Turkey, Iraq, and France, 1964–79.
- Revolutionary leader, 1978–79.
- Supreme Leader of the Islamic Republic, 1979–89.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal ancestry

**Documented/reconstructed account:** Khomeini’s paternal ancestors were sayyed religious scholars associated originally with Nishapur. A branch settled in Kintur near Lucknow in northern India during the eighteenth century. They combined religious scholarship with land cultivation.

His grandfather, **Sayyed Ahmad Musavi Hindi**, left India in the nineteenth century, traveled to Najaf, and there met Yusuf Khan Kamaraʾi of Khomein. Yusuf Khan invited him to serve Khomein’s Shiʿa population. In 1841 Ahmad married Yusuf Khan’s sister, Sakina Khanom. Their son **Sayyed Mostafa**, born in 1856, was Khomeini’s father.

**Competing family account:** Mortaza Pasandideh described the family connection as Kashmiri rather than specifically Kinturi/Lucknow. Migration from Kashmir into the Shiʿa scholarly milieu of Lucknow makes the two traditions potentially compatible, but the exact route is unresolved.

### Parents

**Father:** Sayyed Mostafa Musavi, a cleric, local religious judge, farmer, and substantial landholder. He studied in Khomein, Isfahan, and the Iraqi shrine cities, including under Mirza Hasan Shirazi.

He was murdered on the Khomein–Arak road in March 1903, approximately five months after Ruhollah’s birth. The killer was identified as Jaʿfar-Qoli Khan, connected to a powerful landowning family. After persistent petitions by the family, he was publicly executed in Tehran in May 1905.

Three explanations circulated for the murder:

1. **Post-revolutionary official narrative:** Mostafa was killed for defending poor peasants against landlords.
2. **Historical possibility:** It arose from a dispute involving land or irrigation rights.
3. **Family account attributed to Ahmad Khomeini:** It was revenge connected with Mostafa’s actions as a sharia judge.

The motive cannot be established definitively. The first version became part of revolutionary hagiography.

**Mother:** Hajiyeh Khanom, daughter of Mirza Ahmad Mojtahed Khonsari. She died in 1918.

**Paternal aunt and guardian:** Sahebeh Khanom helped raise Ruhollah after his father’s death and also died in 1918.

### Siblings

The principal male siblings were:

- **Sayyed Mortaza Pasandideh** (1896–1996), cleric, teacher, guardian, and later memoirist. He oversaw Ruhollah’s early education.
- **Nur al-Din Hendi**, who did not pursue a comparable clerical career and died in Tehran in 1976.
- Family reconstructions also record sisters, but published accounts differ on names and total siblings.

When surnames became mandatory in 1925, the brothers adopted different forms: Pasandideh, Hendi, and Mostafavi.

### Marriage

In 1929 or 1930 Khomeini married **Khadijeh Saqafi**, also called Batul or Qods-e Iran, daughter of the Tehran cleric and merchant Hajj Mirza Mohammad Thaqafi Tehrani. Abu’l-Qasem Kashani or, in another account, Mohammad Lavasani arranged the introduction.

Her exact age at marriage is disputed in secondary accounts, which variously place her in her early-to-middle teens. The marriage continued until Khomeini’s death. Private correspondence, including a 1932 letter from Beirut, shows a markedly affectionate relationship inconsistent with the wholly austere public image later constructed around him.

### Children

Sources differ between seven and eight births, apparently because of children who died in infancy. Five survived to adulthood:

- **Mostafa Khomeini**, born December 1930; cleric and political aide; died suddenly in Najaf on 23 October 1977.
- **Sediqeh Mostafavi**, married Shahab al-Din Eshraqi.
- **Farideh Mostafavi**, married Mohammad Hasan Aʿrabi.
- **Zahra/Fahimeh Mostafavi**, married Mahmoud Borujerdi; later politically and academically active.
- **Ahmad Khomeini**, born 1946; his father’s aide, gatekeeper, and political intermediary; died in 1995.

Children reported to have died young include:

- **Ali**, born in or before 1932 and dead before Khomeini returned from the Hajj.
- **Saʿideh**, reportedly dead at about seven months.
- **Latifeh**, reportedly dead in childhood.

### Descendants and dynastic network

Khomeini’s children married into clerical and bazaar-connected families. Ahmad’s son **Hassan Khomeini** became custodian of Khomeini’s mausoleum and a public cleric. The family’s name, archives, shrine administration, and Institute for the Compilation and Publication of Imam Khomeini’s Works became lasting sources of symbolic and institutional influence.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1902–20

Khomeini grew up in a prosperous clerical-landowning household rather than the materially destitute background sometimes suggested by revolutionary imagery. His father’s estate apparently secured the family after the murder.

Khomein remained vulnerable to raids, local feuds, and weak central authority. Khomeini later recalled taking up a rifle with his brothers when a Bakhtiari force raided the town. His statement that he had been “engaged in struggle” since childhood became part memory, part political self-narration.

At approximately seven he memorized the Qurʾan at a local maktab run by Molla Abu’l-Qasem. He studied Arabic with Shaykh Jaʿfar, a maternal relative, and other subjects with Mirza Mahmud Eftekhar al-ʿOlama, his maternal uncle Hajj Mirza Mohammad Mehdi, and Mirza Mohammad Najafi. Mortaza Pasandideh taught him logic, rhetoric, and introductory theology.

The deaths of his mother and guardian-aunt in 1918 consolidated the elder brother’s role in his life.

## Education and Formation

### Arak, 1920–22

In 1920 or 1921 Pasandideh sent him to Arak, an expanding center of Shiʿa learning under **Abd al-Karim Haʾeri Yazdi**. Khomeini studied logic under Mohammad-Reza Golpaygani, jurisprudence with Aqa Abbas Araki, and rhetoric with Mohammad Borujerdi.

### Qom, from 1922 or 1923

When Haʾeri moved to Qom and rebuilt its seminary, Khomeini followed after several months. He lived at the Dar al-Shafa madrasa and completed the advanced textual curriculum by about 1926.

Teachers included:

- Abd al-Karim Haʾeri Yazdi: jurisprudence and legal methodology.
- Mirza Sayyed Ali Yathrebi Kashani.
- Mirza Mohammad-Taqi Khonsari.
- Mohammad-Reza Najafi Masjed-e Shahi.
- Mirza Ali-Akbar Hakim Yazdi.
- Abu’l-Hasan Rafiʿi Qazvini: philosophy and mysticism.
- Javad Aqa Maleki Tabrizi: ethics and spiritual discipline.
- **Mirza Mohammad-Ali Shahabadi:** Khomeini’s most important teacher of theoretical mysticism.

With Shahabadi he studied Ibn Arabi’s *Fusus al-Hikam*, Qaysari’s commentary, and related metaphysical texts. He studied the philosophical tradition of Mulla Sadra and Islamic Neoplatonism, subjects viewed suspiciously by many jurists in Qom. Khomeini later recalled that some clerics considered philosophy and ʿirfan contaminating; one hostile anecdote held that a drinking glass used by his son was treated as ritually impure because of Khomeini’s philosophical interests.

### Formative intellectual strands

Khomeini’s mature thought combined:

- Usuli Twelver jurisprudence.
- Ibn Arabi-influenced theoretical mysticism.
- Mulla Sadra’s transcendent philosophy.
- Shiʿa martyrdom symbolism, especially Karbala.
- The political example of Mirza Hasan Shirazi’s tobacco fatwa.
- The constitutional and anti-autocratic example of **Hasan Modarres**, whom Khomeini visited in Tehran.
- Anti-imperialism and hostility to foreign legal privileges.
- A gradually radicalized doctrine of clerical guardianship.

His early writings were more mystical and ethical than political. Politics increasingly entered his published work during the 1940s and became dominant after 1962.

## Early Career, Teaching, and Pre-Revolutionary Politics

Khomeini began teaching philosophy, ethics, mysticism, jurisprudence, and legal principles in Qom during the late 1920s. His classes created a cadre of students who later staffed the Islamic Republic, among them Mortaza Motahhari, Hossein-Ali Montazeri, Mohammad Beheshti, Ali Khamenei, Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani, and others, though the duration and closeness of individual teacher-student relationships varied.

In March 1932 he traveled through Najaf and Beirut to perform the Hajj. His son Ali died while he was away.

He witnessed Reza Shah’s coercive secularization, dress reforms, repression of clerics, and the 1935 violence at the Goharshad Mosque. These events strengthened his hostility to Pahlavi state-building.

### First political texts

In May 1944 he issued a public appeal invoking Qurʾan 34:46 and calling on Muslims to “rise up for God.”

In 1945 he anonymously published *Kashf al-Asrar* (“Unveiling of Secrets”), written against Ali-Akbar Hakamizadeh’s anti-clerical *Asrar-e Hezar Saleh*. It defended Shiʿa institutions, attacked Reza Shah and Ahmad Kasravi, and sketched a system in which qualified jurists would select and supervise a just ruler. This was not yet identical to his later doctrine of direct rule by the jurist.

Khomeini supported strengthening the Qom seminary under Grand Ayatollah Hossein Borujerdi. He generally avoided an open political challenge while Borujerdi lived, though he cultivated connections with:

- **Abol-Qasem Kashani**, clerical nationalist and Mosaddegh-era political actor.
- **Navvab Safavi**, founder of the militant Fadaʾiyan-e Islam, whose members assassinated intellectual and political opponents.
- Religious activists and bazaar networks later forming the Islamic Coalition Associations.

There is no conclusive evidence that Khomeini ordered Fadaʾiyan-e Islam’s assassinations. His cordial contact with Safavi and shared hostility toward secularism are documented.

After Borujerdi’s death in March 1961, Khomeini allowed publication of his legal rulings and emerged as one of several marajiʿ in Qom.

## Chronological Life History

### 1962–63: Open confrontation

In 1962 Khomeini mobilized against new provincial-council rules eliminating an explicit Qurʾanic oath. He portrayed the change as opening state offices to Bahaʾis. The government withdrew the regulations after seven weeks.

In January 1963 he opposed the shah’s “White Revolution,” which included land redistribution, industrial privatization, rural literacy programs, and women’s suffrage. His objections combined constitutional, clerical, anti-imperialist, and religious arguments. Opposition to women’s enfranchisement was an explicit component and should not be obscured by later claims that his objection concerned only the shah’s procedure.

On 22 March security forces attacked the Fayziyeh seminary. Khomeini compared it with the Goharshad repression.

On 3 June, during Ashura, he delivered a speech attacking Mohammad Reza Shah’s relationship with the United States and Israel. He was arrested early on 5 June. Demonstrations—the **15 Khordad uprising**—were suppressed with lethal force. Exact deaths remain disputed, ranging from government-minimized figures to much larger opposition claims.

He was transferred from prison to house arrest and released in April 1964.

### 1964–65: Capitulations dispute and Turkey

In October 1964 Khomeini denounced legislation extending diplomatic-style immunity to American military personnel and dependents. On 4 November commandos seized him in Qom and deported him to Turkey.

He stayed first in Ankara’s Bulvar Palas Hotel, then apartments, and from 12 November in Bursa under Turkish security supervision. Mostafa joined him in January 1965. He completed much of *Tahrir al-Wasila* there.

### 1965–78: Najaf exile

On 6 October 1965 he moved to Iraq, passing through Kazimayn, Samarra, and Karbala before settling in Najaf. He taught jurisprudence at the Shaykh Murtaza Ansari madrasa.

He maintained cautious relations with senior Iraqi clerics, including Abu’l-Qasem Khoʾi and Mohsen Hakim. He refused Iraqi Baʿathist requests to serve as an anti-shah instrument, reportedly telling officials that Iraq practiced the same repression it condemned in Iran.

Messages, cassette recordings, pilgrims, students, merchants, and clerical networks carried his teachings into Iran.

In lectures delivered in Najaf in January–February 1970 and published as *Hokumat-e Islami* or *Velayat-e Faqih*, he argued that Islamic law required government and that qualified jurists should exercise political guardianship. This represented a significant expansion from supervisory clerical authority to direct clerical rule.

On 23 October 1977 his eldest son Mostafa died suddenly in Najaf.

- **Documented fact:** Death was attributed officially to a heart attack.
- **Allegation:** Followers claimed SAVAK had poisoned or otherwise killed him.
- **Assessment:** Suspicion was widespread and politically catalytic, but publicly available evidence does not establish assassination. Memorial ceremonies for Mostafa became anti-shah demonstrations.

On 7 January 1978 *Ettelaʿat* printed a pseudonymous attack portraying Khomeini as a foreign-connected reactionary. Protests and lethal repression in Qom initiated a cycle of forty-day commemorations, demonstrations, strikes, and further deaths.

### 1978–79: Iraq to France and revolution

Under Iranian pressure, Iraq restricted Khomeini. Kuwait denied him entry. On 12 October 1978 he arrived in France with Ahmad Khomeini and Ebrahim Yazdi. After briefly staying with Abolhassan Bani-Sadr in Cachan, he settled at Neauphle-le-Château.

France provided telephone, press, and international broadcast access unavailable in Najaf. Khomeini gave frequent interviews and received opposition delegations. His public coalition included clerics, bazaar networks, liberals, nationalists, Marxists, Islamists, students, and sections of the urban poor and middle classes.

His statements in France sometimes emphasized popular sovereignty, freedom, and a limited personal role. After victory, the political system concentrated considerably greater authority in the office he occupied. Supporters interpret the statements as contextually consistent with an Islamic definition of freedom; critics treat them as strategic ambiguity or deception. Intent is not independently provable.

The shah left Iran on 16 January 1979. Khomeini returned on 1 February aboard an Air France flight. He went to Behesht-e Zahra cemetery and declared Shapur Bakhtiar’s government illegitimate.

He appointed **Mehdi Bazargan** prime minister of a provisional government on 5 February. Armed conflict and military neutrality ended the monarchy on 11 February.

### 1979: Construction of the Islamic Republic

A referendum on 30–31 March asked voters to choose an “Islamic Republic: yes or no”; official results recorded more than 98 percent approval. No alternative republican or constitutional models appeared on the ballot.

Revolutionary courts under judges including Sadegh Khalkhali executed former officials, generals, security personnel, alleged torturers, drug offenders, and people convicted of sexual offenses.

- **Documented fact:** Proceedings were often rapid and lacked ordinary safeguards, defense counsel, or meaningful appeal.
- **Documented fact:** Khomeini authorized revolutionary justice and appointed Khalkhali.
- **Qualification:** In May 1979 Khomeini ordered that capital punishment be restricted to persons proved responsible for killing or torture, but executions continued and expanded into other categories.

The former prime minister Amir-Abbas Hoveyda was executed in April. Jewish businessman **Habib Elghanian** was executed in May after conviction for alleged espionage and Zionist ties, causing alarm among Iranian Jews. Two men convicted of homosexual acts and another of rape were executed later that month.

In March Khomeini endorsed Islamic dress for women in government workplaces. Large women’s demonstrations opposed compulsory veiling. Enforcement became progressively institutionalized, and the pre-revolutionary Family Protection Law was dismantled.

A first constitutional draft lacked the later Supreme Leader’s extensive powers. The elected Assembly of Experts, dominated by clerics, rewrote it to incorporate *velayat-e faqih*. The December constitution gave the leader supreme command, judicial appointment powers, authority over presidential validation or dismissal, and influence through the Guardian Council.

### Hostage crisis, 1979–81

On 4 November 1979 militant students seized the United States embassy and took American diplomats and staff hostage. Khomeini endorsed the seizure as a “second revolution,” transforming a student action into state policy. Bazargan resigned.

The crisis lasted 444 days. The hostages were released on 20 January 1981 under the Algiers Accords. Khomeini’s endorsement helped marginalize liberals, consolidate radical institutions, and establish confrontation with the United States as a central political identity.

The April 1980 American rescue attempt failed at Desert One near Tabas, killing eight U.S. personnel. Iranian revolutionary narratives described the sandstorm as divine intervention.

### Cultural Revolution and repression, 1980–83

Beginning in 1980 universities were closed for approximately two years and faculty and students deemed insufficiently Islamic, leftist, or “Westoxicated” were purged. Khomeini supported transforming education according to Islamic revolutionary principles.

Opposition moved into escalating confrontation:

- President Abolhassan Bani-Sadr was impeached in June 1981 and fled with MEK leader Massoud Rajavi.
- The MEK and allied groups initiated armed attacks and assassinations.
- A June 1981 bombing killed Mohammad Beheshti and scores of Islamic Republican Party officials.
- President Mohammad-Ali Rajaʾi and Prime Minister Mohammad-Javad Bahonar were assassinated in August.
- The state arrested and executed thousands of alleged MEK members, leftists, and other opponents, frequently after summary proceedings.

Responsibility for specific prison abuses was dispersed across prosecutors, judges, intelligence officials, guards, and political leaders. Khomeini was the constitutional and revolutionary leader, publicly demanded severity toward armed opponents, and defended the system. Not every individual execution is traceable to a personal order.

### Religious and ethnic minorities

The new constitution recognized Jews, Christians, and Zoroastrians but not the Bahaʾi faith. Bahaʾi national and local bodies were dismantled; leaders disappeared or were executed; property was seized; and adherents were excluded from education and employment.

A 1982 United Nations document recorded 81 Bahaʾis killed since the revolution and noted that courts often framed executions as espionage, Zionism, opposition to Islam, or moral offenses. Iranian officials denied that Bahaʾis were executed because of religion. Documentary patterns support institutional religious persecution even when formal charges used political terminology.

Kurdish, Turkmen, and Arab regions saw armed conflict, military campaigns, executions, and competing atrocities by government and insurgent forces.

### Iran–Iraq War, 1980–88

Iraq invaded Iran on 22 September 1980. Khomeini rejected capitulation and mobilized the regular military, Revolutionary Guards, and Basij. Iran recovered most occupied territory by mid-1982.

The decision to continue the war after Iraqi forces had largely been expelled is one of the central controversies of Khomeini’s rule. He sought Saddam Hussein’s overthrow, reparations, and an Islamic political transformation in Iraq. Iranian offensives caused enormous losses without achieving those aims. Iraq used chemical weapons; cities and civilian shipping were attacked by both sides.

Youth and sometimes underage Basij volunteers participated in highly dangerous assaults and mine-clearing operations. “Human-wave” is an imprecise but historically embedded description; the scale, tactics, coercion, and age distribution differed by operation. Revolutionary culture explicitly glorified martyrdom.

Khomeini finally accepted United Nations Security Council Resolution 598 in July 1988, comparing the decision to drinking a “chalice of poison.” The war ended without regime change in Iraq or a decisive Iranian victory.

### Iran–Contra affair, 1985–87

Iranian intermediaries and officials secretly negotiated U.S. and Israeli arms deliveries while Iran publicly denounced both states. The Reagan administration hoped to obtain help releasing American hostages held by Iran-linked groups in Lebanon. U.S. officials diverted some proceeds to Nicaragua’s Contras.

**Documented fact:** Arms were transferred to Iran, and Robert McFarlane secretly visited Tehran in 1986. Oliver North coordinated major aspects of the U.S. operation.

**Unresolved at Khomeini’s personal level:** Evidence establishes Iranian state participation but not how much operational detail Khomeini personally knew. His son Ahmad and senior figures operated within the governing structure.

The exposure of the affair aggravated conflict around Hossein-Ali Montazeri because Montazeri-connected Mehdi Hashemi publicized it. Hashemi was later executed after conviction on charges extending beyond the leak.

### 1988 prison massacres

After the ceasefire decision and the MEK’s Iraqi-backed Operation Forough Javidan/Mersad, Khomeini issued an order declaring imprisoned MEK supporters who remained committed to the organization guilty of *moharebeh* and subject to execution.

The surviving text instructs three-person panels to decide prisoners’ fates and directs officials not to hesitate. A follow-up response, transmitted through Ahmad Khomeini, applied execution even to prisoners already serving fixed terms if they retained support for the MEK.

“Death commissions” interrogated prisoners about organizational loyalty and, in a later phase, leftists about religious belief. Prisoners were executed secretly and buried in unmarked or mass graves. Families were denied truthful information.

- **Documented fact:** The order attributed to Khomeini was reproduced in Montazeri’s memoirs, and Khomeini later referred approvingly to implementing God’s command against the MEK.
- **Institutional findings:** Amnesty International documented at least 2,000 executions; Human Rights Watch reports estimates of approximately 2,800–5,000 in at least 32 cities and classifies the killings as crimes against humanity.
- **Higher claims:** Opposition organizations have claimed totals approaching or exceeding 30,000. Publicly available name lists and documentary work do not presently verify that figure.
- **Government position:** Iranian authorities have never provided a full list and historically characterized accounts as opposition propaganda or justified killings through the MEK’s alliance with Iraq.
- **Legal status:** No Iranian court tried Khomeini. Later foreign proceedings against participants, especially Sweden’s prosecution of Hamid Nouri, supplied judicial examination of the broader events but not a verdict against Khomeini.

Montazeri protested in letters dated 31 July and 4 August 1988 and in an August meeting with commission members. He warned that executing prisoners already sentenced to imprisonment would discredit the revolution.

### Succession conflict

The Assembly of Experts had designated Montazeri as Khomeini’s successor in 1985. Tensions over executions, prison abuse, the Hashemi affair, and political liberalization culminated in Khomeini removing him on 26 March 1989.

Khomeini’s dismissal letter called Montazeri naïve and claimed he would surrender the state to liberals and the MEK. Montazeri later questioned the authenticity of some late letters because Ahmad controlled access while Khomeini was ill.

- **Documented fact:** Ahmad formally received authority in September 1988 to supervise his father’s correspondence.
- **Disputed claim:** Montazeri suspected that Ahmad or other officials shaped purported communications.
- **Assessment:** Direct forgery or usurpation remains unproved; Iranica considers wholesale substitution of Ahmad’s will for Khomeini’s authority improbable.

### Rushdie fatwa, 1989

On 14 February 1989 Khomeini called for the killing of author **Salman Rushdie** and others knowingly involved in publishing *The Satanic Verses*. The declaration was international, not a judicial verdict from a court with jurisdiction over Rushdie.

The edict led to death threats, diplomatic rupture, bounties, hiding and protection for Rushdie, attacks on translators and publishers, and an enduring dispute over whether later Iranian governments could retract it. Rushdie was gravely wounded in a 2022 stabbing by an attacker who denied direct Iranian contact.

### Letter to Mikhail Gorbachev

In January 1989 Khomeini sent a delegation to Mikhail Gorbachev advising him that communism was collapsing but warning against Western materialism. He recommended study of Islamic philosophy and mysticism, including Ibn Arabi. It was both a diplomatic message and a statement of Khomeini’s metaphysical worldview.

## Death, Funeral, and Burial

Khomeini underwent surgery on 23 May 1989 for gastrointestinal bleeding associated with stomach cancer. His daughter said he suffered five heart attacks afterward. He died on 3 June.

A vast public funeral was held on 6 June near Behesht-e Zahra cemetery. Contemporary accounts estimated several million participants; the frequently repeated figure of approximately 10.2 million is inherently difficult to verify. The crowd broke through security, pulled at the shroud, and caused the body to fall or nearly fall from its coffin. The burial was postponed and completed under tighter security.

He was buried in a purpose-built mausoleum south of Tehran. The complex grew into a shrine, mosque, pilgrimage site, ceremonial center, and family-administered institution.

Ali Khamenei succeeded him after constitutional changes removed or relaxed the former requirement that the leader be an acknowledged marjaʿ.

## Offices, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions created or consolidated under his authority

- Islamic Republic of Iran.
- Office of the Supreme Leader.
- Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps.
- Basij militia.
- Revolutionary courts.
- Guardian Council.
- Assembly of Experts.
- Supreme Council of the Cultural Revolution and associated Islamization project.
- Revolutionary foundations or *bonyads*, including the Foundation of the Oppressed and Martyrs Foundation.
- Friday-prayer and mosque networks integrated with the state.
- Export-of-revolution networks that influenced Lebanese Hezbollah and other movements.

Khomeini did not operate conventional companies or inventions. His central “project” was institutional: converting the doctrine of juristic guardianship into a constitutional state.

### Property and declared assets

A declaration dated 14 January 1981, submitted under Article 142 of the constitution, listed:

- A house in the Bagh-e Qalʿeh area of Qom.
- A fractional inherited interest in land at Khomein, shared with Pasandideh and Nur al-Din’s heirs.
- Limited personal gifts or cash.
- Several books.
- No personally owned furnishings of consequence; household furnishings were described as belonging to his wife or the owner of the Jamaran house.
- Religious funds held separately and declared not to belong to him or his heirs.

The declaration was published by an institution devoted to preserving his legacy and therefore is not an independent audit. Nevertheless, no comparably strong documentary evidence located in this research establishes a large personal commercial fortune. The revolutionary foundations controlled vast assets, but institutional control should not automatically be equated with Khomeini’s personal ownership.

The inherited land was reportedly given to poor residents of Khomein in 1984.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Evolution of *velayat-e faqih*

Khomeini’s position changed over time:

1. In *Kashf al-Asrar*, jurists guide or select a just ruler.
2. In the Najaf lectures, the jurist exercises governmental authority because implementing sharia requires a state.
3. In office, he advanced “absolute guardianship,” allowing the Islamic government to override even important secondary religious rules when state interest required it.

This doctrine elevated preservation of the Islamic state into a primary religious obligation.

### Republican and popular elements

Khomeini repeatedly invoked “the people,” elections, and referendums. The resulting order possessed elected institutions, but candidate eligibility, legislation, the military, judiciary, broadcasting, and constitutional interpretation were subject to clerical oversight. It was neither a wholly conventional dictatorship nor an unrestricted electoral republic.

### Social policy

Under his leadership:

- Compulsory veiling developed from workplace directives into general legal enforcement.
- Family law moved toward classical Shiʿa rules, reducing protections introduced under the Pahlavis.
- Alcohol, gambling, and much Western entertainment were prohibited.
- Gender segregation expanded in many institutions.
- Literacy, rural public services, health networks, wartime mobilization, and political participation by religious lower-income groups also expanded.
- Khomeini supported women’s political mobilization within Islamic limits and praised women’s revolutionary participation, despite having opposed the shah’s women’s-suffrage measure in 1963.

### Sexuality and gender transition

His juridical writings prescribed severe classical penalties for same-sex acts. Revolutionary courts executed men convicted of homosexual conduct.

Conversely, Khomeini issued a legal opinion permitting medically supervised gender reassignment. This later provided a religious-legal basis for Iran’s recognition and subsidy of some transition procedures. Recognition of transgender identity did not remove persecution of gay and lesbian people and sometimes created pressure to frame sexuality as gender pathology.

### Anti-imperialism and geopolitical ideology

Khomeini depicted the United States as the “Great Satan,” Israel as an illegitimate occupying project, and the Soviet Union as materialist and oppressive. He divided political Islam into “pure Muhammadan Islam” and “American Islam,” the latter meaning quietist, monarchist, elite, or foreign-aligned religion.

His revolution influenced Shiʿa political movements, Sunni Islamists, anti-colonial activists, and opponents of political Islam.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Teachers and acknowledged influences

- Abd al-Karim Haʾeri Yazdi: seminary master.
- Mohammad-Ali Shahabadi: principal mystical teacher.
- Abu’l-Hasan Rafiʿi Qazvini and Javad Maleki Tabrizi: philosophy and ethics.
- Hasan Modarres: model of clerical resistance to authoritarian and foreign pressure.
- Ibn Arabi and Mulla Sadra: metaphysical foundations.
- Mirza Hasan Shirazi: inherited model of transnational clerical mobilization.

### Core political associates

- Mostafa and Ahmad Khomeini.
- Mortaza Motahhari.
- Mohammad Beheshti.
- Hossein-Ali Montazeri, later estranged.
- Ali Khamenei.
- Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Violence, and Abuses

### Summary evidentiary table

| Matter | Status | Documentary position |
|---|---|---|
| Summary revolutionary executions, 1979 onward | Documented fact | Contemporary reporting and institutional histories establish executions after abbreviated proceedings; Khomeini created and defended the revolutionary framework. |
| 1988 prison massacres | Documented fact / institutional finding | Khomeini’s order survives through Montazeri’s archive; thousands were executed. Human-rights bodies classify the killings as crimes against humanity. |
| Personal order for every post-revolution execution | Unsupported generalization | Authority was hierarchical, but individual decisions often came from judges, prosecutors, prison officials, or other leaders. |
| Persecution of Bahaʾis | Documented institutional policy | Executions, disappearances, seizures, and exclusion are documented; official charges were often framed as espionage rather than religion. |
| Compulsory veiling and rollback of family law | Documented fact | Announced and implemented during his leadership despite public protests. |
| Execution for same-sex conduct | Documented fact | Contemporary execution reports and revolutionary penal policy support it. |
| Permitting gender reassignment | Documented jurisprudential fact | His fatwa became the basis of later Iranian policy. |
| Use of child soldiers | Reported and substantially documented at system level | Underage Basij participation occurred; precise numbers and whether all participation was coerced remain disputed. |
| Knowledge of Iran–Contra operational details | Unresolved | State involvement is established; direct evidence of Khomeini’s detailed knowledge is incomplete. |
| October Surprise agreement with Reagan campaign | Allegation / unresolved controversy | Congressional inquiries found no credible proof; later witnesses and archival discoveries revived debate without establishing Khomeini’s personal participation. |
| SAVAK murder of Mostafa Khomeini | Allegation | Politically powerful but not proved; official cause was heart failure. |
| BBC or Western powers “installed” Khomeini | Conspiracy narrative | Western broadcasting and French residence amplified him; this is not evidence that Britain, France, or the CIA created or controlled his movement. |
| Personal secret fortune | Unverified allegation | His published asset declaration was modest; revolutionary foundations accumulated enormous wealth but were not shown to be his personal property. |
| Ahmad Khomeini forged late directives | Disputed allegation | Ahmad controlled communications; Montazeri voiced suspicion, but conclusive proof of forgery has not emerged. |

### Political promises and strategic ambiguity

Khomeini’s French-period interviews emphasized freedom, national choice, and his own lack of interest in routine government. The eventual constitution placed extensive power in his office, opposition newspapers were closed, and competing organizations were suppressed.

This divergence is documented. Whether it demonstrates premeditated deceit, rapidly changing revolutionary circumstances, or differing definitions of liberty and Islamic government is interpretive and remains disputed.

### War responsibility

Iraq initiated the war. Khomeini therefore was not responsible for the invasion. He was, however, centrally responsible for Iran’s decision to continue offensive warfare after 1982, the refusal of several peace possibilities, and political insistence that Saddam’s fall remained necessary. The precise viability and terms of proposed settlements are disputed.

### Revolutionary terror and counter-terror

The historical record includes violence by both the new state and its opponents. MEK, Forqan, royalist, Kurdish, and other armed organizations conducted assassinations, bombings, insurgency, or collaboration with foreign enemies. These acts provide context but do not legally validate collective punishment, secret trials, torture, or execution of prisoners.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The man in the moon”

**Story:** In late 1978 and early 1979 some Iranians reportedly claimed Khomeini’s face could be seen in the moon.

**Circulation:** Oral revolutionary culture, later memoirs, journalism, and academic discussions of charisma.

**Evidence:** There is evidence that the story circulated; there is no evidence of a literal apparition. How widespread sincere belief was remains uncertain.

**Effect:** It represented the sacralization of a distant exile whose photograph, voice, and name had become omnipresent. Opponents later used it to portray revolutionary crowds as irrational.

### Prophecy of return and invulnerability

Supporters circulated stories that SAVAK could not destroy his influence, that his survival was providential, and that his return fulfilled religious expectation. The failed Tabas rescue became further proof in official mythology that divine forces protected the revolution.

These were theological-political interpretations, not empirically testable facts.

### Indian, Kashmiri, or “foreign” identity

The family’s Indian history was used by Pahlavi propaganda to portray Khomeini as non-Iranian. The ancestry itself is credible; the inference that he was therefore a foreign agent is unsupported. The surname “Hindi” belonged to family history and Khomeini’s poetic practice.

### British creation or “BBC Ayatollah”

**Narrative:** Britain or the BBC manufactured Khomeini to overthrow the shah, punish Iranian modernization, or control oil.

**Underlying facts:** BBC Persian and other foreign broadcasters transmitted news and Khomeini’s messages; France allowed extensive press access; Western governments increasingly judged the shah unstable.

**Evidence against the strong version:** Khomeini had an independent, decades-old clerical network; Western governments feared revolutionary instability; no documentary evidence consulted establishes Western command or creation of his movement.

**Reputational effect:** The story remains common among monarchists and other opponents and draws force from Iran’s history of the 1953 Anglo-American coup.

### Guadeloupe “handover”

A related story holds that leaders at the January 1979 Guadeloupe summit selected Khomeini or ordered the shah removed. Western leaders discussed the shah’s weakening position and transition scenarios. That is not the same as appointing Khomeini, and evidence for a formal handover agreement is lacking.

### October Surprise

**Story:** Reagan campaign representatives secretly negotiated with Iranian intermediaries to prevent release of the American hostages before the 1980 election, promising later arms.

**Circulators:** Former Carter official Gary Sick, journalists, alleged intermediaries, and later authors; recent attention followed testimony about John Connally’s Middle East trip.

**Evidence for concern:** Iran had political reason to deny Carter a victory; later illegal arms contacts indisputably occurred; witnesses reported approaches and meetings.

**Evidence against:** House and Senate inquiries concluded there was no credible proof of the central deal, and important witnesses were contradicted by travel and documentary records.

**Status:** Serious but unproved allegation. Nothing presently establishes that Khomeini personally approved a bargain with William Casey, George H. W. Bush, or Ronald Reagan.

### Mostafa Khomeini as a “martyr”

The belief that SAVAK murdered Mostafa helped transform mourning ceremonies into revolutionary mobilization. Official medical accounts said heart attack; rumors invoked poisoning and suspicious agents. No publicly available autopsy or definitive forensic record resolves the claim.

### Personal asceticism versus institutional wealth

Official iconography shows Khomeini in an austere Jamaran room, presenting him as detached from property. The modest declared assets and simple residence support a personal pattern of austerity. Critics answer that his office controlled huge religious revenues and foundations. Both can be true: personal consumption and institutional economic power are different questions.

## Works by Khomeini

The corpus contains autograph treatises, lecture transcripts, juridical manuals, edited notes, political declarations, correspondence, poetry, and posthumous compilations. Publication dates often substantially postdate composition.

### Mysticism, prayer, ethics, and philosophy

- *Sharh-e Doʿa-ye Sahar* (“Commentary on the Dawn Prayer”), Arabic, composed 1928.
- *Misbah al-Hidaya ila al-Khilafa wa’l-Wilaya* (“Lamp of Guidance to the Caliphate and Guardianship”), Arabic, 1930.
- *Taʿliqat ʿala Sharh Fusus al-Hikam wa Misbah al-Uns*, Arabic notes from studies of Ibn Arabi and mystical metaphysics; posthumously published.
- *Taʿliqa ʿala Sharh Hadith Raʾs al-Jalut*, probably completed 1929; published posthumously in 1996.
- *Sirr al-Salat: Miʿraj al-Salikin wa Salat al-ʿArifin* (“The Secret of Prayer”), Persian, completed 1939; first substantially published in 1981.
- *Sharh-e Chehel Hadith* / *Arbaʿin Hadith* (“Forty Hadith”), Persian, completed 1939; complete edition published posthumously.
- *Adab al-Salat* / *Adab-e Namaz* (“Disciplines of Prayer”), Persian, completed 1942; published posthumously.
- *Jihad al-Akbar* / *Mobaraza ba Nafs* (“The Greatest Jihad” or “Combat with the Self”), edited from Najaf lectures; its status is a lecture-derived compilation, not a conventional autograph monograph.
- *Tafsir-e Sureh-ye Hamd* (“Exegesis of the Sura al-Fatiha”), compiled from 1979–80 televised lectures; incomplete because the lectures ceased.

### Jurisprudence and legal method

- *Anwar al-Hidaya fi al-Taʿliqa ʿala al-Kifaya*, Arabic.
- *Badaʾiʿ al-Durar fi Qaʿidat Nafy al-Darar*, Arabic.
- *Al-Rasaʾil*, including treatises on legal methodology.
- *Kitab al-Tahara*, multivolume Arabic jurisprudence.
- *Kitab al-Makasib*, Arabic.
- *Taʿliqa ʿala al-ʿUrwat al-Wuthqa*, Arabic.
- *Taʿliqa ʿala Wasilat al-Najat*, Arabic.
- *Tawzih al-Masaʾel*, Persian manual of practical rulings, issued from the early 1960s in many editions.
- *Tahrir al-Wasila*, Arabic, chiefly composed in Bursa and completed or revised in Najaf, conventionally dated 1964–69; two-volume comprehensive legal manual.
- *Istiftaʾat*, collected answers to legal questions; edited compilation.
- *Manasik-e Hajj*, ritual handbook.

*Tahrir al-Wasila* addresses worship, commerce, marriage, warfare, punishments, and modern questions. Controversial rulings on sexuality, punishment, and marriage belong to the traditional juridical genre but were also relevant to law after 1979.

### Political works

- *Kashf al-Asrar* (“Unveiling of Secrets”), Persian, composed rapidly and published anonymously in 1945.
- *Hokumat-e Islami: Velayat-e Faqih* (“Islamic Government: Guardianship of the Jurist”), Najaf lectures delivered in 1970; circulated first in pamphlet form and published around 1971–72.
- *Islam and Revolution: Writings and Declarations of Imam Khomeini*, translated and edited by Hamid Algar, 1981; English editorial anthology, not a single original book.
- *The Position of Women from the Viewpoint of Imam Khomeini*, thematic posthumous compilation.
- *Palestine from the Viewpoint of Imam Khomeini*, thematic compilation.
- Numerous messages on Hajj, war, the constitution, elections, universities, clergy, and foreign relations.

### Correspondence, speeches, and collected records

- *Sahifeh-ye Nur*, initially a multivolume state-sponsored collection of speeches, messages, interviews, decrees, and letters.
- *Sahifeh-ye Imam*, later expanded critical/institutional collection in 22 volumes.
- *Kawthar*, collection of speeches.
- *Waʿdeh-ye Didar*, collection of letters to Ahmad Khomeini.
- Political-divine testament, *Wasiyatnameh-ye Siyasi-Elahi*, written and revised during the 1980s and opened after his death.
- Letter to Mikhail Gorbachev, composed December 1988 and delivered January 1989.
- The Rushdie declaration of 14 February 1989.

These collections were edited by institutions controlled by Khomeini’s family and the Islamic Republic. They are indispensable primary sources but should be checked against recordings, manuscripts, contemporary editions, and archival context where authenticity is contested.

### Poetry

- *Divan-e Imam*, posthumous collection of Persian ghazals and mystical verse.
- Some poems use “Hendi” as the pen name.
- The poetry employs wine, tavern, beloved, annihilation, and Sufi imagery. Literal readings that treat this as evidence of alcohol consumption misunderstand conventional Persian mystical poetics.
- Editorial selection and the precise date of many poems are uncertain.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### State cult and commemoration

The Islamic Republic represents Khomeini as “the Imam,” renewer of Islam, champion of the oppressed, philosopher-jurist, and founder. His portrait remains a state symbol; his death anniversary is a public commemoration; his mausoleum is a national ceremonial site; and his sayings appear in education, government offices, and political speeches.

### Religious standing

His direct political theory remains influential but not universally accepted among Shiʿa jurists. Quietist scholars, especially those emphasizing Najaf’s tradition, reject or sharply limit direct clerical sovereignty. His elevation from jurist to quasi-charismatic national imam was exceptional in Iranian Shiism.

### Political influence

His model helped establish or inspire:

- The Islamic Republic’s leadership system.
- Lebanese Hezbollah’s early doctrine and organization.
- Shiʿa Islamist movements in Iraq, the Gulf, Afghanistan, Pakistan, and elsewhere.
- A wider model of Islamist anti-imperialist revolution.
- Counter-movements among secularists, monarchists, feminists, religious reformists, dissident clerics, and minority communities.

### Competing historical interpretations

- **Official/revolutionary:** He restored sovereignty, expelled monarchy and foreign domination, institutionalized Islamic government, defended Iran from Iraq, and empowered previously marginalized populations.
- **Liberal and secular opposition:** He used a broad revolution to establish clerical autocracy, destroyed pluralism, and reversed civil liberties.
- **Left opposition:** He temporarily allied with anti-shah leftists and then eliminated them.
- **Monarchist:** He brought economic destruction and religious reaction, sometimes accompanied by unsupported foreign-installation narratives.
- **Religious reformist:** His political theology was historically contingent and should not be treated as an eternal requirement of Shiism.
- **Human-rights documentation:** His period established institutions and practices of arbitrary imprisonment, torture, discriminatory law, and mass execution.
- **Populist interpretation:** Abrahamian characterizes Khomeinism as a form of clerical populism combining anti-elite rhetoric, welfare claims, nationalism, and religious authority.

### Enduring controversies

The 1988 executions, Rushdie fatwa, compulsory veiling, treatment of Bahaʾis, revolutionary courts, Iran–Iraq War continuation, and constitutional supremacy of the jurist remain central to his reputation. His personal austerity, philosophical sophistication, capacity for mass mobilization, and creation of durable institutions are also strongly supported features of the record.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity is excluded.

### Ibn Arabi

**Label: Documented intellectual influence.** Khomeini studied Ibn Arabi’s *Fusus al-Hikam* under Mohammad-Ali Shahabadi in Qom during the 1920s, annotated its commentary, incorporated Ibn Arabi’s terminology into his mystical writings, and recommended study of Ibn Arabi in his 1989 message to Gorbachev. The relationship was textual and intellectual, not personal.

### Mansur al-Hallaj

**Label: Documented literary and mystical association.** Khomeini’s poetry invoked Hallaj, whose ecstatic language and execution formed part of the Persian-Sufi symbolic repertoire. This was an intellectual-symbolic influence, not a historical encounter.

### Mohammad Mosaddegh

**Label: Documented shared political setting; qualified relationship.** Khomeini was acquainted with Kashani, Mosaddegh’s ally and later rival, and was reportedly at Kashani’s residence when pro-Mosaddegh demonstrators attacked it on 31 July 1953. Khomeini did not become a Mosaddegh activist. Memory of the Anglo-American overthrow of Mosaddegh shaped revolutionary fears behind the 1979 embassy seizure.

### Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi

**Label: Documented principal political rivalry.** Khomeini opposed the shah’s election changes and White Revolution from 1962–63, was arrested after the June 1963 uprising, exiled in 1964, and directed the movement that ended the monarchy in 1979.

### Saddam Hussein al-Tikriti

**Label: Documented political and military enmity.** Khomeini lived under Baʿathist rule in Iraq from 1965 to 1978, resisted Iraqi attempts to enlist him against the shah, and was expelled under Iranian pressure. Saddam’s government invaded Iran in 1980. Khomeini led Iran through the ensuing eight-year war and sought Saddam’s removal.

### Oliver Laurence North

**Label: Documented indirect operational connection.** North coordinated central U.S. elements of the Iran–Contra arms transactions with Iranian intermediaries and officials while Khomeini headed Iran. There is no evidence located here of a personal meeting or direct correspondence between North and Khomeini.

### William Joseph Casey

**Label: Documented indirect connection in Iran–Contra; disputed connection in the October Surprise narrative.** Congressional investigators associated Casey with the Reagan administration’s Iran–Contra network but found no documentary proof that he approved the diversion of profits. Claims that Casey negotiated a 1980 hostage-release delay with Khomeini’s representatives remain unproved.

### George Herbert Walker Bush

**Label: Allegation or theory only.** Some October Surprise narratives placed Bush at a Paris meeting with Iranian representatives in 1980. Congressional investigation rejected the alleged trip and found no credible proof. No documented personal connection with Khomeini was established.

### Benjamin Netanyahu

**Finding: No qualifying personal connection located.** Netanyahu was active in Israeli diplomacy and counterterrorism discourse during Khomeini’s final decade, but the consulted sources did not establish a meeting, correspondence, or specific personal relationship.

### Zoroaster, Abraham, Moses, Jesus of Nazareth, Muhammad ibn Abdullah, Aristotle, Plato, and other ancient roster figures

**Finding: No biographical connection recorded.** Khomeini wrote within Islamic theological and philosophical traditions that discuss prophets and Greek philosophy, but generalized religious or civilizational inheritance is too broad to count as a documented atlas crossing without a specifically sourced acknowledged influence. Ibn Arabi and Hallaj meet that threshold; the remaining names do not on the evidence consulted.

### Remaining atlas roster

**Finding: No supported connection identified.** The research found no evidence sufficient to record meetings, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, family relationship, shared institution, patronage, or explicitly acknowledged influence involving the other listed atlas figures.
