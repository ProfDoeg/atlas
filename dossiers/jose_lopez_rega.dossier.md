# José López Rega: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

José López Rega’s biography is unusually burdened by secrecy, partisan reconstruction, incomplete criminal proceedings, family rebuttals, sensational occult stories, and factual errors repeated in reference works. This dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact:** supported by official records, judicial findings, archival documents, contemporaneous records, or multiple independent sources.
- **Reported fact:** supported by credible testimony or historical research but not independently established here through a primary record.
- **Allegation:** asserted in testimony, an investigation, indictment, press report, or political accusation but not adjudicated against López Rega.
- **Court finding:** determined in a judicial proceeding, including later Triple A cases in which López Rega was not personally tried because he was dead.
- **Disputed claim:** materially contested by witnesses or sources.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally significant story lacking adequate documentary substantiation.

A recurring correction is necessary: López Rega was arrested, extradited, processed, and held in preventive detention, but **died before judgment**. Statements that he was finally convicted of the Triple A murders are inaccurate. Later courts established the existence, criminal nature, and ministerial base of the Triple A and convicted surviving participants, but those judgments were not convictions of López Rega himself.[S1][S2]

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** José López Rega.
- **Occasionally styled:** José López; José López Rega.
- **Birth:** 17 October 1916, Buenos Aires, Argentina.[S3][S4]
- **Erroneous alternative date:** Some English-language summaries give 17 November 1916. Argentine biographical and genealogical records consistently favor **17 October**.
- **Death:** 9 June 1989, Buenos Aires, Argentina, aged seventy-two.[S1][S5]
- **Nationality:** Argentine.
- **Principal residences:** Villa Urquiza, Buenos Aires; Madrid, principally Juan Perón’s Puerta de Hierro residence; Buenos Aires during the 1973–75 governments; Madrid and other European locations after his fall; the Lake Geneva region of Switzerland; the Bahamas; southern Florida; Argentine federal custody after 1986.
- **Occupations:** itinerant seller and commercial employee; Federal Police employee and corporal; amateur or semi-professional singer; astrologer and esoteric writer; private secretary to Juan and Isabel Perón; minister of Social Welfare; political operator; ambassador-at-large.
- **Principal political office:** Minister of Social Welfare, 25 May 1973–11 July 1975, serving successively under Héctor J. Cámpora, Raúl Lastiri, Juan Domingo Perón, and María Estela Martínez de Perón.
- **Police rank:** Originally a junior policeman/corporal. Juan Perón’s Decree 1350 of 3 May 1974 promoted the already retired López Rega directly to **comisario general**, the highest officer grade—an exceptional elevation across the normal division between noncommissioned and commissioned careers.[S6]
- **Nicknames and designations:**
  - **“El Brujo”**—“the Sorcerer” or “Warlock,” originally associated with his astrological writing and later made politically pejorative.
  - **“Lopecito”**—a diminutive used by Perón and political contemporaries, sometimes affectionately and sometimes contemptuously.
  - **“Daniel” / “Hermano Daniel”**—reported initiatic name in the Anael esoteric circle.
  - **“The Argentine Rasputin” / “Rasputin of the Pampas”**—international press analogy based on his occult reputation and influence over Isabel Perón.[S7]
  - **“Faraón”** was not his own title but the name he reportedly used for Juan Perón in his esoteric interpretation of the leader.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and disputed maternal identity

López Rega’s father was **Juan López**, a Spanish immigrant who reportedly earned his living driving a taxi in Buenos Aires. Sources deriving from Marcelo Larraquy’s research describe the family as modest and settled at Guayra 3761 in Villa Urquiza.[S3]

The mother’s identity is inconsistent in accessible records:

- Genealogical indexing and several later summaries name her **Manuela Rega**, born about 1892 and deceased in 1932.[S4]
- Larraquy-derived accounts call her **Rosa Rega** and say she died giving birth to José on 17 October 1916.[S3]
- The birth-death story became central to later accounts of López Rega’s solitary childhood.

The discrepancy cannot be resolved conclusively from the sources consulted. The claim that his mother died in childbirth should therefore be treated as a **reported fact**, not an established civil-record finding.

Both parents are generally described as Spanish immigrants. No reliable evidence of aristocratic, clerical, indigenous, or politically prominent ancestry was located. No documented siblings were identified.

### Marriage and child

On **19 June 1943**, López Rega married **Josefa Flora Maseda Fontenla**—also rendered Flora Josefa Maseda/Maceda—in the parish of Santísima Trinidad in the Núñez/Saavedra area of Buenos Aires.[S3][S4] She had worked as a textile laborer. The marriage appears to have become emotionally and geographically distant during his years in Madrid and power, but it was not formally dissolved in the evidence consulted.

Their only known child was:

- **Norma Beatriz López Rega**, born 17 February 1945 in Buenos Aires.
- Norma trained in fine arts and married **Raúl Alberto Lastiri**, a López Rega associate who became provisional president in 1973.
- After Lastiri’s death she married journalist and official **Jorge Héctor Conti**, who worked in her father’s ministerial and presidential press apparatus. Conti was later convicted in the reopened Triple A proceeding for membership in the unlawful association.[S1][S8]
- Norma López Rega has publicly rejected the assertion that her father created or commanded the Triple A, calling it a historical falsehood. Her denial is an important family account but conflicts with testimony, prosecutorial reconstruction, and later judicial findings concerning the Ministry of Social Welfare.[S8]

### Later companion

**María Elena Cisneros**, a schoolteacher from Entre Ríos, became López Rega’s assistant and companion after his departure from Argentina. Norma López Rega said Cisneros first approached the ministry seeking clothing and shoes for children at her school and later traveled to Madrid.[S9]

Cisneros remained with López Rega during his clandestine years in Europe, the Bahamas, and Florida. Property and bank accounts were reportedly placed in her name, although the extent and origin of the assets remain uncertain. In 1986 her approach to an Argentine consular office concerning passport documentation helped investigators locate him.[S5][S10]

Contemporary reports sometimes called Cisneros his wife or lover. No reliable record of a legal marriage was located.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1916–1943

López Rega grew up in **Villa Urquiza**, then a socially mixed, relatively peripheral Buenos Aires neighborhood. Larraquy’s reconstruction places his family home at **Guayra 3761** and describes him as quiet, withdrawn, imaginative, and preoccupied with religion and invisible forces.[S3]

He attended the **José Félix de Azara primary school**. No evidence was found of secondary-school completion or university study. His later intellectual persona was substantially self-created through private reading.

Reported adolescent and early adult employment included:

- work in a dry cleaner;
- street or door-to-door selling;
- miscellaneous small commercial jobs;
- neighborhood social life at the club **El Tábano**.

He developed an interest in classical singing and cultivated an ambition to become an opera singer. The quality and duration of his formal musical education are unclear.

A particularly doubtful autobiographical claim was that **Aurelia “Potota” Tizón**, Juan Perón’s first wife, taught him singing and that this allowed him to meet Perón in the 1930s. The chronology is problematic: López Rega was a child or young adolescent during much of Tizón’s married life, and no independent record confirms the relationship. This is best classified as **self-created mythology**.

## Education and Formation

López Rega did not possess a documented advanced academic education. His formation came through four overlapping environments:

1. **Neighborhood working-class culture** in Villa Urquiza.
2. **Police employment**, which gave him institutional contacts and familiarity with security structures.
3. **Music and radio**, particularly his aspirations as a baritone.
4. **Esotericism**, encompassing astrology, Spiritualism, Rosicrucian ideas, occult Christianity, numerology, reincarnation, Asian religious motifs, and elements associated with Afro-Brazilian religion.

He reportedly maintained a large personal library and read widely in occult and spiritual literature. His books reveal eclectic compilation rather than adherence to one coherent initiatic school.

### Musical associations

López Rega reportedly sang on **Radio Nacional** and/or **Radio Mitre** around 1950–51. Journalist and broadcasting figure **José María Villone** became an important contact. Exact performance dates and professional status remain incompletely documented.

### Victoria Montero and the Casa de Doña Victoria

Villone reportedly introduced López Rega to **Victoria Montero**, known as “Doña Victoria” or “the Teacher,” at Paso de los Libres, Corrientes. Her house functioned as a meeting place for people associated with the **Fraternidad Antiqua**, Rosicrucianism, Spiritualism, and later the circle known as **Anael**.[S11]

López Rega became a disciple and collaborator, reportedly using the initiatic name **Daniel**. Montero was an important teacher and patron during his transition from obscure policeman to published occultist.

Their relationship eventually broke down. Accounts say Montero expelled him or denounced his political use of spiritual teachings. The details come principally from participant testimony reconstructed decades later and should be treated as **reported fact**.

### Brazil and Afro-Brazilian influence

Records and secondary accounts place López Rega in **Brazil, including Rio de Janeiro, in 1963**.[S4] Later biographies associate this journey with contact with Umbanda or related Afro-Brazilian ritual traditions. His actual initiation status is unproven. Claims that he was a formal Umbanda priest should be treated cautiously.

## Early Career: Police, Singing, and Esoteric Publishing, 1944–1965

### Federal Police

López Rega entered the Argentine Federal Police in **1944 or 1945**. Sources disagree by approximately one year. His work was initially administrative or low-ranking. He became a **cabo primero** rather than a senior investigator or commander.

With support attributed to police chief **Filomeno Velazco**, he received duties connected with presidential security during Juan Perón’s first governments. He was at least intermittently assigned around the **Unzué presidential residence**, but later portrayals of him as Eva Perón’s personal bodyguard exaggerate the evidence.

A photograph showing him near Eva Perón later served his efforts to construct an early personal link with Peronism. The photograph establishes physical proximity, not an intimate relationship.

After the overthrow of Perón in September 1955, López Rega served in the **Guardia de Infantería**. He retired or sought retirement in **1962**, at a junior grade.[S6]

### Esoteric publications

His first substantial book, *Astrología esotérica: secretos develados*, appeared in 1962. Its 737 pages combined zodiacal interpretation, planetary hours, colors, music, perfumes, plants, spiritual counsel, and simplified horoscope construction.[S12][S13]

A hostile review by journalist **Pedro Leopoldo Barraza** is credited with popularizing “El Brujo.” The sobriquet was therefore connected initially to his published occultism, not necessarily to evidence that he performed sinister rites.

During this period he also published *Alpha y Omega* and *La Astroagenda*. His books portray history as a conflict of spiritual forces, emphasize hierarchy and initiation, and combine Christian symbolism with reincarnation and astrological determinism.

## Entry into the Perón Circle, 1965–1972

### Meeting Isabel Perón

The best-supported chronology begins in **1965**, when Juan Perón sent his wife, **María Estela Martínez de Perón (“Isabel”)**, to Argentina to counter the independent political project of metalworkers’ leader **Augusto Timoteo Vandor**.

Competing accounts identify the intermediary who placed López Rega in Isabel’s entourage:

- contacts in the Anael circle, particularly **José Cresto**;
- retired Major **Bernardo Alberte**;
- other Peronist and police contacts.

What is reasonably established is that López Rega joined Isabel’s protection or assistance group, traveled with her during a lengthy Argentine tour, gained her confidence, and in **1966 accompanied or followed her to Madrid**.[S14]

### Puerta de Hierro

At Perón’s home, **Quinta 17 de Octubre** in Puerta de Hierro, López Rega initially performed domestic, logistical, security, secretarial, and gatekeeping functions. He cared for dogs, handled visitors and correspondence, controlled access, and made himself indispensable to Isabel.

Over time he became:

- Isabel’s personal confidant;
- Perón’s private secretary;
- an intermediary between Perón and Argentine emissaries;
- a recorder and sometimes participant in political meetings;
- a filter controlling appointments, documents, and communications.

Visitors commonly described him as obsequious toward Perón but increasingly assertive with politicians and union leaders. His power derived less from formal standing than continuous physical access.

### The remains of Eva Perón

In 1971 Eva Perón’s embalmed body was transferred from Italy to Puerta de Hierro through an operation involving Peronist agents and **Licio Gelli**. López Rega participated in the household in which the body was kept.

Stories subsequently alleged that he:

- conducted rituals beside the corpse;
- tried to transfer Eva’s spirit or “vibrations” to Isabel;
- combed or handled the body;
- told Isabel that she was Eva’s reincarnation.

The presence of the corpse at Puerta de Hierro and López Rega’s occult interests are **documented facts**. The supposed soul-transfer ceremony rests on memoir, hearsay, and later sensational reconstruction. It is a **reported allegation/legend**, not independently established fact.

### Political positioning

López Rega aligned with the anti-Marxist and “orthodox” sectors of Peronism, while Perón simultaneously negotiated with trade unionists, conservative nationalists, centrist politicians, and the revolutionary youth.

He cultivated relationships with:

- Raúl Lastiri;
- José María and Carlos Villone;
- José Miguel Vanni;
- Alberto Villar;
- Luis Margaride;
- Juan Ramón Morales;
- Rodolfo Eduardo Almirón;
- Julio Yessi;
- Felipe Romeo;
- Jorge Conti;
- sectors of the Comando de Organización, Juventud Peronista de la República Argentina, Concentración Nacional Universitaria, and other right-wing Peronist networks.

He became an enemy or political antagonist of figures associated with the Peronist left, Montoneros, independent unionism, and revolutionary Marxism.

## Return of Peronism and the Ministry of Social Welfare, 1972–1973

López Rega accompanied Perón during the leader’s first return to Argentina on **17 November 1972**. His control of access was already conspicuous.

After Héctor Cámpora won the March 1973 election, Perón secured López Rega’s appointment as **minister of Social Welfare**, effective 25 May 1973. The portfolio controlled health, social assistance, pensions, housing, sports, and large discretionary budgets. It also possessed vehicles, buildings, communications, warehouses, and personnel useful for political organization.

### Ezeiza, 20 June 1973

When Perón returned permanently, armed right-wing groups controlling the stage and security perimeter opened fire in the Ezeiza area, producing the **Ezeiza massacre**.

López Rega was politically associated with the event’s right-wing organizers, including Colonel **Jorge Osinde**, but the precise command chain and his personal operational instructions remain disputed. It is accurate to say:

- **Documented fact:** armed right-wing Peronist formations controlled important security positions.
- **Reported allegation:** López Rega helped plan, resource, or politically protect those formations.
- **Unresolved question:** no final judgment established his individual criminal responsibility for Ezeiza.

### Removal of Cámpora and the Lastiri presidency

Cámpora resigned on 13 July 1973. Senate president Alejandro Díaz Bialet was maneuvered out of the line of succession, allowing Chamber of Deputies president **Raúl Lastiri**, López Rega’s son-in-law, to become provisional president.

The transition was formally constitutional but widely described as a palace maneuver orchestrated by Perón’s inner circle, including López Rega. Lastiri’s position enlarged his father-in-law’s institutional reach.

### Origins of the Triple A

The **Alianza Anticomunista Argentina**, generally called Triple A, began publicly claiming threats and attacks during late 1973 and 1974. Later judicial proceedings found that:

- it existed as a criminal unlawful association;
- it operated through cells or subgroups;
- it was anchored in the Ministry of Social Welfare;
- its political command was associated with López Rega;
- ministry officials Conti and Carlos Villone acted as links to operational groups;
- police personnel and civilian militants participated.[S1][S2]

The prosecution cited testimony from former policeman Rodolfo Peregrino Fernández, Salvador Horacio Paino, Aníbal Gordon, Eduardo Luis Duhalde, Mario Gaggero, and others. Weapons were found in or connected with ministerial facilities. Later convictions did not require proof that every participant knew all other cells or all crimes.

López Rega’s daughter and some revisionist writers deny he commanded the organization. This is a **disputed claim**, but the denial conflicts with a substantial body of testimony and later court findings about the ministry’s command structure.[S1][S8]

## Perón’s Third Presidency, October 1973–July 1974

Juan Perón returned to the presidency on 12 October 1973, with Isabel as vice-president. López Rega remained minister and private secretary.

### “Reserved Document” and anti-Marxist campaign

After the 25 September 1973 assassination of CGT secretary-general **José Ignacio Rucci**, the Peronist leadership adopted a “Documento Reservado” calling for an organized struggle against “Marxist infiltration.” The document supplied political authorization for purges, surveillance, and coercion within the movement. Its relation to specific Triple A crimes must be distinguished from its general political effect: it did not by itself prove an order by López Rega to commit a particular murder.

### Triple A violence

Attacks attributed to the Triple A included bombings, kidnappings, death threats, and murders directed against lawyers, politicians, union activists, journalists, priests, artists, academics, and militants.

Frequently cited victims or targets included:

- Senator Hipólito Solari Yrigoyen, injured by a car bomb;
- Silvio Frondizi, murdered 27 September 1974;
- Rodolfo Ortega Peña, murdered 31 July 1974;
- Julio Troxler, murdered 20 September 1974;
- priest Carlos Mugica, murdered 11 May 1974;
- numerous members of the Peronist and non-Peronist left;
- exiles named on published death lists.

For several individual crimes, organizational attribution and personal command remain evidentially uneven. Prosecutors later cited testimony that López Rega had selected or approved prospective victims, including Mugica.[S15] López Rega was processed in cases involving association, kidnapping, and homicide but died before the evidence was resolved at trial.

### Police allies

Perón appointed **Alberto Villar** chief of the Federal Police and **Luis Margaride** to a senior security position. Villar and Margaride were associated with hard-line policing; Villar was later linked to Triple A operations. López Rega’s former police contacts **Juan Ramón Morales** and **Rodolfo Almirón** became central members of his security apparatus.

### Promotion to comisario general

On 3 May 1974, Perón promoted López Rega directly from his retired junior status to **comisario general**. The institutional irregularity became an emblem of his accumulated influence.[S6]

### Missions to Libya

López Rega led an official mission to **Libya in January 1974**, accompanied by officials including Celestino Rodrigo and representatives of YPF and Gas del Estado. Agreements covered petroleum, trade, and investment. An Argentine-Libyan institute and a proposed investment fund were developed; a December 1974 agreement contemplated a Libyan contribution stated as US$200 million in goods, equipment, and merchandise.[S16][S17]

Later allegations associated the “Operativo Libia” with:

- arms negotiations;
- commissions and irregular financial flows;
- covert financing;
- overpriced petroleum;
- personal enrichment.

The mission and agreements are documented. The criminal allegations were never finally adjudicated against López Rega. In his unpublished or late autobiographical writing, he denied buying arms and said his purpose was to secure petroleum.

## Isabel Perón’s Presidency and López Rega’s Apex, July 1974–July 1975

Juan Perón died on **1 July 1974**. Isabel succeeded him.

### Conduct at Perón’s deathbed

Witness-based accounts claim López Rega seized or shook Perón’s legs and cried, “¡No te vayas, Faraón!” while attempting to revive him through prayer or an occult procedure. A 2024 retrospective repeats the story.[S18]

This episode is best classified as **reported fact with legendary elaboration**. López Rega was present and his esoteric behavior was reported by witnesses, but exact words and motions cannot be independently verified.

### Political power

During Isabel’s presidency, López Rega exercised influence well beyond the formal jurisdiction of Social Welfare. He was involved in:

- cabinet formation;
- presidential access;
- security appointments;
- press and propaganda;
- provincial political interventions;
- foreign missions;
- social-welfare patronage;
- economic-policy sponsorship;
- mobilization of right-wing Peronist groups.

Calling him the “de facto ruler” captures contemporary perceptions but oversimplifies a government also constrained by Isabel, the armed forces, union chief Lorenzo Miguel, Congress, provincial leaders, business groups, and competing security institutions.

### Argentina Potencia

López Rega promoted **“Argentina Potencia”** as a state-development and propaganda program. It promised economic independence, housing, welfare, technological advance, and an enlarged international role. The slogan appeared in ministerial publications, ceremonies, and foreign missions.

The project combined genuine public works and social programs with personal political promotion and opaque discretionary spending. A complete audited division between completed projects, abandoned plans, propaganda, and diverted funds remains unavailable.

### Cruzada de Solidaridad Justicialista

The **Cruzada de Solidaridad Justicialista**, associated with Isabel and the Ministry of Social Welfare, collected and distributed donations for charitable purposes. It was designed in part to invoke the memory of the Eva Perón Foundation.

Investigations later alleged:

- irregular checks and false documentation;
- diversion of donated funds;
- personal or political use of charitable resources;
- manipulation of purchases and transfers.

These matters formed part of the corruption and extradition proceedings. A 1976 inquiry sought information from Paraguay concerning commercial transactions linked to López Rega and the former government.[S19]

### El Caudillo

The magazine **El Caudillo de la Tercera Posición**, directed by Felipe Romeo, articulated the anti-left Peronist line and received support connected to the ministry. Investigators and witnesses linked its offices and personnel with Triple A operations. The magazine’s slogan, “el mejor enemigo es el enemigo muerto,” became emblematic of the period.

The publication’s ideological alignment and state support are well documented. Whether every staff member knew of clandestine violence requires individual determination.

### Accusations of narcotics trafficking

A later-reported December 1975 Argentine Army intelligence document allegedly described López Rega as heading a cocaine-trafficking organization using laboratories in Salta, police transport, and diplomatic channels, with proceeds financing the Triple A.

This remains an **intelligence allegation**, not a court finding. The source has circulated widely but was not corroborated in the materials consulted by a completed prosecution, financial trail, or seizure directly proving López Rega’s responsibility.

### International right-wing and intelligence connections

López Rega interacted with or was linked to:

- Licio Gelli and Propaganda Due;
- Spanish Francoist political and security figures;
- European anti-communist militants;
- Argentine police and intelligence personnel;
- foreign officials encountered through state missions.

Claims of direct CIA sponsorship of López Rega or the Triple A are often asserted but are not adequately established by the consulted primary documentation. United States diplomats monitored his power and corruption scandals; observation and political contact do not by themselves prove direction or control.

## Propaganda Due, Licio Gelli, and Transnational Networks

López Rega’s relationship with **Licio Gelli** is more substantial than a mere conspiracy theory. Gelli assisted Peronist operations, helped arrange the recovery of Eva Perón’s remains, cultivated Argentine military and political figures, and received an Argentine decoration during Perón’s last government.

López Rega was identified as connected with the clandestine Italian lodge **Propaganda Due (P2)**. Italian parliamentary investigations later found substantial documentary and accounting support for the authenticity of Gelli’s membership system, although the surviving lists were incomplete and contained different categories of contact.[S20]

The cautious formulation is:

- **Documented/report-supported fact:** López Rega had a political relationship with Gelli and appeared in the P2 documentary universe.
- **Widely accepted reported fact:** he was a P2 affiliate.
- **Unresolved detail:** the exact date, rite, membership number, obligations, and operational significance of his initiation are not clear from the sources consulted.
- **Unverified extension:** assertions that P2, the CIA, and the Vatican jointly created or commanded the Triple A require evidence beyond López Rega’s P2 association.

His P2 connection remains important because it placed him within a transnational network involving anti-communist politics, security officials, businessmen, intelligence figures, and financial intermediaries.

## The Rodrigazo and Fall from Power, June–July 1975

López Rega sponsored or strongly supported **Celestino Rodrigo**, appointed economy minister in June 1975. Rodrigo announced a drastic adjustment involving a major peso devaluation, increases in fuel and public-service prices, and pressure on recently negotiated wages. The program became known as the **Rodrigazo**.

The measures triggered:

- rapid inflation;
- labor unrest;
- mass demonstrations;
- the first general strike by the CGT against a Peronist government;
- confrontation between López Rega and union leader Lorenzo Miguel;
- collapse of Rodrigo’s authority;
- disintegration of López Rega’s political position.

The Rodrigazo was not solely López Rega’s economic design, but he was its principal political sponsor. Its failure converted previously fragmented opposition into a coalition capable of removing him.

On **11 July 1975**, he left the ministry. On **19 July**, he departed Argentina with an official title generally described as ambassador extraordinary or ambassador-at-large, accompanied by security associates. The diplomatic appointment functioned as an organized removal from domestic power and as protection against immediate arrest.

His fall is often described as “exile,” but it began as a state-sanctioned departure with diplomatic status.

## Flight, European Residences, and Financial Questions, 1975–1983

### Spain

López Rega initially returned to Madrid. Spanish political controversy later centered on whether former minister **Manuel Fraga Iribarne** or other Franco-era figures protected him. Fraga denied doing so. Almirón’s later employment in Spanish right-wing security circles intensified suspicion.[S21]

### Switzerland

In approximately 1977 López Rega and Cisneros moved to the Lake Geneva region, reportedly using altered identities and posing as father and daughter. A residence near **Villeneuve** was reportedly considered or acquired through intermediaries.

Swiss-period questions include:

- bank accounts in Cisneros’s or third-party names;
- the source of property funds;
- assets transferred from the Social Welfare Ministry or charitable bodies;
- use of aliases;
- assistance from former Argentine officials.

Juan Gasparini’s *La fuga del Brujo* uses Swiss federal material to reconstruct his concealment and networks.[S22] The available record supports hidden residence and financial opacity but not a complete balance sheet of his wealth.

### Proceedings in Argentina

A criminal complaint against López Rega, Morales, and Almirón concerning the Triple A had been filed on **11 July 1975** by lawyer and judge Miguel Radrizzani Goñi. Corruption cases also concerned malversation, fraud against the public administration, forged public documents, and the Cruzada.

After the 1976 military coup:

- the junta formally sought or contemplated extradition;
- López Rega was placed at the disposal of the executive;
- investigations continued intermittently;
- political and institutional incentives for a comprehensive inquiry remained inconsistent.

By **21 October 1983**, Judge Dibur had processed López Rega and Villone in the Triple A case.[S23]

### Relations with the military regime

López Rega later claimed Admiral **Emilio Eduardo Massera** forced him from Argentina. López Rega and Massera shared anti-left politics and P2 associations but were also rivals. The dictatorship did not restore López Rega; its security apparatus absorbed or reused some personnel associated with pre-coup parapolice structures.

---

## Bahamas, Florida, Discovery, and Arrest, 1983–1986

After exposure of his European location, López Rega and Cisneros moved through the Bahamas and Florida. Reported residences included:

- Freeport, Bahamas;
- Atlantic Boulevard in the Miami area;
- Fort Lauderdale.

They maintained a guarded, semi-clandestine life. López Rega wrote, played or listened to music, and managed worsening diabetes.

In February 1986 Argentine authorities requested his provisional arrest. The request encompassed allegations of fraud, conspiracy, malversation, forgery, theft, unlawful association, kidnapping, and homicide.

On **13 March 1986**, returning from the Bahamas, López Rega surrendered to FBI agents at **Miami International Airport**. Contemporary reports state that:

- a warrant had been issued on 27 February;
- the FBI took him into custody;
- a federal magistrate denied bail;
- Argentina submitted evidence supporting extradition.[S5][S7]

He contested extradition, citing age and diabetes. A United States court found the extradition showing adequate, and Secretary of State George Shultz authorized transfer. López Rega was returned to Argentina in **July 1986**.

---

## Imprisonment, Criminal Proceedings, and Death, 1986–1989

In Argentina López Rega was placed in preventive detention and processed in proceedings involving:

- unlawful association;
- kidnapping;
- homicide;
- misuse of public funds;
- fraud and falsification associated with charitable or ministerial assets.

Prosecutor **Aníbal Ibarra** reportedly sought life imprisonment in the Triple A matter. The case moved slowly, was divided among factual episodes, and never reached a final judgment against him.

His health declined substantially:

- longstanding diabetes worsened;
- he required insulin;
- he suffered impaired vision;
- he became frail and experienced repeated medical treatment.

A journalist who encountered him during a January 1989 fire and evacuation at Unit 22 recalled López Rega, nearly blind and calling for insulin.[S18]

Shortly before death he was transferred to a clinic in the Saavedra area. He died on **9 June 1989**, with aggravated diabetes generally identified as the principal cause.[S10]

His body was reportedly cremated at Chacarita Cemetery and his ashes scattered in the Río de la Plata. No final verdict had been delivered.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

No conventional private industrial empire or patent portfolio has been documented. His material power came principally through institutions he controlled or influenced:

### Argentine Federal Police

Provided his original career, contacts, security knowledge, and later personnel for his ministerial entourage.

### Ministry of Social Welfare

The center of his formal authority and of large budgets for:

- health;
- housing;
- pensions;
- social assistance;
- sports;
- disaster relief;
- publicity;
- international cooperation.

Later courts found that the Triple A’s unlawful association was organizationally anchored there.[S1][S2]

### Cruzada de Solidaridad Justicialista

A nominally charitable organization linked to Isabel and the ministry. It became a focus of fraud, forged-check, and misappropriation allegations.

### Argentina Potencia

An umbrella development and propaganda project rather than a single company. It combined welfare schemes, housing, international commerce, technological-nationalist imagery, and personal promotion.

### Argentine-Libyan institutions

Official agreements contemplated petroleum supply, commercial exchange, and a joint investment mechanism. The planned US$200 million Libyan contribution was to be paid in merchandise and equipment rather than necessarily as a cash transfer.[S16][S17]

### Publishing

López Rega published esoteric books and political testimony under his own name. The precise ownership and financing of his publishers and print runs remain obscure.

### Personal wealth

He lived modestly before entering Perón’s circle but maintained European, Bahamian, and Floridian residences during his fugitive years. Reports describe property, cash, jewelry, and accounts held through Cisneros or intermediaries.

There is strong reason to investigate the mismatch between his known pre-government income and later lifestyle. Nevertheless:

- no reliable total net-worth estimate was found;
- alleged Swiss holdings were not comprehensively recovered or adjudicated;
- the distinction among official travel funds, political funds, personal assets, and stolen public money remains incomplete.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Peronism

López Rega’s early Peronist credentials were amplified retrospectively. His police service occurred during Perón’s first presidency, but sustained high-level political activity is not documented before the 1960s.

By the late 1960s he supported:

- Perón’s personal leadership;
- vertical discipline;
- anti-communism;
- a “Third Position” rejecting both Soviet communism and liberal-capitalist domination;
- corporatist social organization;
- national power and spiritual renewal.

### Anti-communism

His anti-communism increasingly treated political conflict as metaphysical warfare between constructive and destructive forces. This language helped connect his occult cosmology to violent political exclusion.

### Esotericism

His esoteric system included:

- astrology;
- numerology;
- reincarnation;
- karmic causation;
- occult Christianity;
- Rosicrucian symbols;
- the “Great Architect” motif;
- spiritual hierarchies;
- an approaching New Age;
- correspondence among colors, sounds, planets, perfumes, and plants.

The evidence establishes sincere, sustained occult interests. Whether he literally believed every reported claim, manipulated the beliefs of Isabel, or used ritual theatrically for power cannot be conclusively separated.

### Fascism and the far right

López Rega is commonly classified as far-right or fascist because of his anti-Marxism, paramilitary associations, political violence, hierarchical nationalism, and P2 connections. His own published self-description remained Peronist and “Third Position,” not doctrinally liberal, conservative, or openly Nazi.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Juan Domingo Perón

Employer, patron, political leader, and source of López Rega’s formal power. Perón made him private secretary and minister, tolerated his gatekeeping, and granted his extraordinary police promotion. Their relationship was asymmetrical but politically consequential.

### María Estela Martínez de Perón

Closest political and personal ally. Shared spiritual language and continuous dependence produced widespread claims of psychological domination. Her own legal and political agency should not be erased: she appointed, retained, and eventually removed him within a broader power struggle.

### Eva Duarte de Perón

López Rega had limited physical proximity during her lifetime and later lived in the house containing her remains. The image of Evita became central to his effort to transfer charisma to Isabel.

### Victoria Montero

Spiritual mentor and gateway to Anael/Fraternidad Antiqua networks. Their later rupture is reported but poorly documented.

### José María Villone

Broadcasting acquaintance and facilitator of López Rega’s entry into Victoria Montero’s circle.

### Raúl Lastiri

Son-in-law, political associate, and provisional president. Lastiri’s succession greatly expanded López Rega’s power.

### Jorge Conti and Carlos Villone

Ministerial and press officials later convicted as links in the Triple A unlawful association.

### Juan Ramón Morales and Rodolfo Almirón

Police-linked security men repeatedly identified as operational figures in López Rega’s entourage and the Triple A.

### Alberto Villar and Luis Margaride

Hard-line senior policemen and allies in the security apparatus.

### Licio Gelli

P2 leader, transnational political intermediary, participant in matters involving Perón and Eva Perón’s remains.

### José Ber Gelbard

Economy minister and political rival. Gelbard’s developmental program competed with the López Rega circle; witnesses reported Gelbard identifying López Rega and the Triple A as enemies.

### Lorenzo Miguel

Powerful metalworkers’ and CGT leader. Initially part of the orthodox coalition, he became a decisive adversary during the Rodrigazo.

### Montoneros and the Peronist left

Initially participants in Perón’s return coalition; subsequently targets of expulsion, repression, and Triple A violence. López Rega became their principal symbolic internal enemy.

### Armed forces

His relations varied by service and officer. Some tolerated or cooperated with anti-left operations; others viewed his private apparatus as a rival. Military pressure helped force his departure in 1975.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Crimes

### Command of the Triple A

- **Court finding concerning the organization:** The Triple A was an unlawful, violent association based substantially in the Ministry of Social Welfare.[S1][S2]
- **Prosecutorial and testimonial case:** López Rega stood at the top of the ministry and was identified by numerous witnesses as political commander.
- **Personal legal status:** Processed and detained; never finally convicted because he died.
- **Family position:** Norma López Rega denies he created it.[S8]
- **Assessment:** The command allegation has powerful documentary, testimonial, and subsequent judicial support, but must not be mislabeled as a personal final conviction.

### Individual murders and kidnappings

López Rega was accused in connection with numerous attacks. Evidence varied among direct witness claims, organizational responsibility, inference from command, and later reconstruction. No blanket statement that he personally ordered every Triple A crime is justified.

### Misappropriation and fraud

Formal proceedings concerned fraud, malversation, forged documents, theft, and misuse of Cruzada or ministerial funds. These allegations supported extradition but did not culminate in a final conviction before his death.[S5][S19]

### Drug trafficking

Based principally on intelligence reporting and later secondary citation. **Unproven allegation.**

### Intelligence connections

Contacts with police, Argentine intelligence-linked figures, P2, and foreign anti-communist networks are documented or strongly reported. Direct employment by the CIA or another foreign intelligence service remains **unverified**.

### Responsibility for Perón’s death

Some polemical accounts accuse López Rega of hastening or causing Perón’s death through interference with physicians, occult treatment, stress, or deliberate neglect.

Evidence supports complaints that López Rega intruded into medical and household decisions. It does **not** establish poisoning, homicide, or a deliberate medical conspiracy. “López Rega killed Perón” remains a **political theory/legend**.[S24]

### Indemnification claim

After his death, his widow and daughter sought compensation on the theory that he had been politically persecuted. Argentina’s Supreme Court rejected the claim, leaving intact the lower court’s refusal.[S25]

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The sorcerer who hypnotized Isabel”

**Story:** López Rega gained control over Isabel through hypnosis, astrology, drugs, ritual manipulation, or spiritual terror.

**Origins and circulation:** Political opponents, journalists, former officials, and international media circulated it during and after her presidency.

**Evidence:** He was an occultist, acted as her spiritual confidant, and controlled access. There is no clinical or documentary proof of hypnosis or total psychological control.

**Effect:** The story turned López Rega into an Argentine Rasputin and sometimes diminished Isabel’s own political responsibility.

### Transfer of Evita’s spirit

**Story:** He performed rites over Eva Perón’s corpse to transfer her soul or charisma into Isabel.

**Evidence:** Corpse and practitioner occupied the same residence; household testimony supports unusual ritual conduct. Exact ceremonies remain unverified.

**Effect:** It became the most enduring occult image attached to him.

### “No te vayas, Faraón”

**Story:** At Perón’s deathbed he shook the dead president’s legs and invoked him as Pharaoh.

**Evidence:** Repeated witness-based accounts, but no recording or contemporaneous medical notation.

**Effect:** Presented the transfer of power as a grotesque court ritual and reinforced the sorcerer mythology.

### López Rega as an insignificant fool

**Story:** He was merely a ridiculous astrologer accidentally elevated by Isabel.

**Evidence against:** His long cultivation of access, institutional appointments, patronage network, foreign missions, police contacts, and survival across four presidencies demonstrate sustained political skill.

**Effect:** The caricature can obscure the institutional coalition that enabled him.

### López Rega as the sole cause of state violence

**Story:** Removing him would have removed Argentina’s repressive system.

**Evidence against:** The Triple A included police, civilian militants, state officials, union-linked groups, and intelligence actors. Some personnel continued under the 1976 dictatorship. State and non-state political violence had wider origins.

**Effect:** Concentrating blame on “the Brujo” allowed institutions and collaborators to minimize their roles.

### Secret CIA-Vatican-P2 master plan

**Story:** The three institutions jointly installed López Rega and created the Triple A.

**Evidence:** P2/Gelli ties are substantial; foreign governments monitored and interacted with Argentine officials. The consulted evidence does not prove a unified command plan.

**Classification:** **Conspiracy theory incorporating some documented relationships.**

### López Rega as protector or social benefactor

His own propaganda and later family testimony emphasize welfare programs, loyalty to Perón, charitable distribution, and nationalist development. Some ministry projects were real. This self-image does not resolve the evidence of clandestine violence and financial irregularities.

## Works by José López Rega

Bibliographical records are inconsistent, and editions were sometimes privately produced.

1. **_Astrología esotérica: secretos develados_**. Buenos Aires: Editorial Rosa de Libres, 1962. Approximately 737 pages. **Genuine published authorship.** Astrology, planetary correspondences, music, color, perfume, and spiritual instruction.[S12][S13]

2. **_Alpha y Omega: un mensaje para la humanidad_**. 1963. **Genuine published authorship.** Occult Christianity, karma, reincarnation, cosmic duality, technology, and Cold War anxieties.

3. **_La Astroagenda_**. 1965. **Genuine published work.** Daily planetary indications, saints’ days, auspicious hours, horoscopes, and practical advice.

4. **_El sabio hindú_**. Usually dated 1972; some catalogues or editions place it in 1977. **Published attribution genuine; original date disputed.** A spiritual dialogue or parable involving master, disciple, initiation, karma, and dualistic conflict.

5. **_El idioma de la verdad_**. Usually dated 1975. **Autobiographical/apologetic work under his name.** Defends his record, attacks hostile press, describes Perón as a paternal figure, and recounts political missions.

6. **Ministerial speeches and messages**, 1973–75. These include a recorded message summarizing the government and ministry’s work and his national broadcast after Perón’s death. Authorship may include staff drafting, as is normal for official speeches.[S26][S27]

7. **Official mission publications**, including material associated with *Misión argentina en Libia* and Social Welfare/“Argentina Potencia” propaganda. López Rega appears as official speaker or responsible minister; this does not establish sole literary authorship.[S16]

### Announced but unverified/unpublished titles

The jacket of *Astrología esotérica* reportedly announced:

- *Génesis de la nueva era*
- *¡El hombre! ¡Un mundo desconocido!*
- *Conocimientos espirituales*
- *Tratado de canto, impostación y arte escénico*
- *El libro de los desheredados*
- *Libro madre del éxito (Predicciones y guía diaria válida hasta el año 2000)*

No reliable evidence of public distribution was located.

An additional unpublished manuscript, excerpts of which appeared in later journalism, reportedly combined prophecy, political self-defense, accounts of Libya, and attacks on the left. Its final title, completion status, and intended publication history remain uncertain.[S28]

- Marcelo Larraquy, **_López Rega: el peronismo y la Triple A_**. Expanded/reworked editions, including 2011 and 2018. The most substantial modern narrative biography, based on interviews, archives, newspapers, and judicial material.[S14]

- Ignacio González Janzen, **_La Triple A_**. Investigative reconstruction of the organization and its personnel.
- Horacio Verbitsky, writings on Ezeiza, the Peronist right, and state violence.
- Eduardo Luis Duhalde, works and testimony concerning the Argentine counterrevolutionary state and investigations conducted with Rodolfo Ortega Peña.

### Academic and judicial studies

- Federal judgments and prosecutorial dossiers in the reopened Triple A cases are indispensable for separating organizational findings from allegations against deceased suspects.[S1][S2]
- Studies of the Peronist right, parapolice violence, Argentine anti-communism, and the transition from the Triple A to dictatorship provide institutional context.
- Research on Western esotericism in Latin America has begun treating López Rega’s books as intellectual artifacts rather than merely symptoms of madness.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

López Rega’s posthumous image has passed through several overlapping phases:

1. **Contemporary caricature:** the ridiculous astrologer or sinister palace sorcerer.
2. **Democratic-transition symbol:** a shorthand for the corruption and violence of Isabel Perón’s government, used prominently in Raúl Alfonsín’s political critique.
3. **Judicial reconstruction:** later prosecutions shifted attention from occult spectacle to the Ministry of Social Welfare’s personnel, weapons, financing, and command links.
4. **Institutional interpretation:** historians increasingly treat him not as an inexplicable aberration but as a broker among Perón’s household, the Peronist right, police, union and youth factions, international anti-communist networks, and patronage institutions.
5. **Family counter-memory:** Norma López Rega portrays him as loyal, charitable, falsely accused, and made into a scapegoat.
6. **Popular culture:** “El Brujo,” the deathbed rites, the corpse of Evita, and “Argentina’s Rasputin” remain better known than his published doctrines or the technical structure of the Triple A.
7. **Continuing legal relevance:** Later Triple A convictions established that pre-1976 parapolice violence formed part of Argentina’s state-repression history. López Rega’s death prevented the final personal judgment that might have fixed the precise scope of his criminal liability.

His central historical importance lies in the convergence, during his career, of personal access, state welfare patronage, police networks, political mysticism, anti-communism, clandestine violence, and transnational right-wing association.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are listed. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Juan Domingo Perón — **Documented fact**

López Rega entered the household at Puerta de Hierro in Madrid in 1966, became Perón’s private secretary, accompanied his return operations, and was appointed minister of Social Welfare in 1973. Perón retained him in office, promoted him to comisario general on 3 May 1974, and used him as a confidential intermediary.

### María Estela Martínez Cartas de Perón — **Documented fact**

López Rega joined Isabel’s Argentine entourage in 1965, became her secretary and spiritual confidant, traveled with her, and entered the Madrid household through her patronage. She retained him as minister after Juan Perón’s death and removed him under political and military pressure in July 1975.

### Eva Duarte de Perón — **Documented fact / reported legend**

López Rega was briefly in physical proximity to Eva during his police career, later used a photograph to strengthen his Peronist credentials, and lived at Puerta de Hierro when her embalmed body was kept there in 1971–73. Alleged rites intended to transfer Eva’s spirit to Isabel are **legend/report**, not established fact.

### Licio Gelli — **Documented/reported fact**

Gelli interacted with the Perón circle, assisted the return of Eva Perón’s remains, and developed a relationship with López Rega. López Rega appears in the documented P2 network and is widely identified as an affiliate. The precise initiation details remain uncertain.

### Muammar Muhammad Abu Minyar al-Gaddafi — **Reported official connection**

López Rega headed the January 1974 Argentine mission to Libya and negotiated with the Libyan government. Official agreements are documented. Whether López Rega and Gaddafi held a substantive private meeting, rather than contact through ministers and officials, is not clearly established in the consulted sources.

### Augusto Pinochet Ugarte — **No direct connection established**

Both belonged to the wider history of South American anti-communist repression, and Argentine parapolice and Chilean intelligence networks overlapped in the mid-1970s. No documented personal meeting, correspondence, or command relationship between López Rega and Pinochet was found.

### Salvador Guillermo Allende Gossens — **No direct connection established**

López Rega’s rise and Argentina’s relations with post-coup Chile affected the same regional political field, but no documented personal connection was found.

### Fidel Alejandro Castro Ruz — **No direct connection established**

Perón’s government maintained international contacts with Cuba while López Rega promoted domestic anti-communism. No personal meeting or correspondence between López Rega and Castro was found.

### Ernesto “Che” Guevara — **No direct connection established**

López Rega’s anti-Marxist ideology opposed revolutionary movements influenced by Guevara, but no personal encounter or correspondence is supported.

### Grigori Yefimovich Rasputin — **Mythic/symbolic association**

They could not have met: Rasputin died in 1916, the year López Rega was born. Journalists called López Rega the “Argentine Rasputin” because both were portrayed as occult advisers exercising influence over a ruler’s household. This is a reputational analogy, not a historical connection.

### Julius Evola, Miguel Serrano, René Guénon, Helena Petrovna Blavatsky, G. I. Gurdjieff, Carl Gustav Jung, and Aleister Crowley-related currents — **No documented personal connection**

López Rega’s writings belong broadly to the twentieth-century occult milieu, but the consulted sources do not establish meetings, correspondence, direct study under these people, or an explicitly acknowledged decisive influence. Similar vocabulary is insufficient evidence.

### Joaquín Trincado Mateo — **Unresolved possible intellectual proximity**

Trincado’s Argentine spiritualist networks and López Rega’s occult environment occupied related terrain. No source consulted established a direct meeting, organizational tie, or explicit acknowledged influence; the connection is therefore omitted as unproven.

### José López Rega — **Subject**

Included in the atlas roster as the present subject.

For the hundreds of other roster members, no source-supported crossing was identified. That absence is especially unsurprising for figures separated by centuries, but the review also found no defensible direct connection to most twentieth-century Argentine cultural figures listed in the roster.
