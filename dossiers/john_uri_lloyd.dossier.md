# John Uri Lloyd: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary note

This dossier reconstructs the life of John Uri Lloyd (1849–1936) from archival finding aids, institutional histories, patent records, digitized books, professional histories, court records, contemporary or near-contemporary biographical notices, and later scholarship. Lloyd left an exceptionally large paper trail: diaries beginning in adolescence, laboratory notebooks, correspondence, patents, company records, literary drafts, travel papers, scrapbooks, and hundreds—probably thousands—of published pieces. The principal surviving personal archive comprises 58 boxes and 26.25 linear feet at the Lloyd Library and Museum. Its finding aid is the backbone of this dossier.

Not all apparently precise claims in later summaries are equally secure. Three recurring discrepancies are retained rather than silently harmonized:

- Lloyd’s move to Cincinnati and first apprenticeship is dated 1863 in some local accounts and 1864 in Michael A. Flannery’s chapter chronology and in Lloyd’s surviving first diary.
- His professorship at the Eclectic Medical Institute is usually given as 1878–1895 in local histories, but the *Dictionary of Scientific Biography* entry gives 1878–1907, perhaps including a looser continuing instructional affiliation.
- Curtis Gates Lloyd created a library trust in 1917; other sources describe a broader trust arrangement by the three brothers in 1919. These are compatible stages, not necessarily contradictory events.

No reliable evidence was found that John Uri Lloyd was imprisoned, addicted, criminally prosecuted, convicted of fraud, involved in intelligence work or political violence, or personally implicated in money laundering or corruption. Relevant commercial, ideological, medical, and posthumous legal controversies are documented below.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** John Uri Lloyd.
- **Pronunciation:** A northern Kentucky historical source records *Uri* as “YOUR-eye.”
- **Name origin:** Named for his paternal grandfather, John Lloyd III, and maternal grandfather, Uri Webster.
- **Usual professional forms:** John Uri Lloyd; J. U. Lloyd; Professor John Uri Lloyd; John Uri Lloyd, Phar.M. Later commemorative and catalog records also attach Ph.D. and LL.D., principally honorary credentials.
- **Pseudonyms / fictional masks:** No securely documented conventional pseudonym. In *Etidorhpa*, Lloyd presents himself as the reluctant publisher of a manuscript transmitted through the fictional Llewellyn Drury and the being “I-Am-The-Man-Who-Did-It.” The near-rhyme and doubled initials of “Llewellyn Drury” and “John Uri” have encouraged autobiographical readings, but Drury is a literary persona, not a demonstrated pen name.
- **Born:** 19 April 1849, West Bloomfield, Ontario County, New York.
- **Died:** 9 April 1936, Van Nuys, Los Angeles County, California, ten days short of his eighty-seventh birthday.
- **Cause of death:** Pneumonia, according to local histories and obituary-derived accounts.
- **Disposition and burial:** Cremated; ashes interred at Hopeful Lutheran Church Cemetery, Florence, Boone County, Kentucky, near the remains of both wives.
- **Citizenship:** United States.
- **Principal occupations:** Pharmacist, pharmaceutical chemist, pharmacognosist, botanical-drug manufacturer, inventor, professor, professional-association officer, editor, bibliophile and library founder, historian of medicine and pharmacy, novelist, short-story writer, and lecturer.
- **Principal fields:** Pharmacy, pharmacognosy, pharmaceutical chemistry, botany and medicinal plants, economic botany, colloid and adsorption phenomena, alkaloid extraction and assay, Eclectic medicine, American materia medica, local-color fiction, speculative fiction, and occult/scientific allegory.
- **Principal residences:** West Bloomfield, New York (birth–1853); successive locations in Boone and Grant counties, Kentucky, including Burlington, Petersburg, Florence, and Crittenden (childhood); Cincinnati, Ohio (from adolescence); 2604 Harris Avenue, Norwood, Ohio (approximately the 1880s onward); 3901 Clifton Avenue, Cincinnati (later life); and temporary stays during travel in the United States, Europe, the Ottoman Empire, Mexico/Sea of Cortez region, and California.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Nelson Marvin Lloyd** (father) was a teacher, civil engineer, and surveyor. In 1853 he accepted work surveying a proposed Covington-to-Louisville railroad, prompting the family’s migration from New York to northern Kentucky. The Panic of 1854 ended the project; Nelson and Sophia then taught school in several Kentucky communities. Nelson periodically resumed surveying and died in 1882 after becoming ill while working in the Ozarks on a proposed Kansas City–Memphis road. John’s archive preserves material about the medical bills and dispute surrounding his death.

**Sophia Webster Lloyd** (24 May 1820–1903), mother, was a teacher, poet, and short-story writer. She married Nelson in 1847. Her poems appeared from her late teens in such periodicals as the *Philadelphia Saturday Courier*, *Rochester Democrat*, *Buffalo Messenger*, and *Rural New Yorker*. John published a volume of her poetry in 1887. Her literary work and intellectual curiosity are direct family precedents for his combination of science, collecting, reminiscence, and imaginative writing. Her papers contain correspondence, poetry, family history, and recollections.

### Siblings

The surviving family finding aids identify at least the following children of Nelson and Sophia:

- **Nelson Ashley Lloyd** (1851–1925), usually called Ashley, pharmacist and business manager/treasurer of Lloyd Brothers. He also held ownership interests in professional baseball, including the Cincinnati Reds and New York Giants, and collected art.
- **Curtis Gates Lloyd** (17 July 1859–11 November 1926), pharmacist, botanist, mycologist, collector, polemicist on fungal nomenclature, and co-founder of the Lloyd Library and Museum. He built a herbarium of roughly 59,000–60,000 fungal specimens and assembled the mycological side of the library. Never married and without direct heirs, he created the 1917 trust that secured the institution’s continuity.
- **Emma Lloyd Nead**, a sister named in Sophia’s archival biography.
- **Robert Llewellyn Lloyd**, a brother named in the same source; he died young. The exact dates and full life history were not established from the accessible sources.

The three surviving pharmacist brothers made a complementary partnership: John directed scientific research and product development; Ashley ran business operations; Curtis collected botanical literature and specimens and, although briefly admitted to the firm as a partner, was released from ordinary company duties to pursue collecting and mycology.

### Marriages and children

1. **Adeline “Addie” Meader** (c. 1855–1877), a schoolteacher and daughter of N. R. and Maria F. Meader. She and Lloyd married on 27 December 1876. Less than two weeks later, during their honeymoon in New York, she died of what contemporary/family accounts called “acute peritonitis.” The personal archive contains her obituary, funeral bulletin, portraits, and a lock of hair. No children are documented from this marriage. Her sudden death was a major personal catastrophe, though the accessible record does not justify diagnosing a prolonged psychiatric illness.

2. **Emma Rouse Lloyd** (11 April 1858–27 November 1932), childhood friend, daughter of Thomas Rouse and Nancy Ann Henderson, educated at White Haven, another private school in Crittenden, and Sayre Institute in Lexington. They married on 10 June 1880. Emma was a homemaker, civic and religious participant, genealogist, photographer, and active literary collaborator. She took photographs used in *Stringtown on the Pike*, urged Lloyd to add a female figure to *Etidorhpa*—the character who supplied the book’s title—and pushed him to complete *Our Willie*. Her own genealogical book, *Clasping Hands with Generations Past*, appeared in August 1932, shortly before her death.

Children of John and Emma:

- **John Thomas Lloyd**, born 30 April 1884; pharmacist and later company officer. He served in France during the First World War. After his father’s death he contested the mandated sale of the family company, then created a competing firm; the resulting litigation is discussed below.
- **Anna (“Annie”) Lloyd Welbourn**, born 18 November 1886. John died at her home in Van Nuys in 1936.
- **Dorothy Lloyd Brett**, born 28 October 1894.

The accessible sources do not support confident reconstruction of all descendants, spouses of the children, or the family’s full private finances.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1849–1864

Lloyd was born into a literate but economically mobile household in western New York. In 1853 the family moved to Burlington, Kentucky, for Nelson’s railroad-surveying position. When the venture collapsed after the Panic of 1854, the family shifted among Burlington, Petersburg, Florence, and later Crittenden as both parents sought teaching work and Nelson accepted surveying assignments.

The landscape of northern Kentucky became Lloyd’s durable imaginative geography. Plants, caves, springs, farm life, river fish, vernacular speech, Civil War memories, local religious divisions, Black and white folklore, Jewish peddlers, feuds, and rural legend later supplied his fiction and reminiscences. His parents encouraged outdoor observation and household experiment. This was informal formation rather than a conventional scientific curriculum.

During the Civil War, Lloyd witnessed a skirmish or troop action at Florence. He later described the experience in “Northern Kentucky in War Time” and other recollections. The event remained central to his memory, but his later fictional treatment of Confederate Kentucky was nostalgic and at times racially stereotyped; it should not be treated as a transparent factual record of wartime life.

In 1863 or 1864, at about fourteen, he crossed to Cincinnati to begin pharmacy work. One later account says his small stature and Kentucky accent brought taunts of “Johnny Reb” and that he learned to run to avoid being thrown into the canal. The anecdote comes through later biographical retelling and is plausible but not independently corroborated in the sources consulted.

## Education and Formation

### Formal schooling

Lloyd did not complete a college education. Sources variously describe private schooling and local common-school instruction in Kentucky. His scientific education was apprenticeship-based, self-directed, and laboratory-centered. Accounts that he memorized the entire United States Pharmacopeia in Latin express the scale of his self-training; they are repeated in local historical materials but were not independently verified from an examination record.

### Apprenticeship, 1864–1870

His first master was **William John MacAlester Gordon** (often shortened to William J. M. Gordon), a Cincinnati pharmacist. Lloyd began with menial shop tasks, then learned compounding, prescription practice, soda-fountain work, inventory, apparatus, and practical chemistry. His surviving diaries and expense books begin in 1864–65.

He then spent approximately two years with **George Eger**, a German/European-trained pharmacist. Later accounts emphasize that Gordon introduced the American shop tradition while Eger supplied a continental botanical and laboratory orientation. Lloyd’s writing repeatedly returned to both men as formative teachers.

By 1870 he was sufficiently established to buy chemical apparatus from Gordon; the signed agreement, dated 25 March 1870, survives. That transaction marks the transition from apprentice to independent investigator and commercial chemist.

### Formative scientific and medical network

- **John King**, Eclectic physician, teacher, and author/editor of the *American Dispensatory*, helped connect the Lloyds to systematic American botanical medicine.
- **John Milton Scudder**, leading theorist of “specific medication,” argued that definite botanical preparations should be matched in small, targeted doses to definite clinical indications. Lloyd’s company licensed Scudder’s “Specific Medicines” name and made the preparations at scale.
- **Harlow M. Merrell**, proprietor of an Eclectic drug house, hired Lloyd in 1871 to develop and standardize botanical preparations.
- **Curtis Gates Lloyd** supplied botanical research and specimens; **Nelson Ashley Lloyd** supplied managerial continuity.
- **J. Augustus Knapp**, artist and later occult illustrator, became Lloyd’s most important visual collaborator.

Lloyd’s own later formula was “education versus schooling”: he defended empirical knowledge and artisanal pharmacy while also demanding controlled experimentation, reproducible standards, assay methods, and full professional recognition.

## Early Career, 1870–1881

### Entry into pharmaceutical manufacturing

In 1871 John King and John Milton Scudder helped bring Lloyd to **H. M. Merrell & Company**, the principal Cincinnati manufacturer serving Eclectic physicians. Lloyd became laboratory manager/chemist and undertook systematic research on the Eclectic materia medica. His task was not merely compounding existing formulas: he worked on solvent choice, percolation, low-temperature concentration, alkaloid isolation, precipitation, color and stability, standardization, and scalable apparatus.

The company’s genealogical line was:

- 1845: William S. Merrell opened a drugstore at Court and Plum Streets.
- 1852: William S. Merrell & Co.
- 1861–62: T. C. Thorp entered; H. M. Merrell bought William Merrell’s share; H. M. Merrell & Co.
- 1869: Abner Thorp added.
- 1877: Lloyd bought T. C. Thorp’s interest; **Merrell, Thorp & Lloyd**.
- 1881: Nelson Ashley Lloyd replaced H. M. Merrell; **Thorp & Lloyd Brothers**.
- 1886: Curtis replaced Abner Thorp; **Lloyd Brothers**.
- 1924: incorporated as **Lloyd Brothers, Pharmacists, Inc.**

Lloyd’s 1877 purchase made him an owner at twenty-eight. The size and financing of his initial investment are not established in the accessible records, though company account books, contracts, and royalty statements survive.

### Marriage, bereavement, and remarriage

The death of Addie Meader in January 1877 occurred at the same moment Lloyd entered ownership. On 10 June 1880 he married Emma Rouse. The couple settled in Norwood, eventually at 2604 Harris Avenue. The nearby cross street was later renamed Lloyd Avenue. Local sources date the move to about 1882, though the presently standing building is reported in property databases as constructed in 1895, suggesting either an earlier house on the site, later rebuilding, or an imprecise address tradition.

### Teaching

Lloyd became professor of chemistry and pharmacy at the **Eclectic Medical Institute** in 1878. Most local and institutional summaries give 1878–1895; one scientific-biographical source gives 1878–1907. He also taught at the **Cincinnati College of Pharmacy**, 1883–1887. These appointments are notable because he lacked a conventional college degree. Professional mastery and publication substituted for academic credentials.

## Chronological Life History

### 1881–1894: consolidation, publication, and professional authority

With Ashley’s 1881 entry, the company became a family-controlled enterprise. John published the second edition of *The Chemistry of Medicines, Practical* (1881), a 451-page textbook for students, physicians, and pharmacists. His elixir manuals—first appearing in the early 1880s and repeatedly revised—codified formulas and preparation methods at a time when pleasant, stable liquid dosage forms were commercially important.

In 1882 Lloyd received the first of three **Ebert Prizes** for pharmaceutical research. The other awards came in 1891 and 1916. He published heavily on precipitates in fluid extracts, capillarity, solvents, assays, American botanical drugs, and the relation of pharmacy to physics.

Beginning in 1884 John and Curtis issued *Drugs and Medicines of North America*, designed as a comprehensive historical, botanical, chemical, pharmaceutical, and therapeutic treatment of the continent’s medicinal plants. It appeared as a quarterly through 1887 and never fulfilled its plan to cover the entire materia medica. The surviving volumes nonetheless remain a major primary source for late-nineteenth-century American pharmacognosy. Its incompletion was a practical failure of scale rather than of research quality.

In 1886 the three brothers completed the buyout that created Lloyd Brothers. John controlled research and development; Ashley was the commercial organizer; Curtis soon concentrated on collecting. In 1887 John served as president of the **American Pharmaceutical Association (APhA)**. Some later herbalist sources call him a twice-elected president; the accessible official/local summaries securely confirm the 1887 presidency but do not establish a second separate term.

The **Hydrastis controversy** of 1888–89 concerned goldenseal (*Hydrastis canadensis*), its alkaloids, preparations, assays, claims of priority, and/or commercial naming. Lloyd himself preserved a retrospective file titled “The Famous Hydrastis Controversy.” The documentary existence of the dispute is certain; the accessible finding aid does not supply enough evidence to assign wrongdoing. Lloyd continued to publish and manufacture Hydrastis preparations, later using such trade names as Lloyd’s Hydrastis and Lloydrastis.

During this period he increasingly criticized scientific authority when it hardened into credentialism. He did not reject experiment. His complaint was that “regular” academic institutions marginalized Eclectics and self-trained investigators. In later recollection he described receiving nearly a lifetime of the “cold shoulder” from learned men. The tension—outsider identity paired with a drive for professional standardization—underlies both his career and *Etidorhpa*.

### 1895–1904: *Etidorhpa*, literary celebrity, and industrial invention

Lloyd began *Etidorhpa* in the 1880s and privately announced it before publication with an intentionally opaque prospectus. More than 1,200 subscribers reportedly ordered the signed Author’s Edition. The book appeared in 1895, with extensive framing matter and illustrations by J. Augustus Knapp; a commercially distributed sixth edition followed in 1896 through Robert Clarke Company.

Its nested transmission claims are part of the fiction: Lloyd says he assumes responsibility from Llewellyn Drury, who received the account from “I-Am-The-Man-Who-Did-It.” The protagonist is abducted by a secret society, physically transformed, led into a Kentucky cave by a hairless and eyeless guide, exposed to vast fungi and mind-altering food, instructed in gravity, matter, energy, morality, alcohol, and consciousness, and carried toward an interior “Unknown Country.” “Etidorhpa” is “Aphrodite” reversed.

The novel surprised Lloyd’s professional colleagues. It fused practical diagrams and laboratory language with Hollow Earth speculation, alchemy, spiritualism, anti-dogmatic philosophy, temperance imagery, and esoteric initiation. Lloyd promoted it through strategic silence, controlled scarcity, signed copies, elaborate paratexts, and later additions. Contemporary accounts reported continuing sales years after publication; eventually the book went through approximately eighteen editions and translations into seven languages. Etidorhpa clubs appeared, and reports circulated of girls being given the name. These circulation claims are repeated in bibliographical and biographical sources, but a complete edition-by-edition census was not located.

His literary output broadened:

- *The Right Side of the Car* (1897), a Northern Pacific Railway story.
- *Stringtown on the Pike* (published 1900; some catalogs date 1901), a local-color novel based on Florence and northernmost Kentucky.
- *Warwick of the Knobs* (1901), another Stringtown County story.
- The Sam Hill tall tales, many drafted or published around 1902–04.
- *Red Head* (1903), illustrated and decorated by Reginald B. Birch.
- *Scroggins* (1904).

These books preserved dialect, landscape, folk narrative, Civil War recollection, and social types. They also reproduced the racial hierarchy, caricatured dialect, paternalism, and Confederate nostalgia common to white local-color writing. A 2014 scholarly thesis explicitly characterizes *Stringtown* as using racist stereotypes. This is a documented feature of the work, not evidence of a specific criminal or political act.

At the same time, Lloyd patented a succession of evaporation, condensation, and concentration devices. His “cold still” minimized heat damage to volatile or unstable plant constituents. Company history credits patented apparatus and exclusive drug processes with giving Lloyd Brothers a major competitive advantage.

### 1898–1901: war-revenue dispute and federal regulation

The Spanish–American War revenue law imposed taxes on proprietary medicines and related products. Lloyd preserved correspondence, Treasury decisions, exhibits, circulars, and his own account of the controversy. His title “Concerning the War Tax and Embalmed Beef” links pharmaceutical taxation with the broader wartime scandal over preserved military rations. The archive establishes active advocacy and business concern; it does not establish tax fraud or prosecution.

Lloyd favored pharmaceutical standards and later supported the regulatory principles that culminated in the **Pure Food and Drugs Act of 1906**. The Lloyd archive includes Pure Food Bill papers, Pharmacopeia proofs, standards proposals, and his article “The National Pure Food and Drug Act.” Institutional biography credits him as a significant participant in securing the act. The strongest nationally documented principal advocates were Harvey Washington Wiley and reform coalitions; Lloyd’s role should be described as an influential pharmacy/manufacturing advocate rather than sole architect.

### 1902–1913: library expansion and major travel

The brothers’ book collections outgrew the factory and Curtis’s house. In 1902 they bought 224 West Court Street to unite them. They later replaced that building with a four-story structure for books, museum, herbarium, and mycology. A separate library arose at 309 West Court Street and was expanded in 1912. By the mid-1920s the collection held roughly 50,000 volumes.

Lloyd traveled widely inside the United States and abroad. The archival record names Missouri, Wisconsin, Washington/Mount Rainier, Arizona cliff dwellings and Canyon de Chelly, the Sea of Cortez, Loreto, Aden, Asia Minor, Ephesus, Smyrna, Constantinople, and Japan. Not every trip has an established date.

The best-documented major journey is **1906 to the Ottoman Empire**. His papers contain passports, passenger lists, bills, receipts, letters of introduction, shipping lists, consular reports, correspondence with Ahmed Ramsay and Cherif Pasha, research on licorice, mastic, olive oil and Turkish drugs, and material on the American College for Girls at Constantinople. Later popular accounts describe a Smithsonian commission to survey licorice. The trip and licorice research are documented; the exact legal terms of a Smithsonian commission were not located in the consulted primary material. Lloyd also imported olive oil under a personal label.

He formed or maintained relationships with Grover Cleveland, Henry Irving, James Lane Allen, George Cary Eggleston, Frederick V. Coville, Henry S. Wellcome, Eli Lilly & Company, Burroughs Wellcome & Company, and numerous pharmacists and physicians. The archive contains Grover Cleveland correspondence from 1901–07 and Lloyd’s fishing/reminiscence file. Descriptions of Cleveland as a fishing companion are credible, but do not imply political office or patronage.

### 1909–1919: pharmaceutical history, adsorption, and war work

Lloyd turned increasingly toward history and bibliography:

- *Life and Medical Discoveries of Samuel Thomson* (1909) reconstructed Thomsonian medicine from early literature.
- *The Eclectic Alkaloids, Resins, Resinoids, Oleo-Resins and Concentrated Principles* (1910) treated the concentrated remedies of American sectarian pharmacy.
- *History of the Vegetable Drugs of the Pharmacopoeia of the United States* (1911) traced the introduction of official vegetable drugs.
- He and Curtis republished/facsimiled their Hydrastis work (1908) and continued monographs on coca, witch hazel, and other plants.

His most celebrated late scientific work arose from experiments with hydrated aluminum silicate/fuller's earth. He found that the clay adsorbed alkaloids and their salts from solution. Patent **US 1,048,712**, filed 23 February 1912 and granted 31 December 1912, explicitly claims precipitation, separation, or immobilization of alkaloids with aluminum silicate. “Lloyd’s reagent” became an analytical and preparative tool. **Alcresta** products bound alkaloids to the adsorbent, providing a buffered or slowly released form in the pharmaceutical understanding of the period. Lloyd described testing salts of morphine, quinine, cocaine, and other alkaloids on the tip of his tongue; this is documented self-experimentation, not evidence of addiction.

In 1913–19 Lloyd licensed or contracted aspects of his patent work to **Eli Lilly & Company**. Patent deeds, correspondence, and royalty statements for 1919–23 survive. He also sold or supplied apparatus plans to Canada Dry and Burroughs Wellcome. Exact royalty totals are not available from the accessible finding aid.

During the First World War he worked on botanical and alkaloidal preparations, including an atropine sulfate preparation later described in local histories as useful for eye injuries. The archive also documents son John Thomas’s service in France. Lloyd wrote on wartime drug prices and American supply.

His third Ebert Prize came in 1916, associated with his hydrous aluminum silicate/alkaloid work. In 1917 Curtis created the library trust. A broader 1919 trust arrangement by the brothers ensured public access and institutional continuity.

### 1920–1932: honors, incorporated business, family losses, and late writing

In 1920 Lloyd received the **Remington Honor Medal**, widely regarded as American pharmacy’s highest professional honor. His archive preserves the acceptance address. In 1921 the American Drug Manufacturers Association published the first version of *Origin and History of All the Pharmacopeial Vegetable Drugs, Chemicals and Preparations*, with a revised 8th/9th revision edition issued by Caxton Press in 1929.

Lloyd Brothers formally incorporated in 1924. By this time the company had developed approximately **379 Specific Medicines** and sold to Eclectic, homeopathic, and “regular” physicians. The finding aid states that it had effectively cornered its specialized “ethical drug” market by the early twentieth century. “Ethical” here is a period trade term for physician-directed, non-secret professional medicines, not a modern moral certification.

Ashley died in 1925 and Curtis in 1926, leaving John the last of the three founders and principal strategic authority. Curtis’s death transferred much collecting responsibility to the trust. John continued scientific writing, civic speaking, charitable donations, and work on the library.

The family moved in later life to **3901 Clifton Avenue**, a monumental Romanesque house designed by James W. McLaughlin for coal merchant Solomon P. Kineon. Sources disagree on the building’s construction date (1879, 1887–88, or 1890) and on Lloyd’s exact occupancy (purchase in 1909 versus move after the First World War). The National Register securely identifies the address, architect, Romanesque style, and 1973 listing. The house became a visible symbol of Lloyd’s prosperity.

In 1930 Lloyd issued *Felix Moses, the Beloved Jew of Stringtown on the Pike*, based on a real Boone County Jewish peddler (1827–1886), in a signed Author’s Edition of 500 copies with J. Augustus Knapp illustrations. The work records affection and local memory but also frames Moses as an exceptional ethnic “type”; both the memorializing intention and period stereotyping belong in its interpretation.

Emma’s *Clasping Hands with Generations Past* appeared in August 1932. She died on 27 November. John had promised her to complete *Our Willie*, a sequel begun after *Stringtown*; he finished it in 1934, though its publication history is obscure.

### 1933–1936: final years

On 4 January 1933 Lloyd executed a trust agreement/will. He remained active in correspondence, historical writing, and public life. His files include political caricatures from 1932–33, an Albert Einstein dinner on 15 March 1933, and a return visit to Boone County with reporter Frank Y. Grayson.

In 1934 he received the **Procter International Award** of the Philadelphia College of Pharmacy and Science. He worked on *The Land That Was, but Yet Exists*, conceived as a sequel or successor to *Etidorhpa*, and corresponded with Knapp about it in 1935. The project remained unfinished.

While visiting daughter Annie in Van Nuys, California, he contracted pneumonia and died on 9 April 1936. His heirs donated his personal papers to the Lloyd Library that year.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Lloyd Brothers, Pharmacists

**Ownership and management.** Lloyd entered Merrell, Thorp & Lloyd as a partner in 1877; family control expanded in 1881 and 1886. John’s contribution was research, proprietary process design, scientific publication, and professional credibility. Ashley managed finances and sales. Curtis provided botanical literature/specimens but was largely freed from operations.

**Products.** The firm manufactured tinctures, fluid extracts, “Specific Medicines,” alkaloidal preparations, and specialized botanical remedies. Surviving specimens include preparations of Hydrastis, Echinacea, Lobelia, Nux vomica, Pulsatilla, Sanguinaria, Stillingia, Stramonium, saw palmetto, witch hazel, and many other plants. Some ingredients—opium, cocaine/coca, belladonna, stramonium, nux vomica—are dangerous or controlled today. Their manufacture was legal in the relevant period; historical sale is not evidence of criminal trafficking.

**Market position.** By the early 1900s the company supplied Eclectic, homeopathic, and regular physicians nationally. It used scientific literature, dose books, price lists, physician education, trademarks, and patented apparatus. Its commercial success funded substantial property, travel, collecting, philanthropy, and the library.

**Finances.** Exact personal net worth, annual profit, and transaction price for the 1938 sale were not established. A 1942 federal appellate opinion reveals that John Thomas Lloyd’s inherited share of proceeds from his father’s stock exceeded **$100,000**, a substantial sum at the time, demonstrating that John Uri’s holdings were valuable. The same opinion confirms that John Thomas had first tried unsuccessfully in Ohio courts to stop the sale.

**Posthumous corporate chain.** John’s will required sale of the company within five years. John Thomas Lloyd and executor Thomas Rouse contested this, but S. B. Penick & Company purchased it in 1938. A local management consortium acquired stock in 1948; Richard Lockton-led interests took control in 1958; Intercontinental Chemical Corporation, a Farbwerke Hoechst holding company, bought it in 1960; it became Hoechst Pharmaceuticals in 1967 and a division of American Hoechst in 1968; Cincinnati operations ended with consolidation in New Jersey in 1969.

### Technical significance and limits

- **Low-temperature extraction/concentration:** Lloyd’s evaporators and cold still sought to conserve volatile oils and heat-sensitive constituents. This was a genuine manufacturing innovation.
- **Standardization:** He worked on comparative assays and reproducible strength when botanical preparations varied significantly by source, season, storage, and process.
- **Lloyd’s reagent:** Hydrated aluminum silicate/fuller's earth adsorbed alkaloids from solution. Patent documentation makes the discovery and claims precise.
- **Alcresta:** Commercial alkaloid–clay complexes grew from the adsorption work and involved Eli Lilly licensing/royalties.
- **“Father of colloidal chemistry”:** This honorific appears in the Lloyd archive’s institutional biography and older pharmacy tributes. It overstates his place in the international history of colloid science, which included Thomas Graham, Wolfgang Ostwald, and many others. A more defensible formulation is that he was an early and important American pharmaceutical investigator of colloidal and adsorption phenomena.
- **Therapeutic efficacy:** Historical standardization and manufacturing quality do not establish that every Specific Medicine was clinically effective by modern controlled-trial standards. Many were therapeutically active; some were toxic; indications were often based on Eclectic experience rather than modern trials.

### Lloyd Library and Museum

The library began as working collections for John’s laboratory research and Curtis’s botany/mycology. It became a separate physical institution after 1902. Curtis’s 1917 trust and the broader trust arrangements ensured continuation and public access. A purpose-built modern building opened at 917 Plum Street in 1970. The institution now preserves not only rare books in pharmacy, botany, natural history, and Eclectic medicine but the archives of the Lloyd family, company, Eclectic Medical Institute, and related figures.

The library is Lloyd’s most durable institutional legacy. Claims that John alone accumulated all later holdings should be avoided: Curtis was essential, Ashley’s business success funded the enterprise, and later librarians continued collecting.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Politics and civic work

Later accounts identify Lloyd as a lifelong Democrat. He held no major elected state or federal office. In Norwood he participated in civic planning and served as president of a planning commission. He criticized municipal politicians, poor street layout, water policy, and speculative development while promoting Norwood as a “city of the future.” His archive contains “To the Taxpayers and Water Consumers of Norwood,” “Norwood’s Opportunity,” political caricatures, Americanization committee minutes (1928), and writings on elections and civic ethics.

His surviving network included politicians, but it is inaccurate to infer an intelligence or covert-political role. Grover Cleveland correspondence and companionship were social/personal; the Ottoman journey had consular introductions and State Department reports typical of scientific-commercial travel.

### Civil War memory, race, and regional ideology

Lloyd’s boyhood occurred in a border-state slave society and during Civil War conflict. His local-color fiction preserved white northern Kentucky memory, often sympathetically depicting Confederate figures and the defeated South. It also used racial dialect, caricature, paternalism, and stereotyped Black characters. These are features of the published texts and have been identified in modern scholarship.

This record does **not** establish membership in the Ku Klux Klan, participation in racial violence, or prosecution for civil-rights abuse; no such evidence was located. The relevant controversy is literary and commemorative: his romantic regional mythology can obscure slavery, Black agency, and Unionist Kentucky.

### Eclecticism and professional ideology

Lloyd’s Eclecticism combined:

- suspicion of heroic bleeding, drastic purging, and indiscriminate surgery;
- commitment to indigenous and introduced botanical drugs;
- respect for empirical and vernacular knowledge, including some Native American plant use;
- small, “specific” doses matched to signs and conditions;
- insistence on assay, purity, stable manufacture, and accurate labeling;
- resistance to monopolization of legitimacy by “regular” medical schools;
- skepticism toward isolated “active principles” when whole-plant preparations behaved differently.

He was neither simply an anti-scientific herbalist nor fully aligned with emerging corporate pharmacology. His factory, patents, national distribution, and Lilly contracts made him part of the pharmaceutical industry; his ideology defended the older pharmacist-manufacturer and botanical materia medica against standardization that erased therapeutic pluralism.

### Religion and metaphysics

Family and archive materials indicate conventional Christian participation—church history, Bible verses on healing, church-society talks, Hopeful Lutheran burial—alongside broad heterodox curiosity. *Etidorhpa* treats a Creator, spiritual evolution, moral purification, alchemy, secret societies, unseen forces, and the inadequacy of materialist dogma. Lloyd’s language moves between natural theology and occult initiation.

No evidence establishes formal membership in Theosophy, Spiritualism, an occult order, or Freemasonry. His archive includes a reminiscence of Odd Fellows Lodge membership. The novel’s Masonic-like kidnapping is literary engagement with the William Morgan affair; it should not be converted into proof that Lloyd was exposing a lodge from personal initiation.

### Artistic practice and collaboration

Lloyd understood books as designed objects. *Etidorhpa*’s signed limited edition, typography, plates, author photograph with skeleton, nested documents, and maps are integral to its effect. J. Augustus Knapp visualized the eyeless guide, giant fungi, abduction, experiments, the goddess Etidorhpa, and the internal-earth diagram. Reginald B. Birch illustrated *Red Head*. Emma Lloyd provided photographs and editorial pressure. Lloyd also commissioned or preserved portraits, pastels, and elaborate illustrations of Stringtown characters.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Professional exclusion and sectarian conflict — documented controversy

Eclectic medicine was opposed by organized “regular” medicine, and its schools lost authority as licensing and medical education consolidated. Lloyd experienced exclusion yet gained unusually broad respect because of his experimental work. Both sides’ claims require separation: regular critics correctly questioned variable remedies and weak clinical evidence; Lloyd correctly identified credentialism, commercial interest, and the loss of valuable botanical knowledge.

### Hydrastis controversy, 1888–89 — documented dispute, details unresolved here

Lloyd preserved extensive material under “The Famous Hydrastis Controversy.” It involved assays, preparations, nomenclature, and priority in goldenseal chemistry. No consulted court or disciplinary finding establishes fraud. Further judgment requires the original correspondence and trade-journal exchanges in Box 2 and related pharmaceutical files.

### Proprietary medicines and dangerous substances — documented commercial practice

Lloyd Brothers sold preparations of toxic and psychoactive plants and alkaloids under the laws and standards of the period. Lloyd also patented nicotine extraction and wrote on coca, opium, belladonna, and other potent drugs. Modern danger does not itself prove historical misconduct. Conversely, “ethical drugs” and botanical origin did not guarantee safety or efficacy.

### Self-experimentation — documented, addiction unproved

In discussing alkaloidal adsorption, Lloyd stated that he placed salts including morphine, quinine, and cocaine on his tongue. This is evidence of hazardous laboratory self-testing. No evidence located supports claims that he was addicted or that *Etidorhpa* was written under chronic drug intoxication.

### Drug-induced origin of *Etidorhpa* — speculation, not established fact

Later writers have proposed cannabis, opium, nightshade, henbane, jimsonweed, or psychoactive mushrooms as sources of the novel’s visions. The book’s pharmacological imagery and Lloyd’s access make the hypothesis imaginable; no diary, laboratory record, confession, medical record, or contemporaneous witness consulted proves intentional visionary dosing. It belongs under rumor and interpretive speculation.

### Race and Confederate nostalgia — documented textual issue

Lloyd’s Stringtown fiction includes racial stereotypes and romanticized Confederate memory. This can be demonstrated from the books and is noted by literary scholarship. It should neither be erased nor inflated into unsupported allegations of violent or clandestine activity.

### Pure Food and Drug reform — documented advocacy, magnitude debated

Lloyd’s archive and institutional biography show involvement in standards and pure-food legislation. Claims that he was a “major player” are plausible within organized pharmacy but less documented in national legislative histories than Harvey Wiley’s role. His position also served reputable manufacturers by disadvantaging adulterated competitors; public-health and business motives were compatible.

### Wealth and patents — documented, totals unavailable

Lloyd grew wealthy through a family firm that used patents, trademarks, proprietary processes, physician marketing, and a vertically integrated research library. This is not evidence of corruption. The absence of accessible balance sheets prevents a reliable personal-net-worth estimate.

### Posthumous will contest and unfair-competition case — formal court findings

John’s will ordered sale of his stock within five years. Son John Thomas unsuccessfully fought the sale through the Ohio courts, received more than $100,000, resigned as vice-president, and formed John T. Lloyd Laboratories in 1938. He took a copy of the old company’s customer mailing list and competed with similar preparations.

In **John T. Lloyd Laboratories, Inc. v. Lloyd Brothers Pharmacists, Inc., 131 F.2d 703 (6th Cir. 1942)**, the federal courts addressed trademark confusion, use of the Lloyd name, customer lists, trade secrets, and claims to be successor to John Uri’s business. The district court enjoined several uses; the Sixth Circuit reviewed the decree. These were acts of the son and successor businesses after John Uri’s death, but the litigation clarifies the inheritance, sale, value, and contested ownership of his commercial legacy.

### Criminality and intelligence allegations — no evidence found

No reliable source consulted reports arrest, indictment, conviction, imprisonment, intelligence-service employment, political violence, money laundering, or personal corruption by John Uri Lloyd. The existence of consular reports, State Department introductions, and elite correspondence in a traveler’s archive does not establish intelligence activity.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. *Etidorhpa* as a true transmitted manuscript

1. **Story:** A mysterious manuscript was dictated by a transformed initiate to Llewellyn Drury and reluctantly published by Lloyd.
2. **Appearance:** Built into the 1895 Author’s Edition and expanded in subsequent editions through letters, editorial notes, and pseudo-documentary apparatus.
3. **Circulators:** Lloyd himself as literary impresario; reviewers, occult readers, Hollow Earth enthusiasts, and conspiracy communities.
4. **Evidence:** The surviving drafts and literary manuscripts in Lloyd’s archive identify it as his composed and revised work. No independent Drury manuscript or historical “I-Am-The-Man” has been established.
5. **Effect:** The hoax-like frame gave the novel enduring ambiguity and allowed readers to treat speculation as revelation.

### 2. “I-Am-The-Man-Who-Did-It” was William Morgan

1. **Story:** The abducted narrator is William Morgan, whose threatened Masonic exposé preceded his disappearance in 1826.
2. **Appearance:** The novel closely echoes the Morgan affair; later commentators make the identification explicit.
3. **Circulators:** Anti-Masonic readers, occult historians, Hollow Earth enthusiasts, and literary critics.
4. **Evidence:** Lloyd’s mother grew up near the geographical and cultural memory of the Morgan affair, and the abduction narrative unmistakably invokes it. But the book does not straightforwardly name the protagonist Morgan, and the subterranean sequel is fiction.
5. **Effect:** The association ties *Etidorhpa* to American anti-Masonry and makes it attractive to conspiracy traditions.

### 3. Lloyd as Freemason or secret-order initiate

1. **Story:** Lloyd encoded actual Masonic/occult initiations in the book.
2. **Appearance:** Modern esoteric readings and promotional descriptions.
3. **Circulators:** Occult publishers, conspiracy forums, and readers of Knapp’s later work with Manly P. Hall.
4. **Evidence:** The archive documents Odd Fellows reminiscence, not confirmed Masonic membership. Literary knowledge of Masonic imagery was widely available after Morgan exposés.
5. **Effect:** Recast Lloyd from pharmacist-author into occult adept. The claim remains unverified.

### 4. The novel as a drug trip or first “psychedelic” plant novel

1. **Story:** Lloyd’s giant fungi, ego dissolution, time distortion, and cosmic visions record personal psychoactive-drug experience.
2. **Appearance:** Twenty-first-century psychedelic criticism and online discussion; sometimes linked to Lloyd’s documented taste-testing of alkaloids.
3. **Circulators:** Drug historians, psychedelic readers, Public Domain Review commentary, occult culture.
4. **Evidence:** Strong thematic parallels and access to psychoactive plants; no direct documentary proof of visionary intoxication. Taste testing for analytical work is not the same as consuming hallucinogenic doses.
5. **Effect:** Revived the novel for modern psychonautic readership.

### 5. Lloyd as prophet of free energy, relativity, string theory, or modern physics

1. **Story:** *Etidorhpa* predicted gravity changes inside Earth, matter–energy transformation, cars powered without fuel, or later physics.
2. **Appearance:** Newspaper comparisons by 1928 and modern popular profiles.
3. **Circulators:** Journalists, admirers, alternative-science communities.
4. **Evidence:** The novel speculates imaginatively about gravity, energy, matter, and internal Earth geometry. Some individual observations resemble later ideas; many physical claims are wrong, derivative, metaphorical, or too vague to count as scientific prediction.
5. **Effect:** Helped create the posthumous “scientist and seer” image.

### 6. Hollow Earth geography as literal claim

1. **Story:** Lloyd possessed knowledge of a habitable subterranean world reached through Kentucky caves.
2. **Appearance:** Hollow Earth reading lists and conspiracy sites.
3. **Circulators:** Alternative cosmology and subterranean-civilization communities.
4. **Evidence:** The archival drafts, allegorical structure, and impossible biology support classification as fiction/scientific allegory. Kentucky cave geography and Mammoth Cave traditions supplied a real landscape.
5. **Effect:** Kept Lloyd’s name alive outside pharmacy after his regional fiction faded.

### 7. “Father of colloidal chemistry”

1. **Story:** Lloyd founded colloidal chemistry.
2. **Appearance:** Pharmacy memorials and institutional summaries.
3. **Circulators:** Professional admirers and local history.
4. **Evidence:** He made meaningful pharmaceutical contributions to adsorption/colloid practice; the international field predates and exceeds his work.
5. **Effect:** Elevated a genuine specialist achievement into a sweeping paternity claim.

### 8. A direct line from Lloyd Brothers to Eli Lilly

1. **Story:** Lloyd Brothers became, or was the foundation of, today’s Eli Lilly and Company.
2. **Appearance:** Some Cincinnati popular histories.
3. **Circulators:** Local journalists and simplified corporate genealogies.
4. **Evidence:** Lloyd licensed patents to Eli Lilly, but Lloyd Brothers was sold to S. B. Penick and ultimately Hoechst, not merged into Lilly during Lloyd’s life. Later corporate consolidations make broad modern-brand claims easy to confuse. It is more accurate to describe Lilly as a licensee/business counterparty, not Lloyd Brothers’ direct original identity.
5. **Effect:** Connects regional pharmaceutical history to a familiar modern company but distorts the corporate chain.

## Works by John Uri Lloyd

### Bibliographical caution

No complete published bibliography of Lloyd’s thousands of articles exists. His archive devotes Boxes 53–57 to published articles, arranged A–O and P–W, and additional boxes to manuscripts. ### Scientific, pharmaceutical, botanical, and historical books

- **Pharmaceutical Preparations: Elixirs, Their History, Formulae, and Methods of Preparation**. First editions in the early 1880s; substantially revised and issued under titles including **Elixirs and Flavoring Extracts: Their History, Formulae, and Methods of Preparation** (copyright 1892; later editions/reprints including 1910). ## Death and Later Life

The deaths of Ashley (1925), Curtis (1926), and Emma (1932) progressively narrowed Lloyd’s personal and professional circle. He continued working after all three losses. He completed Emma’s requested *Our Willie*, published *Felix Moses*, accepted the Procter award, organized his estate, and planned an *Etidorhpa* successor.

Pneumonia caused his death at Annie Welbourn’s Van Nuys home on 9 April 1936. There is no evidence of suicide, overdose, assassination, or mysterious disappearance. The family returned his cremated remains to Florence. His will’s compulsory-sale clause ended direct family control of the pharmaceutical company and produced the later succession conflict.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Pharmacy and medicine

Lloyd remained a canonical figure in American pharmacy history for botanical-drug standardization, extraction apparatus, alkaloid assay, hydrated aluminum silicate adsorption, professional leadership, and historical bibliography. The decline of Eclectic medical schools after the 1930s reduced clinical use of his system. Later herbalists recovered Lloyd Brothers texts as unusually detailed records of nineteenth-century American plant pharmacy.

Modern reassessment separates several things once blended together:

- sound pharmaceutical engineering and analytical work;
- historically important but variably evidenced therapeutic practice;
- commercially successful proprietary products;
- respect for traditional/empirical plant knowledge;
- polemics against institutional medicine;
- and retrospective claims of scientific priority that can be exaggerated.

### Library and archive

The Lloyd Library and Museum transformed a company research collection into a globally significant independent institution. Its survival makes detailed scrutiny—including criticism of Lloyd—possible. The library’s value is collective: John’s pharmacy, Curtis’s mycology and book hunting, Ashley’s business, Emma’s documentation, family trusts, and later professional stewardship.

### Literature

The Stringtown novels once had mainstream regional popularity but fell from general circulation. They now interest scholars of Kentucky local color, border-state memory, dialect, race, and the literary construction of community. *Etidorhpa* followed the reverse trajectory: always eccentric, it became a cult classic among science-fiction historians, occultists, artists, psychonauts, Hollow Earth enthusiasts, book designers, and conspiracy readers.

Its legacy includes:

- nested pseudo-documentary storytelling;
- visualization of altered states and nonhuman guides;
- a synthesis of laboratory reasoning and esoteric initiation;
- Kentucky caves as metaphysical geography;
- Knapp’s influential occult imagery;
- and a marketing strategy based on scarcity, secrecy, and reader participation.

Claims that H. P. Lovecraft admired the book circulate widely, but the sources consulted did not locate a primary Lovecraft letter or essay confirming the degree of influence. This should remain a reported association, not an established literary relationship.

## Atlas Connections

### Albertus Magnus

- **[T1-]** In *Etidorhpa* (1895), Lloyd described Albertus Magnus as a skilled medieval investigator and believer in alchemical transmutation. **Documented fact.** [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37775/pg37775-images.html)

### Aristotle

- **[T1-]** Lloyd discussed Aristotle repeatedly in *Etidorhpa*, particularly Aristotle’s proposed ether, alleged observational errors, and the attribution to him of the alchemical *Secret of Secrets*. **Documented fact.** [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37775/pg37775-images.html)

### Cagliostro

- **[T1-]** *Etidorhpa* contains an extended assessment of Giuseppe Balsamo, Count Cagliostro, portraying him as an extraordinary but discredited synthesis of alchemist, magician, astrologer, mesmerist, Freemason, performer, and “pretender.” **Documented fact.** [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37775/pg37775-images.html)

### Francis Bacon

- **[T1-]** While praising Roger Bacon in *Etidorhpa*, Lloyd expressly compared his intellect with that of his “illustrious namesake, Lord Bacon,” meaning Francis Bacon. The reference is brief rather than a sustained treatment. **Documented fact.** [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37775/pg37775-images.html)

### Johannes Kepler

- **[T1-]** In *Etidorhpa*’s appended account of the historical scientist Daniel Vaughn, Lloyd listed Kepler among the major scientific authors represented in Vaughn’s working library. This is a documented textual reference, not evidence of a stronger intellectual lineage. **Documented fact.** [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37775/pg37775-images.html)

### Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

- **[T1-]** Lloyd named Leibniz—spelled “Leibnitz” in the text—among the scientific authorities in Daniel Vaughn’s library in the appendix to *Etidorhpa*. **Documented fact.** [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37775/pg37775-images.html)

### Lord Kelvin

- **[T1-]** In *Etidorhpa*, Lloyd’s fictionalized scientific discussion directed readers to the experiments of Sir William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, concerning the convertibility and correlation of heat, light, and electricity. **Documented fact.** [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37775/pg37775-images.html)

### Isaac Newton

- **[T1-]** Lloyd characterized Newton in *Etidorhpa* as a dividing light between older speculative reasoning and modern experimental science, while criticizing later scientists for carrying Newtonian material analysis too far. Newton also appears among the authorities in Daniel Vaughn’s library. **Documented fact.** [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37775/pg37775-images.html)

### Paracelsus

- **[T1-]** Lloyd identified Paracelsus in *Etidorhpa* as an alchemical authority and discussed his belief that occult philosophy offered greater insight than experimental chemistry into metallic transmutation. **Documented fact.** [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37775/pg37775-images.html)

### Pierre-Simon Laplace

- **[T1-]** The appendix to *Etidorhpa* states that Daniel Vaughn respected Laplace as a scientist and writer but rejected important parts of his nebular hypothesis; Lloyd reproduced Vaughn’s technical criticism of Laplace’s account of solar-ring motion. **Documented fact.** [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37775/pg37775-images.html)

### Ramon Llull

- **[T1-]** Lloyd named Ramon Llull under the conventional Anglicized form “Raymond Lully” among the alchemical authorities systematically investigated by the narrator of *Etidorhpa*, later describing “Raymond” as an intense but short-lived alchemical enthusiast. **Documented fact.** [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37775/pg37775-images.html)

### Roger Bacon

- **[T1-]** *Etidorhpa* gives Roger Bacon unusually sustained treatment: Lloyd praised his intellect, recounted his imprisonment and Hermetic beliefs, described his claims about alchemical gold and predictive mirrors, and quoted his anticipation of rapidly moving horseless vehicles. **Documented fact.** [Primary text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/37775/pg37775-images.html)

No reliable evidence was found for correspondence, friendship, personal meetings, shared-site transmission, or prophetic fulfillment between Lloyd and other roster members. In particular, Lloyd’s archive contains a folder titled “Albert Einstein Dinner, March 15, 1933,” but the finding aid does not establish that Lloyd attended or met Einstein; the dinner itself was held at New York’s Hotel Commodore, so no T4 or T5 connection is assigned. Likewise, claims that H. P. Lovecraft read or was influenced by *Etidorhpa* remain insufficiently supported by a located primary Lovecraft source and are omitted.
