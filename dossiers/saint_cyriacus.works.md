## Works by Saint Cyriacus of Rome

### No securely attributable obra

- **Late third or early fourth century:** No text, inscription, building, artwork, or other independently identifiable object can be securely attributed to Cyriacus. This is an inference from the standard hagiographic repertory: the *Bibliotheca Hagiographica Latina* catalogues a substantial Cyriacus dossier (BHL 2056–2066), but these are anonymous or later texts **about** Cyriacus, not works made by him; even his traditional date of death, often given as 303, is uncertain.

### Legendary projects and ritual manifestations

- **No securely recoverable date:** Assistance to the laborers constructing the Baths of Diocletian. The later passion tradition represents this as charitable service and, in some versions, forced labor; it does not identify any portion of the baths as Cyriacus’s obra, and the Bollandist tradition regards the narrative as historically unreliable.

- **No securely recoverable date:** Baptisms traditionally attributed to Cyriacus. The passion credits him with baptizing Artemia, the supposed daughter of Diocletian, and—after a journey to Persia—Jobia, King Sapor, and hundreds of others. These would be ritual acts or manifestations rather than durable authored works, and their historicity is seriously disputed: Diocletian’s known daughter was Valeria, while the story’s Persian chronology is incompatible with the reigns of the kings named.

- **No securely recoverable date:** Exorcisms and healings traditionally attributed to Cyriacus. The passion relates cures of blind prisoners and exorcisms of Artemia and Jobia; in the narrative these are divine manifestations mediated by the saint, not objects he “made.” They belong to the same historically unreliable legendary cycle and cannot be entered as securely attested works.

### Posthumous creations not made by Cyriacus

- **Fourth century onward:** The cult, feast, passions, churches, relic collections, translations, images, and liturgical texts associated with Cyriacus are creations of later communities and authors, not works by the saint. The earliest secure evidence is the *Depositio martyrum* preserved in the Chronography of 354, which records Cyriacus and companions at the seventh milestone of the Via Ostiensis on 8 August but attributes no work to him.
