# Jorge Luis Borges: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs the documented life, work, associations, politics, controversies, and posthumous history of Jorge Luis Borges. It distinguishes:

- **Documented fact:** supported by primary records, archival evidence, Borges’s publications, or multiple reliable sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated in reputable biographies, journalism, memoirs, or interviews but not independently verified here from an original record.
- **Disputed claim:** supported by competing or incomplete evidence.
- **Allegation:** an accusation not established by a final judicial or documentary finding.
- **Rumor or myth:** a culturally significant story lacking adequate proof.
- **Inference:** a conclusion drawn from documented circumstances rather than directly stated by a source.

The bibliographies emphasize books and substantial collections. Borges’s complete periodical output—hundreds of poems, reviews, essays, prologues, interviews, lectures, and collaborative texts—requires specialist bibliographies, particularly those of Annick Louis and Florian Ziche, Horacio Jorge Becco, and David William Foster.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full civil and baptismal names**

- Civil registry: **Jorge Francisco Isidoro Borges**.
- Baptismal record, 20 June 1900: **Jorge Francisco Luis Isidoro Borges**.
- Standard literary name: **Jorge Luis Borges**.
- Family name and childhood usage: **Georgie**.
- Frequently abbreviated in scholarship: **JLB**.

**Pseudonyms and collective personae**

- **Nemo:** used for the juvenile story “El rey de la selva,” published in a school magazine in 1913.
- **H. Bustos Domecq:** collective pseudonym used with Adolfo Bioy Casares. “Bustos” came from a Borges ancestor and “Domecq” from Bioy’s family.
- **B. Suárez Lynch:** another collective pseudonym used with Bioy.
- Numerous fictional authors, editors, bibliographers, and translators appear inside Borges’s work, but these are literary personae rather than ordinary publishing pseudonyms.

**Birth**

- **24 August 1899**
- Family home at **Tucumán 840, Buenos Aires, Argentina**.

**Death**

- **14 June 1986**, aged eighty-six.
- Geneva, Switzerland.
- Contemporary accounts and his lawyer identified **liver cancer** as the terminal illness.

**Nationality and occupations**

- Argentine.
- Poet, short-story writer, essayist, critic, translator, anthologist, lecturer, editor, librarian, and director of Argentina’s National Library.

**Languages**

Borges grew up bilingual in Spanish and English. He learned French, German, and Latin in Geneva and later studied Old English and Old Norse. His reading also extended, through translations and collaboration, to Hebrew, Arabic, Icelandic literature, Japanese traditions, and other bodies of writing.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

Borges’s father, **Jorge Guillermo Borges Haslam** (1874–1938), was a lawyer, teacher, translator, novelist, and philosophical anarchist influenced by Herbert Spencer. His mother, **Frances Haslam**, was English-speaking and of English and Staffordshire family background. Jorge Guillermo possessed the extensive English-language library Borges later called the central event of his life.

His father published the novel *El caudillo* in 1921 and attempted other literary work. Progressive hereditary eye disease forced him away from legal practice. The same familial condition affected Borges.

### Maternal family

His mother, **Leonor Acevedo Suárez** (1876–1975), belonged to an established Argentine-Criollo family. She became her son’s reader, amanuensis, translator, household manager, and frequent travel companion after his blindness. She translated works from English, including material associated with William Saroyan, Herbert Read, and Faulkner.

The maternal family cultivated a military-patriotic memory. Important ancestors included:

- **Colonel Isidoro Suárez**, Borges’s great-grandfather, who fought at Junín in 1824 during the wars of independence.
- **Francisco Borges Lafinur**, his paternal grandfather, a colonel killed in 1874 during the rebellion of Bartolomé Mitre.
- **Isidoro Acevedo Laprida**, maternal grandfather, whose death in 1905 Borges later treated in “Página para recordar al coronel Suárez, vencedor en Junín” and “La noche que en el Sur lo velaron.”

These lineages fed Borges’s recurring mythology of swords, frontier warfare, courage, defeat, and remembered ancestors. The heroic family tradition was real, but his literary treatment transformed genealogy into a consciously stylized private mythology.

### Sibling

His only sibling was **Leonor Fanny Borges Acevedo**, known as **Norah Borges** (1901–1998), a painter, illustrator, and art critic. She illustrated several early Borges publications and married the Spanish writer and critic **Guillermo de Torre** in 1928. Their children, Borges’s nephew and niece, were **Miguel** and **Luis Guillermo de Torre Borges**.

### Marriages, attachments, and children

Borges had **no documented children**.

Important romantic relationships or attachments included:

- **Concepción Guerrero:** an early attachment, associated with several youthful poems.
- **Norah Lange:** Borges was reportedly in love with her; she instead formed a lasting relationship with Oliverio Girondo. The precise intimacy of Borges and Lange remains partly reconstructed from memoir and literary circles.
- **Estela Canto:** writer and translator whom Borges met in the 1940s. His letters document intense courtship. She did not accept the conventional marriage his family desired. Borges dedicated “El Aleph” to her and gave her its manuscript. Canto later published *Borges a contraluz*, an indispensable but personal and sometimes hostile memoir.
- **Margarita Guerrero:** dancer, writer, collaborator, and the dedicatee or addressee of emotionally charged material. Recent scholarship emphasizes Borges’s romantic attachment to her, although the exact reciprocity remains uncertain.
- **Elsa Astete Millán:** an earlier acquaintance whom Borges married in Buenos Aires in 1967 after she had been widowed. They separated in 1970; a February 1971 judgment recorded divorce “through the fault of both.” Because Argentina then lacked full vincular divorce, the legal consequences became important when Borges later married María Kodama.
- **María Kodama Schweizer** (1937–2023): student, literary collaborator, secretary, reader, and extensive travel companion. Borges and Kodama married by proxy in Paraguay on **26 April 1986**, while residing in Geneva. The marriage’s validity and the handling of Borges’s estate became subjects of prolonged public and legal controversy.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1899–1914

Borges spent his earliest years in central Buenos Aires and then in the Palermo district, then a peripheral area carrying associations of knife fighters, neighborhood bosses, courtyards, and low houses. The Palermo of his later poetry and fiction was partly remembered place, partly family narrative, and partly deliberate literary construction.

He grew up inside his father’s library, reportedly containing more than a thousand English books. English literature entered his life before much of the Spanish canon. He later said he first encountered *Don Quixote* in English and initially regarded the Spanish original as resembling a translation.

His earliest literary exercises included:

- At approximately seven, a summary of Greek mythology in English.
- At approximately eight, “La visera fatal,” based on an episode in *Don Quixote*.
- A Spanish translation of Oscar Wilde’s “The Happy Prince,” published by *El País* on **25 June 1910**. Because the newspaper printed “Jorge Borges,” some readers reportedly assumed the translator was his father.
- “El rey de la selva,” published under **Nemo** in a school magazine in 1913.

He received much instruction at home. School records show enrollment in a Palermo primary school in March 1911, followed by withdrawal after bullying. In 1913 he passed entrance examinations for the Colegio Nacional de Buenos Aires but did not pursue the ordinary course there because the family soon left for Europe.

## Education and Formation, 1914–1921

### Geneva

In February 1914 the Borges family traveled to Europe, initially seeking treatment for Jorge Guillermo Borges’s eyesight. The First World War stranded them in neutral Switzerland. Borges attended the **Collège Calvin** in Geneva from 1914 to 1918.

He studied French and Latin, learned German largely through self-directed reading, and encountered:

- Schopenhauer, who became one of his most durable philosophical influences.
- German Expressionist poetry.
- Heine, Nietzsche, Carlyle, Chesterton, Stevenson, and Whitman.
- French symbolism.
- Medieval and Germanic literature.

He completed the Collège Calvin course without taking the final examinations and therefore did not receive the usual secondary diploma. He never earned a conventional university degree, though universities later awarded him numerous honorary doctorates.

Geneva remained an emotionally privileged city: the place of adolescence, linguistic formation, and ultimately death and burial.

### Switzerland, France, Italy, and Spain

After the war the family moved through Switzerland and France, traveled in Italy, and settled for periods in Spain, especially **Mallorca, Seville, and Madrid**.

In Spain, Borges entered the **Ultraist** avant-garde. He associated with or learned from:

- **Rafael Cansinos Assens**, whom he treated as an intellectual mentor.
- Ramón Gómez de la Serna.
- Guillermo de Torre.
- Jacobo Sureda.
- The circles around *Grecia*, *Ultra*, and related journals.

He published poems, reviews, translations of German Expressionists, and Ultraist manifestos. Later Borges diminished or revised this avant-garde period, suppressing early books and presenting his development as cleaner and more inevitable than the documentary record shows.

## Early Career, 1921–1937

### Return to Buenos Aires

The family returned to Buenos Aires in March 1921. Borges rediscovered the city as both home and invented landscape. With Eduardo González Lanuza, Guillermo Juan Borges, and others, he helped produce the mural magazine **Prisma** and subsequently **Proa**.

He promoted Ultraism in Argentina but gradually exchanged its compressed metaphors for a local poetry of Buenos Aires streets, patios, outskirts, dusk, and ancestral history.

### Early books and suppression

His first book, *Fervor de Buenos Aires* (1923), was privately printed in approximately three hundred copies. Norah Borges designed the cover. Borges reportedly inserted copies into the coats of visitors at the journal *Nosotros* rather than mounting a conventional sales campaign.

It was followed by:

- *Luna de enfrente* (1925).
- *Inquisiciones* (1925).
- *El tamaño de mi esperanza* (1926).
- *El idioma de los argentinos* (1928).
- *Cuaderno San Martín* (1929).

Borges later excluded *Inquisiciones*, *El tamaño de mi esperanza*, and *El idioma de los argentinos* from his authorized collected works, bought up copies when possible, and rewrote early poems. This was not a literal destruction of the entire print record, as later legend sometimes suggests; surviving copies and recovered texts allow comparison between youthful nationalism, linguistic experimentation, and later revisions.

### Literary factions

He was associated with the cosmopolitan **Florida group**, although the famous Florida-versus-Boedo division was partly retrospective literary publicity. He contributed to *Martín Fierro* and mixed with writers including Macedonio Fernández, Oliverio Girondo, Leopoldo Marechal, and Ricardo Güiraldes.

**Macedonio Fernández** was a crucial personal and intellectual influence. Borges later described long conversations with him and credited his metaphysical wit and skepticism, though Borges’s homage also helped construct Macedonio’s posthumous legend.

### Victoria Ocampo and *Sur*

Borges became closely associated with **Victoria Ocampo’s** journal *Sur*, founded in 1931. *Sur* provided a major institutional network connecting him with Adolfo Bioy Casares, Silvina Ocampo, José Bianco, Waldo Frank, and international writers.

### Journalism and criticism

From 1936 through 1939 Borges wrote a regular books-and-authors column for the women’s magazine **El Hogar**. These compact reviews and biographical notices reveal the practical formation of his later fictional method: invented bibliographies, compressed intellectual biographies, and the treatment of criticism as imaginative literature.

### *Historia universal de la infamia*

Published in 1935, *Historia universal de la infamia* reworked sources concerning criminals, impostors, pirates, gunmen, and religious frauds. Borges openly described the pieces as exercises involving deliberate distortion. They are neither conventional history nor simple translation; they demonstrate his practice of appropriation, rewriting, false attribution, and documentary masquerade.

## Chronological Life History, 1937–1955

### Municipal library employment

In 1937 Borges obtained stable employment as a first assistant at the **Miguel Cané Municipal Library** in the working-class district of Boedo. He catalogued books and reportedly completed his assigned work quickly, reading and writing during the remaining hours. The job supplied financial stability but little professional satisfaction.

### Death of his father and 1938 medical crisis

Jorge Guillermo Borges died in February 1938.

On Christmas Eve 1938, Borges struck his head on an open casement or freshly painted window while climbing stairs. The wound became infected; accounts describe septicemia and a period when death or neurological damage was feared. Borges later said he worried that his literary ability had been destroyed. His first major experiment afterward was “Pierre Menard, autor del Quijote,” published in 1939.

The often-repeated claim that the accident “turned Borges from poet into fiction writer” is an oversimplification. He had already written narrative prose, but the crisis did precede the mature fictional sequence that made his international reputation.

### Major fiction

During the 1940s he produced many of his most consequential stories:

- “Pierre Menard, autor del Quijote.”
- “Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius.”
- “La lotería en Babilonia.”
- “La biblioteca de Babel.”
- “El jardín de senderos que se bifurcan.”
- “Funes el memorioso.”
- “La muerte y la brújula.”
- “El milagro secreto.”
- “Tema del traidor y del héroe.”
- “Tres versiones de Judas.”
- “El Sur.”
- “El Aleph.”

*El jardín de senderos que se bifurcan* appeared in 1941. After it failed to receive the National Prize, writers associated with *Sur* produced a special issue honoring Borges. The enlarged collection *Ficciones* appeared in 1944 and received the Argentine Writers’ Society’s Grand Prize of Honor in 1945.

### Bioy Casares collaboration

Borges met **Adolfo Bioy Casares** in the early 1930s; their close friendship and working partnership lasted for decades. With Bioy and **Silvina Ocampo**, Borges edited *Antología de la literatura fantástica* (1940) and *Antología poética argentina* (1941).

Borges and Bioy wrote detective stories, parodies, film scripts, reviews, and satirical sketches under H. Bustos Domecq and B. Suárez Lynch. Their method was intensely oral: conversation, jokes, competing phrases, and collaborative revision.

Bioy’s enormous posthumously published diary *Borges* preserves thousands of conversations. It is a primary witness to Borges’s private speech but not a neutral transcript: Bioy selected, reconstructed, and edited entries, sometimes years afterward.

### Anti-fascism and the Second World War

Borges publicly opposed Nazism, fascism, racial nationalism, and Argentine Germanophilia. He participated in anti-racist and anti-antisemitic activity and wrote:

- “Yo, judío” (1934), responding satirically to a right-wing attempt to expose supposed Jewish ancestry.
- “Una pedagogía del odio” (1937), criticizing Elvira Bauer’s antisemitic children’s propaganda.
- “Deutsches Requiem,” exploring rather than endorsing the mind of a defeated Nazi.
- Essays condemning Hitlerism and arguing that Nazi victory would corrupt even Germany.

His anti-Nazism is a documented counterweight to later evidence of racial prejudice in private conversations. The two bodies of evidence cannot simply be collapsed into either “Borges was an antiracist” or “Borges was a racist.”

### Peronism and dismissal

Borges and his family opposed Juan Domingo Perón. His mother and sister participated in opposition activity and were detained in 1948; Leonor received house arrest because of age, while Norah spent approximately a month in jail.

In 1946 the municipal administration transferred or proposed transferring Borges from the library to an inspector’s position involving poultry and rabbits in public markets. Borges resigned. At a dinner held in protest, Pedro Henríquez Ureña read Borges’s speech because of Borges’s severe public shyness.

The “promotion to poultry inspector” became a canonical story of Peronist humiliation. **Documented core:** Borges lost his library position through an unsuitable reassignment after Perón’s rise and resigned. **Uncertain detail:** versions differ over the exact title—inspector of poultry, rabbits, eggs, or markets—and the degree to which Borges polished the episode into comic political theater.

He then supported himself through lectures and teaching, overcoming his fear of public speaking. He served as president of the Argentine Society of Writers from 1950 to 1953.

## National Library, Blindness, and International Fame, 1955–1973

### Appointment

Following Perón’s overthrow in September 1955, the new government appointed Borges director of the **Biblioteca Nacional**. He held the post until 1973.

He supported the 1955 anti-Peronist overthrow and the removal of Peronists from public institutions. His anti-Peronism remained one of his strongest and least skeptical political commitments.

As director, he helped establish the National School of Librarians, inaugurated in 1956. José Edmundo Clemente served as a close institutional collaborator and deputy director.

### Blindness

By the mid-1950s hereditary retinal disease and repeated operations had left Borges functionally blind. He retained perceptions of light and, according to his descriptions, the color yellow or amber.

The coincidence of becoming director of a national library while losing the ability to read became the basis of “Poema de los dones”:

> “Nadie rebaje a lágrima o reproche / esta declaración de la maestría / de Dios, que con magnífica ironía / me dio a la vez los libros y la noche.”

Blindness transformed his compositional process. He dictated prose and poetry to his mother, secretaries, students, and collaborators. He returned to meter and rhyme because they aided memory. Claims that blindness “destroyed” his style come partly from Borges’s own remarks and should be read alongside the substantial work he produced afterward.

### University appointment

In 1956 Borges became professor of English and American literature at the **University of Buenos Aires** despite lacking a conventional university degree. His appointment recognized literary knowledge rather than academic credentials.

### International breakthrough

The 1961 **Prix International/Formentor Prize**, shared with Samuel Beckett, brought Borges broad European and North American attention. English-language collections, especially *Labyrinths* and *Ficciones*, accelerated his international reputation.

He traveled extensively for lectures and honors:

- United States and university circuits.
- Britain and continental Europe.
- Israel.
- Iceland, connected to his study of Old Norse.
- Japan, often with María Kodama.
- Latin American countries, including Chile and Peru.

At Harvard in 1967–1968 he delivered the **Charles Eliot Norton Lectures**, later reconstructed from recordings as *This Craft of Verse*.

### First marriage

Borges married Elsa Astete Millán in 1967. They lived uneasily with Borges’s dependence on his mother and established habits. They separated in 1970. Accounts differ in tone: Elsa and Borges’s biographers describe incompatibility, lack of intimacy, family interference, and Borges’s desire to return to his mother’s household.

### Mother’s death

Leonor Acevedo died in 1975 at ninety-nine. Her death ended the central domestic relationship of Borges’s adult life. María Kodama increasingly became his reader, collaborator, and traveling companion.

## Political, Intellectual, Religious, and Artistic Development

### Philosophical orientation

Borges repeatedly rejected systematic philosophy as dogma while treating metaphysical systems as resources for literature. Major interests included:

- Idealism: Berkeley, Hume, Schopenhauer.
- The problem of personal identity.
- Infinite regress and cyclical time.
- Labyrinths, mirrors, doubles, and branching alternatives.
- Nominalism versus realism.
- Zeno’s paradoxes.
- Gnosticism, Kabbalah, Sufism, Buddhism, and Christian heresies.
- The possibility that the world is dream, text, memory, or divine language.
- The relation between author, reader, translation, and tradition.

He described metaphysics as a branch of fantastic literature. This was neither a settled creed nor mere joke; it was a working aesthetic principle.

### Religion

Borges was raised in a family divided among Catholic, Protestant, skeptical, and freethinking traditions. He frequently called himself an agnostic and sometimes expressed disbelief in personal immortality. He was fascinated by theology, Christ, heresy, Swedenborg, Dante, Buddhism, Jewish mysticism, and the idea of ethical salvation.

Near death he asked to receive both a Catholic priest, in memory of his mother, and a Protestant minister, in memory of his English grandmother. The Geneva funeral was ecumenical. Claims of a final conversion to Catholicism are **unverified and disputed**; the documented ritual was plural rather than an unequivocal adherence to one confession.

### Anarchism, liberalism, and conservatism

From his father Borges inherited philosophical anarchism: the hope that the state might eventually become unnecessary. In youth he briefly expressed sympathy for the Russian Revolution and composed now-lost or suppressed politically inflected work.

His mature politics combined:

- Individualism.
- Anti-nationalism in theory.
- Opposition to fascism and Nazism.
- Hostility to communism.
- Fierce anti-Peronism.
- Skepticism toward mass democracy and centralized government.
- Cultural attachment to nineteenth-century Argentine liberalism.
- At times, support for authoritarian governments believed to oppose Peronism or communism.

Calling Borges simply “liberal,” “conservative,” “anarchist,” or “reactionary” suppresses these contradictions.

### Argentine tradition

Early Borges attempted a linguistic and cultural **criollismo**, developing a stylized Buenos Aires idiom and mythology of the outskirts. He later rejected narrow nationalism in “El escritor argentino y la tradición,” arguing that Argentine writers inherited the entire Western tradition and need not advertise local color.

His treatment of gauchos, knife fighters, soldiers, and compadritos moved between documentary history, family myth, aesthetic fascination with courage, and parody.

## Political Activity and Controversy, 1973–1986

### Perón’s return

When Peronism returned to power in 1973, Borges resigned or retired as director of the National Library. He remained a prominent anti-Peronist public voice and delivered barbed statements about Perón, Eva Perón, mass politics, and political violence.

### Support for military governments

**Documented fact:** Borges initially welcomed Argentina’s 1976 military coup. At a luncheon with General **Jorge Rafael Videla**, he thanked the armed forces for having rescued Argentina from what he called chaos, abjection, or ignominy.

**Documented fact:** In September 1976 Borges visited Chile, met **Augusto Pinochet**, accepted the Grand Cross of the Order of Bernardo O’Higgins, and publicly praised the restoration of “order” against communism.

These were not merely accusations by opponents: the meetings, decoration, photographs, and statements were contemporaneously reported.

### Revision after evidence of disappearances

As evidence of kidnapping, torture, and disappearance accumulated, Borges changed his public position. In 1980 he met relatives of the disappeared, supported a statement associated with the Mothers of Plaza de Mayo, and acknowledged that his earlier confidence had been mistaken.

In 1985 he attended the **Trial of the Juntas** and heard survivor **Víctor Basterra** testify about torture and captivity at ESMA. Borges published a newspaper account emphasizing the concrete horror of the testimony.

The chronology therefore shows both:

1. Initial public legitimation of military rulers.
2. Later public repudiation after confrontation with evidence of state terror.

Neither phase cancels the other.

### Falklands/Malvinas War

Borges opposed the 1982 war between Argentina and Britain. He described it as two bald men fighting over a comb—a phrase also attributed in variants to others, making sole authorship uncertain. His poem “Juan López y John Ward” imagined one Argentine and one English soldier as young men killed by governments and nationalism.

### Democracy

Borges supported Raúl Alfonsín’s democratic government after 1983 and regarded the prosecution of junta leaders as historically significant. His late political position was markedly more attentive to constitutional democracy and state crimes than his 1976 statements had been.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Adolfo Bioy Casares

**Documented fact.** Friend, daily conversational partner, co-author, anthologist, and creative counterpart from the 1930s onward. Their collaborations created Bustos Domecq and influenced modern detective fiction, parody, and fantastic literature.

The relationship was not devoid of asymmetry. Bioy’s posthumous diary exposed Borges’s prejudices, mockery, rivalries, and private judgments. María Kodama criticized the book and reportedly characterized Bioy as Borges’s “Salieri,” a hostile assessment not supported by the long documentary record of collaboration.

### Silvina Ocampo

**Documented fact.** Friend, writer, and co-editor of *Antología de la literatura fantástica* and *Antología poética argentina*. She belonged to Borges’s central *Sur* network and was married to Bioy.

### Victoria Ocampo

**Documented fact.** Founder of *Sur*, publisher, host, institutional patron, and sometimes political ally. Borges contributed extensively to her magazine. Their relationship contained disagreements, but *Sur* was indispensable to his career.

### María Kodama

**Documented fact.** Student from the 1950s, Old English collaborator, reader, secretary, travel companion, co-author of *Breve antología anglosajona* and *Atlas*, wife, sole testamentary beneficiary, and later literary executor.

### Norman Thomas di Giovanni

**Documented fact.** Translator and collaborator central to Borges’s English-language presence in the late 1960s and 1970s. Their translations were jointly shaped rather than mechanically converted from Spanish. After Borges’s death, the estate’s licensing decisions allowed many di Giovanni versions to fall out of print. Scholars and translators have debated whether this restricted access to an important collaborative phase.

### Estela Canto

**Documented fact.** Friend and romantic object of Borges’s intense correspondence. Recipient of the manuscript of “El Aleph.” Her memoir supplies intimate testimony but must be read as the account of an involved, later estranged participant.

### Rafael Cansinos Assens

**Documented fact.** Mentor in Madrid’s Ultraist circles. Borges repeatedly exaggerated Cansinos’s literary greatness, partly from loyalty and gratitude.

### Xul Solar

**Documented fact.** Artist, invented-language creator, occultist, and close friend in Buenos Aires avant-garde circles. Borges wrote about Xul and shared his fascination with symbolic languages, mystical systems, and invented worlds.

### Macedonio Fernández

**Documented fact.** Friend of Borges’s father, conversational mentor, metaphysical humorist, and formative Argentine precursor. Borges’s tributes were crucial to Macedonio’s canonization.

### Rivals and antagonisms

Borges’s targets included:

- Perón and Peronism.
- Literary nationalism.
- Psychoanalysis, often mocked without sustained engagement.
- Pablo Neruda’s communism, although Borges praised Neruda’s poetic gifts.
- Writers and critics disparaged in private conversations recorded by Bioy.
- Elements of the postwar Latin American left that treated him as politically reactionary.

His relationship to Julio Cortázar was mixed rather than a simple rivalry: Borges published Cortázar’s “Casa tomada,” but their politics diverged sharply.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Finances

### Employment

- Contributor and editor in avant-garde magazines, 1920s.
- Journalist and reviewer, including regular work for *El Hogar*, 1936–1939.
- Municipal librarian at Miguel Cané Library, 1937–1946.
- Lecturer and public speaker after 1946.
- President, Argentine Society of Writers, 1950–1953.
- Director, National Library of Argentina, 1955–1973.
- Professor of English and American literature, University of Buenos Aires, beginning in 1956.
- International visiting lecturer and writer.

### Finances

Borges was not a major businessman and had no documented companies, patents, or industrial investments. His finances centered on:

- Family support and property.
- Municipal and university salaries.
- Magazine and newspaper payments.
- Lecture fees.
- Prizes and honorary awards.
- Royalties and translation rights.

He did not become financially secure primarily from book sales until international fame in later life. The 1960s English-language market, lecture tours, and awards materially changed his circumstances.

A Buenos Aires property record cited in research on his estate identifies an apartment at **Rodríguez Peña 1228**, acquired in November 1984. Press accounts after María Kodama’s death described manuscripts, copyrights, apartments, personal objects, bank accounts, and foundation holdings as potentially belonging to the combined Borges-Kodama estate, but no reliable comprehensive public valuation has been established.

### Technologies and formal innovations

Borges held no patents and built no technology company. His importance to technological culture is conceptual and posthumous:

- “The Library of Babel” became a model for discussions of total information systems.
- “The Garden of Forking Paths” anticipated critical language about branching narratives and hypertext.
- “Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius” modeled collective construction of synthetic worlds.
- His fictional reviews and false bibliographies anticipated forms of metafiction and documentary simulation.
- Computer scientists and digital theorists frequently invoke his work, though claims that Borges literally “invented the Internet” or predicted a particular computer architecture are **mythic exaggerations**.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Legal Questions

### Racial prejudice

**Documented evidence:** Bioy Casares’s diaries attribute repeated racist descriptions of Black people, Brazilians, Indigenous people, Asians, and national groups to Borges. A peer-reviewed study by Paulo Dutra finds that racial hierarchy and a self-constructed whiteness appear repeatedly in these conversations and some literary texts.

**Qualification:** Bioy’s diary is mediated testimony, not an audio transcript, and Borges’s published record also includes opposition to Nazi racism and antisemitism. This contradiction is substantive, not resolved by declaring one archive authentic and the other irrelevant.

### Antisemitism versus philosemitism

**Documented fact:** Borges opposed Nazi antisemitism, defended Jews in “Yo, judío,” joined anti-racist initiatives, admired Jewish writers and Spinoza, studied Kabbalah, visited Israel, and received the Jerusalem Prize.

**Documented complication:** Scholars have identified generalized or stereotypical representations of “the Jew” even within his philosemitic writing. Admiration could involve exoticization. The evidence supports committed anti-antisemitism alongside culturally stereotyped language.

### Support for dictatorships

**Documented fact:** Borges publicly praised Videla and Pinochet in 1976 and accepted a Chilean state decoration.

**Documented later reversal:** He met relatives of the disappeared, admitted error, supported their appeals, and attended the Trial of the Juntas.

Claims that Borges consistently supported the Argentine dictatorship until death are therefore false. Claims that he never supported it are also false.

### Franco

Borges made favorable comments about Francisco Franco, especially as a barrier to communism. Some statements may have been deliberately provocative conversational performances, but there is no sound basis for treating all of them as jokes. They belong to the same anti-communist and anti-mass-politics strand that facilitated his approval of military authority.

### The Nobel Prize

**Documented fact:** The Nobel archive records repeated nominations.

**Reported explanation:** Many commentators argue that the 1976 Pinochet visit destroyed or damaged his prospects.

**Unresolved question:** Nobel deliberations remain confidential for fifty years, and the currently public nomination archive does not establish a single definitive reason. Literary objections, genre preferences, committee dynamics, politics, and competition may all have contributed. The proposition “Pinochet cost Borges the Nobel” remains plausible but not judicially or archivally established as the exclusive cause.

### Peronist “poultry inspector” episode

**Documented core:** The municipal administration reassigned Borges to an unsuitable market-inspection post in 1946; he resigned.

**Disputed details:** Retellings vary among poultry, rabbits, eggs, and combined duties. Borges’s own comic retellings helped stabilize the most humiliating version. It should be treated as an event with a reliable core and variable wording.

### Marriage to María Kodama

Borges’s Paraguayan proxy marriage occurred while Argentine records still described him as divorced or separated from Elsa under a legal system that had not recognized full remarriage in the modern sense.

**Disputed legal history:** Critics alleged that the Paraguayan procedure was designed to bypass Argentine restrictions and secure inheritance. Kodama and Borges’s representatives maintained that Borges freely chose the marriage and testamentary arrangements.

No consulted source establishes that Kodama forged Borges’s signature, coerced him, or criminally manipulated the testament. Those claims must remain allegations.

### Testament and estate

Borges executed wills in 1979 and 1985, the latter reportedly naming Kodama universal heir. After his death:

- Relatives and representatives disputed property and status.
- Kodama litigated aggressively to control copyrights and representations of Borges.
- A Paris court found a published accusation of “testamentary manipulation” defamatory and imposed penalties on Pierre Assouline and the responsible publication.
- Kodama founded the **Fundación Internacional Jorge Luis Borges** in 1988.

After Kodama died intestate in March 2023, Argentine proceedings declared five nieces and nephews—children of her deceased brother—her universal heirs in June 2023. This included control of the inherited Borges rights. Early reports that the estate might pass to the state were superseded by the relatives’ appearance and judicial recognition.

### Pablo Katchadjian and *El Aleph engordado*

In 2011 Kodama initiated a criminal copyright complaint against writer **Pablo Katchadjian**, who had produced a small edition of *El Aleph engordado* by adding material to Borges’s story.

**Documented legal trajectory:** Katchadjian was prosecuted and at one stage had assets embargoed. Literary figures defended the work as transformative experimentation. The case was ultimately dismissed; there was no conviction. The dispute became a prominent test of copyright, appropriation, and the difference between literary transformation and piracy.

### Norman Thomas di Giovanni translations

**Documented publishing dispute:** Borges and di Giovanni jointly produced English versions. Following Borges’s death and changes in estate licensing, many went out of print.

**Critical claim:** Scholars argue that this “silenced” a distinctive collaborative English Borges.

**Qualification:** This was a copyright and editorial-control controversy, not proof of theft or fraud.

### Plagiarism and source appropriation

Borges repeatedly transformed encyclopedia entries, biographies, translations, and earlier literature. Critics have occasionally called this plagiarism.

**Documented literary practice:** Borges signaled rewriting, false attribution, quotation, and pseudo-scholarship as central techniques. Some early pieces closely follow source texts without modern scholarly citation.

**Assessment of status:** There is no major established judicial finding that Borges committed actionable plagiarism. The issue is chiefly one of literary appropriation, period citation conventions, and deliberate instability of authorship.

### Sexuality and psychological speculation

Biographers and acquaintances have speculated about impotence, sexual fear, repression, dependence on his mother, and possible visits to prostitutes arranged by his father.

These accounts depend heavily on memoir, hearsay, and psychobiography. Borges’s unhappy courtships and failed first marriage are documented; diagnoses of sexual dysfunction or a particular psychological disorder are **unverified claims**, not medical findings.

### Adversarial search, 2024–2026

A dedicated search combining Borges’s name with controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuits, fraud, accusations, conspiracy, criticism, racism, dictatorship, and inheritance found:

- Continued scholarly and journalistic scrutiny of his racist private remarks.
- Renewed discussion of his support for Videla and Pinochet.
- Ongoing debate about whether politics cost him the Nobel.
- Continued estate and copyright discussion following Kodama’s 2023 death.
- Publication of previously inaccessible lecture notebooks in 2025, raising editorial and archival questions but no new criminal allegation.
- No credible new evidence in 2024–2026 of personal fraud, corruption, money laundering, intelligence service work, criminal violence, or a previously unknown criminal prosecution against Borges.

Absence of such evidence is not proof that no unknown material exists; it is the result of the source search conducted for this dossier.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Borges was secretly Jewish”

**Story:** Right-wing Argentine writers alleged Jewish ancestry, sometimes using “Borges Acevedo” genealogically or politically.

**Appearance and circulation:** Most famously in the nationalist journal *Crisol* in 1934.

**Evidence:** No established Jewish ancestry has been demonstrated in the relevant line. Borges responded with “Yo, judío,” mocking the premise that Jewish ancestry would be shameful.

**Reputational effect:** The accusation became a celebrated episode in his anti-antisemitic reputation and later encouraged symbolic descriptions of Borges as an honorary or spiritual Jewish writer.

### “He remembered every book he read”

**Story:** Borges possessed an essentially total or photographic memory.

**Evidence:** He had exceptional literary memory and could recite long passages, especially after blindness made memorized composition necessary. No clinical evidence establishes eidetic or total recall.

**Effect:** The myth merges Borges with Funes, the protagonist cursed by perfect memory.

### “He invented magical realism”

**Story:** Borges single-handedly founded the Latin American Boom or magical realism.

**Evidence:** His compressed fantastic fiction profoundly influenced later writers, but magical realism has multiple sources, including modernismo, avant-garde fiction, Indigenous and Afro-Latin American traditions, Carpentier, Asturias, Kafka, and others. Borges’s own mode often differs from the historical and social textures called magical realism.

### “He predicted the Internet”

**Story:** The Library of Babel is the Internet in advance.

**Evidence:** The story imagines a combinatorial total library, not packet networking, search engines, or a global computer network.

**Effect:** It has become a powerful cultural metaphor for digital abundance, meaningless data, search, artificial intelligence, and information overload.

### “The accident created the mature Borges”

**Story:** Before the 1938 head injury Borges was only a poet; afterward he became the author of the great stories.

**Evidence:** The accident and infection were real, and “Pierre Menard” followed them. Borges had already written narrative prose and *Historia universal de la infamia*. The story is best understood as a retrospective conversion narrative.

### “Perón personally made him a chicken inspector”

**Story:** Perón personally ordered Borges to inspect chickens as revenge.

**Evidence:** The reassignment occurred under a Peronist municipal administration. Direct personal intervention by Perón has not been established.

**Effect:** It condensed state cultural retaliation into a memorable personal confrontation.

### “Pinochet alone cost him the Nobel”

**Story:** The Swedish Academy was prepared to award Borges the prize but canceled it after Chile.

**Evidence:** The visit was public and reputationally damaging. No released committee document presently proves this single-cause narrative.

**Effect:** It became a cautionary tale about writers, prizes, and authoritarian politics.

### “Kodama kidnapped or controlled a helpless Borges”

**Story:** Kodama isolated, coerced, married, or removed Borges to Geneva solely to seize his estate.

**Evidence for:** Late marriage, proxy procedure, inheritance, exclusion of some relatives and collaborators, and aggressive later copyright litigation created suspicion.

**Evidence against:** Their documented association extended over decades; they studied, translated, traveled, and published together; Borges signed wills before the final journey. A French court treated allegations of testamentary manipulation as defamatory.

**Status:** Disputed narrative and hostile mythology; coercion or fraud has not been established.

### “Borges chose Geneva to reject Argentina”

**Story:** Burial abroad was an act of repudiation.

**Evidence:** Borges had longstanding emotional ties to Geneva and reportedly wished to die and be buried there. Competing recollections cite earlier remarks favoring the family vault at Recoleta.

**Status:** His final conduct supports Geneva, but the meaning assigned to it—cosmopolitan choice, escape from publicity, rejection of Argentina, or attachment to youth—is interpretive.

## Illness, Injury, Disability, and Death

Borges inherited progressive eye disease from his father’s family. He underwent several operations and progressively lost vision. By approximately 1955 he could no longer read or write conventionally.

His 1938 head injury led to infection and a grave medical crisis. Reports commonly identify septicemia. Later dramatizations sometimes exaggerate the certainty of imminent death or direct neurological transformation.

In late 1985 doctors diagnosed incurable liver cancer following examination and biopsy, according to contemporary statements by his lawyer. Borges and Kodama left Argentina for Europe. He settled in Geneva and kept the severity of his illness largely private.

He died on 14 June 1986. Father Pierre Jacquet and Protestant pastor Édouard de Montmollin attended him near death. An ecumenical funeral was held at St. Pierre Cathedral on 18 June.

He was buried in Geneva’s **Cimetière des Rois**, or Plainpalais Cemetery. His gravestone incorporates Old English and Old Norse-associated inscriptions and warrior imagery:

- “And ne forhtedon na” — “And they were not afraid.”
- “Hann tekr sverthit Gram ok leggr i methal theira bert” — an allusion to Sigurd taking the sword Gram and placing it between himself and Brynhild.

The tomb materializes Borges’s lifelong fusion of personal identity, northern European literature, bravery, and textual quotation.

## Posthumous Estate, Reputation, and Legacy

Borges’s death produced overlapping literary, familial, commercial, and national claims.

María Kodama controlled the copyrights, authorized editions, founded the Borges Foundation, opposed unauthorized adaptations, and pursued litigation. Supporters regarded her as a necessary protector against distortion and commercial exploitation. Critics regarded her administration as restrictive and confrontational. Both descriptions are supported by documented conduct; accusations of criminal testamentary fraud are not established.

After Kodama’s death in 2023, the recognition of her five nieces and nephews resolved the immediate question of legal succession, though future editorial policy remains historically significant.

Borges’s reputation expanded in several directions:

- Canonical figure in Spanish-language literature.
- Precursor of metafiction, postmodernism, hypertext, and speculative fiction.
- Model for writers including Italo Calvino, Umberto Eco, Gabriel García Márquez, Julio Cortázar, John Barth, Donald Barthelme, Salman Rushdie, Paul Auster, Roberto Bolaño, W. G. Sebald, and many others.
- Central reference in translation theory and comparative literature.
- Symbol of the library, blindness, infinite books, mirrors, labyrinths, and the double.
- Contested political figure because of anti-Peronism and his 1976 statements.
- Subject of renewed scrutiny concerning race, class, gender, private speech, collaboration, and estate control.

His reputation in Argentina passed through vanguard marginality, anti-Peronist cultural authority, international canonization, left-wing suspicion, democratic reassessment, popular celebrity, and broad incorporation into public culture. Argentina designated 24 August as the **Día del Lector** in his honor.

## Atlas Connections

The following entries record only documented personal contacts, collaborations, institutional relationships, or explicit intellectual influence involving names on the supplied atlas roster.

### Adolfo Bioy Casares

**Documented fact.** Borges’s closest sustained literary collaborator and friend from the 1930s until Borges’s death. Buenos Aires; joint anthologies, detective fiction, screenplays, Bustos Domecq texts, and decades of recorded conversation.

### Silvina Ocampo

**Documented fact.** Friend and co-editor with Borges and Bioy of *Antología de la literatura fantástica* (1940) and *Antología poética argentina* (1941), centered in Buenos Aires and the *Sur* network.

### Victoria Ocampo

**Documented fact.** Publisher, patron, friend, and founder of *Sur*, which printed a large body of Borges’s work from the 1930s onward.

### Xul Solar

**Documented fact.** Close friend and fellow Buenos Aires avant-garde figure. Shared interests included invented languages, mysticism, symbolic systems, and visionary art.

### Alberto Gerchunoff

**Documented fact.** Older Argentine-Jewish writer whom Borges acknowledged as a friend and mentor. Borges wrote about Gerchunoff and placed him within his conception of Argentine and Jewish literary tradition.

### Julio Florencio Cortázar Descotte

**Documented fact.** Borges selected and published Cortázar’s “Casa tomada” in *Los Anales de Buenos Aires* in 1946. They later diverged politically, particularly over Cuba and revolutionary politics, but Cortázar acknowledged Borges’s literary importance.

### Gabriel José García Márquez

**Reported fact/documented influence.** García Márquez repeatedly acknowledged Borges as a major writer and read him during his formation. They belonged to the international Latin American literary field but no close personal collaboration is established here.

### Federico García Lorca

**Reported fact.** Both moved through Spanish-language literary networks, and Lorca visited Buenos Aires in 1933–1934. Borges later spoke dismissively about aspects of Lorca’s reputation. A significant sustained personal relationship has not been established; this is principally a documented literary response, not a friendship.

### Augusto Pinochet Ugarte

**Documented fact.** Borges met Pinochet in Santiago, Chile, in September 1976, praised anti-communist order, and accepted the Grand Cross of the Order of Bernardo O’Higgins. The meeting became central to Borges’s political controversy and Nobel mythology.

### Juan Domingo Perón

**Documented political antagonism; no necessary personal meeting established.** Borges opposed Perón and Peronism throughout the latter’s political career. A Peronist municipal administration reassigned Borges from his library post in 1946. Borges supported Perón’s overthrow in 1955.

### Eva Duarte de Perón

**Documented political antagonism, not a documented personal relationship.** Borges and his circle opposed Eva Perón’s political symbolism and the regime she represented. Remarks attributed to Borges about her are often hostile; private quotations require comparison with Bioy’s mediated diary.

### Domingo Faustino Sarmiento

**Documented intellectual-historical connection.** Borges wrote repeatedly about the Argentine civilization/barbarism tradition associated with Sarmiento and reworked its categories. This was influence and argument across generations, not personal contact.

### Dante Alighieri

**Documented explicit influence.** Borges regarded the *Divine Comedy* as among literature’s supreme works, lectured and wrote extensively on it, and published *Nueve ensayos dantescos*.

### Miguel de Cervantes Saavedra

**Documented explicit influence.** *Don Quixote* was foundational from Borges’s childhood onward. “Pierre Menard, autor del Quijote” transformed Cervantes into a central case concerning authorship, historical context, and reading.

### Edgar Allan Poe

**Documented explicit influence.** Borges credited Poe’s detective stories and theory of short fiction as essential precursors. Borges and Bioy’s detective writing developed from this tradition.

### William Blake

**Documented explicit influence.** Borges translated, quoted, and discussed Blake, particularly his visionary compression and the conception of infinity in finite things.

### Emanuel Swedenborg

**Documented explicit influence.** Borges lectured and wrote about Swedenborg, admiring his visionary cosmology while withholding doctrinal assent.

### Friedrich Nietzsche

**Documented intellectual influence and criticism.** Borges read Nietzsche in German during or after his Geneva formation and repeatedly discussed eternal recurrence, nationalism, and literary style.

### James Augustine Aloysius Joyce

**Documented literary connection.** Borges was among the early Spanish-language commentators and translators of Joyce, publishing a version of the last page of *Ulysses* in 1925. He admired Joyce’s ambition while criticizing the scale and readability of *Ulysses*.

### Marcel Proust

**Documented literary response.** Borges read and discussed Proust but was frequently skeptical of his work. The connection is critical engagement, not personal contact.

### Franz Kafka

Kafka is not separately named in the supplied roster. No Atlas entry is therefore created, despite his central influence on Borges.

### George Orwell

**Documented literary response.** Borges reviewed Orwell and commented on *Nineteen Eighty-Four*, criticizing its political simplifications while recognizing its force. No personal meeting is established.

### Herbert George Wells

**Documented explicit influence.** Borges repeatedly praised Wells’s scientific romances and essays and wrote prologues or criticism concerning him.

### Howard Phillips Lovecraft

**Documented literary response.** Borges’s “There Are More Things” was dedicated to Lovecraft, though Borges expressed reservations about Lovecraft’s style. There was no meeting.

### J. R. R. Tolkien

**Reported literary awareness, no substantial personal connection established.** Borges knew Old English and Germanic literature independently. Mere shared interest does not demonstrate influence between them; no forced Atlas connection is asserted.

### René Descartes, George Berkeley, David Hume, Immanuel Kant, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, and Schopenhauer

Only Descartes, Kant, and Leibniz appear in the roster; Schopenhauer, Berkeley, and Hume do not.

- **René Descartes — documented intellectual engagement:** dreams, skepticism, and the cogito recur in Borges’s essays and fiction.
- **Immanuel Kant — documented intellectual engagement:** Borges read and discussed Kant, often through Schopenhauer, without adopting a systematic Kantian position.
- **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz — documented intellectual engagement:** possible worlds, identity, combinatorics, and the labyrinthine universe intersect Borges’s stated philosophical interests.

### Aristotle and Plato

**Documented explicit intellectual engagement.** Borges repeatedly used Platonic forms, Aristotelian categories, and the nominalism-realism dispute as literary material.

### Herodotus of Halicarnassus, Plinio el Viejo, and Gaius Julius Solinus

**Documented source relationship.** Borges drew on classical histories, natural histories, and wonder traditions in works such as *El libro de los seres imaginarios*. This constitutes textual influence, not personal connection.

### Maimonides, Moses de León, Judah Loew ben Bezalel, and the Kabbalistic tradition

**Documented intellectual engagement.** Borges studied Jewish theology and Kabbalah through secondary and translated sources. Moses de León and the *Zohar*, the golem tradition associated with Judah Loew, and Jewish philosophical writing fed poems and essays. Direct scholarly mastery of Hebrew sources should not be presumed.

### Ibn Arabi

**Reported/documented textual interest.** Borges referred to Islamic mysticism and the visionary journey tradition. The extent of direct reading versus reading through European Orientalist sources varies by text.

### Walt Whitman and Arthur Schopenhauer

Neither appears on the supplied roster. They are nonetheless among Borges’s most important acknowledged influences; their absence from this Atlas subsection is a roster limitation, not a biographical judgment.

### W. G. Sebald

**Documented literary influence after Borges.** Sebald’s documentary-fiction method and labyrinthine intertextuality have often been read in relation to Borges. This is posthumous influence, not personal contact.

### Additional roster finding

No reliable evidence was found for personal, financial, familial, intelligence, corporate, or political connections between Borges and the great majority of the 896-person roster. Shared appearance in later scholarship, common cities, or thematic resemblance was not treated as a connection.
