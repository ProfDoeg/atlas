# Manuel Antonio Noriega Moreno: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs Manuel Antonio Noriega Moreno’s life from the surviving judicial record, declassified diplomatic and intelligence documents, Panamanian and international human-rights reporting, contemporaneous journalism, memoirs, and major biographies.

Labels used throughout:

- **Documented fact:** established by an official record, contemporaneous document, or uncontested public event.
- **Court finding / conviction:** adjudicated by a named court. A conviction in absentia is identified as such.
- **Reported fact:** supported by credible reporting or multiple secondary accounts but not independently established here by a primary record.
- **Allegation:** asserted by a witness, official, litigant, journalist, or government but not finally adjudicated.
- **Disputed claim:** materially contested by Noriega or other participants.
- **Rumor or myth:** culturally or politically influential but inadequately substantiated.
- **Unresolved question:** available evidence permits no secure conclusion.

Noriega’s birth year, the beginning and financial scale of his American intelligence relationship, responsibility for particular disappearances, the circumstances of Omar Torrijos’s death, the casualty count from the 1989 invasion, and the motives behind successive changes in United States policy remain areas of disagreement.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Manuel Antonio Noriega Moreno.

**Recorded birth name:** Some records shorten it to Manuel Noriega Moreno.

**Titles and offices:**

- General.
- Commander-in-chief of the Panama Defense Forces, August 12, 1983–December 20, 1989.
- De facto ruler or “strongman” of Panama during that period.
- Chief of Panamanian military intelligence, commonly called G-2, approximately 1970–1982.
- He was never constitutional president of Panama.

**Alternative names, nicknames, and designations:**

- **Tony:** used by some acquaintances.
- **El Man:** supportive or familiar popular usage.
- **El Hombre Fuerte / the Strongman:** political and journalistic designation.
- **Cara de Piña / Pineapple Face:** hostile nickname referring to his severely pitted complexion.
- **Old Pineapple Face:** English-language variation.
- **Maximum Leader of National Liberation:** political designation adopted during the confrontation with the United States.
- **CIA asset, agent, informant, or intelligence contact:** overlapping journalistic descriptions. The existence of a paid intelligence relationship is documented; “agent” can misleadingly imply formal CIA employment or American control, neither of which follows automatically from the record.

**Birth:** Usually given as February 11, 1934, Panama City, Panama.

**Birth-date uncertainty:** Sources and identity records have variously supplied 1934, 1936, and 1938. Panamanian reporting says his records may have been altered so that he remained eligible for admission to the Chorrillos Military School. February 11, 1934 is the conventional date, not an entirely secure one.

**Death:** May 29, 2017, Panama City, Panama, following complications connected with surgery for a brain tumor.

**Nationality:** Panamanian.

**Religion:**

- Raised or nominally identified as Roman Catholic.
- Reportedly interested in occult and syncretic practices while in power.
- Announced an evangelical Christian conversion while imprisoned in Miami and was baptized in 1992.

**Spouse:** Felicidad Sieiro de Noriega, a former schoolteacher.

**Children:** Three publicly documented daughters: Lorena, Sandra, and Thays. A 1988 congressional reprinting of a press profile also referred to an illegitimate son; that assertion remains a **reported allegation**, not securely documented in the sources consulted.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and social background

Noriega was born into a poor, racially mixed family in Panama City. Accounts variously locate his childhood in El Terraplén, San Felipe, Santa Ana, Guachimango, or the broader El Chorrillo working-class environment. These names can refer to adjoining or historically changing urban districts, but the exact birth address is uncertain.

His father is usually identified as **Ricaurte Noriega**, an accountant. His mother is identified in English-language accounts as **María Feliz Moreno**, although spelling varies. She reportedly worked as a cook, domestic servant, or laundress and was not married to his father.

His mother died of tuberculosis while he was very young. Accounts say both biological parents were dead by the time he was approximately five. A godmother or foster family subsequently raised him in poor circumstances, reportedly in a one-room dwelling.

Descriptions of his ancestry generally identify African, Indigenous Panamanian, and Spanish components. Assertions that he possessed aristocratic Spanish ancestry are poorly substantiated. A later donation associated with a Spanish parish named Noriega was interpreted by a hostile Panamanian psychological profile as an attempt to construct prestigious Spanish origins; that interpretation is speculative.

### Siblings and half-siblings

A half-brother, **Luis Carlos Noriega**, became important to his education. While attached to the Panamanian diplomatic or consular mission in Peru, Luis Carlos reportedly helped Manuel obtain a Peruvian scholarship and gain admission to the Chorrillos Military School despite the age problem in his records.

The full number and identities of his siblings have not been securely reconstructed from the consulted public sources.

### Marriage and children

Noriega met **Felicidad Sieiro**, reportedly in David, Chiriquí, during the 1960s. They married in the late 1960s and had three daughters:

- Lorena Noriega.
- Sandra Noriega.
- Thays Noriega.

Felicidad’s family was described as being of Basque background and initially opposed to the marriage. Published biographies report repeated marital infidelity by Noriega and at least one period in which Felicidad considered divorce. Such intimate details depend substantially on biographical interviews and hostile accounts rather than court records.

During the 1989 invasion, Felicidad, daughters, a son-in-law, and an infant grandchild reportedly found refuge in the Cuban diplomatic residence. Felicidad remained legally married to Noriega until his death and received his ashes after a private cremation.

## Childhood and Early Life, approximately 1934–1958

Noriega grew up in neighborhoods marked by poverty, port activity, racial hierarchy, political agitation, and the proximity of the United States-controlled Canal Zone.

He attended:

1. **Escuela República de México**, a primary school.
2. **Instituto Nacional de Panamá**, the country’s prominent public secondary school.

The Instituto Nacional was a center of Panamanian nationalism and produced many political and professional leaders. Its political culture emphasized sovereignty over the Canal Zone and resentment of American privilege in Panama.

Accounts differ over his first professional ambition. Several biographies say he wanted to study medicine or psychiatry but lacked the money. The military scholarship to Peru offered a path to status, education, and state employment.

Biographers portray his early personality in sharply conflicting terms: intelligent, observant, socially ambitious, resentful of elite prejudice, adept at cultivating information, and sensitive about his complexion and poverty. These are retrospective characterizations, not clinical findings.

His facial scarring has been attributed to childhood acne or another skin illness. The nickname *Cara de Piña* later transformed this physical feature into political caricature.

---

## Education and Formation, 1958–1962 and Later Training

### Chorrillos Military School

In 1958 Noriega traveled to Lima, Peru, and entered the **Escuela Militar de Chorrillos** with help from his half-brother Luis Carlos. He graduated in 1962 as an engineering officer.

**Disputed record alteration:** Panamanian reporting and major biographies say his birth record was altered to satisfy Chorrillos’s age limit. The conflicting birth years support the possibility, but the original and amended records have not been independently compared here.

At Chorrillos he met or became acquainted with **Roberto Díaz Herrera**, a Panamanian studying in Peru who later became his ally, chief of staff, rival, and public accuser.

Peruvian military education exposed Noriega to the professional officer corps, counterinsurgency doctrine, intelligence work, Latin American nationalism, and the military’s conception of itself as a modernizing political institution.

### American military instruction

Noriega later attended courses connected to the United States military, including training commonly associated with the **School of the Americas** in the Panama Canal Zone. Accounts identify instruction in intelligence, counterintelligence, counterinsurgency, jungle operations, or psychological operations, but published summaries differ about exact courses and dates.

Assertions that the School of the Americas “created” Noriega are an interpretive simplification. He received American training, but his political formation also arose from Panamanian nationalism, Peruvian military culture, the Panamanian National Guard, Omar Torrijos, and his own intelligence career.

---

## Early Military Career, 1962–1968

Noriega returned to Panama in 1962 and entered the **Guardia Nacional** as a second lieutenant. Panama had no conventional army; the Guard combined military, police, internal-security, and political functions.

His early assignments included Colón and Chiriquí Province. He developed a reputation for intelligence collection and political surveillance.

### Early misconduct allegations

Biographies and later profiles attribute several episodes to Noriega in the 1960s, including drunkenness, violence, sexual misconduct, and an alleged 1966 rape of a thirteen-year-old girl followed by an assault on her brother.

These claims must be labeled carefully:

- **Allegation:** The alleged rape appears in biographies and derivative accounts.
- No conviction or full contemporaneous case file was located in the consulted sources.
- Some accounts say military patronage protected him from discipline.
- The episode should not be treated as a proved criminal conviction.

Noriega’s advancement continued despite such accusations. His talent for gathering information and cultivating superiors became more important than conventional field command.

## The 1968 Coup and Alliance with Omar Torrijos, 1968–1981

### October 1968 coup

On October 11, 1968, National Guard officers removed President **Arnulfo Arias Madrid** only days after his inauguration. The coup initially elevated a group of officers rather than a single undisputed leader.

Noriega supported the military regime and aligned himself with **Omar Torrijos Herrera**, who emerged as Panama’s dominant leader.

### December 1969 countercoup

While Torrijos was abroad in Mexico in December 1969, Colonels Amado Sanjur and Ramiro Silvera attempted to remove him. Noriega was in Chiriquí and played an important communications and operational role in enabling Torrijos’s return and defeating the plotters.

This was a decisive career success. Torrijos promoted Noriega to lieutenant colonel and installed him as head of military intelligence, replacing Alejandro Araúz Valencia.

### Chief of G-2

As G-2 chief, Noriega controlled surveillance, informants, foreign liaison, political files, and internal-security operations. Torrijos reportedly described intelligence in categories—public, private, secret between himself and Noriega, and information known only to Noriega—an anecdote that captures Noriega’s growing autonomy but survives principally through later recollection.

The G-2 position gave him contact with:

- Panamanian politicians and business figures.
- Cuban intelligence.
- American military intelligence, the CIA, and the DEA.
- Israeli intelligence and security personnel.
- Central American governments and insurgencies.
- Exiles, arms dealers, smugglers, and dissidents.

A 1976 State Department/CIA memorandum explicitly recorded that Noriega maintained liaison relationships with representatives of Cuban and Israeli intelligence services. That is a **documented fact**, not proof that either service controlled him.

### American intelligence relationship

Noriega’s relationship with United States intelligence began no later than the 1960s and may have originated in the 1950s while he was a student activist or military cadet.

A prosecution financial filing later stated that the United States Army and CIA had paid him approximately **$322,000 between 1955 and 1986**. Other accounts place individual or annual payments much higher, including claims of approximately $100,000 authorized during George H. W. Bush’s CIA directorship and $200,000 in 1986.

The safe conclusions are:

- **Documented fact:** Noriega was a long-term paid source and liaison for United States intelligence.
- **Documented fact:** He supplied political and security intelligence and assisted American operations.
- **Disputed amount:** The complete payment total is obscured by classified records, differing accounting methods, and the possibility of operational funds not treated as personal compensation.
- **Disputed status:** “CIA asset” is supportable; “CIA employee” or “CIA puppet” overstates what the released record proves.

Services attributed to him included:

- Reporting on Torrijos and Panamanian politics.
- Monitoring Cuban influence and regional leftist movements.
- Allowing or facilitating American intelligence collection from Panama.
- Acting as an intermediary with Cuba.
- Supporting operations involving Nicaragua and El Salvador.
- Assisting hostage, narcotics, or counterterrorism contacts.
- Permitting training, transit, banking, and logistical arrangements.

At the same time, American agencies received allegations that he was involved in corruption, arms trafficking, drug protection, election manipulation, and political violence. The central institutional controversy is not whether Washington knew of serious allegations—it did—but why cooperation continued and how competing strategic priorities affected enforcement.

### Canal-treaty politics

Torrijos sought the transfer of the Panama Canal from the United States. Noriega’s intelligence service supported Torrijos during the negotiations that produced the 1977 Carter–Torrijos Treaties.

Noriega was useful to both sides: to Torrijos as an intelligence chief and to Washington as a source inside the Panamanian regime. He also maintained channels with Cuba, allowing Panama to function as an intermediary rather than a simple American client.

### Human-rights abuses during the Torrijos period

Later investigations associated the 1968–1989 military regime with unlawful detention, torture, extrajudicial killing, and disappearance.

A major unresolved case is **Heliodoro Portugal**, a labor activist detained in 1970 and subsequently disappeared. His remains were identified decades later. The Inter-American Court of Human Rights held Panama responsible for the disappearance and failure to investigate. Proceedings concerning Noriega’s individual responsibility remained unfinished or suspended; therefore:

- Portugal’s abduction and disappearance are **documented facts**.
- State responsibility is an **international court finding**.
- Noriega’s personal criminal responsibility was alleged and investigated but not finally adjudicated before his death.

## From Torrijos’s Death to Supreme Command, 1981–1983

Omar Torrijos died in an airplane crash near Coclesito on July 31, 1981. The official explanation was an accident.

### Sabotage narratives

Numerous narratives have alleged that Torrijos was assassinated:

- by the CIA because of the Canal treaties or regional diplomacy;
- by Noriega, who benefited politically;
- through a bomb concealed in recording equipment;
- by other domestic or foreign enemies.

No publicly established judicial finding proves sabotage or Noriega’s responsibility. The stories are historically important but remain **allegation, theory, or myth**.

After Torrijos’s death, military power passed through **Florencio Flores Aguilar** and **Rubén Darío Paredes**. Noriega maneuvered through this succession while retaining control of intelligence.

An informal succession arrangement sometimes called the **“Torrijos Plan”** or military rotation pact was supposed to distribute command among senior officers. Noriega outmaneuvered Paredes and other rivals. On August 12, 1983, he became commander of the National Guard, received the rank of general, and reorganized the security establishment as the **Panama Defense Forces**.

## De Facto Rule, 1983–1989

### Political structure

Noriega ruled indirectly. Civilian presidents retained constitutional form while the Defense Forces controlled coercion, intelligence, significant patronage, and final political decisions.

Presidents serving during his ascendancy included:

- Ricardo de la Espriella.
- Jorge Illueca.
- Nicolás Ardito Barletta.
- Eric Arturo Delvalle.
- Manuel Solís Palma.
- Francisco Rodríguez.

The ruling political instrument was principally the **Democratic Revolutionary Party (PRD)**, founded in the Torrijos tradition. Noriega used the PRD, electoral institutions, the Defense Forces, intelligence organs, and later civilian paramilitary groups.

### 1984 election

The 1984 presidential election pitted Noriega-backed **Nicolás Ardito Barletta** against veteran politician **Arnulfo Arias**.

Evidence from observers, later testimony, and reporting indicates that vote counts were manipulated to award Barletta a narrow victory. This is best described as a **well-documented electoral fraud**, though the precise lawful final tally cannot now be reconstructed with certainty.

### Drug-trafficking arrangements

The United States prosecution alleged that Noriega transformed Panama into a protected transit, meeting, manufacturing, and money-laundering environment for the Medellín cartel.

Evidence at his trial included:

- Testimony that he met cartel leaders.
- A cartel ledger bearing his name.
- Testimony about a $500,000 cash payment.
- Evidence that associates received millions of dollars.
- Evidence concerning protection of shipments and precursor chemicals.
- Sanctuary for cartel members after the 1984 assassination of Colombian justice minister Rodrigo Lara Bonilla.
- Use of Panamanian banking channels for narcotics proceeds.

The credibility of individual prosecution witnesses was contested. Several were convicted traffickers who expected or received consideration. Carlos Lehder’s testimony was at times confused, and the defense argued that American prosecutors built the case from compromised witnesses after Noriega ceased to be geopolitically useful.

Nevertheless, the evidentiary status is not merely allegation: in 1992 a federal jury convicted Noriega on eight counts after trial, and the convictions survived appeal.

### The Spadafora murder, 1985

**Hugo Spadafora**, a physician, former Torrijos associate, guerrilla participant, and increasingly outspoken critic of Noriega, publicly accused the general of drug trafficking and corruption.

In September 1985 Spadafora traveled from Costa Rica toward Panama. He was detained near the border. His tortured, decapitated body was found in Costa Rica, reportedly inside or associated with a United States postal sack; his head was never recovered.

The killing produced a political crisis. President Barletta attempted to establish an independent inquiry. Noriega forced him from office.

A Panamanian court later convicted Noriega in absentia and sentenced him to twenty years for Spadafora’s murder. Thus:

- Spadafora’s detention, torture, and murder are **documented facts**.
- Noriega’s responsibility became a **Panamanian court conviction**, initially in absentia.
- Noriega denied ordering the killing.
- Questions remain about the precise chain of command and identities of every participant.

### Repression, surveillance, and Dignity Battalions

Opposition parties, journalists, business organizations, students, and civil-society groups faced censorship, detention, beatings, intimidation, and economic retaliation.

The **Batallones de la Dignidad**, created during the confrontation with Washington, were civilian paramilitary or militia formations loyal to the regime. They became associated with attacks on opposition demonstrators. The 1989 beating of opposition vice-presidential candidate **Guillermo Ford**, photographed beside the body of a slain bodyguard, became an international symbol of the regime’s violence.

Human-rights reporting and Panama’s later Truth Commission documented torture, cruel treatment, disappearance, and political killing under the 1968–1989 military regime. Attribution to Noriega must be made case by case: command responsibility and institutional control are broad historical findings, while his direct role in each incident was not always adjudicated.

### Removal of Barletta and Delvalle

Noriega forced Barletta’s departure in 1985 after the Spadafora inquiry.

In February 1988 President **Eric Arturo Delvalle**, under American pressure, attempted to dismiss Noriega. The military and Noriega-aligned legislature instead removed Delvalle. Delvalle continued to claim the presidency from hiding or exile.

### Díaz Herrera accusations and 1987 crisis

In June 1987 Colonel **Roberto Díaz Herrera**, recently forced into retirement, publicly accused Noriega of:

- manipulating the 1984 election;
- responsibility for Spadafora’s death;
- involvement in Torrijos’s death;
- corruption and drug trafficking;
- violating the senior-officer succession agreement.

The accusations ignited sustained demonstrations by the **Civic Crusade**.

Evidentiary distinctions:

- Díaz Herrera was a former senior insider and participant in the regime.
- His account is significant testimony but was also issued during a personal and institutional rupture.
- Election manipulation is strongly corroborated.
- Noriega’s responsibility for Spadafora was later adjudicated.
- The Torrijos-assassination accusation remains unproved.

### United States indictments, 1988

Federal grand juries in Miami and Tampa indicted Noriega in February 1988 on narcotics, racketeering, and related charges.

The indictments converted a longstanding intelligence partner into a formally accused fugitive. They also intensified debate over whether prosecution reflected newly actionable evidence, changing Cold War priorities, political embarrassment over Iran–Contra, canal negotiations, or a mixture of these factors.

### Sanctions and failed negotiations

The Reagan administration imposed economic pressure, restricted payments, and attempted to isolate the regime. Panama suffered bank closures, cash shortages, unemployment, and political disorder.

American representatives explored arrangements under which Noriega might leave Panama and receive relief from prosecution. The negotiations failed amid disagreements within the United States government, Noriega’s demands, Panamanian opposition, and doubts about enforceability.

### Failed coup of October 1989

On October 3, 1989, Major **Moisés Giroldi Vera** and other officers attempted to overthrow Noriega and briefly held him. The rebels did not transfer him to United States custody, and American forces did not decisively intervene. Loyal forces recovered Noriega.

Giroldi and other captured officers were executed after surrender or summary proceedings in what became known as the **Albrook massacre**. A Panamanian court later convicted Noriega in absentia in connection with Giroldi’s murder.

## Foreign Relations, Intelligence, and Covert Operations

### Cuba and Fidel Castro

Noriega maintained communication with Cuba while also supplying intelligence to the United States. He served as an intermediary in sensitive matters and met **Fidel Castro**, including a documented or litigated 1984 meeting.

The American prosecution suggested that contacts with Cuba related partly to narcotics. Noriega’s defense said he met Castro on behalf of, or with the knowledge of, American agencies. The trial court ordered disclosure of some government records concerning these contacts.

The relationship demonstrates Noriega’s strategy of making himself useful to mutually hostile governments. It does not establish that he was ideologically communist; his practice was transactional and nationalist.

### Nicaragua and the Contras

During the 1980s Noriega assisted American-backed operations against Nicaragua’s Sandinista government by providing facilities, contacts, training, transit, or proposed sabotage assistance. At other times, arms moved through Panamanian networks toward leftist forces.

An August 23, 1986 White House electronic message documented a proposal conveyed after a meeting between **Oliver North** and a Noriega representative. Noriega offered collaboration against Nicaragua. In September 1986, North met Noriega in London; congressional material records proposed attacks on Nicaraguan targets, including an oil refinery and airport.

This created a documented contradiction in American policy: the State Department and law-enforcement agencies increasingly viewed Noriega as a liability, while elements of the CIA, Defense Department, and National Security Council continued using or encouraging him.

### Israel and other intelligence services

A declassified American memorandum recorded liaison with Israeli as well as Cuban intelligence. Israeli security advisers and arms relationships were part of the wider regional network. Public records do not permit a complete reconstruction of operational details.

### Arms traffic

Noriega and associates were repeatedly linked to weapons moving among governments, insurgencies, and covert American projects.

Not every arms shipment was illegal under Panamanian law or undertaken for personal profit. The record includes official covert activity, tolerated brokerage, and alleged criminal commerce. These categories frequently overlapped and were deliberately obscured.

## The 1989 Election and Final Political Crisis

Presidential elections occurred on May 7, 1989. The principal opposition candidate was **Guillermo Endara**, with Ricardo Arias Calderón and Guillermo Ford as running mates. Noriega supported Carlos Duque.

The opposition won decisively according to independent tallies. The regime annulled the result. Security forces and regime supporters attacked opposition leaders.

In December 1989 the Noriega-controlled legislature declared that a state of war existed with the United States and granted him enlarged authority as “Maximum Leader.”

On December 16, U.S. Marine First Lieutenant **Robert Paz** was shot and killed at a Panamanian roadblock. A U.S. naval officer and his wife were detained and reportedly abused. These incidents became immediate elements in President George H. W. Bush’s public justification for military action.

---

## United States Invasion, Flight, and Surrender, December 1989–January 1990

### Operation Just Cause

On December 20, 1989, approximately 27,000 United States troops, joining forces already stationed in Panama, attacked the Panama Defense Forces.

President Bush stated four principal purposes:

- protect American lives;
- defend democracy;
- preserve the Panama Canal treaties;
- apprehend Noriega for trial.

The invasion destroyed the Defense Forces as an institution and installed Endara, who was sworn in at a United States military facility shortly before or during the operation.

### Casualties and destruction

The number of Panamanian dead remains disputed.

- Official American figures were comparatively low.
- Panamanian organizations and critics proposed totals ranging into the high hundreds or thousands.
- Human Rights Watch/Americas Watch found violations and serious deficiencies but did not substantiate the highest claims.
- El Chorrillo, near the PDF headquarters at La Comandancia, suffered extensive fire and destruction.
- Claims of deliberately concealed mass graves remain disputed; exhumations and later commissions continued examining the dead.

These casualty disputes concern the invasion rather than proof of Noriega’s own conduct, but they profoundly shaped his post-1989 symbolic role.

### Manhunt and the nunciature

American special operations destroyed or disabled Noriega’s private aircraft and boat, limiting escape. Contrary to later dramatic accounts, American intelligence did not initially know his location.

On December 24, Noriega entered the **Apostolic Nunciature**, the Vatican diplomatic mission, under Archbishop José Sebastián Laboa.

American forces surrounded the compound and broadcast loud rock music. The playlist reportedly included songs selected for taunting titles, although popular lists have accumulated tracks not securely verified in contemporaneous logs.

The music was stopped after diplomatic and command objections. Laboa used isolation, religious argument, news of collapsing support, and the prospect that the embassy staff might leave to persuade Noriega to surrender.

On January 3, 1990, Noriega left the nunciature in uniform and surrendered to United States forces. He was flown to Florida.

## United States Prosecution and Imprisonment, 1990–2010

### Legal status and prisoner-of-war ruling

Noriega argued that:

- he was a head of state entitled to immunity;
- his seizure through an invasion violated international law;
- American misconduct required dismissal;
- classified evidence would show that charged activities were authorized intelligence work;
- he qualified as a prisoner of war under the Third Geneva Convention.

The federal courts did not accept immunity or the manner of capture as grounds for dismissal. They held that Washington had never formally recognized Noriega as Panama’s head of state and that courts could try a defendant brought before them despite the circumstances of capture.

The district court did recognize him as a **prisoner of war**. This affected confinement conditions and permitted treatment distinct from ordinary prisoners but did not prevent trial or punishment for pre-capture crimes.

### Trial

His trial began in Miami in 1991. The prosecution relied heavily on traffickers, pilots, brokers, bankers, and former associates.

The defense attacked:

- paid or rewarded witnesses;
- inconsistent testimony;
- the government’s earlier cooperation with Noriega;
- restrictions on classified evidence;
- the claim that intelligence-authorized activity could be separated neatly from the charged conduct.

On April 9, 1992, the jury convicted Noriega on eight of ten counts involving racketeering, conspiracy, narcotics importation and distribution, and related conduct.

He was sentenced to forty years. The sentence was later reduced, ultimately making him eligible for release after approximately seventeen years of imprisonment, with credit and adjustments.

The Eleventh Circuit upheld the essential conviction and rejected his head-of-state and abduction arguments.

### Intelligence evidence at trial

The court allowed the defense to establish that Noriega had worked with American intelligence but limited classified details considered irrelevant or excessively prejudicial.

This generated two competing interpretations:

- **Prosecution position:** legitimate intelligence work did not authorize protection of cartel cocaine operations.
- **Noriega’s position:** Washington selectively concealed the operational context, rewarded trafficker-witnesses, and criminalized activities previously requested or tolerated.
- **Documentary conclusion:** the intelligence relationship and American foreknowledge of allegations are real; the federal convictions are also real. The former does not legally erase the latter.

### Communications controversy

American authorities recorded some of Noriega’s prison telephone calls, including calls with defense lawyers, and portions reached news organizations. Litigation followed over attorney-client privilege and trial prejudice. The courts did not overturn the convictions on that basis.

### Religious conversion

While awaiting trial, Noriega received evangelical visitors including **Clift Brannon**, **Rudy Hernandez**, and lay minister Joe Ponceti. He declared that he had rejected occult practices and accepted evangelical Christianity.

He was baptized by immersion in October 1992 in a portable pool or tub under secure conditions.

Evidentiary status:

- His profession of conversion and baptism are **documented reported events**.
- Ministers regarded it as sincere.
- Skeptics interpreted it as prison adaptation or image management.
- Inner sincerity cannot be historically adjudicated.

### Memoir

From prison, Noriega worked with journalist **Peter Eisner** on *America’s Prisoner: The Memoirs of Manuel Noriega* (1997).

The book presented him as:

- a Panamanian nationalist;
- a useful but ultimately betrayed American intelligence partner;
- a mediator in regional conflicts;
- the victim of a politically selective prosecution;
- an opponent of United States domination.

Eisner’s participation and introduction did not constitute endorsement of every claim. The work combines interviews, Noriega’s dictated or recounted account, journalistic framing, and self-defense; it should be used as a primary source for Noriega’s version, not as an independently verified chronology.

## French Proceedings and Imprisonment, 2010–2011

France had convicted Noriega **in absentia in 1999** for laundering narcotics proceeds through French banks and Paris property.

When his American custodial sentence ended, France sought extradition. Noriega argued that his prisoner-of-war status required repatriation to Panama. The United States courts and Supreme Court did not prevent extradition.

He was transferred to France on April 26, 2010 and received a new trial.

In July 2010 a Paris court convicted him of laundering drug proceeds and sentenced him to seven years. The proceedings concerned millions of dollars routed through accounts and used to acquire luxury Paris apartments.

Noriega said the funds came from:

- CIA payments;
- his brother’s inheritance;
- his wife’s resources;
- legitimate state or intelligence transactions.

The French court rejected that account. Approximately €2.3 million or $3.6 million in frozen assets was reportedly subject to confiscation, though currency conversions and reported totals differ.

He was held in **La Santé Prison** and other French custodial or medical facilities.

---

## Return to Panama, Imprisonment, Illness, and Death, 2011–2017

France extradited Noriega to Panama in December 2011.

He was imprisoned at **El Renacer**, near the Panama Canal, under Panamanian sentences totaling several decades. Because of Panamanian rules governing concurrent sentences and maximum imprisonment for offenses committed under earlier law, headline totals such as sixty years did not necessarily represent the time legally serviceable.

His convictions included responsibility for:

- the murder of Hugo Spadafora;
- the killing of Moisés Giroldi;
- other killings, unlawful detentions, or disappearances reported in Panamanian proceedings.

Some cases remained pending, including proceedings connected to Heliodoro Portugal. Amnesty International called for broader investigation rather than treating the existing convictions as a complete accounting.

### Health

By his return, Noriega was elderly and suffered various reported conditions, including:

- hypertension;
- cerebrovascular problems or a prior stroke;
- respiratory or cardiac concerns;
- prostate problems in some reports;
- a brain tumor, later identified as a meningioma.

His lawyers repeatedly sought hospital treatment or house arrest. Critics suspected preferential treatment; physicians and family cited substantial medical risk.

In January 2017 a court granted temporary house arrest so he could prepare for surgery.

### Brain surgery and death

In March 2017 surgeons operated to remove the brain tumor. Postoperative bleeding required another operation. He remained in critical condition.

Noriega died on May 29, 2017, in Panama City, aged eighty-three under the conventional 1934 birth date.

His body was cremated privately. His ashes were delivered to Felicidad Sieiro.

No state funeral or broad official commemoration followed.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Noriega was not principally an inventor, industrialist, or conventional entrepreneur. No patents or verified technological inventions were identified.

His material power depended on institutions and opaque financial networks:

### Institutions controlled or influenced

- Panama National Guard.
- G-2 military intelligence.
- Panama Defense Forces.
- Democratic Revolutionary Party political machinery.
- Dignity Battalions.
- State broadcasting and censorship bodies.
- Customs, immigration, police, and airport authorities.
- Panamanian diplomatic and commercial channels.

### Banking and laundering structures

Panama’s bank-secrecy environment allowed legal offshore activity as well as tax evasion, capital flight, covert state transfers, and criminal laundering.

Court evidence connected Noriega or his associates to:

- accounts in Panama and France;
- BCCI-related financial channels in some published accounts;
- Paris real estate;
- intermediary companies and nominees;
- cash deliveries from traffickers;
- payments routed through military or intelligence associates.

### Personal fortune

Estimates of his fortune ranged widely, from several million dollars to tens or hundreds of millions. The highest estimates are inadequately documented.

Reliable conclusions are narrower:

- He received secret foreign-intelligence payments.
- United States and French courts found that he received or laundered narcotics-derived proceeds.
- He accumulated assets inconsistent with an ordinary Panamanian officer’s salary.
- The total fortune, beneficial ownership of particular properties, and division between personal money, operational funds, family assets, and nominee holdings remain incompletely reconstructed.

### Cocaine-processing site

The prosecution contended that Noriega protected a cocaine-processing laboratory in Panama and later ordered it raided during a dispute or after political pressure. Drug witnesses described the raid as part of his bargaining relationship with the Medellín cartel; Noriega portrayed anti-drug actions as proof of cooperation with the DEA. The raid occurred, but its motive remained disputed.

## Political, Ideological, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Panamanian nationalism

Noriega’s most consistent public ideology was Panamanian sovereignty, especially control of the Canal and resistance to overt American direction.

His nationalism was compatible with extensive secret cooperation with Washington. He interpreted no contradiction in taking American money while claiming freedom of maneuver for Panama.

### Torrijismo

He inherited the language and institutions of **Torrijismo**:

- nationalism;
- military-led social modernization;
- populist identification with poorer and racially mixed Panamanians;
- nonalignment and diplomacy with Cuba;
- organized civilian political support;
- military guardianship over electoral institutions.

Under Noriega, the redistributive and charismatic features associated with Torrijos weakened while intelligence control, patronage, coercion, and personal survival became more prominent.

### Anti-communism and transactional diplomacy

Noriega provided anti-communist intelligence and assisted Contra-related projects. Simultaneously, he dealt with Cuba, the Sandinistas, and leftist movements.

It is more accurate to describe him as transactional and regime-centered than as consistently communist or anti-communist.

### Occult and symbolic practices

American troops and journalists reported ritual objects, religious images, candles, animal remains, or occult materials in residences and offices after the invasion.

Some accounts connected him to Santería, spiritualism, witchcraft, or protective rituals. The record is complicated by:

- Panama’s syncretic religious culture;
- hostile military propaganda;
- sensational photography of decontextualized objects;
- Noriega’s own later evangelical testimony that he had abandoned occult practices.

It is supportable that he showed interest in ritual or esoteric protection. Claims that black magic controlled his political decisions, or that supernatural practices explain his survival, belong to **hostile mythology and self-protective legend**.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Omar Torrijos Herrera

Patron, superior, and political model. Noriega helped restore Torrijos after the 1969 countercoup and became his intelligence chief. Torrijos depended on him but reportedly distrusted his accumulation of secrets.

### Roberto Díaz Herrera

Military-school acquaintance and later chief of staff. Initially an ally and member of the senior command, Díaz Herrera became Noriega’s most damaging insider accuser in 1987.

### Hugo Spadafora

Former regime associate turned opponent. His narcotics accusations and 1985 murder became central to Noriega’s collapse and later Panamanian conviction.

### Rubén Darío Paredes

Predecessor as Guard commander and eventual rival. Noriega reportedly encouraged Paredes’s presidential ambitions and then displaced him.

### Nicolás Ardito Barletta

Civilian president installed after the disputed 1984 election. Forced from office when he pursued an independent inquiry into Spadafora’s death.

### Eric Arturo Delvalle

Civilian president who tried to dismiss Noriega in February 1988 and was himself removed.

### Guillermo Endara, Ricardo Arias Calderón, and Guillermo Ford

Opposition electoral leadership in 1989. Their victory was annulled; Endara became president during the American invasion.

### Medellín cartel figures and intermediaries

Trial testimony connected Noriega with Pablo Escobar, Jorge and Fabio Ochoa, Gustavo Gaviria, Carlos Lehder, and numerous intermediaries. Direct relationships varied; some meetings were proved through witness evidence accepted by the jury, while other claims rested on compromised cooperating witnesses.

### Floyd Carlton-Cáceres

Pilot and former associate who became a major prosecution witness. The defense characterized him as self-interested and unreliable; the jury nevertheless accepted enough of the prosecution case to convict.

### José Blandón

Former Panamanian official and consul who supplied extensive allegations about Noriega’s covert relationships, including American contacts. Valuable insider source, but also a defector with political and legal interests.

### United States intelligence and political officials

Noriega dealt with officials across the CIA, Defense Department, DEA, State Department, and National Security Council. Policy toward him was not unified. Some treated him as an intelligence source; others pursued narcotics allegations or sought his removal.

### Clift Brannon and Rudy Hernandez

Evangelical ministers who counseled Noriega in American custody and supported the sincerity of his conversion.

### Peter Eisner

Journalist and coauthor/editorial collaborator on Noriega’s prison memoir.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

### Adjudicated matters

1. **United States narcotics and racketeering case — conviction:** Federal jury conviction in 1992 on eight counts. Sentence initially forty years, later reduced.
2. **French money laundering — conviction:** Convicted in absentia in 1999, retried and convicted in Paris in 2010; seven-year sentence.
3. **Hugo Spadafora murder — Panamanian conviction:** Twenty-year sentence imposed in absentia.
4. **Moisés Giroldi/Albrook killings — Panamanian conviction:** Noriega was convicted in connection with the failed 1989 coup’s aftermath.
5. **Other Panamanian cases:** Multiple sentences were reported for killings, disappearances, corruption, or unlawful detention, but public summaries do not always agree on exact case totals and procedural status.

### Documented institutional conduct without complete individual adjudication

- Electoral manipulation.
- Censorship and suppression of opposition media.
- Political surveillance.
- Use of military and paramilitary violence.
- Torture and cruel treatment under the military regime.
- Enforced disappearances.
- Secret foreign-intelligence relationships.
- Covert regional arms and counterinsurgency activity.

### Sexual-violence allegation

The reported 1966 rape of a minor was not shown in the consulted sources to have produced a conviction. It remains a grave but unadjudicated allegation.

### Drug-witness reliability

Many prosecution witnesses were criminals negotiating their own treatment. This does not automatically invalidate testimony, but it is essential context.

The federal jury heard cross-examination and still convicted. Later criticism has argued that:

- the government minimized Noriega’s intelligence usefulness;
- witnesses obtained extraordinary concessions;
- classified-information restrictions limited the defense;
- the invasion gave prosecutors custody they could not have obtained through ordinary extradition.

Those criticisms concern fairness, geopolitics, and evidentiary completeness. They do not alter the formal status of the convictions.

### American complicity and selective enforcement

The declassified record supports the following:

- American agencies knew substantial accusations against Noriega long before 1988.
- Strategic agencies continued working with him.
- American policy branches sent conflicting signals.
- His usefulness in Cuba, Nicaragua, El Salvador, intelligence collection, and Canal politics delayed a clean rupture.

The stronger claim—that the CIA expressly authorized all the narcotics conduct for which he was convicted—was Noriega’s defense and remains unproved.

### Operation Just Cause legality

The United States characterized the invasion as self-defense, treaty protection, defense of democracy, and law enforcement. Critics described it as an unlawful intervention or aggression.

The United Nations General Assembly condemned the intervention as a “flagrant violation of international law.” The Organization of American States also deplored it. American domestic courts did not treat the invasion’s legality as a bar to Noriega’s prosecution.

### Invasion casualties

Figures remain disputed because of incomplete records, rapid burials, destroyed neighborhoods, and political incentives on all sides. Neither the lowest official number nor the highest activist estimates should be presented as uncontested.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The CIA created Noriega”

**Story:** Noriega was recruited, trained, financed, and placed in power by the CIA, then discarded when inconvenient.

**Circulation:** Anti-intervention literature, documentaries, left-wing journalism, conspiracy narratives, and compressed popular histories after 1988.

**Evidence for:** Long-term paid intelligence relationship; American training; declassified liaison records; years of American tolerance; meetings with senior officials.

**Evidence against or limiting:** He arose primarily within Panama’s Guard and Torrijos network; Washington did not control his decisions; American agencies frequently opposed one another; he simultaneously dealt with Cuba and other actors.

**Assessment:** Based on a real relationship but mythologized into total control.

### “Noriega was only prosecuted because he defied Washington”

**Story:** Drug allegations were a pretext; his real offense was nationalism or refusal to obey American policy.

**Evidence for:** Allegations were known while he remained useful; the timing of aggressive enforcement followed political rupture; American officials attempted to negotiate his departure; classified context was restricted.

**Evidence against:** Extensive narcotics evidence preceded the final rupture, and juries in the United States and France convicted him.

**Assessment:** Political timing and selective tolerance are well supported; complete fabrication of the narcotics case is not.

### “Noriega killed Omar Torrijos”

**Story:** Noriega arranged the 1981 plane crash to clear his path to power.

**Circulation:** Díaz Herrera’s 1987 accusations, exile politics, biographies, and conspiracy literature.

**Evidence for:** Noriega benefited eventually and controlled sensitive intelligence.

**Evidence against:** He did not immediately succeed Torrijos; no public forensic or judicial proof establishes sabotage or his role.

**Assessment:** Unresolved allegation, often repeated as fact without adequate evidence.

### “The CIA killed Torrijos”

**Story:** American intelligence assassinated Torrijos because of the Canal treaties, Japan-related canal proposals, or regional independence.

**Evidence:** Motive is asserted through geopolitical conflict; versions involve a bomb in a tape recorder.

**Problems:** No conclusive released operational record or judicial finding proves the story.

**Assessment:** Influential anti-imperial narrative; unproved.

### Rock music “drove him out”

**Story:** American troops defeated Noriega psychologically by blasting Van Halen, the Clash, Bon Jovi, Rick Astley, or other rock music until he surrendered.

**Evidence for:** Loud music was actually broadcast outside the nunciature, and a military playlist existed.

**Evidence against:** The broadcasts were curtailed; Laboa’s diplomacy, isolation, collapse of military support, crowd hostility, and fear of losing sanctuary provide stronger explanations. Some songs in internet playlists are later embellishments.

**Assessment:** A real tactic inflated into a comic legend that trivializes both diplomacy and the invasion.

### “Noriega was a black magician”

**Story:** He relied on Santería, witchcraft, animal sacrifice, protective talismans, or demonic power.

**Evidence for:** Ritual and religious objects were reportedly found; Noriega later said he had renounced occult interests.

**Problems:** Objects were interpreted by hostile occupiers and sensational media; syncretic practice was conflated with criminality.

**Assessment:** A probable personal interest expanded into racialized and political demonology.

### Pineapple-face mythology

His scarred face became a visual shorthand for corruption, ugliness, duplicity, and dictatorship. This hostile mythology converted physical appearance into moral evidence and appeared in cartoons, headlines, masks, and military propaganda.

### Nationalist David against Goliath

Regime propaganda depicted Noriega as a racially mixed, popular Panamanian defending sovereignty against an American empire.

The image drew on genuine Canal nationalism and American interventionism. It obscured electoral fraud, domestic repression, and his long secret cooperation with United States agencies.

### “The invasion ended narcotics trafficking in Panama”

American rhetoric associated Noriega’s removal with victory in the drug war. Critics subsequently noted that regional and Panamanian trafficking continued and that rival networks benefited.

**Assessment:** A political claim disproved as a broad prediction; removing one protector or network did not eliminate Panama’s structural role in illicit finance and transshipment.

## Lawsuit Against Activision

In 2014 Noriega sued **Activision Blizzard** in California over his depiction in *Call of Duty: Black Ops II*. He alleged unauthorized commercial use of his name and likeness and objected to being portrayed as a kidnapper, murderer, and enemy.

Judge William H. Fahey dismissed the complaint under California’s anti-SLAPP framework. The court held that the expressive and transformative use was protected and that the game’s commercial value did not principally derive from Noriega’s likeness.

This was a **court dismissal**, not a finding that every fictional event in the game was historically accurate.

## Works by or Under the Name of Manuel Noriega

### Book-length work

**Manuel Noriega with Peter Eisner, *America’s Prisoner: The Memoirs of Manuel Noriega*.** New York: Random House, 1997.

- Spanish title commonly rendered as *El prisionero de América*.
- Based on prison interviews, dictated recollection, and collaboration with Eisner.
- Best classified as an authorized, coauthored or journalistically shaped memoir.
- It is genuine as Noriega’s account but not independently reliable in every claim.
- It defends his intelligence work, challenges the drug prosecution, and recounts dealings with George H. W. Bush, Oliver North, Fidel Castro, and other officials.

### Speeches and declarations

No authoritative collected edition of Noriega’s speeches was identified. Surviving items are dispersed across:

- Panamanian television and radio archives.
- Defense Forces ceremonies.
- PRD and Dignity Battalion rallies.
- Press conferences during the 1987–1989 crisis.
- Statements declaring a state of war with the United States.
- Recorded statements after his surrender and during litigation.

These were official or public utterances, often prepared with staff assistance. Individual authorship cannot always be established.

### Interviews and prison statements

Important categories include:

- Interviews incorporated into *America’s Prisoner*.
- Statements to American, French, and Panamanian courts.
- Prison correspondence with evangelical ministers.
- Religious testimonial material concerning his conversion.
- Media interviews defending his record and alleging American betrayal.

No verified diaries, technical papers, patents, artistic works, or independently authored scholarly publications were found.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Noriega’s reputation remains divided among several documentary and symbolic roles:

1. **Convicted trafficker and money launderer:** Established through American and French convictions.
2. **Military ruler responsible for political violence:** Supported by Panamanian convictions, human-rights documentation, and the institutional record.
3. **American intelligence partner:** Established by declassified records and courtroom disclosures.
4. **Example of American strategic tolerance:** His continued usefulness despite known accusations became a case study in the conflict between covert policy and law enforcement.
5. **Symbol of Panamanian nationalism:** Retained in some anti-intervention narratives, especially regarding Canal sovereignty and the 1989 invasion.
6. **Precedent in foreign-leader prosecution:** His immunity, capture, and prisoner-of-war litigation remain relevant to later cases involving foreign rulers seized or prosecuted by the United States.
7. **Popular-culture villain:** Facial caricature, rock-music stories, films, and video games detached him from Panamanian history and converted him into a generic late-Cold War dictator.
8. **Witness to covert Cold War networks:** His career connected official diplomacy, intelligence liaison, counterinsurgency, narcotics enforcement, banking secrecy, arms traffic, and criminal brokerage.
9. **Incomplete human-rights accounting:** His death ended pending proceedings without resolving every disappearance or chain of command.
10. **Contested invasion memory:** For many Panamanians, his removal cannot be separated from civilian deaths, destruction in El Chorrillo, and the precedent of unilateral American force.

Recent 2025–2026 commentary has revived Noriega chiefly as a legal and historical precedent for capturing and prosecuting a de facto foreign ruler. Such comparisons illuminate immunity doctrine but can obscure major differences between individual cases.

- As Reagan’s CIA director, Casey oversaw an agency that continued dealing with Noriega.
- Declassified 1987 briefing material shows Casey receiving policy analysis about how to influence or manage Noriega.
- Former Panamanian official José Blandón reported that Noriega traveled to Washington for meetings with Casey in the early 1980s.
- The policy and institutional connection is documented; every alleged private meeting is not independently confirmed.

- North, a National Security Council official, dealt with Noriega and his representatives in connection with support and proposed sabotage against Nicaragua.
