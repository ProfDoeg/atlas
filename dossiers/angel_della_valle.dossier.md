# Ángel della Valle: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Ángel della Valle was an Argentine painter, illustrator, teacher, and arts organizer associated with the emergence of professional artistic institutions in Buenos Aires during the late nineteenth century. The surviving record is heavily weighted toward his training, exhibitions, teaching, and artworks. Published sources disclose remarkably little about his mother, siblings, marital status, children, religion, personal finances, health, or burial.

Two basic chronological traditions conflict:

- The Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes (MNBA), the Buenos Aires municipal collection catalogue, and the identification supplied for this dossier use **1852–1903**.
- Guiomar de Urgell’s monographic research, repeated by *La Nación*, Proyecto Tramas, auction catalogues, and several authority records, gives **10 October 1855–16 July 1903**.

The detailed 1855 account identifies him as his parents’ third child and places his birth at their San Telmo house. The consulted online sources do not reproduce a baptismal or civil birth record capable of settling the disagreement. Consequently, the exact birth year remains unresolved here.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by institutional catalogue data, archival objects, contemporary reporting, or multiple strong sources.
- **Reported fact:** found in later biographical literature but not independently established from a primary document consulted here.
- **Interpretation:** an art-historical reading, not a biographical event.
- **Allegation or theory:** a claim for which the consulted evidence does not establish intent or occurrence.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulated story without adequate documentary confirmation.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Ángel Domingo Juan del Sagrado Corazón de Jesús Della Valle.
- **Usual professional name:** Ángel Della Valle; frequently normalized as Ángel della Valle.
- **Other catalogue forms:** Angel Della Valle; Angelo della Valle; Della Valle, Ángel.
- **No documented pseudonym, title, or stable nickname** was found.
- **Nationality:** Argentine.
- **Occupation:** Painter, portraitist, illustrator, drawing and painting teacher, institutional organizer, and briefly director of a workers’ drawing school.
- **Principal classifications:** Argentine realism, academic naturalism, costumbrismo, rural or “criollo” painting, and the artistic “Generation of ’80.”
- **Birth:** Buenos Aires, either **10 October 1852** or **10 October 1855**.
- **Death:** **16 July 1903**, Buenos Aires.
- **Age at death:** either fifty or fifty-one if born in 1852, or forty-seven if born in 1855. A contemporary-contextual MNBA publication called him fifty-one, supporting the 1852 tradition.
- **Principal residences and workplaces:**
  - Family home on Calle Luján in San Telmo, Buenos Aires—later renumbered as José Modesto Giuffra 334 or, in another source, 356.
  - Florence, Italy, approximately 1875–1883; some later biographies extend the stay to about eleven years by beginning it in 1872.
  - Workshop at Calle Luján 156, Buenos Aires, after his return.
  - From 1898, a studio or “local” in the Bon Marché building, now Galerías Pacífico, at Florida and Córdoba.
- **Best-known work:** *La vuelta del malón* (1892), oil on canvas, 186.5 × 292 cm, MNBA inventory 6297.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father

**José Della Valle** was an Italian immigrant and builder.

The *Diccionario Biográfico Italo-Argentino* reports that he was born near Lake Maggiore, participated in political movements in Italy, and emigrated to Argentina in **1848**. Other modern catalogues say that he settled in Argentina in **1842**. The discrepancy remains unresolved.

In Buenos Aires he reportedly began as a bricklayer and became a recognized building contractor, working for the Anchorena family and other affluent clients. Municipal and secondary accounts also associate him with buildings commissioned under the government of **Juan Manuel de Rosas**. Ángel later painted a portrait of his father in holiday dress.

The family’s financial situation is described incompatibly:

- Municipal, collection, and dictionary accounts say José acquired a comfortable position that financed Ángel’s education in Florence.
- A detailed *La Nación* profile calls him a modest contractor who died during the **1871 yellow-fever epidemic**, leaving the family nearly destitute. It represents Ángel’s Italian training as the achievement of a determined mother under financial strain.
- A contemporary quotation reproduced in the same profile described the young painter’s “silent battle,” sometimes marked by poverty and insufficient state support.

These versions might describe prosperity followed by collapse after José’s death, but the sources consulted do not document the estate closely enough to reconcile them.

### Mother, siblings, spouse, and descendants

Later accounts describe Ángel as the **third child** of an Italian immigrant couple, but the consulted sources do not name his mother or siblings. No dependable record of a wife, partner, or children was found. “The artist’s family” controlled *La vuelta del malón* after his death, but that phrase does not identify heirs or prove direct descendants.

No family relationship has been established between the painter and other notable Della Valle families.

## Childhood and Early Life

Della Valle grew up in **San Telmo**, in the Calle Luján house where the family lived and where he would later work. The precise modern number is disputed: one account gives Giuffra 334, while the 1966 memorial plaque is reported at Giuffra 356.

He entered the **Colegio San José** in **1871**, according to the Colección AMALITA biography. There he met **Pedro Lagleyze**, later a prominent ophthalmologist and his closest documented lifelong friend. Some accounts call them childhood friends; the school date, if correct, makes them adolescent schoolmates.

His earliest art instruction is reported under:

- **José Bouchet**
- **Martín Boneo**
- **Ignacio Manzoni**

The evidence for the exact sequence and duration of these lessons is limited. Buenos Aires then lacked a stable academy comparable to the European institutions available to aspiring painters. Private lessons, workshops, and study abroad were the ordinary routes for advanced artistic formation.

If the 1855 birth date is correct, Della Valle was about fifteen or sixteen when his father died in 1871 and twenty when he sailed in 1875. If born in 1852, he was eighteen or nineteen at his father’s death and twenty-two or twenty-three at departure.

## Education and Formation

### Colegio San José

Della Valle reportedly began at Colegio San José in 1871. The Colección AMALITA account explicitly cautions that no records consulted by its author establish how long he remained there; continuation until 1875 is an inference, not a documented fact.

### Florence

In **1875**, supported or authorized by his family, Della Valle traveled to Italy and settled in **Florence**. Sources that use the 1852 birth year sometimes say he traveled at twenty; those using 1855 make that age internally consistent.

He studied in the free school or **Società Cooperativa di Studenti** associated with the Swiss-Italian painter **Antonio Ciseri**. Ciseri had established a private school in Florence in 1849/1853 and also taught at the Accademia di Belle Arti. His atelier joined formal instruction with an international environment of student sociability.

Argentine and Río de la Plata artists associated with Ciseri’s teaching included Martín Boneo, Mariano Agrelo, Claudio Lastra, Juan Manuel Blanes, and Della Valle. Della Valle’s reported fellow students included:

- José Bouchet
- Lucio Correa Morales
- Francisco Cafferata
- Augusto Ballerini

Ciseri’s precise draftsmanship, controlled composition, polished surfaces, religious and historical subjects, and near-photographic realism shaped Della Valle’s academic technique. Municipal catalogue scholarship also detects his exposure to:

- Italian **verismo**
- The **Macchiaioli**
- Painters and imagery of the **Risorgimento**
- Academic copying of old masters
- Figure and nude studies
- Orientalist and exotic subjects

Della Valle apparently began producing Pampa subjects while still in Florence. This is important because the most famous later image of the Argentine frontier was not necessarily the result of firsthand residence among Indigenous communities. No source consulted documents an expedition by him to frontier settlements or Indigenous territories.

### Length of the Italian period

The best-supported working chronology is **1875–1883**, approximately eight years. A competing tradition places his departure around 1872 and describes roughly eleven years in Florence. The latter may derive from attempts to harmonize an 1852 birth year with statements that he traveled at twenty. No passenger record or academy register was found in the consulted material to resolve it.

## Early Career

### *Prometeo encadenado*

Della Valle’s first clearly reported institutional success was *Prometeo encadenado*. Sources variously date it to **1881** or associate it with the **Continental Exhibition of 1882**. The painting received or was selected for a silver medal. The difference may reflect a painting date of 1881 and exhibition in 1882.

### Return to Buenos Aires

He returned to Argentina in **1883** and installed a studio in the family house. The return shifted his activity from European academic formation to the small but increasingly organized Buenos Aires art world.

He initially found a market in portraiture, then became especially associated with horses, gauchos, soldiers, railway scenes, rural labor, bandits, and the Pampa. A commercial-gallery survey estimates about eighty rural works, but this is not a complete catalogue raisonné and should not be treated as a definitive oeuvre count.

### Illustration

From about **1883–1884**, Della Valle collaborated with *La Ilustración Argentina*. The image *Las amigas* is catalogued by Google Arts & Culture as an 1884 work; the accompanying record calls his depiction of a popular Rosista figure his earliest known contribution to the periodical.

Illustration connected him to the growing illustrated press and extended his practice beyond easel painting. No complete checklist of his magazine illustrations was located.

## Chronological Life History

### 1883–1887: return and professional establishment

After returning from Florence, Della Valle worked as a portraitist and rural-subject painter from San Telmo. He became part of a network that included Eduardo Sívori, Eduardo Schiaffino, Reinaldo Giudici, Ernesto de la Cárcova, Augusto Ballerini, Lucio Correa Morales, Francisco Cafferata, and Severo Rodríguez Etchart.

Sources disagree over the beginning of his formal teaching:

- Colección AMALITA gives **1885**, when he and Reinaldo Giudici replaced or succeeded Francesco Romero.
- Scholarship on Italian artistic influence gives **1887**, when he joined the academy of the Sociedad Estímulo de Bellas Artes.
- Later memorial accounts summarize his service as fifteen or eighteen years.

He taught drawing and painting, apparently without salary for much or all of this service. Classes were held three evenings per week according to later biographical summaries.

In **1887** he sent or exhibited Pampa-related paintings including *En la pampa* and *La banda lisa*. MNBA scholarship treats them as preliminary thematic approaches to *La vuelta del malón*, though not necessarily literal sketches.

### 1888–1891: consolidation

Works attributed to these years include:

- *El tren en la Pampa* or *Tren de la Pampa* (1888)
- *Atardecer en la laguna* / *Anochecer en la laguna* (1889)
- *La diosa del Amor* (1890)
- *La Literatura*, visible in a circa-1890 photograph of Della Valle at Pedro Lagleyze’s house
- *Juan Moreira*, exhibited in 1891 in a show organized by the Sociedad de Nuestra Señora del Carmen

*Juan Moreira*, based on the famous gaucho-outlaw narrative, shows that Della Valle’s rural imagery was not uniformly celebratory. Art historians have read it alongside *La captura del bandido* as representing the defeated or persecuted gaucho, a figure situated ambiguously between criminality, resistance, and abuse by state power.

Della Valle, Ballerini, and De la Cárcova are also reported as founders of **La Colmena**, an organization that staged painting and humorous-art exhibitions involving Argentine and foreign artists. Documentation located for this dossier is secondary rather than a foundation charter.

### 1892: *La vuelta del malón*

Della Valle completed *La vuelta del malón* expressly for the Argentine submission to the **World’s Columbian Exposition** in Chicago. It was first publicly displayed in July 1892 in the shop window of **Nocetti y Repetto**, a hardware and paint business on Calle Florida. Buenos Aires did not yet possess a developed system of permanent commercial galleries, so shop windows were important exhibition spaces.

Contemporary notices appeared in:

- *La Revue Illustrée du Rio de la Plata*
- *Sud-América*
- *El Diario*
- *La Prensa*
- *Buenos Aires Ilustrado*
- *La Ilustración Sud-Americana*

Carlos Ripamonte later recalled crowds overflowing from the sidewalk into the street to see the painting. It was called, in later institutional summaries of its reception, the first “genuinely national” work of Argentine art. That formula records canon formation and contemporary nationalist reception; it is not an objectively verifiable artistic first.

The monumental canvas shows mounted Indigenous raiders crossing a wet Pampa after an attack. Their booty includes livestock, luggage, Christian liturgical objects, severed heads, and a semi-conscious white captive carried by a male rider. The storm is clearing at dawn. The cross, lance, dark sky, illuminated horizon, captive, and apparently desecrated sacred objects organize a contrast between “civilization” and “barbarism.”

According to a contemporary **August 1892 interview by Julio Botet**, Della Valle said the subject derived from a raid led by the cacique **Cayutril** with the capitanejo **Caimán**, though he did not identify the attacked settlement. *Sud-América* instead associated the episode with **25 de Mayo**. No independent record consulted establishes that the depicted event occurred exactly as painted.

Della Valle served as a juror in the first Salón del Ateneo that year. The work’s public popularity did not immediately produce a buyer.

### 1893: Chicago

*La vuelta del malón* traveled to the World’s Columbian Exposition in Chicago in 1893 and received a medal or award of merit. Accounts link Pedro Lagleyze closely to the journey; some say he accompanied or carried the painting to Chicago. In gratitude, Della Valle gave him a reduced replica, later called the **“Malón chico.”**

The small version is frequently described as a preparatory sketch, but MNBA scholarship states that it was a later gift replica. Della Valle also produced at least three separate paintings based on fragments of the large composition, including the warrior and captive and the rider brandishing a cross.

Other works dated 1893 include:

- *La estación de Lomas de Zamora*
- *Corrida de sortija en día patrio*

The latter, a large oil on canvas measuring approximately 123 × 210 cm, remained in private hands according to the commercial record consulted.

### 1894–1897: Ateneo, portraiture, and public roles

*La vuelta del malón* appeared in the **second exhibition of the Ateneo** in May or October 1894, depending on how sources distinguish the event’s opening and contemporary notices. Press discussion was extensive but not unanimous. Some critics admired the technical control and national subject; others mocked the captive’s appearance and pose or found the painting theatrically arranged.

Works associated with 1894 include:

- *La cautiva*, oil on canvas, now in a private collection and catalogued as number 106 in Urgell’s catalogue.
- *La captura del bandido*.
- *El general Roca pasando revista*, reported in later sources but not located in an institutional object record during this research.

Pedro Lagleyze mediated Della Valle’s access to **President Julio Argentino Roca**, whom Lagleyze treated as an ophthalmologist. Della Valle painted Roca’s portrait. Whether Roca commissioned *La vuelta del malón* is a separate question: no evidence consulted shows that he did.

In 1896, Della Valle exhibited a large *Retrato de Pedro Lagleyze*, 140 × 90 cm, at the Ateneo’s fourth annual exhibition and first triennial. Josefina Monty de Lagleyze later donated it to the Asociación Médica Argentina.

That year Della Valle also served on an Ateneo jury with Eduardo Schiaffino, Rafael Obligado, Ernesto de la Cárcova, Lucio Correa Morales, and Rafael Igarzábal.

On **16 July 1897**, a government decree created the **Comisión Nacional de Bellas Artes**. Della Valle was appointed to its painting section with Schiaffino, De la Cárcova, Ballerini, and Sívori.

He participated in the commission that selected works from the collection of **Aristóbulo del Valle** for the young MNBA. This placed him not only among producers of Argentine art but among those shaping the national collection.

### 1898–1901: Bon Marché studio and teaching

In **1898**, Della Valle moved or added his studio to the **Bon Marché** building. The Sociedad Estímulo academy occupied the second floor, while his atelier was on the third. He taught within the academy and also gave private studio instruction.

His painting *El taller* depicts the studio, with *La vuelta del malón* visible within it and a separate canvas showing a rearing horse. Later writers interpreted the empty studio retrospectively as an omen of his death there; this is symbolic interpretation, not evidence that he intended a premonition.

Della Valle continued producing Pampa subjects around 1900, including:

- *Incendio en la Pampa*
- *Apartando*
- *El taller*

In 1900, temporary Ateneo exhibitions operated within the Bon Marché. In 1901, a group exhibition assembled Della Valle with Sívori, Schiaffino, De la Cárcova, Ballerini, Emilio Caraffa, and Severo Rodríguez Etchart.

### 1901–1903: workers’ education

Della Valle became the first director of a **School of Drawing** established by the Sociedad de Educación Industrial for working men and artisanal trades. The curriculum was meant to serve painters, decorators, cabinetmakers, sculptors, metalworkers, upholsterers, and related occupations.

The initial school enrolled only twenty-six students despite capacity for more than one hundred. The institution attributed the low number to its novelty, workers’ late hours, distance, and irregular attendance. It discontinued the project the following year for lack of funds; recent scholarship suggests Della Valle’s death may also have delayed its revival. Reopened by 1907, the school later became a large institution, but that expansion occurred after his death.

## Teaching, Students, and Institutional Significance

Della Valle’s teaching consumed a substantial part of his mature life and helps explain the relative scarcity of his paintings.

Later accounts variously say that he:

- taught for fifteen or eighteen years;
- gave three classes per week;
- sacrificed evenings and portions of his days;
- served without remuneration;
- taught both academy students and private pupils.

Identified pupils or younger artists who studied in the Sociedad Estímulo environment under him include:

- **Faustino Brughetti**
- **Cesáreo Bernaldo de Quirós**
- **Walter de Navazio**
- **Valentín Thibon de Libian**
- **Miguel Carlos Victorica**
- **Carlos Ripamonte**
- **Eugenio Daneri**
- **Emilio Boggio**

The exact teacher-student relationship is strongest where a pupil’s biography specifically names Della Valle. Institutional attendance alone does not establish that he personally supervised every artist often included in generalized student lists.

His role belonged to a collective institutional project. The Sociedad Estímulo de Bellas Artes, founded in 1876, supplied systematic instruction before Argentina possessed a mature state academy. Its school later passed into national control and became part of the genealogy of Argentina’s official fine-arts education.

## Artistic and Intellectual Development

### Academic formation

His Florentine work alternated among:

- life studies and nudes;
- historical and mythological subjects;
- Orientalist or exotic scenes;
- formal exercises derived from academic practice.

His handling of drawing, anatomy, chiaroscuro, equine movement, and large historical composition reflects this training.

### Argentine subjects

After returning, Della Valle redirected European academic technique toward local subject matter:

- horses and cattle;
- gauchos and rural workers;
- frontier soldiers;
- railway stations and trains;
- bandits and popular literary figures;
- raids, captives, and Indigenous warriors.

This did not make the work an unmediated record of rural life. Many paintings translated Buenos Aires literary, political, and visual conventions into images of the countryside. The Pampa could function simultaneously as observed landscape, national emblem, marketable “native” subject, and imagined frontier.

### Realism, naturalism, and romantic narrative

Della Valle is regularly called a realist, yet *La vuelta del malón* combines naturalistic surfaces with romantic drama and history painting. Its composition has been compared with European battle and “barbarian” pictures, including those of Ulpiano Checa. The light and brushwork also disclose familiarity with Italian developments beyond strict academic finish.

### Political meaning

No manifesto, diary, or political correspondence by Della Valle was found. Therefore, his personal political ideology cannot safely be reconstructed from his pictures alone.

His principal canvas nonetheless participated in the public imagery of the **Generation of ’80**. Painted thirteen years after Roca’s 1879 military campaign, it presented Indigenous violence as a danger that the modern nation had supposedly overcome. Its planned destination—the Columbian Exposition celebrating four centuries since Columbus—strengthened this national and colonial framework.

The MNBA commentary by Laura Malosetti Costa interprets it as:

- a synthesis of arguments used to justify the Conquest of the Desert;
- an inversion that foregrounded Indigenous raiding while suppressing state conquest and dispossession;
- an implicit glorification of Roca;
- a visual alignment of the 1879 campaign with the longer conquest of the Americas.

Those are influential scholarly interpretations. There is no consulted letter or commission document in which Della Valle explicitly states that he meant to glorify Roca or endorse extermination.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Pedro Lagleyze

**Documented fact.** Pedro Lagleyze was Della Valle’s oldest and best-documented friend, probably first met at Colegio San José in 1871. Their relationship included:

- social visits documented in a circa-1890 photograph;
- Della Valle’s major portrait of Lagleyze;
- decorative paintings for the Lagleyze household;
- Lagleyze’s assistance with professional contacts;
- mediation of the portrait of President Roca;
- assistance with the Chicago exhibition;
- receipt of the *Malón chico* replica.

A surviving decorative tapestry, *Cacería de jabalí con perros*, includes the initials “PL” and portraits of Josefina Monty de Lagleyze. Its provenance was the Lagleyze family, and Guiomar de Urgell supplied authentication.

### Antonio Ciseri

**Documented fact.** Ciseri was Della Valle’s principal teacher in Florence. The relationship supplied his academic foundation and connected him with an international workshop culture.

### Reinaldo Giudici

**Documented fact.** Giudici was his teaching colleague at the Sociedad Estímulo. They jointly taught drawing and painting after Francesco Romero.

### Eduardo Schiaffino

**Documented fact.** Painter, critic, institution-builder, and first director of the MNBA. He served with Della Valle in exhibitions, juries, and the Comisión Nacional de Bellas Artes. After Della Valle’s death, Schiaffino requested *La vuelta del malón* for the museum and delivered a graveside or funeral tribute.

### Eduardo Sívori, Ernesto de la Cárcova, Augusto Ballerini, Lucio Correa Morales, Francisco Cafferata, and Severo Rodríguez Etchart

**Documented or strongly reported professional network.** These men shared exhibitions, juries, teaching institutions, workshops, and campaigns to establish an Argentine art system. Sources often call them friends, though the surviving evidence more securely establishes professional association.

### Julio Argentino Roca

**Documented professional contact; disputed ideological meaning.** Della Valle portrayed Roca after Lagleyze facilitated access. Later sources also attribute *El general Roca pasando revista* to him. No evidence establishes that Roca commissioned *La vuelta del malón*. Scholars interpret that painting as an implicit celebration of Roca’s frontier campaign.

### Students

**Documented at varying levels.** Thibon de Libian, Victorica, Ripamonte, Boggio, Daneri, and others entered later Argentine art history after studying at the institution where Della Valle taught. Thibon de Libian’s drawing of his dying teacher became a particularly personal record of the relationship.

### Enemies and rivals

No documented personal enemy, prolonged feud, or identifiable artistic rival was found. Critical disagreement over his work should not be converted into a personal rivalry without evidence.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Della Valle had no known incorporated company, patent, invention, or industrial investment.

His institutional involvements included:

- Colegio San José.
- Antonio Ciseri’s Florentine school or cooperative.
- *La Ilustración Argentina*.
- Sociedad Estímulo de Bellas Artes.
- La Colmena.
- El Ateneo.
- Comisión Nacional de Bellas Artes.
- Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, as selector, exhibitor, and posthumously represented artist.
- Sociedad de Educación Industrial and its School of Drawing.

### Income and property

Portrait commissions and private teaching probably supplied income, but surviving accounts do not permit a reconstruction of earnings. His family owned or occupied the San Telmo house; whether Ángel inherited legal title is not established.

He rented a Bon Marché studio from 1898. No investments, debts, bankruptcy, tax disputes, or estate inventory were found.

The major documented posthumous transaction involved *La vuelta del malón*:

1. Schiaffino requested it from the family after Della Valle’s death.
2. The family donated it to the Sociedad Estímulo with instructions that it be sold to the MNBA.
3. The intended proceeds were to establish an annual Ángel Della Valle painting prize.
4. The MNBA acquired it in **1909 for 5,000 pesos**, through an open regulated competition called in connection with 1910.

The consulted record does not show whether the annual prize operated exactly as envisioned or for how long.

## Successes and Failures

### Significant successes

- Silver-medal recognition for *Prometeo encadenado*.
- Long professional training in Florence.
- Participation in the organization of Argentina’s first regular art exhibitions.
- Large public response to *La vuelta del malón* in 1892.
- Award at the 1893 World’s Columbian Exposition.
- Appointment to the Comisión Nacional de Bellas Artes.
- Influence through teaching over approximately fifteen to eighteen years.
- First directorship of the Sociedad de Educación Industrial drawing school.
- Posthumous acquisition of his principal work by the MNBA and the creation or intended creation of a memorial prize.

### Limitations and failures

- *La vuelta del malón* failed to find a private buyer during his lifetime despite publicity and an international award.
- His commitment to unpaid or poorly remunerated teaching restricted his output and possibly his finances.
- The initial workers’ drawing school attracted only twenty-six students and was suspended for insufficient funds.
- His work received uneven contemporary criticism.
- His posthumous reputation became concentrated so heavily on one painting that portraits, illustrations, decorative work, and other rural subjects largely disappeared from general narratives.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, or Financial Questions

### Personal misconduct

A dedicated adversarial search in Spanish and English combined Della Valle’s name with terms for controversy, scandal, accusations, lawsuits, fraud, crime, conspiracy, corruption, and criticism, including results dated 2024–2026.

**Finding:** no substantive evidence was located of:

- criminal charges or convictions;
- lawsuits;
- fraud or forgery committed by him;
- corruption or financial misconduct;
- sexual or domestic scandal;
- addiction;
- imprisonment or exile;
- intelligence activity;
- political violence personally committed or directed by him;
- professional disciplinary action.

This is an absence of located evidence, not proof that every aspect of his private life is known.

### *La vuelta del malón* and colonial violence

**Documented fact:** the painting was made in 1892 for an international commemoration of Columbus’s arrival, after the military destruction and subordination of Indigenous societies in southern Argentina.

**Interpretation:** major modern scholarship argues that it naturalizes a white, European, Christian, and patriarchal national order. Indigenous figures are shown with stolen Christian objects, severed heads, cattle, and a white captive, while state violence and territorial seizure remain outside the frame.

**Interpretation:** some scholars describe it as visual legitimation of, or retrospective propaganda for, the Conquest of the Desert.

**Unproven claim:** a direct “message of support” to Roca. Della Valle had a professional portrait connection with Roca, but no source consulted supplies a commission, payment, letter, or statement proving that Roca directed the painting’s political program.

### Was it documentary history?

**Documented fact:** the canvas was painted well after the frontier campaigns it appears to evoke.

**Unresolved question:** whether the Cayutril–Caimán story was a specific historical source, an oral tradition, or a narrative framework supplied during publicity. The contemporary Botet interview supports the fact that Della Valle circulated that account, not necessarily the event’s accuracy.

**Critical issue:** textbooks and popular reproductions have sometimes used the image as if it transparently documented frontier events. Educational researchers identify this as a methodological error: it is a later, ideologically structured work of history painting, not an eyewitness record.

### The captive: rape, protection, or consensual flight?

Contemporary viewers remarked upon the erotic closeness of the white woman and Indigenous rider. Some thought she looked too urban or elegantly posed for a violently abducted captive.

Later interpretations divide:

- The standard nationalist reading sees abduction, sexual threat, and violated civilization.
- Malosetti Costa emphasizes an unsettling sensual ambiguity.
- Daniel Santoro has speculated that the pair may appear to be lovers or participants in an encounter.
- A 2026 study by Milena García Leguizamón proposes rereading the “rape of the captive” as a possible flight undertaken by a man and woman from different worlds.

These are interpretive hypotheses. No evidence identifies real models or proves a consensual narrative intended by Della Valle.

### Authenticity and attribution in the market

Auction records show replicas, decorative works, and paintings supported by certificates or letters from Guiomar de Urgell. The existence of repetitions and a small *Malón* creates potential confusion between preparatory studies, replicas, fragments, and later copies. No documented forgery prosecution involving Della Valle’s work was found.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The first genuinely national Argentine painting”

- **Story:** *La vuelta del malón* was the first genuinely national work of Argentine art.
- **Appearance:** the phrase is associated with its early reception and has been repeated by museums, catalogues, and popular histories.
- **Evidence for:** its Argentine frontier subject, monumental scale, intense Buenos Aires reception, and international exhibition made it a landmark in national canon formation.
- **Evidence against literal priority:** Argentine artists had painted local people, wars, landscapes, and political subjects before 1892. The phrase expresses critical acclaim and canon-building, not a demonstrable chronological first.
- **Effect:** it elevated Della Valle above a broader and more varied oeuvre while making the painting a national symbol.

### The eyewitness “painter of the Pampa”

- **Story:** Della Valle directly captured the Pampa, gauchos, Indigenous people, and frontier life from close experience.
- **Evidence:** he painted many rural subjects and had exceptional knowledge of horse anatomy.
- **Counterevidence:** no consulted source documents sustained frontier travel, Indigenous contact, or participation in a raid. He spent much of his youth in Florence and his mature career in Buenos Aires. MNBA research says he began Pampa imagery while still in Italy.
- **Effect:** the myth supports the work’s aura of authenticity while obscuring its dependence on literature, press reports, oral stories, earlier painting, urban models, and academic convention.

### Eleven years in Florence

- **Story:** Della Valle spent eleven years in Florence.
- **Evidence:** compatible with a departure around 1872 and return in 1883.
- **Counterevidence:** several detailed institutional accounts say he left in 1875, giving an eight-year stay.
- **Status:** unresolved chronological tradition.

### Death “while teaching”

- **Story:** he collapsed and died in the act of teaching a class.
- **More precise evidence:** the detailed Bon Marché catalogue says he died unexpectedly on the afternoon of 16 July 1903 **while waiting for the hour of his class**. Later accounts simplify this to death while teaching.
- **Effect:** the compressed story reinforced his posthumous image as a self-sacrificing teacher.

### *El taller* as a premonition

- **Story:** the empty studio painting predicted his solitary death in that room.
- **Origin:** retrospective commentary noting that the work depicts the same Bon Marché studio in which he died.
- **Evidence:** none for conscious prophecy.
- **Status:** posthumous symbolic interpretation.

### “Forgotten in poverty”

- **Story:** Della Valle died forgotten or destitute.
- **Evidence for hardship:** his father’s death may have damaged family finances; a contemporary newspaper referred to artistic poverty and lack of state support; he gave extensive unpaid instruction.
- **Counterevidence:** he maintained a studio, obtained portrait commissions, served on national commissions and juries, won an international award, and was surrounded by prominent colleagues.
- **Status:** exaggeration built from genuine financial uncertainty and later neglect, not a documented estate finding.

## Death and Immediate Aftermath

On the afternoon of **16 July 1903**, Della Valle was in his Bon Marché atelier awaiting the scheduled time of his class. He died suddenly there. Later biographies often say he died while actually teaching, but the detailed MNBA study places the event shortly before class.

No consulted source identifies:

- a certified medical cause of death;
- a preceding illness;
- an accident or injury;
- substance use;
- a hospital;
- his burial site;
- the contents or value of his estate.

The absence of a documented cause makes diagnoses such as heart attack or stroke speculative.

Students and colleagues were present or quickly gathered. Thibon de Libian drew the scene. Castillo photographed the room or death scene, and *Caras y Caretas* published a postmortem image.

At the funeral, Eduardo Schiaffino remembered Della Valle as a loyal, generous colleague who had not been “egoistic enough to live”—language that helped establish the image of an artist who sacrificed personal production and material advantage to teaching.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Acquisition and canonization

After Della Valle’s death, Schiaffino sought *La vuelta del malón* for the MNBA. The family transferred it through the Sociedad Estímulo, and the museum acquired it in 1909 for 5,000 pesos.

The painting became one of the canonical images of nineteenth-century Argentine art. It has remained on view, appeared in national surveys, schoolbooks, catalogues, films, and political debate, and is frequently reproduced alongside works by Sívori, De la Cárcova, and Juan Manuel Blanes.

### Exhibitions and memorials

His works appeared in later historical exhibitions including:

- *Un Siglo de Arte en la Argentina*, Buenos Aires, 1936.
- *Obras de Arte Argentinas desde Pueyrredón a nuestros días*, Buenos Aires, 1940.
- *La Pintura y Escultura Argentina de este Siglo*, Buenos Aires, 1952–1953.

In **1966**, a commemorative plaque was installed at the San Telmo house associated with him, reported as José Modesto Giuffra 356.

### Teacher’s legacy

Della Valle’s influence persisted through artists trained at the Sociedad Estímulo. His institutional importance lies less in founding an individual “school” of style than in supplying sustained instruction before state art education was fully organized.

### Revision and counter-memory

From the late twentieth century onward, scholarship shifted from celebrating *La vuelta del malón* solely as a technical and national masterpiece to examining:

- the construction of Indigenous people as racial enemies;
- the erotic politics of the white captive;
- the suppression of military conquest and land seizure;
- the relationship between frontier imagery and European academic art;
- the timing of the work after Indigenous military resistance had largely been defeated;
- its function at a Columbian exposition;
- its continued use in textbooks and public memory.

Artists and filmmakers including Daniel Santoro, Alberto Passolini, Francisco Revelli, Identidad Marrón, Andrea Servera, and Laura Casabé have quoted, inverted, or contested the picture. These reworkings have not erased Della Valle’s canonical position; they have changed the questions attached to it.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity, shared nationality, or appearance on the same broad roster is excluded.

### Julio Argentino Roca

**Documented fact:** Della Valle painted Roca after Pedro Lagleyze, Roca’s ophthalmologist, facilitated the contact. A second composition, *El general Roca pasando revista* (1894), is reported in later biographical sources.

**Interpretation, not documented commission:** *La vuelta del malón* has been read by Laura Malosetti Costa and later scholars as an implicit glorification of Roca’s 1879 Conquest of the Desert. No evidence consulted establishes that Roca ordered, financed, or approved the painting.

### Juan Manuel José Domingo Ortiz de Rozas y López de Osornio — Juan Manuel de Rosas

**Reported fact:** Della Valle’s father, José, worked as a builder on Buenos Aires projects commissioned during Rosas’s government. This is an indirect family and economic connection, not a documented meeting between Rosas and Ángel, who was born around the end of Rosas’s rule or afterward.

### Domingo Faustino Sarmiento

**Documented intellectual context; no personal meeting established:** modern institutional and scholarly sources interpret *La vuelta del malón* through the “civilization/barbarism” opposition canonically articulated in Sarmiento’s *Facundo*. No source consulted proves personal correspondence, meeting, or an explicit acknowledgment by Della Valle that Sarmiento directly influenced him.

### Pío Collivadino

**Documented institutional proximity; no direct teacher-student bond established:** Collivadino belonged to the succeeding generation of Argentine artists and later directed institutions descended from the art-education system in which Della Valle taught. The sources consulted did not establish a personal meeting or direct instruction by Della Valle, so no stronger connection is claimed.

### Ángel della Valle

The roster includes the subject himself; this is an identity match, not an interpersonal connection.

### Absence finding

No reliable crossing was found between Della Valle and the other listed atlas figures. In particular, no connection should be inferred merely because artists such as Xul Solar, Carlos Gardel, Alejandro Christophersen, or Francisco Salamone later worked in Buenos Aires or used imagery related to national history.
