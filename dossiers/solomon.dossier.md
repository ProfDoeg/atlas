# King Solomon: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Method

Solomon is presented in the Hebrew Bible as the son and successor of David and as king of a united Israelite monarchy centered on Jerusalem. No surviving inscription universally accepted as contemporary with Solomon names him. Consequently, a modern reconstruction must distinguish among:

- **Biblical narrative:** principally 2 Samuel 12; 1 Kings 1–11; and 1 Chronicles 22–29 and 2 Chronicles 1–9.
- **Probable historical core:** conclusions scholars consider possible or plausible after comparing the texts with Iron Age archaeology and ancient Near Eastern institutions.
- **Later interpretation:** Josephus, rabbinic literature, the Qur’an and Islamic tradition, Ethiopian royal tradition, Christian interpretation, magic, folklore, art, and political symbolism.
- **Legend or pseudepigraphy:** writings and stories attributed to Solomon many centuries after his presumed lifetime.

The conventional dates approximately **970–931 BCE** are reconstructed from biblical regnal chronology, not from contemporary Solomon-dated documents. Dates elsewhere range roughly from the mid-tenth century to the early ninth century BCE, depending on chronological system.

## Source Base and Its Problems

### Primary literary sources

1. **2 Samuel 12:24–25** records Solomon’s birth and the name Jedidiah.
2. **1 Kings 1–2** narrates his disputed accession and elimination of rivals.
3. **1 Kings 3–11** supplies the principal account of his reign.
4. **1 Chronicles 22–29 and 2 Chronicles 1–9** retell and reshape his career, suppressing or softening much of the violence, apostasy, and political opposition.
5. **Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs, Psalms 72 and 127**, and several later works invoke or claim Solomon’s authorship or patronage.
6. **Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities* VIII**, written in the first century CE, expands the biblical account and claims that correspondence between Solomon and Hiram remained in Tyrian archives. No such archive has survived for verification.
7. **The Qur’an**, especially surahs 2, 21, 27, 34, and 38, presents Sulaymān as a prophet-king commanding winds, animals, and jinn.
8. **Rabbinic, Ethiopian, Christian, and magical texts** progressively enlarge Solomon’s biography.

### Composition and historical distance

The books of Kings reached their surviving form centuries after Solomon’s presumed reign, commonly through stages associated with the late monarchic and exilic periods. Chronicles is later still, usually placed in the Persian or early Hellenistic era. The narratives may preserve older administrative notices, court stories, architectural descriptions, annalistic formulas, and traditions, but their age and reliability must be assessed passage by passage.

The portrait is internally divided:

- Solomon is the divinely chosen king, wise judge, temple founder, wealthy diplomat, and organizer.
- He is also a ruler installed through palace intrigue, responsible for executions and forced labor, whose foreign marriages and cultic patronage produce divine judgment.
- Chronicles largely idealizes him, omitting the succession killings, the two prostitutes’ case, foreign cults, and most of his adversaries.

Ronald Hendel describes the problem as an “archaeology of memory”: Solomon’s representation reflects historical memory, royal ideology, literary design, and the political needs of later communities rather than an unmediated court biography. [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/10720/chapter/158792534)

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

According to biblical genealogy:

- Solomon’s father was **David son of Jesse**, from Bethlehem.
- David belonged to the tribe of Judah.
- Later genealogies trace the dynasty through Judah, Jacob, Isaac, and Abraham.
- The Tel Dan inscription, dated to the ninth century BCE, contains the widely accepted reading “House of David.” It supports the existence of a Davidic dynasty by that period, but it neither names Solomon nor verifies the full biblical account of David’s or Solomon’s reign. [Jewish Museum](https://thejewishmuseum.org/exhibitions/tel-dan-stele/)

### Mother: Bathsheba

Bathsheba had been married to **Uriah the Hittite**, a soldier in David’s army. According to 2 Samuel 11, David impregnated her and arranged Uriah’s death after attempts to conceal the pregnancy failed. Their first child died. Solomon was born afterward.

Bathsheba’s father is called **Eliam** in 2 Samuel 11:3. If he is identical with David’s warrior Eliam son of Ahithophel in 2 Samuel 23:34, Bathsheba was Ahithophel’s granddaughter, but the identification is plausible rather than certain.

Bathsheba became decisive in the succession crisis. Warned by the prophet Nathan that Adonijah was being proclaimed king, she reminded David of an alleged oath that Solomon would succeed him. David publicly reaffirmed Solomon’s succession. Whether the oath reflects a prior historical arrangement or the literary justification of a successful palace faction cannot be established.

### Siblings and half-siblings

David’s sons named in biblical texts include:

- Amnon
- Chileab or Daniel
- Absalom
- Adonijah
- Shephatiah
- Ithream
- Shammua or Shimea
- Shobab
- Nathan
- Solomon
- Ibhar
- Elishua or Elishama
- Elpelet
- Nogah
- Nepheg
- Japhia
- additional sons and at least one named daughter, Tamar

The lists vary. Nathan, Shammua/Shimea, and Shobab are listed with Solomon as sons of Bathsheba in 1 Chronicles 3:5, although Samuel’s infancy narrative does not supply a complete maternal sibling list.

### Spouses and concubines

The sources name only a small fraction of Solomon’s partners.

- **Naamah the Ammonite**, mother of Rehoboam.
- An unnamed **daughter of Pharaoh**, married as part of an Egyptian alliance.
- The Deuteronomistic narrator claims **700 royal wives and 300 concubines**, including Moabite, Ammonite, Edomite, Sidonian, and Hittite women.

The numbers may function as royal hyperbole and as a literary demonstration that Solomon violated Deuteronomy’s warning that a king must not multiply wives. They cannot be independently verified.

The Egyptian princess’s father has sometimes been identified as **Siamun** or, less often, **Psusennes II**, but the text gives no name and no Egyptian source confirms the marriage. The accompanying claim that Pharaoh conquered Gezer and gave it as his daughter’s dowry is similarly unconfirmed. [Scholasticahq/JANES study](https://janes.scholasticahq.com/article/2320-solomon-and-the-daughter-of-pharaoh-intermarriage-conversion-and-the-impurity-of-women/attachment/6261.pdf)

### Children

Children explicitly associated with Solomon include:

- **Rehoboam**, son of Naamah; successor to Solomon.
- **Taphath**, daughter of Solomon and wife of Ben-abinadab, a district governor.
- **Basemath**, daughter of Solomon and wife of Ahimaaz, another district governor.

No biblical source names the mothers of Taphath and Basemath.

**Menelik I**, reputed son of Solomon and the Queen of Sheba, belongs to Ethiopian royal legend, not the Hebrew Bible or contemporary evidence. He appears centrally in the medieval *Kebra Nagast*.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Birth and divine naming

After the death of David and Bathsheba’s first child, 2 Samuel 12:24–25 says Bathsheba bore Solomon. David named him Solomon, while the prophet Nathan conveyed the name Jedidiah “because of YHWH.”

No text gives Solomon’s age at accession. David calls him “young and inexperienced” in 1 Chronicles 22:5 and 29:1, while Solomon describes himself as “a little child” in 1 Kings 3:7. These may be formulas of humility or political inexperience rather than literal age statements.

### Education and formation

There is no documentary curriculum. The narratives imply formation within the royal household and associate several figures with his advancement:

- **David:** father, dynastic predecessor, and—in Chronicles—planner of the Temple and transmitter of its design.
- **Bathsheba:** mother and political advocate.
- **Nathan the prophet:** participant in Solomon’s naming and succession.
- **Zadok the priest:** anointed Solomon and became the principal priest of the new regime.
- **Benaiah son of Jehoiada:** military supporter and later commander.
- **Hushai, Shimei, and Rei:** listed among the loyalists who refused Adonijah’s faction.

No teacher can be securely identified as the source of Solomon’s reputed wisdom. Later Jewish and Christian traditions treated his wisdom as divinely infused rather than educationally acquired.

## Accession Crisis

### Adonijah’s bid for power

Near the end of David’s life, **Adonijah**, an older surviving son, “exalted himself” and assembled chariots, runners, and supporters. His faction included:

- **Joab son of Zeruiah**, David’s veteran commander.
- **Abiathar**, a senior priest.
- Royal princes other than Solomon.
- Judahite officials invited to a sacrificial feast at En-rogel.

The opposing faction included Bathsheba, Nathan, Zadok, Benaiah, and David’s elite guard.

### Solomon’s emergency coronation

Bathsheba and Nathan separately approached the incapacitated David. David ordered Solomon placed on the royal mule and taken to the Gihon spring. Zadok anointed him from sacred oil, trumpets sounded, and the crowd acclaimed him.

Adonijah’s gathering dissolved. Adonijah took sanctuary by grasping the altar’s horns. Solomon initially spared him on condition of loyalty.

**Evidentiary classification:** detailed biblical court narrative. It may preserve memory of a contested succession, but no external record confirms its speeches or sequence.

### David’s final instructions

In 1 Kings 2, David instructed Solomon to:

- observe the law of Moses;
- deal with Joab for killing Abner and Amasa;
- reward Barzillai’s family;
- punish Shimei for cursing David.

The instructions combine piety, dynastic security, and unfinished vengeance. Scholars differ over whether the passage preserves court apologetic, hostile irony, Deuteronomistic editing, or some combination.

## Consolidation of Power

### Adonijah’s execution

Adonijah requested permission to marry **Abishag the Shunammite**, David’s former attendant. Bathsheba transmitted the request. Solomon interpreted it as a renewed claim to the throne—possession of a predecessor’s woman could carry dynastic significance—and ordered Benaiah to kill him.

**Status:** biblical report; political motive plausible but unverified.

### Abiathar’s dismissal

Solomon removed Abiathar from the priesthood and sent him to his estate at Anathoth. The narrator interprets this as fulfillment of judgment on Eli’s priestly house. Abiathar was not killed because he had carried the Ark and suffered with David.

The dismissal elevated Zadok’s line and may preserve memory of an institutional struggle between priestly houses.

### Joab’s killing

Joab fled to the altar and grasped its horns. Solomon ordered Benaiah to kill him there, citing Joab’s murders of Abner and Amasa. Benaiah replaced him as army commander.

This is both:

- a reported judicial reckoning for earlier killings; and
- a purge of the principal military supporter of Adonijah.

### Shimei’s confinement and execution

Solomon ordered Shimei to live in Jerusalem and warned him not to cross the Kidron. Three years later, Shimei traveled to Gath to retrieve escaped slaves. Solomon treated the journey as breach of oath and had Benaiah execute him.

The account concludes: “the kingdom was established in the hand of Solomon.”

## Chronological Life History and Reign

## Early Reign: Alliance, Worship, and Wisdom

### Marriage alliance with Egypt

Solomon married Pharaoh’s daughter and brought her to the City of David while construction proceeded. In ancient Near Eastern diplomatic terms, such a marriage would indicate alliance and prestige. However:

- the pharaoh is unnamed;
- Egyptian kings generally gave daughters abroad only reluctantly or not at all in better-documented earlier periods;
- no Egyptian text confirms the event;
- proposed identification with Siamun remains conjectural.

### Sacrifice at Gibeon and dream

Solomon sacrificed at the “great high place” at Gibeon. In a dream, God offered him a gift. He asked for a “listening heart” to govern and distinguish good from evil. God granted wisdom and additionally promised wealth and honor.

The story defines ideal royal wisdom as judicial competence rather than abstract erudition.

### Judgment of the two women

Two women, described as prostitutes living in one house, claimed the same surviving infant. Solomon proposed dividing the child with a sword. The woman willing to surrender him was identified as the mother.

**Status:** wisdom tale within the royal biography. It cannot be independently documented and resembles international folktale patterns in which an apparently cruel test reveals concealed truth.

## Administration and State Structure

### Senior officials

1 Kings 4 lists a royal administration including:

- Azariah son of Zadok, priest or chief official;
- Elihoreph and Ahijah, secretaries;
- Jehoshaphat son of Ahilud, recorder;
- Benaiah, army commander;
- Zadok and Abiathar, priests—although Abiathar’s appearance creates tension with his earlier removal;
- Azariah son of Nathan, over district officers;
- Zabud son of Nathan, priest and king’s friend;
- Ahishar, palace administrator;
- Adoniram, over forced labor.

The discrepancies suggest that the lists may come from different administrative traditions or editorial layers.

### Twelve districts

The kingdom was divided into twelve provisioning districts, each supporting the court for one month annually. Judah is absent or treated separately in the surviving list. The system may indicate:

- an effort to weaken old tribal organization;
- rotation of taxation and supplies;
- a northern administrative structure remembered by later writers;
- or an idealized bureaucratic scheme.

Two district governors married Solomon’s daughters, illustrating the integration of administration and dynastic patronage.

### Court consumption

The text reports daily provisions of thirty *cors* of fine flour, sixty *cors* of meal, cattle, sheep, deer, gazelles, roebucks, and poultry. These enormous figures express court scale but cannot be audited historically.

## Temple and Palace Construction

### Temple chronology

1 Kings places the beginning of the Temple in Solomon’s fourth regnal year, 480 years after the Exodus, and completion in his eleventh year after seven years of construction. The 480 years are widely understood as schematic—twelve generations of forty years—rather than an independently verifiable interval.

### Phoenician partnership

Solomon contracted with **Hiram, king of Tyre**, for cedar and cypress timber and skilled labor. Israel supplied grain and oil. The text also describes:

- 30,000 rotating laborers sent to Lebanon;
- 70,000 burden bearers;
- 80,000 stonecutters;
- 3,300 or, in another passage, 3,600 supervisors;
- compulsory labor under Adoniram.

The precise numbers vary and may be inflated, but Phoenician participation is architecturally plausible. Temple and palace designs have parallels in Levantine long-room temples and north-Syrian or Phoenician monumental architecture.

### Hiram the bronze worker

A craftsman also named **Hiram** or **Huram**, son of a widow from Naphtali or Dan and a Tyrian father, cast the Temple’s bronze furnishings:

- the two pillars Jachin and Boaz;
- the “Sea” supported by twelve oxen;
- wheeled stands and basins;
- utensils.

This craftsman is distinct from King Hiram. Later Masonic legend transformed him into **Hiram Abiff**, the murdered master builder. The biblical Hiram is not murdered and is not called architect of the entire Temple.

### Temple plan and contents

The biblical structure measured sixty cubits long, twenty wide, and thirty high, with porch, main hall, inner sanctuary, side chambers, carved cherubim, palm trees, flowers, gold plating, and bronze equipment.

Solomon transferred the Ark of the Covenant into the inner sanctuary and delivered a dedication prayer emphasizing that heaven cannot contain God and that the Temple is a place toward which prayers may be directed.

### Historical and archaeological status

No securely excavated remains are universally identified as Solomon’s Temple. Archaeology on the Temple Mount is exceptionally constrained. The description is consistent in important respects with ancient Levantine temple forms, but architectural plausibility does not prove that the building existed in precisely the form or date described.

### Palace complex

The palace took thirteen years to build, according to Kings, and included:

- the House of the Forest of Lebanon;
- the Hall of Pillars;
- the Hall of Judgment;
- Solomon’s residence;
- a separate house for Pharaoh’s daughter.

The thirteen-year palace versus seven-year Temple timetable has been read either as normal—because the palace complex was larger—or as implicit criticism of royal priorities.

## Fortifications, Cities, and Infrastructure

The narrative credits Solomon with work at:

- Jerusalem’s wall and the Millo;
- Hazor;
- Megiddo;
- Gezer;
- Lower Beth-horon;
- Baalath;
- Tamar or Tadmor, depending on textual reading;
- store cities;
- chariot and cavalry cities;
- Ezion-geber near Elath on the Gulf of Aqaba.

### The “Solomonic gates” debate

Twentieth-century excavators attributed similar six-chambered gates and monumental complexes at Hazor, Megiddo, and Gezer to Solomon, partly because 1 Kings 9:15 names the three cities together. Subsequent stratigraphic and radiocarbon debates complicated that attribution.

Major positions include:

- **Conventional or modified conventional chronology:** portions of this monumental horizon may belong to the tenth century and could reflect state formation associated with David and Solomon.
- **Low chronology:** much of the architecture belongs to the ninth-century Omride kingdom of northern Israel.
- **Intermediate positions:** tenth-century political development occurred, but the buildings cannot be attached personally to Solomon.

Megiddo’s famous “Solomon’s stables” are now generally dated later, often to the Omride or still later Israelite period. The broader United Monarchy debate remains unresolved. [Finkelstein and Mazar debate](https://www.sbl-site.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/FinkelsteinMazar.pdf) [Antiquity](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/recent-radiocarbon-results-and-king-solomon/712ABFF6C5A3E189BDBE8D111889DF36) [Zachary Thomas review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146107916639208)

## Labor, Taxation, and Social Costs

### Forced labor

The texts distinguish, although not consistently, between:

- Israelites conscripted for rotating labor;
- surviving Canaanite populations reduced to hereditary labor;
- free Israelites serving as soldiers, officers, and officials.

After Solomon’s death, northern representatives told Rehoboam that Solomon had made their “yoke” heavy. Rehoboam’s refusal to reduce forced service and taxation immediately preceded the secession of the northern tribes.

### Historical assessment

Corvée labor was a normal mechanism of ancient Near Eastern states, but that does not establish the biblical totals. The persistent role of Adoniram, later stoned during Rehoboam’s crisis, suggests that memories of compulsory labor were politically important.

## Diplomacy, Trade, and Foreign Relations

### Hiram of Tyre

Hiram is Solomon’s most developed foreign partner. Their reported relationship covered:

- timber and craftsmen;
- food shipments;
- maritime ventures;
- a treaty;
- exchange of territory.

Solomon gave Hiram twenty towns in Galilee. Hiram disliked them and called the region **Cabul**, explained in the narrative as expressing worthlessness. Hiram nevertheless supplied 120 talents of gold. The story may preserve a dispute over debt, territory, or unequal exchange.

Josephus reproduces supposed correspondence and says copies survived in Tyrian archives in his day. This assertion cannot now be checked. [Josephus, *Antiquities* VIII](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/josephus/ant-8.html)

### Red Sea fleet and Ophir

Solomon reportedly maintained ships at Ezion-geber, with Tyrian sailors assisting his servants. The fleet brought gold from **Ophir**.

Ophir’s location is unresolved. Proposals have included:

- southern Arabia;
- the Horn of Africa;
- East Africa;
- regions farther toward India;
- sites connected through multi-stage trade rather than a single destination.

The text’s exotic products—gold, precious stones, ivory, apes or monkeys, and peacocks or other birds—reflect long-distance luxury exchange, but the passages may combine different routes or later commercial knowledge.

### Horses and chariots

Solomon imported horses and chariots through Egypt and **Kue**, probably Cilicia, and resold them to Hittite and Aramean kings. This picture resembles later first-millennium trade networks. Whether it accurately describes tenth-century commerce or projects later practice backward is disputed.

### Territorial claims

1 Kings describes Solomon as ruling from the Euphrates or Tiphsah to the Philistine frontier and Egypt. The same text also records adversaries in Edom, Aram, and the northern tribes.

No contemporary imperial inscription confirms a Solomonic empire. Archaeological interpretations range from:

- a substantial early state exercising regional authority;
- a modest highland kingdom with limited northern reach;
- a later literary idealization of a golden united monarchy.

## Wealth and Financial Structures

### Reported revenue

The text gives Solomon’s annual gold income as **666 talents**, excluding merchants, traders, Arabian kings, and provincial governors.

A talent was a large weight rather than a coin, and exact standards varied. If treated literally, 666 talents would represent tens of metric tons of gold annually—an extraordinary amount without independent confirmation.

### Royal assets

The narrative lists:

- gold shields;
- an ivory throne overlaid with gold;
- gold drinking vessels;
- merchant fleets;
- horses and chariots;
- fortified and storage cities;
- palace and Temple treasuries;
- luxury imports;
- territorial payments and diplomatic gifts.

Silver is said to have become as common as stone in Jerusalem, an unmistakable prosperity formula rather than a measurable market report.

### Debt and property relationships

The transfer of twenty Galilean towns to Hiram may signify:

- repayment for building materials;
- security for debt;
- diplomatic gift;
- or a later etiological story explaining a border district.

The text does not allow a decisive choice.

### Queen of Sheba’s gift

The queen reportedly gave Solomon 120 talents of gold, spices, and precious stones. Solomon gave her “all that she desired,” in addition to royal gifts. No surviving Sabaean inscription names either encounter or payment.

## The Queen of Sheba

### Biblical story

An unnamed queen of Sheba heard of Solomon’s fame, came to Jerusalem with a camel caravan, tested him with riddles, observed his court, and declared that his wisdom and prosperity exceeded the reports. Solomon answered her questions.

The biblical account contains no romance, sexual relationship, conversion, marriage, or child.

### Historical context

The Sabaean kingdom was located in South Arabia, principally modern Yemen, with commercial links through incense routes. The securely documented chronology of major Sabaean state formation and the date of the biblical narrative remain debated. No contemporary inscription confirms the queen’s identity or journey.

### Jewish and Josephian elaboration

Josephus calls her queen of Egypt and Ethiopia. Rabbinic literature elaborates the riddles and, in some traditions, debates whether the expression means a female monarch or the kingdom itself.

### Qur’anic version

Qur’an 27:15–44 recounts that:

- Solomon understood the speech of birds;
- the hoopoe reported a woman ruling a prosperous people who worshipped the sun;
- Solomon sent her a letter;
- she consulted her council and sent a gift;
- her throne was transported before her arrival;
- she mistook a glass floor for water and uncovered her legs;
- she submitted with Solomon to God.

The Qur’an does not name her. **Bilqīs/Balqīs** is supplied by later Islamic tradition. [Qur’an 27](https://legacy.quran.com/27/15-71)

### Ethiopian version

The medieval *Kebra Nagast* names her **Makeda** and makes her the mother of Solomon’s son **Menelik**. In the story:

- she visits Solomon for wisdom;
- Solomon obtains intercourse through an oath-and-water stratagem;
- Menelik later travels to Jerusalem;
- the Ark of the Covenant is taken to Ethiopia;
- Menelik becomes founder of Ethiopia’s Solomonic dynasty.

This narrative gave sacred-historical legitimacy to Ethiopian emperors. It is not contemporaneous evidence for Solomon, Makeda, Menelik, or the Ark’s removal. [*Kebra Nagast* translation](https://www.sacred-texts.com/chr/kn/kn000-0.htm)

## Wisdom, Learning, and Intellectual Reputation

### Biblical claims

Solomon’s wisdom is said to exceed that of:

- “all the people of the east”;
- Egypt;
- Ethan the Ezrahite;
- Heman;
- Calcol;
- Darda.

He allegedly composed:

- 3,000 proverbs;
- 1,005 songs;
- discourses about trees, from the cedar of Lebanon to hyssop;
- discourses about animals, birds, reptiles, and fish.

No manuscripts of a tenth-century Solomonic botanical or zoological corpus survive.

### Nature of royal wisdom

The portrait combines:

- judicial wisdom;
- administrative competence;
- riddling and verbal skill;
- international scholarly prestige;
- natural knowledge;
- theological discernment.

These attributes resemble ancient Near Eastern ideals of the learned king. Egyptian instructional literature and Mesopotamian royal scholarship supply broad cultural parallels without demonstrating direct authorship by Solomon.

### Ambivalence

The narrative deliberately contrasts wisdom with failure:

- the wisest king cannot control his appetites;
- international alliances become channels of religious compromise;
- vast building and trade programs generate forced labor;
- the stable kingdom fragments immediately after his death.

Whether this irony belonged to early narrative or was sharpened by later editors is debated.

## Religious Development

### Early worship

Solomon initially sacrifices at high places, particularly Gibeon. The narrator excuses this on the ground that the Temple had not yet been built.

### Temple centralization

The dedication establishes Jerusalem as the focal sanctuary. Yet the prayer also insists that God cannot literally be contained by the building.

### Foreign cults

In old age, according to 1 Kings 11, Solomon’s foreign wives turned his heart toward other gods. The text associates him with:

- **Ashtoreth**, goddess of the Sidonians;
- **Milcom**, associated with Ammon;
- **Chemosh**, god of Moab;
- **Molech**, associated with Ammon.

He reportedly built high places east of Jerusalem for foreign worship.

### Evidentiary distinctions

- **Biblical accusation:** Solomon patronized foreign cults.
- **Theological interpretation:** this apostasy caused the future division of the kingdom.
- **Historical possibility:** royal courts commonly accommodated diplomatic spouses and multiple cults.
- **Unresolved:** whether Solomon personally worshipped these gods, merely permitted their cults, or was made the symbolic target of later centralizing reformers.

Chronicles omits the apostasy entirely.

## Adversaries, Rivals, and Political Opposition

### Hadad the Edomite

Hadad, an Edomite royal survivor of David and Joab’s campaigns, fled through Midian and Paran to Egypt, married into Pharaoh’s household, and later returned as Solomon’s adversary.

This story preserves a hostile memory of Davidic violence in Edom and an Egyptian network opposed to Solomon.

### Rezon son of Eliada

Rezon deserted Hadadezer of Zobah, gathered a band, seized Damascus, and became an adversary. His exact relation to later Aramean kings is uncertain.

### Jeroboam son of Nebat

Jeroboam was:

- an Ephraimite from Zeredah;
- son of Nebat and the widow Zeruah;
- recognized by Solomon as industrious;
- appointed over the labor force of the house of Joseph.

The prophet Ahijah symbolically gave him ten pieces of a torn garment, predicting rule over ten tribes. Solomon sought to kill him, and Jeroboam fled to **Shishak/Shoshenq I** in Egypt.

After Solomon’s death, Jeroboam returned and became king of the northern kingdom.

### Internal resistance

The greatest structural opposition appears after Solomon’s death: northern Israelites demanded relief from his heavy service and yoke. This suggests that the regime’s apparent stability depended on personal control and coercive extraction.

## Successes and Failures

### Reported successes

- Defeated rival succession faction.
- Consolidated royal power.
- Established a central administrative corps.
- Formed alliances with Tyre and Egypt.
- Built or sponsored the Jerusalem Temple.
- Developed palace, fortress, storage, and transport infrastructure.
- Expanded royal trade.
- Established an enduring reputation for judicial and literary wisdom.
- Became an archetype across three major religious traditions.

### Reported or implied failures

- Accession accompanied by executions and political purges.
- Heavy taxation and corvée labor.
- Possible indebtedness or disputed land transfer to Tyre.
- Failure to integrate northern constituencies.
- Suppression of Jeroboam rather than resolution of grievances.
- Patronage of competing cults, according to Kings.
- Emergence of Edomite and Aramean adversaries.
- Immediate fracture of the kingdom under Rehoboam.

## Controversies and Disputed Historical Questions

## Did Solomon exist?

### Evidence supporting a historical core

- A ninth-century polity recognized as the **House of David** existed.
- Jerusalem was occupied in the tenth century BCE.
- Political consolidation in parts of the highlands occurred during the Iron I–II transition.
- The texts contain plausible institutional details, geography, administrative terminology, and memories of Tyrian cooperation.

### Evidence limiting certainty

- No uncontested contemporary inscription names Solomon.
- No securely identified royal seal, tomb, palace inscription, or archive belongs to him.
- The biblical histories were written or edited substantially later.
- Monumental sites once assigned to Solomon have contested dates.
- The scale of the empire and wealth has no external confirmation.

### Conclusion

A historical Solomon remains a defensible reconstruction, but his precise biography cannot be separated cleanly from dynastic memory and literary construction.

## Extent of the United Monarchy

Positions range from:

1. A major centralized kingdom broadly resembling the biblical picture.
2. A real but smaller early state whose achievements were enlarged.
3. A Jerusalem-centered chiefdom or modest kingdom later credited with northern institutions.
4. A principally literary golden age assembled from later realities.

The debate turns on archaeological chronology, settlement patterns, state formation, the dating of monumental architecture, and confidence in biblical sources. There is no single archaeological consensus covering every claim.

## Pharaoh’s daughter and Gezer

- **Textual claim:** Pharaoh captured Gezer, killed its Canaanite population, and gave it as dowry.
- **Theory:** the pharaoh was Siamun.
- **Evidence for:** rough chronological compatibility and an Egyptian relief sometimes associated with a Levantine campaign.
- **Evidence against or limiting:** no name, no explicit Egyptian record, disputed interpretation of the relief, and uncertain archaeological correlation.
- **Status:** plausible hypothesis, not documented identification.

## Wealth figures

The 666 talents, immense court consumption, and ubiquity of gold are literary claims. They may preserve memories of trade and tribute but cannot be treated as audited accounts.

## Mass labor and exploitation

The existence of royal corvée is plausible and textually central. Exact numbers are unverifiable. The later northern complaint supports a remembered grievance but is embedded in a literary explanation of national division.

## Religious apostasy

Kings formally accuses Solomon; Chronicles omits the charge. The account may preserve genuine plural cult patronage, later condemnation of foreign worship, or both.

## Modern political use

Solomon’s empire and Temple have entered modern disputes over:

- the antiquity and territorial scale of Israelite statehood;
- Jerusalem’s sacred and political history;
- control and interpretation of the Temple Mount/Haram al-Sharif;
- nationalist and religious claims.

Archaeological arguments have accordingly circulated in apologetic, nationalist, anti-nationalist, confessional, and polemical settings. Their political use neither verifies nor falsifies the underlying archaeological claims.

## Death and Succession

1 Kings 11:41–43 reports that Solomon:

- reigned forty years in Jerusalem;
- died or “slept with his fathers”;
- was buried in the City of David;
- was succeeded by Rehoboam.

No illness, injury, accident, assassination, or specific cause of death is reported. Josephus, writing many centuries later, says Solomon lived ninety-four years and reigned eighty, contradicting Kings’ forty-year reign. Josephus’s figures are not independently supported.

No tomb has been securely identified as Solomon’s.

### Collapse after death

Rehoboam traveled to Shechem for confirmation as king. Jeroboam and the assembly requested lighter labor and taxation. Rehoboam rejected the elders’ conciliatory advice and followed younger advisers, threatening harsher rule. The northern tribes seceded.

The biblical account thus places the visible consequences of Solomon’s fiscal and labor system immediately after his death.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

## Solomon the magician and exorcist

### The story

Solomon possessed divinely granted command over demons, spirits, animals, winds, and hidden knowledge. A ring or seal allowed him to bind demons and compel them to build the Temple.

### Earliest development

Josephus reports traditions connecting Solomon with incantations and exorcism. He describes an exorcist named Eleazar using a ring and invoking Solomon before Vespasian.

The **Testament of Solomon**, a Greek pseudepigraphical work with Jewish and Christian layers, gives the fullest early demonological narrative. Solomon interrogates demons, learns their powers, associated stars, and defeating angels, and forces them to work on the Temple.

### Evidence

There is no Iron Age evidence that Solomon practiced demon-commanding magic. The traditions document the development of his reputation in late antiquity.

### Effect

Solomon became an international patron figure for:

- Jewish protective magic;
- Christian demonology;
- Islamic occult sciences;
- astrological and talismanic literature;
- European ceremonial magic.

## The Seal or Ring of Solomon

### Story

A signet ring marked with a pentagram or hexagram gave Solomon authority over spirits and animals.

### Development

The ring motif grew from late antique exorcistic and demonological traditions. Pentagrams and hexagrams had many earlier uses and were not originally exclusive Solomon symbols. “Seal of Solomon” later became a flexible name for magical diagrams.

Museum objects document the later association, not the existence of Solomon’s own ring. [Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/449683)

## Solomon and Ashmedai/Asmodeus

In the Babylonian Talmud, especially *Gittin* 68a–b:

- Solomon needs the *shamir*, a supernatural cutting agent, to build without iron.
- Benaiah captures Ashmedai, king of demons.
- Ashmedai later tricks Solomon, takes his ring, throws him far away, and temporarily occupies or disrupts his throne.
- Solomon wanders declaring, “I, Koheleth, was king over Israel in Jerusalem.”

Versions differ over whether he regained the throne, whether he remained fearful, and whether “was king” implies deposition.

**Status:** rabbinic legend, historically important for Solomon’s magical and morally unstable image.

## The shamir

The *shamir* is described as a tiny creature or substance capable of cutting stone without metal. It was supposedly used for the Temple stones. Later interpreters variously imagined it as:

- a worm;
- a stone;
- a supernatural cutting agent;
- a lost technology.

No archaeological or textual evidence identifies it as a historical technology.

## Solomon’s flying carpet

Islamic and later folklore depicts Solomon traveling on a vast carpet carried by the wind, accompanied by humans, jinn, birds, and animals. The Qur’an says the wind was subjected to him but does not describe every later carpet detail.

**Status:** expansion of Qur’anic motifs through commentary and folklore.

## Solomon’s death concealed from the jinn

Qur’an 34:14 says Solomon died while leaning on his staff. The jinn continued working because they did not know he was dead. A creature of the earth ate the staff; when it collapsed, Solomon fell, revealing that the jinn did not know the unseen.

The narrative has no biblical counterpart and functions theologically against exaggerated claims for jinn knowledge.

## Solomon and Bilqīs’s legs

Later commentators elaborated the Qur’anic glass-floor scene, sometimes claiming reports that Bilqīs had hairy legs, donkey-like feet, or partial jinn ancestry. These details are absent from the Qur’anic text and reflect exegetical folklore.

## Solomon and Menelik

The *Kebra Nagast*’s descent narrative established Solomon and Makeda as ancestors of Ethiopian emperors. The dynasty’s use of this genealogy gave the story constitutional and sacral force. Emperor Haile Selassie I’s titles and Ethiopia’s 1955 constitution continued the Solomonic claim.

**Status:** medieval dynastic tradition; no contemporary evidence verifies the genealogy.

## Solomon as Freemasonic archetype

Freemasonry made the building of Solomon’s Temple a foundational symbolic drama. Solomon, Hiram king of Tyre, and the legendary Hiram Abiff became principal figures in ritual architecture and moral allegory.

The murder of Hiram Abiff is not biblical history. It is a later initiatory legend formed from sparse biblical references to the Tyrian craftsman.

## Solomon’s Mines and Ophir

The uncertain location of Ophir generated identifications in Arabia, Africa, India, Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, and elsewhere. H. Rider Haggard’s 1885 novel *King Solomon’s Mines* relocated the legend into imperial adventure fiction in southern Africa.

Claims of discovering Solomon’s personal mines have repeatedly outpaced the evidence. Ancient mining in regions such as Timna is real, but attribution to Solomon requires chronological and political evidence that has not been secured.

## The Solomon–Sheba romance

The biblical encounter is diplomatic and sapiential. Romance, seduction, marriage, and offspring emerge in later Jewish, Islamic, and Ethiopian elaborations. These stories profoundly shaped literature and dynastic identity but are not corroborated biography.

## Institutions, Projects, and Economic Interests

Solomon is associated with no “companies” in the modern sense. The relevant structures were royal household, palace, temple, labor administration, tribute, and diplomatic commerce.

### Principal institutions and projects

- Davidic monarchy at Jerusalem
- Jerusalem Temple
- Royal palace complex
- House of the Forest of Lebanon
- Central treasury
- Twelve provisioning districts
- Army and chariot force
- Forced-labor administration
- Tyrian timber and craft partnership
- Red Sea fleet at Ezion-geber
- Ophir gold trade
- Horse and chariot brokerage
- Fortification and storage network
- Royal marriage alliances

### Technologies and crafts

No patent-like invention can be assigned to Solomon. Technologies associated with his projects include:

- ashlar masonry;
- timber transport by sea;
- monumental bronze casting;
- wheeled bronze stands;
- maritime navigation;
- chariot organization;
- scribal administration;
- water, storage, and fortification works.

Later legend falsely or symbolically credits him with technologies of demon control, magical seals, instantaneous transport, stone cutting by the *shamir*, alchemy, and occult astronomy.

## Illness, Psychological Crisis, and Personal Habits

No source documents a specific disease, disability, addiction, accident, or medical treatment.

The biblical account says:

- he “loved many foreign women”;
- his heart turned away in old age;
- he became the target of political adversaries.

These are moral-theological and political statements, not clinical diagnoses. Later claims that he suffered sexual addiction, narcissism, dementia, depression, or other psychiatric conditions are retrospective speculation and cannot be established.

Ecclesiastes’ expressions of futility and despair cannot be used as Solomon’s autobiography because genuine Solomonic authorship is unproved and generally rejected in critical scholarship.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Jewish tradition

Solomon became:

- builder of the First Temple;
- wisest of kings;
- authorial patron of wisdom books;
- judge and master of riddles;
- ruler who rose, sinned, and in some legends lost his throne;
- exorcist and controller of demons;
- example in debates about whether repentance restored him.

Rabbinic traditions preserve both admiration and suspicion. His foreign marriages, wealth, horses, and wives demonstrate the danger of believing a wise king can safely violate royal restrictions.

### Christian tradition

Christian interpretation treated Solomon as:

- ancestor of Jesus in Matthew’s genealogy;
- typological predecessor of Christ;
- builder of a Temple fulfilled or superseded in Christian theology;
- model of wisdom;
- negative example of apostasy;
- literary voice of Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs, and Wisdom.

Jesus’ saying that “something greater than Solomon is here” and the comparison with “Solomon in all his glory” made Solomon a measure of wisdom and splendor.

### Islamic tradition

Sulaymān is a prophet, righteous servant, divinely empowered judge, and ruler over humans, jinn, winds, birds, and animals. Qur’an 2:102 explicitly denies that Solomon disbelieved, countering traditions that associated his kingdom with illicit sorcery.

Later Islamic culture linked him with:

- talismanic science;
- architecture and ancient ruins;
- control of jinn;
- the hoopoe;
- Bilqīs;
- miraculous travel;
- seals, rings, and hidden treasure.

### Ethiopian tradition

Solomon’s union with Makeda became the foundation of a sacred national genealogy. Menelik’s removal of the Ark transferred chosen status to Ethiopia within the *Kebra Nagast*’s theology.

### European occultism

Solomon’s name became the most powerful authorship claim in Western ceremonial magic. Grimoires bearing his name promised:

- spirit conjuration;
- protective circles and pentacles;
- planetary magic;
- treasure finding;
- knowledge and authority.

The attribution functioned as authorization through antiquity, wisdom, monarchy, and divine favor.

### Art, music, and literature

Major treatments include:

- Handel, **Solomon**, oratorio, 1749.
- Charles Gounod, **La reine de Saba**, opera, 1862.
- Karl Goldmark, **Die Königin von Saba**, opera, 1875.
- H. Rider Haggard, **King Solomon’s Mines**, novel, 1885.
- Ottorino Respighi, **Belkis, regina di Saba**, ballet, 1932.
- Numerous medieval cathedral sculptures of Solomon and Sheba.
- Renaissance and Baroque cycles presenting Solomon as wise ruler, judge, ancestor of Christ, or failed idolater.
- Islamic manuscript paintings showing Sulaymān enthroned among humans, birds, animals, jinn, and *divs*.
- Ethiopian church painting cycles of Makeda, Solomon, Menelik, and the Ark.

A fifteenth-century Sienese marriage chest at the Metropolitan Museum uses Solomon and Sheba as an image of wisdom, wealth, and idealized union. [Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/437012)
