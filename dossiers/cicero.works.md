## Works by Marcus Tullius Cicero

### Forensic and political speeches preserved substantially

- **81 BCE:** *Pro Quinctio*. A published forensic defence; the surviving text is a literary revision of courtroom speech rather than a stenographic transcript. [S1]
- **80 BCE:** *Pro Sexto Roscio Amerino*. A forensic defence against a charge of parricide, substantially preserved. [S1]
- **Probably 77–76 BCE:** *Pro Roscio Comoedo*. A civil-court speech surviving incompletely; its date remains disputed. [S1]
- **Probably 72–71 BCE:** *Pro Tullio*. A fragmentarily preserved civil action concerning violence by enslaved men; both date and original extent are uncertain. [S1]
- **70 BCE:** *Divinatio in Caecilium*. A preliminary courtroom speech arguing that Cicero, rather than Caecilius, should prosecute Verres. [S1]
- **70 BCE:** *In Verrem, Actio Prima*. The first delivered prosecution speech against Verres. [S1]
- **70 BCE:** *In Verrem, Actio Secunda* I–V. Five elaborately published prosecution speeches on Verres’ career and Sicilian administration; Verres had already withdrawn, so these are literary courtroom speeches that were never delivered in their transmitted form. [S1]
- **69 BCE:** *Pro Fonteio*. A forensic defence surviving only in an incomplete text. [S1]
- **69 or 68 BCE:** *Pro Caecina*. A substantially preserved civil-court defence; the precise year remains disputed. [S1]
- **66 BCE:** *De imperio Cn. Pompei* (also *Pro lege Manilia*). A deliberative public speech supporting the transfer of command against Mithridates to Pompey. [S1]
- **66 BCE:** *Pro Cluentio*. A substantially preserved forensic defence involving poisoning, bribery, and the authority of earlier verdicts. [S1]
- **63 BCE:** *De lege agraria contra Rullum* I–III. A set of three surviving speeches against Rullus’ agrarian proposal—one addressed to the Senate and two to the people; a fourth speech is lost. [S1]
- **63 BCE:** *Pro Rabirio perduellionis reo*. A political defence in an archaizing treason proceeding; the ending is lost. [S1]
- **63 BCE:** *In Catilinam* I–IV. Four consular speeches against Catiline, two to the Senate and two to the people, subsequently shaped into a published sequence. [S1]
- **63 BCE:** *Pro Murena*. A forensic and political defence against an electoral-corruption charge. [S1]
- **62 BCE:** *Pro Sulla*. A defence of Publius Sulla against complicity in the Catilinarian conspiracy. [S1]
- **62 BCE:** *Pro Archia poeta*. A citizenship defence that also acts as an encomium of poetry and literary commemoration. [S1]
- **59 BCE:** *Pro Flacco*. A forensic defence against extortion charges, substantially preserved. [S1]
- **57 BCE:** *Post reditum in senatu*. A speech of thanks to the Senate after Cicero’s return from exile. [S1]
- **57 BCE:** *Post reditum ad Quirites*. A companion speech of thanks addressed to the Roman people. [S1]
- **57 BCE:** *De domo sua ad pontifices*. A legal-religious plea seeking restoration of Cicero’s confiscated house. [S1]
- **56 BCE:** *De haruspicum responso*. A political-religious speech contesting Clodius’ interpretation of a haruspical response. [S1]
- **56 BCE:** *Pro Sestio*. A political trial speech defending Sestius and articulating Cicero’s program of civic concord. [S1]
- **56 BCE:** *In Vatinium testem interrogatio*. A hostile interrogation of Vatinius preserved and circulated as a speech-text. [S1]
- **56 BCE:** *Pro Caelio*. A forensic defence notable for its dramatic characterization of Clodia and its adaptation of comic modes. [S1]
- **56 BCE:** *De provinciis consularibus*. A senatorial policy speech on assigning consular provinces and retaining Caesar’s Gallic command. [S1]
- **56 BCE:** *Pro Balbo*. A citizenship defence involving the legal effects of Rome’s treaties. [S1]
- **55 BCE:** *In Pisonem*. A published political invective against Lucius Calpurnius Piso, closer to an adversarial intervention than a neutral description. [S1]
- **54 BCE:** *Pro Plancio*. A defence against an electoral-corruption charge. [S1]
- **54 BCE:** *Pro Scauro*. A defence on an extortion charge; only substantial fragments survive, so its placement among “preserved” speeches is conventional rather than evidence of completeness. [S1][S2]
- **54 BCE:** *Pro Rabirio Postumo*. A defence arising from the restoration of Ptolemy XII and the recovery of Egyptian loans. [S1]
- **52 BCE:** *Pro Milone*. The transmitted defence of Milo is a polished publication substantially different from the unsuccessful speech delivered at the trial. [S1][S2]
- **46 BCE:** *Pro Marcello*. A senatorial thanksgiving to Caesar for pardoning Marcellus; whether the transmitted text should be understood simply as Cicero’s delivered speech has been questioned, though it remains in the standard Ciceronian corpus. [S1]
- **46 BCE:** *Pro Ligario*. A successful appeal to Caesar for the pardon of Quintus Ligarius, simultaneously forensic speech and political performance. [S1]
- **45 BCE:** *Pro rege Deiotaro*. A defence delivered before Caesar for King Deiotarus, accused of plotting Caesar’s murder. [S1]
- **44–43 BCE:** *Philippicae* I–XIV. Fourteen speeches against Mark Antony, some delivered to the Senate or people and some circulated in forms whose relationship to delivery is uncertain; together they operate as a sustained republican intervention. [S1]

### Speeches surviving only in fragments

- **Probably 77–76 BCE:** *Pro Vareno*. A forensic defence known from quotations and testimonia; even its circumstances are uncertain. [S2]
- **Date uncertain, probably 70s BCE:** *Pro Fundanio*. A forensic speech surviving only through a few quoted fragments. [S2]
- **Date uncertain, before 70 BCE:** *Pro Scamandro*. A fragmentary prosecution associated with the poisoning proceedings later discussed in *Pro Cluentio*. [S2]
- **Date uncertain, before 70 BCE:** *Pro Oppio* I–II. One or possibly two forensic speeches represented by extremely small fragments; the existence of a distinct second speech is disputed. [S2]
- **67–66 BCE:** *Pro Manilio*. A projected or prepared forensic defence, not the extant *Pro lege Manilia*; only fragments and testimonia remain, and it is uncertain whether Cicero delivered it. [S2]
- **66 BCE:** *Pro Cornelio* I–II. Two political trial defences preserved in numerous fragments, especially through Asconius; enough remains to reveal two separate speeches but not their complete texts. [S2]
- **64 BCE:** *In toga candida*. A campaign invective against Cicero’s consular rivals, preserved through fragments and ancient commentary. [S2]
- **63 BCE:** *De proscriptorum liberis*. A consular speech opposing restoration of political rights to descendants of Sulla’s proscribed victims; only fragments remain. [S2]
- **63 BCE:** *Contra contionem Q. Metelli*. Cicero’s reply to Metellus Nepos in a public meeting, known only fragmentarily. [S2]
- **Probably 65 or 56 BCE:** *De rege Alexandrino*. A fragmentary political speech concerning Egypt; scholars dispute whether its occasion belongs to 65 or 56 BCE. [S2]
- **59 BCE:** *Pro L. Valerio Flacco? / Pro Vatinio?* Fragmentary notices have generated uncertain identifications with otherwise known proceedings; they resist secure treatment as independently titled works. [S2]
- **61 BCE:** *In Clodium et Curionem*. A political invective preserved in fragments; its circulated text may derive from a senatorial altercation rather than a conventional formal oration. [S2]
- **56 BCE:** *Pro Gallio*. A forensic defence represented only by quotations and testimonia. [S2]
- **53 BCE:** *De aere alieno Milonis*. A speech concerning Milo’s debts, surviving in minute fragments. [S2]
- **52 BCE:** *Pro Milone, excepta oratio*. The reportedly taken-down version of Cicero’s actual trial speech, distinct from the polished extant *Pro Milone* and known only fragmentarily. [S2]
- **Fragmentary corpus, dates various:** *Unplaced fragments of speeches*. Modern critical editions preserve words attributed to Cicero’s orations that cannot securely be assigned to a named speech; these are textual remains, but they resist classification as separate obras. [S2]

### Lost, unpublished, or merely attested speeches

- **63 BCE:** *De lege agraria contra Rullum IV*. A fourth agrarian speech attested but lost. [S1][S3]
- **Dates various:** *Lost and unpublished orations catalogued by Crawford*. Ancient evidence indicates at least eighty further speeches beyond the substantially preserved and fragmentary published corpus; many have no recoverable title or wording, and some may never have become authored “texts” rather than events of speaking. [S3][S4]
- **48 BCE attribution now rejected:** *In P. Servilium Isauricum*. Earlier fragment editions treated this as Cicero’s speech, but Crawford and Dyck omit it after accepting emendation of the sole attribution; it therefore belongs in the history of the corpus, not securely among Cicero’s works. [S2]

### Rhetorical treatises and pedagogical protocols

- **Probably 91–87 BCE:** *De inventione*. A youthful, unfinished two-book handbook on rhetorical invention; Cicero later regarded it as immature, but it survives substantially complete. [S5]
- **55 BCE:** *De oratore*. A three-book dialogue making the ideal orator a synthesis of eloquence, philosophy, law, history, and civic judgment. [S5]
- **Probably 54 BCE, though disputed:** *Partitiones oratoriae*. A question-and-answer rhetorical protocol cast as instruction for Cicero’s son; its date is uncertain. [S5]
- **46 BCE:** *Brutus*. A dialogue and historical catalogue of Roman orators that also intervenes in contemporary debates over style. [S5]
- **46 BCE:** *Orator*. A treatise addressed to Brutus defining the ideal orator and the functions of rhetorical style. [S5]
- **Probably 46 BCE:** *De optimo genere oratorum*. A short preface or essay defending Demosthenes and Aeschines as models; it may have introduced Cicero’s now-lost translations of their rival speeches. [S5]
- **44 BCE:** *Topica*. A rapid instructional treatise for Trebatius on finding arguments, adapting Aristotelian topical theory into a Roman legal-rhetorical protocol. [S5]
- **Not by Cicero; probably late second or early first century BCE:** *Rhetorica ad Herennium*. This anonymous rhetorical handbook was transmitted for centuries under Cicero’s name, but modern scholarship rejects his authorship; it is a manifestation of the Ciceronian corpus, not his obra. [S6]

### Political and philosophical dialogues, treatises, and consolations

- **54–51 BCE:** *De re publica*. A six-book dialogue on constitutions, justice, and Roman political order, surviving incompletely in a palimpsest, quotations, and the separately transmitted *Somnium Scipionis*. [S7]
- **Probably begun in the 50s and revised in the 40s BCE:** *De legibus*. A dialogue projecting laws for an idealized Roman commonwealth; three incomplete books survive, and Cicero may never have finished or published the project. [S7]
- **46 BCE:** *Paradoxa Stoicorum*. Six short rhetorical demonstrations recasting Stoic paradoxes for a Roman audience. [S8]
- **46 BCE:** *Cato* or *Laus Catonis*. A laudatory work on Cato the Younger, now lost except for possible fragments and testimonia; scholars have variously understood it as encomium, biography, or dialogue. [S9]
- **45 BCE:** *Consolatio*. A self-consolatory philosophical work composed after Tullia’s death, now surviving only in fragments and testimonia; the complete text published under its name in 1583 is a Renaissance fabrication. [S8]
- **45 BCE:** *Hortensius*. A lost dialogue exhorting its readers to philosophy, recoverable only through fragments and testimonia. [S8]
- **45 BCE:** *Academica priora*. The first, two-book version of Cicero’s inquiry into Academic scepticism, comprising the lost *Catulus* and the substantially preserved *Lucullus*. [S8]
- **45 BCE:** *Academica posteriora*. Cicero’s recast four-book version of the *Academica*; part of Book I and scattered fragments survive, making this a revised obra rather than merely another manuscript edition. [S8]
- **45 BCE:** *De finibus bonorum et malorum*. A five-book dialogue testing Epicurean, Stoic, and Academic-Peripatetic accounts of the highest good. [S8]
- **45 BCE:** *Tusculanae disputationes*. Five philosophical disputations on death, pain, grief, emotion, and the sufficiency of virtue. [S8]
- **45 BCE:** *De natura deorum*. A three-book dialogue setting Epicurean, Stoic, and Academic arguments about the gods against one another. [S8]
- **45–44 BCE:** *De divinatione*. A two-book dialogue between Cicero and Quintus, first presenting and then criticizing arguments for divination. [S8]
- **44 BCE:** *De fato*. An incomplete dialogue on fate, causation, and human agency; major portions of the text are lost. [S8]
- **44 BCE:** *Cato Maior de senectute*. A dialogue in which the elder Cato presents a defence of old age. [S8]
- **44 BCE:** *Laelius de amicitia*. A dialogue on friendship, memory, virtue, and political community. [S8]
- **44 BCE:** *De gloria*. A two-book dialogue or treatise on glory, now lost apart from fragments and testimonia; a manuscript apparently known to Petrarch later disappeared. [S9]
- **44 BCE:** *De officiis*. A three-book ethical treatise addressed to Cicero’s son, offering practical protocols for honourable and advantageous conduct. [S8]
- **Probably 44 BCE:** *De virtutibus*. A lost work on the virtues, known only from testimonia; its exact form and relationship to Cicero’s other ethical writings are uncertain. [S9]
- **Probably 44 BCE:** *De auguriis*. A lost work on augury whose title and existence are attested but whose form and completion cannot be securely reconstructed. [S9]
- **Date uncertain:** *De iure civili in artem redigendo*. A lost or possibly unfinished project to organize civil law systematically; it is better classified as a protocol-making project than as a securely published book. [S9]
- **Date uncertain:** *De consiliis suis*. A confidential or apologetic account of Cicero’s political counsels, known from testimony and perhaps intended for posthumous circulation; its title, scope, and completion remain debated. [S9]
- **Date uncertain:** *Admiranda*. An attested but lost compilation of remarkable phenomena or stories; its precise genre and authorship history remain obscure. [S9]
- **59 BCE, abandoned:** *Geographical work* (often called *Chorographia* or *De geographia*). Cicero’s correspondence records an attempted geographical project that he found difficult and apparently abandoned; the conventional titles are modern labels, so this is an unfinished project rather than a securely titled obra. [S9]

### Translations and adaptations

- **Youthful, date uncertain:** *Oeconomicus*. A Latin translation or adaptation of Xenophon’s household-management dialogue, now lost except for fragments; its exact date is uncertain. [S9]
- **46 BCE:** *Timaeus*. A surviving fragment of Cicero’s Latin translation of Plato’s cosmological dialogue; whether he translated the whole work is uncertain. [S9]
- **Probably 46 BCE:** *De corona* translations. Cicero reports translating the rival speeches of Demosthenes and Aeschines as models of the best oratory; the translations are lost and may have been intended to accompany *De optimo genere oratorum*. [S5]
- **Dates various:** *Translations from Homer and Greek tragedy*. Cicero made Latin verse translations of passages from Homer, Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides, now preserved mainly as quotations embedded in his prose; many cannot be shown to have belonged to independent, complete works. [S10]

### Poetry

- **Probably 90s–80s BCE, with possible later revision:** *Aratea*. A Latin hexameter translation of Aratus’ astronomical poem, surviving in substantial fragments; scholars dispute the stages and dates of composition. [S10]
- **Date uncertain, perhaps revised around 60 BCE:** *Prognostica* (or *Diosemeia*). Cicero’s rendering of the weather-sign portion of Aratus survives in a handful of fragments; it may have formed part of the *Aratea* rather than an independent obra. [S10]
- **Date uncertain:** *Marius*. A hexameter epic on Gaius Marius, surviving in five fragments; proposed dates range from Cicero’s youth to the later 50s BCE. [S10]
- **Date uncertain, probably youthful:** *Pontius Glaucus*. A juvenile poem known only by title or testimony; its content, extent, and even precise form cannot be recovered. [S10]
- **Date uncertain:** *Alcyones*. A lost poem represented by only a minute quotation; almost nothing secure is known of its subject or form. [S10]
- **Date uncertain:** *Limon*. A poem or poetic miscellany surviving in a few lines quoted by Suetonius; its title suggests a “meadow,” but its structure is unrecoverable. [S10]
- **Date uncertain:** *Nilus*. A lost or fragmentary poem associated by title with the Nile; neither its extent nor its subject can be securely reconstructed. [S10]
- **Date uncertain:** *Uxorius*. A poem known only from fragmentary attribution; its conventional title and independent-work status remain uncertain. [S10]
- **60 BCE:** *De consulatu suo*. A three-book autobiographical-historical epic celebrating Cicero’s consulship, surviving in fragments, including a long self-quotation. [S10]
- **Probably 55–54 BCE:** *De temporibus suis*. A three-book autobiographical epic about Cicero’s exile and return, surviving only in a few lines. [S10]
- **Dates various:** *Occasional poems and unidentified verse fragments*. The critical poetic corpus includes epigrams, improvisations, translations, and unattributed remnants that cannot reliably be separated into individually titled works; their resistance to becoming discrete obras is part of their fragmentary status. [S10]

### Letters and letter-collections

- **68–43 BCE:** *Epistulae ad Atticum*. A posthumously transmitted collection of Cicero’s letters to Atticus in sixteen books; the letters are Cicero’s documentary acts, but the collection and its arrangement are manifestations made by ancient editors rather than a book authored by Cicero in its surviving form. [S11]
- **62–43 BCE:** *Epistulae ad familiares*. A sixteen-book posthumous collection containing Cicero’s letters and letters written to him by numerous correspondents; its modern title, selection, and arrangement are editorial, so the whole cannot be attributed to Cicero without qualification. [S11]
- **59–54 BCE:** *Epistulae ad Quintum fratrem*. Three books of letters to Cicero’s brother Quintus; the transmitted collection is probably posthumous, while the individual letters are Cicero’s own documentary works. [S11]
- **43 BCE:** *Epistulae ad Brutum*. A transmitted two-book correspondence containing letters attributed to Cicero and Brutus; the authenticity of some or all of the collection has repeatedly been disputed, although many modern scholars accept a substantial authentic core. [S11]
- **Dates various, now lost:** *Letters to Caesar, Pompey, Octavian, Cicero’s son, and other correspondents*. Ancient testimony and the surviving correspondence reveal additional collections or groups that have disappeared; without their texts or stable ancient arrangements, they are better treated as lost documentary corpora than as securely bounded books. [S11]
- **44 BCE, planned:** *An edited collection of Cicero’s letters*. Cicero instructed Atticus that Tiro should collect and revise copies before publication; the projected authorial collection cannot be identified with certainty with any surviving corpus and may never have been completed. [S11]

### Memoranda, documentary acts, and compilations

- **63 BCE:** *Commentarius consulatus*. Cicero produced a prose account or memorandum of his consulship, apparently in Greek and possibly also in Latin; the versions and titles reported by ancient evidence are lost and difficult to distinguish. [S12]
- **60 BCE:** *Greek poem on Cicero’s consulship*. Cicero reports composing a Greek verse account alongside his other self-commemorative works; it is lost and its title is not securely known. [S12]
- **46–45 BCE:** *Apophthegmata* or *Dicta collectanea*. A collection of notable sayings that Cicero says he was assembling; whether it reached a finished, independently circulated form is unknown. [S9]
- **Dates various:** *Legal, political, and administrative letters, decrees, recommendations, reports, and provincial dispatches*. These were acts made to accomplish business rather than literary “books”; many survive inside the letter collections, while others are lost, and the boundaries between document, protocol, and obra are inherently unstable. [S11]

### A commemorative project in another medium

- **45 BCE, unfinished:** *Fanum for Tullia*. After Tullia’s death Cicero repeatedly planned, researched sites for, and attempted to commission a monumental shrine or consecrated memorial to her; no completed building is known, so the work is the sustained commemorative project rather than an extant structure. [S9]

### Works transmitted under Cicero’s name but not accepted as his

- **Probably 64 BCE, attribution disputed since antiquity:** *Commentariolum petitionis*. A campaign handbook transmitted with Cicero’s letters to Quintus and conventionally attributed to Quintus Cicero; some scholars have defended Marcus’ authorship or regarded it as later fabrication, so it cannot securely be listed as Marcus’ work. [S11]
- **Ancient or late-antique, date uncertain:** *Pridie quam in exilium iret*. A speech transmitted among the post-return orations but generally rejected as pseudo-Ciceronian. [S6]
- **Late antique or medieval, dates uncertain:** *Synonyma Ciceronis* and other grammatical works. These compilations entered the Ciceronian tradition through false attribution; they are manifestations of Cicero’s authority, not things Marcus Tullius Cicero made. [S6]
- **1583 CE:** *Consolatio* attributed to Cicero. The complete work published by Carlo Sigonio is a Renaissance reconstruction or forgery, distinct from the authentic lost *Consolatio* of 45 BCE. [S6][S8]
