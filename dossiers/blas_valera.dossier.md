# Blas Valera: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Note

Blas Valera’s biography is unusually difficult to reconstruct. The surviving evidence falls into three categories:

1. **Contemporaneous or near-contemporaneous documentation:** Jesuit admission records, personnel catalogues, correspondence, institutional decisions, death notices, and the testimony of Inca Garcilaso de la Vega.
2. **Lost or fragmentarily transmitted writings:** principally Valera’s Latin history of the Incas, preserved through quotations and paraphrases by Garcilaso and later writers.
3. **The disputed Naples or Miccinelli documents:** privately held manuscripts publicized in the late twentieth century that claim Valera’s official death was fabricated, that he secretly returned to Peru, and that he was substantially responsible for Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala’s *Nueva corónica*. Most specialists reject crucial parts of this collection as modern fabrications or contaminated documents; its defenders have argued for authenticity on paleographic, material, and scientific grounds.

This dossier treats the securely documented Valera separately from the alternative biography constructed by the Naples documents.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Usual name:** Blas Valera
- **Expanded modern form:** Blas Valera Pérez
- **Religious designation:** Padre Blas Valera, S.J.
- **Latinized references:** Blasius Valera; Blas Valera appears without a stable Latinized surname form in most scholarship.
- **Purported self-designation in a disputed manuscript:** *Exsul Immeritus Blas Valera Populo Suo*—approximately, “The Undeserving Exile Blas Valera to His People.”
- **Occupation:** Jesuit priest, missionary, linguist, translator, teacher, historian, chronicler, ethnographic collector.
- **Status in colonial society:** Natural—that is, extramarital—mestizo son of a Spanish conquistador and an Indigenous woman.
- **Birth:** Probably 1544 or 1545; 1545 is the conventional date.
- **Birthplace:** Chachapoyas region, northern Peru. Levanto and the now-lost encomienda settlement of Quitaya have both been proposed. An exact birthplace is unproved.
- **Death according to Jesuit documentation:** 2 April 1597, Málaga, Spain.
- **Cause of death:** Not securely established. Later retellings sometimes associate his final illness or death with injuries from the 1596 English sack of Cádiz, but the surviving record does not securely establish death “at the hands of English pirates.”
- **Alternative death narrative:** The disputed Miccinelli corpus says the 1597 death was juridical or fictitious and that Valera survived into the seventeenth century. This is not accepted as established biography.

No verified portrait of Valera is known. Reproductions sometimes identified with him should not be treated as authenticated likenesses.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Documented fact:** Valera’s Jesuit admission declaration identifies him as the natural son of **Luis Valera** and **Francisca Pérez**, and as a native of Chachapoyas.

### Luis Valera

Luis Valera was a Spaniard involved in the conquest and settlement of the Chachapoyas region. Sources describe him as:

- a captain or military participant under Alonso de Alvarado;
- commander of crossbowmen or arquebusiers in regional expeditions;
- an encomendero connected with Chibalta/Chivalta and Quitaya; some older sources instead give Tiapulli/Tiapullo;
- a lieutenant-governor in Chachapoyas during the civil-war period among the Spanish conquerors.

Garcilaso called Blas’s father “Alonso de Valera,” probably through error or confusion. The Jesuit admission text naming **Luis Valera** is stronger evidence.

Luis Valera does not appear securely among the men who shared Atahualpa’s Cajamarca ransom. Claims that he personally witnessed Atahualpa’s seizure or execution are therefore uncertain and may derive from later reconstruction.

### Francisca Pérez

Very little secure information survives concerning Francisca Pérez beyond her name and Indigenous origin.

Competing claims include:

- **Documented minimum:** She was an Indigenous woman associated with Chachapoyas.
- **Reported or inferred claim:** She may have been a Quechua-speaking migrant or member of an Inca-affiliated family settled in Chachapoyas.
- **Tradition:** She was connected with Atahualpa’s court.
- **More elaborate genealogical theory:** She was related to Cayo Túpac Rimachi, described as an Inca governor of Chachapoyas and kinsman of Huayna Cápac.
- **Unverified claim:** She was an “Inca princess.”

No presently identified contemporary genealogy proves royal descent. “Princess” should therefore be understood as later biographical tradition, not established fact.

### Siblings

A probable brother was **Jerónimo Valera**, approximately eleven years younger, who became a Franciscan friar, teacher, theologian, and author of *Commentarii ac quaestiones in universam Aristotelis ac subtilissimi doctoris Ioannis Duns Scoti logicam* (Lima, 1610).

Sabine Hyland considers Jerónimo likely to have been another son of Luis Valera and Francisca Pérez. The relationship is plausible and supported by early Franciscan testimony, though surviving documentation is less direct than Valera’s own Jesuit admission record.

Other siblings appearing in online genealogies are not sufficiently documented.

### Marriage and Descendants

As a Jesuit priest, Valera did not marry. No securely documented spouse, partner, or child is known.

The accusation leading to his punishment may have involved sexual conduct with a woman, but neither the woman’s identity nor the facts of the alleged relationship are securely recoverable.

## Childhood and Early Life, c. 1544–1567

Valera grew up in the recently conquered Chachapoyas region, where Spanish encomienda society coexisted with Chachapoya communities, Inca resettlement legacies, Quechua-speaking populations, and the violent aftereffects of conquest.

He probably learned Quechua during childhood. Whether it was his mother’s first language is not definitively documented, but his later proficiency and his family environment make early acquisition likely.

Later scholars have proposed that his position between a Spanish encomendero household and Indigenous maternal kin gave him access to competing memories of:

- Inca administration;
- Atahualpa and the civil war with Huáscar;
- the conquest;
- Spanish labor exactions;
- Indigenous ritual, poetry, oral history, and political claims.

That formative reconstruction is plausible but partly inferential. There is no surviving childhood memoir.

Valera was sent to **Trujillo**, where he studied grammar and Latin. He then studied in **Lima**, reportedly completing approximately two years of arts and one year of theology before entering the Society of Jesus. His ability in Latin later drew exceptional praise from Garcilaso, who described his historical prose as “elegantísimo latín.”

## Education and Formation

### Secular and Preparatory Education

- **Trujillo:** Latin and grammar.
- **Lima:** Arts and theology before entering the Jesuits.
- **Languages:** Spanish, Latin, and Quechua are securely or strongly attested. Knowledge of Aymara is frequently attributed to him because of his work in southern Peru and the Third Council’s translation program, but the exact level of fluency is disputed.

### Admission to the Society of Jesus

Valera was received into the Jesuit college at Lima in November 1568. Sources give either **20 November** or **29 November**; the surviving copied admission formula has the twentieth, while the modern Jesuit institutional biography gives the twenty-ninth.

The admission declaration says that:

- he was the natural son of Luis Valera and Francisca Pérez;
- he was from Chachapoyas;
- he had been examined as a student;
- he had seen the papal bulls, constitutions, and rules;
- he consented to observe them.

He was among the earliest mestizos admitted to Jesuit formation in Peru.

### Jesuit Formation and Mentors

The Jesuits had arrived in Peru only shortly before his admission. Important institutional figures in his formation and career included:

- **Jerónimo Ruiz de Portillo:** first Jesuit provincial of Peru and the official who received him.
- **José de Acosta:** provincial, theologian, organizer of missionary language work, and author whose catechetical program Valera helped translate.
- **Alonso de Barzana:** linguist and missionary collaborator.
- **Bartolomé de Santiago:** collaborator in translation.
- **Claudio Acquaviva:** superior general of the Society of Jesus, whose decisions shaped Valera’s punishment and proposed removal from Peru.
- **Esteban Cabello:** Jesuit procurator whose later favorable assessment reportedly helped mitigate Valera’s treatment in Andalusia.
- **Cristóbal Méndez:** Andalusian provincial who reportedly permitted him limited teaching and confessional duties rather than enforcing the harshest confinement.

No single teacher can be identified as his principal intellectual mentor. His formation combined scholastic theology, Renaissance Latin humanism, Jesuit missionary linguistics, Andean oral knowledge, and colonial political experience.

## Early Career and Missionary Work, 1568–1581

### Huarochirí, 1571

Valera was assigned to **Huarochirí**, a major Andean religious region east of Lima. The assignment exposed him to local ritual systems and Indigenous religious vocabulary. It preceded the better-known seventeenth-century Huarochirí extirpation campaign of Francisco de Ávila and should not be conflated with it.

### Ordination and Cusco

Valera was ordained a priest at **Cusco in 1573**. An older biography gives 1574, but the Jesuit institutional chronology favors 1573.

By 1576 he was teaching Latin and preaching to Indigenous audiences in Cusco. His combination of humanistic learning and Quechua competence was unusual.

### Cofradía del Nombre de Jesús

Valera became associated with the Jesuit-sponsored **Confraternity of the Name of Jesus** in Cusco, whose membership included Inca nobles. The confraternity provided an institutional setting in which Christian devotion, Inca elite corporate identity, and the defense of noble privileges could coexist.

When Jesuit authorities attempted to transfer Valera and Alonso de Barzana away from Cusco, members of the Inca elite reportedly objected. This indicates that Valera possessed a significant Indigenous constituency.

Claims that he was the formal leader of a secret “neo-Inca Christian” political movement go beyond the secure evidence. His work did, however, articulate a Christian interpretation more favorable to Inca civilization than the ideological program associated with Viceroy Francisco de Toledo.

### Juli and the Southern Andes

Valera worked at **Juli**, the important Jesuit mission near Lake Titicaca, around 1577. Juli became a center of Indigenous-language evangelization. He also worked or traveled in **La Paz, Copacabana, and Potosí**.

At Potosí he may have organized or supported another Name of Jesus confraternity. The evidence is less complete than for Cusco.

### Wider Travels

Institutional biographies associate him with travel or missionary collection in:

- Quito
- Cajamarca
- Chachapoyas
- Trujillo
- Huamachuco
- Huánuco
- Tarma
- Lima
- Pachacámac
- Chincha
- Cusco
- Juli
- Copacabana
- La Paz
- Potosí

Not every destination can be dated independently, and some lists may combine documented assignments with reconstructed research journeys.

## The Third Council of Lima and Linguistic Work, 1582–1583

The **Third Provincial Council of Lima** sought standardized Christian instructional materials for Indigenous-language evangelization.

Valera collaborated with Jesuit linguists including Alonso de Barzana and Bartolomé de Santiago on translations connected with:

- *Doctrina christiana y catecismo para instrucción de los indios*;
- the confession manual;
- sermon collections;
- Spanish, Quechua, and Aymara versions of ecclesiastically approved texts.

The Council’s books were institutionally commissioned, collectively produced, revised, and published anonymously or corporately. Valera’s individual contribution cannot always be isolated.

### Linguistic Position

Fragments attributed to Valera present Quechua as a language capable of expressing sophisticated political and theological concepts. He analyzed terms such as:

- *huaca/waka*;
- *Pachacámac*;
- names and titles of Inca rulers;
- religious and administrative vocabulary;
- poetry, oral traditions, and quipu practices.

He appears to have favored selective accommodation: Indigenous terminology and pre-Christian concepts could, after correction or reinterpretation, prepare Andean peoples for Christianity.

This position differed from approaches that treated Indigenous religious language primarily as an instrument of idolatry requiring eradication.

## Intellectual and Political Development

### Defense of Inca Civilization

The Valera fragments preserved by Garcilaso describe Inca society as:

- politically ordered;
- linguistically sophisticated;
- capable of moral and religious understanding;
- supported by historical memory transmitted through oral authorities and quipus;
- in some respects providentially prepared for Christianity.

He disputed the idea that Spanish conquest alone brought order or rationality to the Andes.

### Inca Sovereignty

Some modern scholars, especially Hyland, interpret Valera as believing that the Incas possessed legitimate sovereignty and that Spanish rule required moral and juridical justification. His reported sympathy for Atahualpa and Indigenous corporate rights placed him at odds with the historical program developed under Viceroy Toledo, which portrayed the Incas as recent tyrants and Spanish conquest as liberation.

The extent to which Valera advocated restoration of Inca government is uncertain. Secure fragments support a favorable account of Inca rule; the stronger claim that he organized an anti-colonial political project depends partly on disputed or retrospective evidence.

### Andean Christianity

Valera’s apparent theological model was not a simple preservation of pre-Christian religion. He remained a Jesuit priest and missionary. His distinctive position lay in arguing that:

- Andean religious knowledge contained partial truths;
- Quechua could function as a theological language;
- some Inca concepts could be redirected toward Christianity;
- evangelization need not require total degradation of Indigenous civilization.

The classification of this position as “heresy” is largely interpretive. There is no surviving Inquisition conviction or formal theological condemnation of a Valera text.

## Imprisonment, Discipline, and Suppression, c. 1583–1593

### What Is Documented

Valera fell under severe internal Jesuit discipline during the 1580s. The surviving record indicates:

- removal from ordinary priestly and teaching functions;
- confinement or imprisonment within Jesuit institutions;
- an order lasting several years;
- compulsory prayer and menial duties;
- regular “mortifications,” which could include penitential corporal discipline;
- prohibition on teaching Indigenous languages;
- consideration of dismissal from the Society;
- eventual transfer or exile to Spain.

His punishment was handled internally by the Society of Jesus, not through a public Inquisition prosecution.

### Date Disagreements

Modern accounts variously date the beginning of confinement to **1583**, **1585**, or **1588**. The disagreement reflects different surviving decisions and stages of the case: accusation, initial restriction, Roman deliberation, and formal enforcement.

A reasonable reconstruction is that difficulties began around 1583–1585 and culminated in a prolonged regime of confinement and restriction extending into the early 1590s.

### The Sexual-Misconduct Charge

**Reported institutional accusation:** Valera was accused of sexual misconduct, often summarized as fornication or an offense against chastity.

The details were considered too sensitive or secret to set out fully in surviving routine correspondence. No complete trial dossier, named complainant, confession, or adjudicated factual narrative is known.

### Alternative Interpretation

Hyland argues that the sexual explanation does not adequately account for the severity and secrecy of the punishment. She proposes that the underlying issue was Valera’s Indigenous advocacy, linguistic theology, favorable interpretation of Inca religion, and political danger to the colonial order.

This is an influential scholarly interpretation, not a formally proven finding. Relevant considerations include:

- the punishment was unusually severe compared with some other sexual cases;
- Jesuit authorities sought high-level decisions from Europe;
- Valera’s ideas conflicted with Toledan political theology;
- mestizo clergy increasingly encountered institutional suspicion;
- in 1582 the Peruvian Jesuits restricted further admission of mestizos.

Possible explanations are not mutually exclusive: an actual or alleged sexual offense may have intersected with institutional distrust of his ideas and alliances.

### No Inquisition Conviction

There is no identified evidence that the Lima Inquisition tried, convicted, or imprisoned Valera. Statements that he was “condemned by the Inquisition” are unsupported.

### Response to the Proceedings

Valera reportedly maintained his innocence and declined an opportunity to transfer to another religious order. He requested permission to travel to Rome and plead his case before Claudio Acquaviva. That request was not granted in the form he desired.

---

## Exile and Final Years in Spain, c. 1593–1597

Valera left Peru in the early 1590s. Accounts commonly place his departure late in **1592** or in **1593**. He may have traveled through **Quito** before crossing the Atlantic.

He reached **Spain**, probably Seville, by approximately 1595. He was assigned within the Jesuit province of Andalusia.

Favorable reports concerning his conduct led to some mitigation of his punishment. He reportedly:

- taught humanities or grammar at the Jesuit college in Cádiz;
- heard men’s confessions;
- remained subject to restrictions and did not regain his former missionary autonomy.

### Sack of Cádiz, 1596

An Anglo-Dutch force under the Earl of Essex and Lord Howard attacked Cádiz in 1596. Garcilaso states that Valera’s historical papers were largely destroyed in the sack.

Garcilaso later received surviving fragments from the Jesuit **Pedro Maldonado de Saavedra**, then teaching Scripture in Córdoba. This transmission is the principal reason portions of Valera’s lost history remain recoverable.

The statement that Valera personally lost all five volumes while fleeing flames is a conventional reconstruction. Garcilaso’s narrower testimony is that the papers were ruined in the sack and only remnants survived.

### Death

Jesuit documentation places Valera’s death at **Málaga on 2 April 1597**.

He was approximately fifty-two if born in 1545. No securely documented terminal illness, medical diagnosis, accident, addiction, or psychological crisis is known.

Older sources sometimes say he died at Cádiz or Alcalá de Henares; Málaga is supported by the stronger institutional record.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Luis Valera

Father, conquistador, and encomendero. His position gave Blas access to Spanish colonial society while his natural-son status and maternal ancestry marked him as mestizo.

### Francisca Pérez

Mother and likely source of early Indigenous-language and cultural formation. Claims of Inca royal ancestry remain unproved.

### Jerónimo Ruiz de Portillo

Jesuit provincial who admitted Valera to the Society.

### Alonso de Barzana

Jesuit missionary and Indigenous-language specialist; colleague in missionary and translation work.

### Bartolomé de Santiago

Collaborator in ecclesiastical translation.

### José de Acosta

Provincial, theologian, and organizer of the Third Council linguistic program. Valera worked within Acosta’s institutional project, although their precise personal relationship and intellectual disagreements are incompletely documented.

### Inca Nobles of Cusco

Members of the Name of Jesus confraternity supported Valera and reportedly resisted his transfer. Their names are not consistently preserved in the accessible accounts, but the corporate relationship is important evidence of his local standing.

### Claudio Acquaviva

Jesuit superior general. He received reports about Valera and directed or approved major elements of his discipline and removal.

### Esteban Cabello and Cristóbal Méndez

Jesuit administrators associated with the easing of Valera’s restrictions in Andalusia.

### Pedro Maldonado de Saavedra

Jesuit who gave the surviving remnants of Valera’s papers to Inca Garcilaso de la Vega around 1600.

### Inca Garcilaso de la Vega

No meeting or correspondence is documented. Their relationship was posthumous and textual: Garcilaso obtained, translated, quoted, summarized, and contested Valera’s surviving Latin papers in the *Comentarios reales*.

Garcilaso praised Valera’s linguistic ability and scholarship while disagreeing with him on certain dynastic matters, particularly interpretations touching Atahualpa and Huáscar.

### Giovanni Anello Oliva

Later Jesuit historian whose writings appear to preserve or echo Valeran materials. Direct collaboration is chronologically impossible or unproved; Oliva’s relationship was one of later textual use.

## Institutions, Projects, and Material Interests

### Society of Jesus

The Society was Valera’s employer, religious order, school, disciplinary authority, patron, censor, and custodian of his writings.

### Jesuit College of Lima

Site of admission and formation.

### Jesuit Missions at Huarochirí and Juli

Centers of missionary work, linguistic accommodation, and collection of Andean religious knowledge.

### Cofradía del Nombre de Jesús

A religious corporation linking Jesuits with Indigenous and Inca elites. It offered devotional practice, community organization, and defense of status.

### Third Council of Lima

The principal institutional setting for Valera’s translation and standardization work.

### Finances and Property

No reliable evidence has been found of personal investments, businesses, patents, commercial partnerships, or independent property. As a professed Jesuit, Valera operated within corporate religious structures rather than as an independent proprietor.

His father’s encomienda was economically and politically important to his upbringing, but no clear inheritance by Blas is documented.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Investigations

### 1. Accusation of Sexual Misconduct

- **Status:** Reported institutional accusation.
- **Evidence:** Jesuit disciplinary correspondence and later reconstructions refer to an offense against chastity.
- **Missing evidence:** No full accusation, witness statement, confession, verdict, or identified partner survives in the accessible record.
- **Conclusion:** It cannot be established whether the accusation was true, exaggerated, pretextual, or combined with ideological concerns.

### 2. Alleged Heresy or Dangerous Accommodation

- **Status:** Scholarly interpretation; no formal heresy conviction.
- **Claim:** Valera treated Inca religion as a providential preparation for Christianity and used Quechua concepts too freely in theology.
- **Evidence:** Ideas preserved in fragments, the political context of his punishment, and the severity of Jesuit discipline.
- **Limitation:** No surviving document explicitly sentences him for a defined heretical proposition.

### 3. Political “Inca Nationalism”

- **Status:** Interpretive label.
- **Evidence:** Defense of Inca civilization and sovereignty, association with Inca nobles, and opposition to the ideological effects of Toledo’s historical program.
- **Limitation:** No secure evidence demonstrates that Valera planned armed rebellion or belonged to a conspiratorial independence organization.

### 4. Alleged Authorship of the *Nueva corónica*

- **Status:** Claim made by disputed manuscripts; rejected by most specialists.
- **Claim:** Valera and Jesuit collaborators composed much of the *Nueva corónica*, using Felipe Guamán Poma as a public “screen” in exchange for material compensation.
- **Evidence offered by supporters:** Statements within the Miccinelli documents, claimed signatures, associated quipus and textiles, parallels between Valera’s known ideas and the *Nueva corónica*, and some material tests consistent with old components.
- **Evidence against:** The claim depends on documents with insecure provenance; linguistic and historical anomalies; conflict with securely documented chronology; and evidence that at least one decisive drawing was traced from the retouched image in Paul Rivet’s 1936 facsimile.
- **Present assessment:** Not established and substantially undermined by forensic findings. Guamán Poma remains the historically supported author.

### 5. Alleged Faking of Valera’s Death

- **Status:** Miccinelli-document allegation.
- **Claim:** The Jesuits declared Valera legally dead in 1597, after which he secretly returned to Peru.
- **Against:** Jesuit records place his death at Málaga; no independent seventeenth-century witness securely documents his survival; the narrative appears in the same contested corpus containing probable modern fabrications.
- **Conclusion:** Not accepted as historical fact.

### 6. Claims of Garcilaso’s Plagiarism

Some twentieth-century scholars argued that Garcilaso possessed more of Valera’s history than he admitted and appropriated it for the *Comentarios reales*.

Arguments include:

- Garcilaso quotes or invokes Valera beyond the surviving fragments;
- Valera’s history may not have been as thoroughly destroyed as Garcilaso claimed;
- the Cádiz capitulation reportedly protected Jesuit persons and papers;
- sections of Garcilaso’s work align closely with Valera’s known interests.

Counterarguments include:

- Garcilaso openly and repeatedly names Valera;
- he describes the transmission of the damaged manuscripts;
- manuscript loss does not mean every usable passage vanished;
- extensive use of another chronicler was normal in early modern historiography.

No legal plagiarism proceeding occurred. The extent of unacknowledged dependence remains a textual question.

### 7. Criminality, Fraud, and Corruption

No substantiated evidence identifies Valera himself as participating in fraud, money laundering, commercial corruption, homicide, political violence, or secular criminal proceedings.

The principal fraud controversy concerns manuscripts attributed to him centuries after his death, not conduct securely attributable to Valera.

### Dedicated Adversarial Search, 2024–2026

Searches combining Valera’s name with controversy, scandal, fraud, forgery, accusation, conspiracy, lawsuit, and criticism produced no new lawsuit, criminal discovery, or authenticated biographical scandal during 2024–2026.

Recent scholarship continues to focus on:

- Guamán Poma’s authorship and sources;
- modern contamination or fabrication within the Miccinelli collection;
- anachronistic borrowings in *Exsul Immeritus*;
- the afterlife of the Valera-authorship theory.

A recent study reports that parts of *Exsul Immeritus* draw on editions of Dante first published in the twentieth century, further supporting modern fabrication. The exact implications for every physical component in the Miccinelli archive remain a matter for item-by-item analysis; proof that one component is forged does not automatically date every associated piece, but it destroys the corpus’s reliability as a unit.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### The Inca-Princess Mother

**Story:** Francisca Pérez was an Inca princess or court noble related to Atahualpa.

**Appearance and circulation:** Developed in later Peruvian biography and was repeated in popular accounts emphasizing Valera’s mestizo bridge between Spain and the Incas.

**Evidence:** His mother was Indigenous; possible Inca affiliation is plausible in Chachapoyas’s resettlement history.

**Against:** No contemporary genealogy establishes royal descent.

**Effect:** The story has strengthened Valera’s symbolic status as a biological and intellectual heir to both conquest and empire.

### The Secret Neo-Inca Christian Movement

**Story:** Valera led a clandestine movement seeking a Christianized restoration of Inca rule.

**Evidence:** His ties to Inca nobles, confraternity work, and favorable account of Inca sovereignty.

**Against:** The fully organized clandestine movement appears most clearly in interpretations influenced by disputed evidence.

**Effect:** It transformed him from missionary-scholar into a proto-nationalist or anti-colonial conspirator in modern retellings.

### Murder by English Pirates

**Story:** Valera was killed by English attackers during or after the sack of Cádiz.

**Evidence:** His papers were associated with the 1596 destruction; he died the following year.

**Against:** The recorded place of death is Málaga, months after the raid; no secure account identifies homicide by English forces.

**Effect:** It gives his life a dramatic martyr-like ending and links the destruction of his history to his death.

### The Ghost Chronicler

**Story:** Valera became a “ghost chronicler”: officially dead but secretly returned to Peru to write, organize resistance, and preserve hidden Inca knowledge.

**Origin:** The Naples/Miccinelli manuscripts publicized by Clara Miccinelli and Laura Laurencich Minelli from the 1980s–1990s onward.

**Evidence claimed:** *Historia et rudimenta linguae Piruanorum*, *Exsul Immeritus*, signatures, quipus, seals, textiles, and purported chains of custody.

**Evidence against:**

- private and discontinuous provenance;
- historical and linguistic anomalies;
- failure to establish the collection’s authenticity as a whole;
- a contractual drawing demonstrably dependent on a retouched illustration published in 1936;
- syllabic-quipu structures resembling Raimondo de Sangro’s eighteenth-century speculative system;
- reported dependence on twentieth-century Dante editions.

**Effect:** The controversy revived international interest in Valera, generated conferences and editions, and inserted him into disputes over Guamán Poma, quipu literacy, conquest historiography, and modern forgery.

### Secret Phonetic Quipus

**Story:** Valera knew an Inca system capable of recording phonetic or syllabic language through colored, knotted cords and attached symbols.

**Evidence:** Primarily the disputed Naples materials.

**Against:** Comparative analysis links aspects of the supposed system to Raimondo de Sangro’s 1750 *Lettera apologetica*, rather than securely pre-Hispanic practice.

**Effect:** The claim has circulated widely in alternative histories of lost technology and Indigenous writing. It should not be confused with the well-supported fact that genuine khipus encoded complex quantitative and possibly semasiographic information.

### Valera as the “First Peruvian Historian”

**Story or commemorative title:** Valera was Peru’s first historian.

**Origin:** Particularly visible in twentieth-century Peruvian scholarship, including Esteban Hidalgo Santillán.

**Basis:** He was a Peru-born mestizo who undertook a systematic history of the Incas.

**Limitation:** “First” depends on definitions of Peru, historian, authorship, and survival; earlier Indigenous, Spanish, and mestizo historical testimony existed.

**Effect:** The title incorporated Valera into national literary history despite the loss of his principal work.

## Works by or Attributed to Blas Valera

## Securely Attested or Strongly Supported

### *Historia Occidentalis* / History of the Incas

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Date:** Composed during the later sixteenth century, probably across many years.
- **Extent:** Traditionally described as five books or volumes.
- **Status:** Lost except for quotations, translations, summaries, and possible echoes.
- **Contents:** Inca origins, dynasties, religion, government, language, poetry, quipus, regional histories, conquest, and Atahualpa.
- **Transmission:** Fragments survived the 1596 sack of Cádiz and reached Garcilaso through Pedro Maldonado de Saavedra.
- **Original title:** *Historia Occidentalis* is conventional; the precise authorial title is not certain.

### Fragments Preserved in *Comentarios reales de los Incas*

- **Original author:** Valera.
- **Translator/editor:** Inca Garcilaso de la Vega.
- **Publication:** Lisbon, 1609.
- **Status:** Genuine Valeran material is strongly supported, but Garcilaso selected, translated, framed, and sometimes disputed it.
- **Topics:** The name “Peru”; *Pachacámac*; *huaca*; Inca theology and ritual; poetry; language; quipus; royal succession; temples; Atahualpa; regional customs.

### Contributions to Third Council of Lima Publications

Probable collaborative contributions include:

- *Doctrina christiana y catecismo para instrucción de los indios* (Lima, 1584);
- *Confesionario para los curas de indios* (Lima, 1585);
- *Tercero catecismo y exposición de la doctrina cristiana por sermones* (Lima, 1585).

These were corporate ecclesiastical productions. Valera should be listed as a contributor or translator, not sole author.

## Disputed or Uncertain Attributions

### *Arte y vocabulario en la lengua general del Perú llamada quichua, y en la lengua española*

- **Publication:** Lima, Antonio Ricardo, 1586.
- **Original attribution:** Anonymous.
- **Candidates proposed:** Blas Valera, Alonso de Barzana, and others.
- **Assessment:** Valera may have contributed, but sole authorship is unproved. Modern editions placing his name prominently on the work do not settle the sixteenth-century attribution.

### *De las costumbres antiguas de los naturales del Pirú*

- **Alternative Latin title used in scholarship:** *De Tahuantinsuyus prischis gentibus*.
- **Status:** Anonymous colonial relation, frequently attributed to Valera or associated with Valeran material.
- **Publication history:** Edited in modern collections, including Francisco A. Loayza’s 1945 volume.
- **Assessment:** Attribution remains disputed. It should not be treated as an autograph Valera text without qualification.

### *Relación anónima*

This designation is sometimes applied to *De las costumbres antiguas*. Its textual relationship to Valera, Luis López, and Jesuit ethnographic compilations remains debated.

## Attributed in the Miccinelli Collection

### *Exsul Immeritus Blas Valera Populo Suo*

- **Purported date:** 1618.
- **Purported author:** Blas Valera.
- **Modern publication:** Edited and translated by Laura Laurencich Minelli and collaborators, with editions in 2005, 2007, and 2009 contexts.
- **Status:** Disputed attribution; substantial evidence of modern fabrication or contamination.
- **Contents claimed:** Valera’s survival, secret return, Inca religion, conquest crimes, quipus, ceque systems, and authorship of the *Nueva corónica*.

### *Historia et Rudimenta Linguae Piruanorum*

- **Purported authors:** Joan Antonio Cumis and Joan Anello Oliva, with testimony about Valera.
- **Purported dates:** Seventeenth century.
- **Status:** Part of the disputed Naples corpus.

### *Primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno*

- **Actual manuscript attribution:** Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala.
- **Purported Valera role:** Principal or secret author according to the Naples documents.
- **Assessment:** The Valera attribution is unsupported by reliable evidence and contradicted by forensic findings concerning the Miccinelli materials.

## Posthumous Transmission and Reception

### Inca Garcilaso, 1600–1609

Garcilaso’s acquisition of Valera’s damaged papers rescued his name from near-total disappearance. He presented Valera as:

- a learned fellow Peruvian;
- a gifted Latinist and linguist;
- a diligent investigator;
- an authority whose surviving testimony deserved preservation.

Garcilaso also subordinated Valera’s work to his own narrative and corrected him where their dynastic loyalties or evidence differed.

### Seventeenth-Century Readers

Valeran material or references appear in works associated with:

- Francisco de Herrera Maldonado, *Apología de la fidelidad de la Historia* (1620);
- Alonso de Sandoval, *De instauranda Aethiopum salute* and later editions;
- Giovanni Anello Oliva’s histories.

These references suggest that either additional Valera material survived for a time or later writers knew him through intermediaries.

### Nineteenth and Early Twentieth Centuries

Peruvian and European scholars attempted to reconstruct Valera through Garcilaso, Jesuit sources, and anonymous colonial relations. Manuel González de la Rosa and later Esteban Hidalgo Santillán helped promote him as a foundational Peruvian historian.

Francisco A. Loayza’s 1945 edition grouped *Las costumbres antiguas del Perú* with a reconstructed history of the Incas under Valera’s name, increasing access while also encouraging uncertain attributions.

### Late Twentieth-Century Revival

The Miccinelli revelations after 1990 transformed Valera into the center of a contentious international debate. Conferences in Lima, Rome, Seville, Bologna, and elsewhere addressed:

- manuscript authenticity;
- Guamán Poma’s authorship;
- Jesuit secrecy;
- quipu writing;
- the conquest of Peru;
- Valera’s supposed clandestine survival.

### Current Reputation

Valera is now studied primarily as:

- an early mestizo Jesuit intellectual;
- a major, though fragmentarily preserved, source on Inca culture;
- an advocate of Indigenous-language Christianity;
- a figure in the history of colonial censorship and mestizo clergy;
- an important source for Garcilaso;
- the involuntary center of a modern manuscript-forgery controversy.

His posthumous identity oscillates among “Jesuit rebel,” “first Peruvian historian,” “Inca nationalist,” “ghost chronicler,” linguistic humanist, and suppressed missionary. Only some of those titles correspond closely to documented sixteenth-century evidence.

Popular biographies, genealogical pages, unattributed encyclopedia entries, and nationalist commemorations frequently repeat:

- Levanto as certain birthplace;

- Inquisition imprisonment;
- death during the Cádiz attack;
- secret authorship of Guamán Poma.

## Death and Present Status of the Evidence

The strongest documentary conclusion is that Valera died in the Jesuit house at Málaga on **2 April 1597**.

Unresolved matters include:

- exact birth date and village;
- precise maternal ancestry;
- full details and truth of the chastity accusation;
- whether ideological and political concerns determined his punishment;
- complete itinerary during confinement and exile;
- exact contents and title of his lost history;
- the amount of Valera’s writing incorporated into Garcilaso and later Jesuit histories;
- authorship of *De las costumbres antiguas* and the 1586 *Arte y vocabulario*.

The Naples documents do not provide a reliable basis for extending his life beyond 1597.

---

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Valera’s historical influence rests on a paradox: his principal work disappeared, but its surviving fragments substantially affected one of the canonical histories of the Andes.

His legacy includes:

- preservation of Indigenous terminology and historical traditions;
- a learned defense of Quechua’s intellectual capacity;
- an account of Inca civilization less hostile than the Toledan model;
- influence on Garcilaso’s representation of Inca religion, government, poetry, and historical memory;
- evidence for Indigenous–Jesuit intellectual exchange;
- a case study in the institutional limits imposed on mestizo clergy;
- continuing debate over accommodation, syncretism, colonial sovereignty, and ecclesiastical censorship;
- modern symbolic appropriation as a proto-nationalist, Indigenous advocate, secret rebel, or suppressed historian;
- a major cautionary case in provenance, manuscript authentication, anachronism, and the use of genuine old materials in modern forgeries.

No conclusion about Valera’s historical importance requires accepting the Miccinelli documents.
