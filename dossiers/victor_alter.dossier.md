# Victor Alter: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary cautions

This dossier reconstructs the life of **Wiktor (Victor/Viktor) Alter**, Polish Jewish socialist, Bund leader, trade-union organizer, municipal politician, economic writer, and Soviet political prisoner. The evidence is uneven. Alter left a substantial printed record, but no full autobiography; much of his private and organizational record was dispersed or destroyed by war. His final years are documented through Polish diplomatic papers, Bund memorial publications, Soviet security files opened late in the twentieth century, and later historical studies. Older reference works repeated the Soviet government's false or shifting accounts of his death. Accordingly, this dossier distinguishes:

- **documented facts** supported by contemporaneous or archival records;
- **retrospective testimony**, which may preserve otherwise unavailable details but can contain errors;
- **official Soviet allegations**, for which no substantiating evidence has emerged;
- **later hypotheses** about Stalin's motives, which remain inferential;
- **commemorative narratives**, especially the paired memory of “Erlich and Alter.”

The best compact biographical sources are Daniel Blatman's entry in the *YIVO Encyclopedia of Jews in Eastern Europe* and the translated entry from the *Leksikon fun der nayer yidisher literatur* hosted by the Congress for Jewish Culture. For the Soviet case, the most important later work is based on the NKVD files: Gertrud Pickhan's study of the files; Szymon Rudnicki and Victoria Mochalova's “Prisoners Nos. 41 and 42”; Shimon Redlich's documentary history of the Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee; and the records summarized in Gennady Estraikh's *Jews in the Soviet Union: A History*, volume 3. The dedicated YIVO collection **RG 1459, Erlich–Alter Case (1913–2003)** contains official records, correspondence, press material, photographs, rehabilitation material, and Alter's prison essay on physics.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Principal Polish name:** Wiktor Alter.
- **English form:** Victor Alter.
- **Russian form:** Виктор Альтер (*Viktor Al'ter*).
- **Yiddish:** וויקטאָר אַלטער (*Viktor Alter*).
- **Documented pseudonyms:** **M. Lorman** (used most conspicuously during the 1921 Comintern mission); **Vic/Vik Fort** (used for early French-language socialist journalism in Belgium). The Congress for Jewish Culture lexicon records both.
- **Birth:** Most specialist biographical sources give **7 February 1890**, Mława, then in the Płock Governorate of the Russian Empire's Congress Kingdom of Poland. A competing Polish-language reference tradition gives **20 December 1890** and names his mother as Syma née Horn. No digitized civil birth record was located in the sources consulted here; 7 February is retained as the best-attested date, with the discrepancy left open.
- **Death:** **17 February 1943**, Kuibyshev, Russian SFSR, Soviet Union (now Samara, Russia), shot by Soviet state-security personnel. This date is supported by the post-Soviet archival record. Older accounts gave December 1941 or December 1942 because Soviet authorities concealed and misreported the chronology.
- **Age at death:** 53 if the 7 February date is correct.
- **Citizenship/national status:** Born a subject of the Russian Empire; after 1918 a citizen and public official of the Second Polish Republic. Soviet authorities later claimed Soviet jurisdiction over him and, in January 1942, told the Polish embassy that he was a Soviet citizen. The Polish government rejected that claim and maintained that he remained a Polish citizen.
- **Occupations and roles:** Electromechanical/mechanical engineer by training; factory worker and engineer; socialist organizer; Bund Central Committee member; trade-union and cooperative administrator; journalist, editor, pamphleteer, translator, and lecturer; Warsaw municipal councillor and city executive member; member of the governing board of the Warsaw Jewish religious community; representative to the Labour and Socialist International; proposed secretary of an international Jewish anti-fascist committee.
- **Languages:** Yiddish and Polish were his principal public languages; his education and publications show command of French, English, and Russian. The biographical lexicon says he excelled in languages and mathematics and translated from English and French into Russian.
- **Burial:** Unknown. No authenticated grave has been located. A cenotaph for Alter and Henryk Erlich stands in the Jewish cemetery on Okopowa Street in Warsaw.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and household

Alter was born into a large, prosperous Hasidic merchant family in Mława. His father is identified as **Yisroel/Izrael Alter**, a timber or wood merchant. The Congress for Jewish Culture's biographical lexicon describes Wiktor as the youngest of fourteen children and says that his father's ancestry belonged to an extensive, wealthy Hasidic lineage. His mother is identified in a Polish biographical tradition as **Syma (Sima) née Horn**.

Yisroel Alter died when Wiktor was approximately one year old. The surviving household moved to Warsaw. His mother subsequently married **Yisroel-Volf (Israel Wolf) Gliksman**, a *maskil*—a partisan of Jewish Enlightenment—and a *ḥovev Tsiyon*, or early “Lover of Zion,” from Kutno. The contrast between the father's Hasidic milieu, the stepfather's Haskalah and proto-Zionist commitments, and Alter's later secular, socialist, Yiddishist, anti-Zionist Bundism is one of the clearest formative tensions in his background. It should not be made into a simple conversion narrative: the surviving sources do not record a single break with religion or a decisive family quarrel.

### Siblings and half-siblings

The biographical lexicon's statement that Alter was the youngest of fourteen children is not accompanied by a complete sibling list. Two relations are recoverable:

- **Jerzy (Yuri) Gliksman** (23 June 1902–4 September 1958), Alter's half-brother through their mother, became a lawyer, Bund activist, Warsaw city councillor, Soviet prisoner, author of *Tell the West* (1948), and later émigré in the United States. His papers are YIVO **RG 1464**.
- **Ester/Esther Iwańska**, described in a Warsaw wartime memoir tradition as Alter's sister, was a Bund activist. During the siege of Warsaw, Mayor Stefan Starzyński reportedly approached her when German authorities demanded Jewish hostages.

Other siblings are not securely identified in the sources consulted. A reference on the Congress for Jewish Culture page suggests that a brother **Meyer-Dovid Alter** may have left an unpublished memoir, but the comment is tentative and not a catalogued finding.

### Marriage, child, and descendants

While studying in Belgium, Alter married **Mélanie/Melanie Lorraine**, a Belgian woman. She remained in Belgium, living in Brussels until her death in 1974. The couple had one known son:

- **Jean Wiktor Alter** (born Warsaw, 1926; died Boulder, Colorado, 2012).

A granddaughter, **Nora M. Alter**, became a scholar of film and media. These details appear in a family correction appended to the Congress for Jewish Culture entry rather than in its original 1956 text; they are plausible and specific, but the page does not reproduce the underlying civil documents.

The available public record does not establish whether Alter and Mélanie lived continuously together, maintained a transnational marriage, separated, or divorced. No other spouse, partner, or child is securely documented. No reliable evidence was found concerning his personal wealth, inherited property, estate, or provision for his family.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1890–1905

Alter's childhood began in Mława and continued in Warsaw after his father's death. He attended the private secular **Wojciech Górski Gymnasium** in Warsaw. The translated Yiddish biographical lexicon says that he was notably strong in mathematics and languages.

The Revolution of 1905 brought mass strikes, demonstrations, school boycotts, and demands for Polish-language education into Warsaw. At fifteen Alter participated in, and according to later biographies helped lead, a student strike. He was briefly arrested and expelled from the Górski school, losing the right to continue there. Sources differ on whether he joined the Bund in **1904** or **1905**. Both versions place his entry into underground socialism in mid-adolescence, during the revolutionary crisis.

The school strike is the earliest recurring episode in Alter's political biography. It became part of the later Bundist image of him: intellectually precocious, organizationally forceful, and willing to accept imprisonment from a young age. The broad event and expulsion are well attested in biographical sources; the exact scale of his leadership is preserved chiefly through later commemorative accounts.

## Education and Formation, 1906–1912

After expulsion, Alter went to Belgium. Accounts variously describe study at a polytechnic in **Liège**, the **University of Ghent**, or both. The date of graduation also varies:

- the Yiddish biographical lexicon says he completed study in **1910** as an **electromechanical engineer**;
- several Polish accounts give study from **1906 to 1911** and a mechanical-engineering diploma at Ghent;
- English summaries often give a Ghent mechanical-engineering degree in **1912**.

The most conservative reconstruction is that he studied engineering in Liège and/or Ghent between roughly 1906 and 1911, qualified as an electromechanical or mechanical engineer, and returned permanently to Warsaw in 1912. The consulted sources do not identify a thesis, professor, laboratory, or student register.

Belgium was also his political school. He participated in the **Belgian Socialist Party** and Bundist student circles and wrote for the French socialist press as **Vik Fort**. His training joined technical rationalism to socialist economic analysis. That combination remained visible in his later writing on planning, unemployment, cooperation, social organization, statistics, and—during prison—modern physics.

He married Mélanie Lorraine during or soon after this Belgian period.

## Early Career, Tsarist Arrest, Exile, and Britain, 1912–1917

### Return to Warsaw

Alter returned to Warsaw in 1912. He worked for a Jewish cultural organization identified in the Yiddish lexicon as **YIKO** while continuing illegal Bund work. In 1913 he represented the Bund on a joint electoral committee with the **Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland and Lithuania (SDKPiL)** and the left wing of the **Polish Socialist Party (PPS-Lewica)** in a campaign connected with workers' insurance.

### Arrest and Siberian exile

Tsarist authorities arrested him in April 1913. After several months in prison, he was administratively exiled to the **Narym region of Siberia**. He escaped, reached western Europe, and spent another short period in Belgium. The consulted sources do not fix the prison, route, date of escape, accomplices, or false papers used.

### Britain during the First World War

After war began in 1914, Alter went to England. He initially worked as a laborer and later as an engineer in a factory. Politically he worked among Yiddish-speaking Jewish laborers in London, participated in the socialist association and union **Veker**, and wrote for **Arbeter-Vort** (*Workers' Word*), the organ of the London Jewish socialist organization.

He was associated with the anti-militarist **British Socialist Party** and campaigned against conscription or for conscientious objection. Some short biographies say that he personally refused military service. The sources consulted establish his anti-conscription work more securely than the precise legal circumstances of any personal refusal. He is also sometimes said to have joined the Labour Party; this is repeated in reference biographies, although the more detailed Yiddish entry emphasizes socialist organizations and the British Socialist Party.

## Revolution and Civil War, 1917–1919

### Petrograd, Kyiv, and Minsk

After the February Revolution of 1917, Alter returned to the former Russian Empire. He stayed briefly in Petrograd, attended the Bund's Southern Conference in **Kyiv in August 1917**, and spoke for the Bund's **Internationalist** current. At the Bund's Eighth Congress in Petrograd in December 1917 he was elected to its Central Committee.

He moved to **Minsk**, worked at the Central Committee office, and wrote for the Bund daily **Der Veker** (*The Awakener*). When German forces approached Minsk in 1918, he moved to Moscow.

### Anti-Bolshevik workers' conference and Cheka arrest

In July 1918 Alter took part in a non-Bolshevik workers' conference in Moscow. The Cheka arrested him with other delegates; later Bundist sources say he faced possible execution and was freed after intervention by prominent Bolsheviks. The identities of those interveners are not consistently specified. The arrest marked his early direct collision with Bolshevik rule.

He remained in Moscow for a time, translating from English and French into Russian and writing for Bundist Yiddish publications. His essay **“Marks un di visnshaft fun geshikhte”** (*Marx and the Science of History*) appeared in the Bundist memorial anthology *Zum ondenk fun Karl Marks* in 1918 and subsequently as a pamphlet. In 1919 he returned to Warsaw and the newly independent Polish state.

## Chronological Life History in Interwar Poland, 1919–1939

### Building the Polish Bund

From 1919 Alter and Henryk Erlich sat on the Central Committee of the Polish Bund. Together with **Yekusiel/Noach Portnoy** and **Beynish Mikhalevitch**, they formed the party's principal leadership. A YIVO entry describes Portnoy, Erlich, and Alter as its “charismatic ruling triad.” Their functions overlapped but were distinguishable:

- Erlich was a lawyer, parliamentarian in style, editor, and international political spokesman;
- Alter concentrated heavily on economic policy, trade unions, cooperatives, municipal government, and theory;
- Portnoy was an organizer and party tactician.

Alter and Erlich became a paired international representation of the Polish Bund, especially after they joined the executive machinery of the **Labour and Socialist International** around 1930.

### The Comintern dispute and second Soviet arrest, 1921

The Polish Bund split over affiliation with the Communist International. In June 1921 Alter traveled clandestinely to Moscow as **“M. Lorman,”** one of three Warsaw delegates negotiating possible entry at the Comintern's Third Congress. A majority was prepared to accept most of the Twenty-One Conditions; the minority led by Portnoy, Mikhalevitch, Erlich, and Alter resisted full submission to Communist discipline.

On 5 August 1921 Soviet authorities arrested Alter. The stated pretext was that he had accepted a letter from a Left Socialist-Revolutionary describing Bolshevik terror, intending to convey it to the English Communist **Sylvia Pankhurst**; one later account mistakenly gives “Fannie” Pankhurst. Alter began a hunger strike lasting nine days and was released. He returned to Poland. Bundist histories treated the arrest as a demonstration of the political consequences of Comintern discipline and as a factor that weakened pro-Communist sentiment inside the party.

### Trade unions, cooperatives, and credit institutions

Alter became the Polish Bund's leading figure in labor economics and Jewish trade-union organization. From 1923 he served on the central trade-union commission and chaired the national council of Jewish unions. He attended union congresses and represented the Bund at international socialist meetings.

He also served as a director of the **Arbeter-Kredit-Tsentrale** (Workers' Credit Center) and sat on a “Joint Foundation” supporting Jewish credit institutions in Poland. These bodies formed part of an institutional network through which Jewish artisans, workers, cooperatives, and small producers obtained credit. Alter's role was managerial and political; the evidence found does not show that he personally owned these institutions, held equity in them, or accumulated a private financial interest.

His 1921 Yiddish pamphlet on the basic principles of proletarian cooperation placed cooperatives within a socialist transition rather than treating them simply as mutual-aid businesses. Later works proposed planning, full employment measures, democratized economic power, and coordination between socialist parties and unions.

### Warsaw municipal politics

Alter served on the **Warsaw City Council from 1919 to 1934**, with some accounts noting repeated elections rather than one uninterrupted term. From 1927 he also served in the city executive or magistracy. His municipal work placed Bundism inside the administration of housing, welfare, schools, labor conditions, public services, and minority rights in a city whose Jewish population exceeded 350,000 by the 1931 census.

In **1936**, after a strong Bund electoral advance, he became a member of the governing board of the **Warsaw Jewish Religious Community (kehilla)**. The office did not imply religious observance: the Bund contested communal elections in order to redirect funds and authority toward secular education, welfare, health, labor, and democratic representation. Some short biographies imprecisely call him “head” of the community; “board member” or elected executive member is safer.

### Journalism and publishing

Alter contributed continually to the Warsaw Bund daily **Folkstsaytung** and other Yiddish and Polish socialist periodicals. From 1935 to 1938 he published and edited **Myśl Socjalistyczna** (*Socialist Thought*). He wrote in an analytic, programmatic mode on political strategy, economic planning, unemployment, antisemitism, national autonomy, Palestine, colonialism, and the relation of individual freedom to social structure.

### Palestine journey and anti-Zionism

Alter traveled to Mandatory Palestine in **1924**; his report appeared in 1925 as **Der emes vegn Palestine** (*The Truth about Palestine*). It belongs to the Bund's anti-Zionist literature but is also travel reporting and political economy. Alter opposed the proposition that mass Jewish emigration and territorial state-building in Palestine could solve the condition of Jews in Poland. His positive alternative was Bundist *doikayt*—political action “here,” where Jews lived—combined with socialist transformation, equal citizenship, Yiddish culture, and national-cultural autonomy.

His critique was not simply indifference to Arab Palestinians. Surviving discussion of his writing presents him as attentive to the Arab population and to imperial and colonial power. In *Socjalizm walczący* he attacked colonial violence and the rhetoric of European “civilizing missions,” while arguing that socialists should support liberation without automatically endorsing every nationalist leadership. His terminology also carried racial hierarchies common in contemporary European socialist writing: he used words now recognized as derogatory and described places such as Morocco and Syria as being at a “lower cultural level.” Both the anti-colonial argument and the paternalist vocabulary belong to the record.

### International work and the United States, 1929

In 1929 Alter toured the United States on a Bund mission. Contemporary Jewish press notices advertised him as a Warsaw alderman and Bund leader; one announced a San Francisco address. The trip strengthened ties between the Polish Bund and American Jewish labor organizations. A complete itinerary and financial account were not found in the consulted online sources.

### Socialist International and united-front policy

Alter represented the Bund in the **Labour and Socialist International**, joining its executive in or around 1930. He opposed both fascism and the Communist International's organizational control. His position was not static anti-Communism. In 1935 he drafted a resolution for the organic unity of the socialist and Communist internationals and one workers' party in each country, and conducted unsuccessful talks with the Communist Party of Poland. This was a united-front strategy under the pressure of fascism, not acceptance of Soviet one-party rule.

His French works—*Comment réaliser le socialisme?* (1932) and *Esquisse d'un programme économique socialiste* (1934)—and the Polish *Gdy socjaliści dojdą do władzy…!* (1934) set out problems of transition and governance: what socialist parties should do after attaining office, how planning should be structured, and how democratic legitimacy could be preserved. He rejected a politics consisting only of denunciation; programs and institutions were necessary.

### Spanish Civil War, 1937

In 1937 Alter traveled to Republican Spain on a socialist solidarity and journalistic mission. He observed the war rather than serving as a combatant. With Austrian socialist and Republican military figure **Julius Deutsch**, he published **Hiszpania w ogniu: rok wojny domowej** (*Spain in Flames: A Year of Civil War*) in Warsaw in 1937. His support for the Republic fit the Bund's anti-fascist mobilization while remaining distinct from Communist control of much international antifascist activity.

### Antisemitism and the late 1930s

Alter used economic data to counter claims that Polish Jews were “unproductive” intermediaries or parasites. **Antysemityzm gospodarczy w świetle cyfr** (*Economic Antisemitism in the Light of Figures*, 1937) addressed boycotts, occupational restrictions, and arguments for removing Jews from the economy. A broader collective Polish volume, **O Żydach i antysemityzmie** (1936), joined socialist writers including Zygmunt Zaremba and Adam Próchnik.

The Bund's electoral and union strength increased in the late 1930s as antisemitic exclusion intensified. Alter's public authority consequently broadened, but the party remained outside national governing power. The central failure of his political program was structural rather than a documented personal scandal: democratic socialist, Jewish-autonomist politics did not prevent the destruction of Polish democracy, the German invasion, Soviet repression, or the Holocaust.

## War, Flight, and First NKVD Case, 1939–1941

### Warsaw, Lublin, Kowel

After Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, the Bund leadership advised prominent members to leave Warsaw so the party could retain leaders outside an expected occupation. Alter went east. On 9 September he learned of a Central Committee decision that leaders should return and help defend Warsaw. He and several Bundists attempted to turn back, detouring through **Lublin** because of military restrictions and the collapse of transport.

Under bombing, Alter issued a one-off Yiddish newspaper, **Lubliner Shtime** (*Voice of Lublin*), dated 14 September. As German forces approached, he continued east and reached **Kowel on 17 September**, the day the Red Army entered eastern Poland.

The NKVD arrested him in Kowel on **26 September 1939** according to the detailed Bundist biography and several Polish studies; YIVO's archival guide gives **29 September**. He was moved through prisons in Kowel and **Łuck/Lutsk**, then to Moscow, including the Lubyanka/Butyrka system. The arrest was not a rescue from German persecution followed by ordinary internment: it opened a political prosecution based on his Bund activity and alleged hostile networks.

### Interrogation and charges

The investigation lasted roughly twenty to twenty-two months. NKVD interrogators demanded an extensive organizational history of the Polish Bund and information about its members, finances, international connections, and relations with Polish authorities. Alter's and Erlich's prison materials later became important because they preserved a remarkable institutional history under coercive conditions.

The accusations shifted. Soviet authorities associated Alter with “anti-Soviet” and “counter-revolutionary” activity, the Polish political police or intelligence service, British/international bourgeois interests, and later Germany. There is no evidence in the opened case material substantiating espionage for Germany or an appeal for Red Army surrender. Later scholarship based on the files classifies the prosecution as political fabrication.

Bundist accounts say Alter conducted hunger strikes, endured severe interrogation, and refused to sign confessions. The broad record confirms prolonged coercive imprisonment; the exact number and duration of hunger strikes are less securely documented in accessible summaries.

### Sentence and commutation

On **20 July 1941**, after Germany had invaded the Soviet Union, Alter was sentenced to death. The sentence was commuted within days—sources give 30 July or within two days—to **ten years' imprisonment in a labor camp**. Erlich received a parallel death sentence on 2 August, commuted on 22 August.

The German invasion and the **Sikorski–Mayski agreement** of 30 July 1941 changed Soviet relations with Polish citizens. Alter was unexpectedly released on **13 or 14 September 1941**; Erlich had been released one or two days earlier. The releases were not exonerations. The NKVD retained control and soon recruited or induced the two men to undertake an international propaganda project.

## Release, Anti-Fascist Committee Project, and Polish Relations, September–December 1941

### Proposed international Jewish committee

Alter and Erlich proposed or accepted a Soviet proposal for a **world/international Jewish anti-fascist committee** that would mobilize Jews abroad—particularly in the United States and Britain—for support of the Soviet war effort. Their plan designated:

- Henryk Erlich as chairman;
- Wiktor Alter as secretary-general;
- actor and theater director **Solomon Mikhoels** as a third member of the presidium.

They corresponded with **Lavrentii Beria** and **Joseph Stalin**, drafted a program, and used their international socialist and Jewish-labor connections. After their re-arrest, the Soviet state created a differently controlled **Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee**, headed by Mikhoels and supervised through Sovinformburo and Solomon Lozovsky. Alter and Erlich were therefore initiators of an important concept, but they never headed the organization that formally emerged in 1942.

### Polish embassy and Anders Army

Alter and Erlich also worked closely with Polish ambassador **Stanisław Kot**. They served as intermediaries on welfare for Polish citizens released under the amnesty and supported recruitment of Polish Jews into the army being formed by General **Władysław Anders**.

A dispute survives in memoirs. Anders later implied that Alter and Erlich initially requested distinct Jewish units and accepted his integrationist policy only after discussion. Kot rejected that version, saying the two Bundists had opposed the Revisionist Zionist proposal for segregated Jewish units from the outset. A letter signed by Alter and Erlich on **31 October 1941** endorsed a unified Polish army based on equal treatment without distinction of nationality or religion, demanded suppression of antisemitism, and called on Jewish Polish citizens to enlist. The contemporary letter supports Kot more strongly than Anders on their formal position.

When Moscow and foreign embassies evacuated to **Kuibyshev** in October, the Polish embassy arranged Alter's and Erlich's evacuation. They lived with Bundist **Lucjan Blit** in one room of a hotel used by diplomats and correspondents, at Polish expense. Soviet security monitored or recorded their conversations. YIVO's guide says reports to Stalin included their discussion of rumors that Polish officers, including Polish Jews, had been murdered at Katyn. Whether this discussion directly caused the arrests is not established.

## Second Arrest, Imprisonment, and Death, 3 December 1941–17 February 1943

### Disappearance and arrest

On 3 December 1941 Alter and Erlich received a late-night summons from an NKVD contact. Erlich later wrote that they expected work on the anti-fascist committee to resume. After being made to wait for about twenty hours, they were told that Moscow had ordered their arrest. They were separated in the internal prison of the Kuibyshev oblast. The Polish account that they disappeared en route to a diplomatic restaurant describes how their absence was first perceived; the NKVD acknowledged custody only after inquiries.

No timely charge, open trial, defense, or public judgment followed. Erlich complained to the Presidium of the USSR Supreme Soviet on 27 December that he had received no explanation even orally.

### Diplomatic efforts

The Polish embassy protested immediately. Soviet replies asserted that the men had acted for Germany and later claimed them as Soviet citizens, returning their Polish passports on 28 January 1942 and telling the ambassador that further Polish inquiries were improper. Kot repeatedly requested their release and offered to take them out of the USSR. **Andrei Vyshinsky** reportedly repeated the German-spy allegation. International labor and socialist bodies protested. Appeals were made by, among others, **William Green** of the American Federation of Labor, **Eleanor Roosevelt**, **Albert Einstein**, and **Wendell Willkie**; the U.S. government indicated willingness to grant visas. Stalin did not release them.

### Erlich's death and Alter's isolation

The paired formula “Erlich and Alter were executed together” entered contemporary memory because their arrest, disappearance, Soviet announcement, and international case were joint. Post-Soviet internal records separate their deaths:

- **Henryk Erlich** hanged himself in his cell on the night of 14–15 May 1942, after more than five months in renewed solitary confinement and interrogation.
- **Wiktor Alter** remained imprisoned for another nine months and was shot on **17 February 1943**.

The record summarized by Rudnicki and Mochalova says Alter was executed **without a new sentence**. A YIVO guide mentions reports of a death decision dated 23 December 1941 and a Molotov note stating that Stalin personally approved execution; the later execution report fixes the actual shooting in February 1943. The available documents do not reveal why he was kept alive after Erlich's death, whether he was interrogated further, or why the execution occurred precisely then.

### Official announcement and its contradictions

The Soviet government concealed both men's fate throughout 1942. On 23 February 1943 ambassador **Maksim Litvinov** informed AFL president William Green that they had been tried a second time and executed in December 1942. The official allegation said that, after release, they had adopted a hostile position, appealed to Soviet troops to stop fighting and make peace with Germany, and acted as agents of Polish intelligence or Hitler.

The public account changed again: a later Litvinov communication moved the supposed joint execution to December 1941. The versions were internally incompatible. They also conflict with the security records showing Erlich alive until May 1942 and Alter alive until February 1943. The charge was especially implausible in light of the men's documented October 1941 call for Polish Jews to take up arms against Hitler and their work on an anti-Nazi mobilization committee. No trial record or independent evidence supporting the surrender/espionage allegation has emerged.

The Polish government protested on 8 March 1943. Its National Council in London adopted a resolution on 16 April after a motion by Bund representative **Szmul Zygielbojm**. American Jewish and non-Jewish trade-union leaders held protest meetings. The affair placed Allied labor organizations in conflict with a Soviet Union then essential to the war against Germany.

### Cause, manner, and place of death

- **Immediate cause:** gunshot execution.
- **Manner:** state killing by Soviet security personnel.
- **Date:** 17 February 1943, according to the execution report opened after the Soviet period.
- **Place:** Kuibyshev/Samara; the precise execution site is not established in the public sources consulted.
- **Body/burial:** location unknown.
- **Legal status:** the first death judgment had been commuted; the second detention did not yield a valid documented trial in the opened record; post-Soviet authorities rehabilitated him in 1991.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Economic Development

### Bundism and national-cultural autonomy

Alter's mature politics combined Marxist class analysis, parliamentary and municipal democracy, independent unions, cooperative institutions, Yiddish cultural autonomy, and equal citizenship in Poland. The Bund rejected both assimilation into a nationality-free abstraction and the Zionist territorial solution. Jewish workers constituted a national-cultural community entitled to schools, language, representation, and collective rights where they lived.

### Religion

He emerged from a Hasidic family into secular socialism. Some Polish retrospective writing describes him as hostile to Jewish tradition and religion. His public program was secular and he fought clerical control of communal institutions. The record consulted does not document atheistic polemics as a central personal obsession, private religious practice, or a formal declaration of unbelief. His service on the Warsaw kehilla board was political and administrative, not rabbinical.

### Marxism without Bolshevik dictatorship

Alter considered Marxism a method of historical and social analysis, not a warrant for a single-party security state. His 1918 Marx essay, later economic programs, and 1938 *Man in Society* joined structural analysis to a defense of human personality and democratic agency. His repeated negotiations with Communists—1918 worker politics, the 1921 Comintern mission, and 1935 unity talks—show that opposition to Bolshevik methods did not prevent tactical cooperation. His arrests in 1918, 1921, 1939, and 1941 made the conflict material rather than merely theoretical.

### Planning, cooperation, and unemployment

Alter's engineering formation shaped an institutional socialism concerned with mechanisms: credit, production, trade unions, cooperatives, employment, and a program for taking power. His socialism was neither private-capitalist ownership nor unchecked administrative command. The exact model requires study of the largely untranslated Polish, Yiddish, and French works; available descriptions emphasize planning combined with democratic organization and an insistence that indignation without policy is insufficient.

### Humanism and science

His final prewar book, *Der mentsh iz der gezelshaft / Człowiek w społeczeństwie* (*Man in Society*, 1938), treated the relation of individuality and social order. A sentence frequently quoted from it defines humanism as the negation of vulgarity, falsehood, human oppression, and propaganda of hatred. In Moscow prison in 1940–1941 he wrote **Di naye fizik** (*The New Physics*), an essay on modern physics later published posthumously. It is the clearest surviving expression of the continuity between his technical education and political intellectual life.

No evidence was found that Alter worked for an intelligence service. His extensive international contacts, Soviet surveillance, dealings with the Polish embassy, and proposed propaganda role made him an intelligence target, not a documented intelligence officer or agent.

## Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

- **General Jewish Labour Bund:** underground member from 1904/1905; Central Committee member from 1917 and of the Polish Bund from 1919; theorist, organizer, and international representative.
- **Jewish trade-union movement in Poland:** chairman of the national council of Jewish unions and central trade-union commission member from 1923.
- **Arbeter-Kredit-Tsentrale:** director. The institution coordinated workers' credit; no personal ownership interest is documented.
- **Joint Foundation for Jewish credit institutions:** board/member role; no evidence of private enrichment.
- **Warsaw municipal government:** councillor, 1919–1934; city executive/magistracy role from 1927.
- **Warsaw Jewish Religious Community:** elected board/executive member from 1936.
- **Labour and Socialist International:** Bund delegate and executive member from approximately 1930.
- **Folkstsaytung:** permanent contributor.
- **Myśl Socjalistyczna:** publisher and editor, 1935–1938.
- **Lubliner Shtime:** one-off wartime publisher/editor, 14 September 1939.
- **Proposed international Jewish anti-fascist committee:** intended secretary-general in 1941; never allowed to assume the role.

No company ownership, patents, inventions, securities, real-estate portfolio, or private business investment has been identified. His “financial interests” were institutional work in labor credit, cooperatives, and socialist economic policy. Claims of fraud, corruption, money laundering, or personal enrichment are absent from the record consulted.

## Arrests, Allegations, Political Violence, and Controversies

### Arrest record

1. **1905, Warsaw:** brief arrest associated with the school strike; later biography.
2. **1913, Russian Poland:** arrest for underground Bund activity; prison and exile to Narym, Siberia; escaped.
3. **July 1918, Moscow:** Cheka arrest at an anti-Bolshevik workers' conference; released after intervention.
4. **5 August 1921, Moscow:** arrested during Comintern negotiations over a letter describing Bolshevik terror; nine-day hunger strike; released.
5. **26 or 29 September 1939, Kowel:** NKVD arrest; prolonged interrogation; death sentence on 20 July 1941; commuted; released September 1941.
6. **3 December 1941, Kuibyshev:** NKVD re-arrest without disclosed cause; held until execution on 17 February 1943.

### Soviet accusations: status of evidence

| Accusation | Status | Evidence assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Anti-Soviet/counter-revolutionary Bund activity | **Documented political characterization** | Alter openly opposed Bolshevik dictatorship and Comintern control. This was political activity, not proof of espionage or sabotage. |
| Agent of Polish police/intelligence | **Official allegation** | No substantiating evidence identified; rejected by Polish government; nullified by rehabilitation. His contact with the Polish embassy was open political and relief work. |
| British/international bourgeois agent | **Interrogation/propaganda allegation** | Based on foreign socialist and labor connections; no operational evidence found. |
| German/Hitler agent | **Official Soviet accusation and propaganda** | Contradicted by his anti-fascist record, work for anti-Nazi mobilization, and call for enlistment against Germany; no supporting archival evidence emerged. |
| Called on Red Army soldiers to stop fighting and make peace with Germany | **Soviet public claim of 1943** | No corroborating text, broadcast, witness, or trial record; inconsistent with internal chronology and opened files. |
| Discussed Katyn rumors | **Reported surveillance content** | YIVO's case guide says monitored conversations included rumors of murdered Polish officers. The conversation is not evidence of a crime, and its causal role in his arrest remains hypothetical. |

### Political disagreements not reducible to crimes

- Alter's anti-Zionism produced sustained polemic with Zionist parties. It was a programmatic conflict over Jewish political geography, class, language, and nationhood.
- He opposed Comintern subordination but pursued united-front negotiation in 1935. Communist opponents portrayed Bund autonomy as counterrevolutionary; some non-Communist opponents saw his unity efforts as naïve.
- Anders and Kot left conflicting accounts about separate Jewish units in the Polish army. The signed 31 October letter documents Alter's support for one equal, non-segregated Polish army.
- His colonial writing combined direct condemnation of imperial violence with Eurocentric and racialized terms. Both are present in the text.

No credible record was found of private criminality, corruption, financial fraud, addiction, domestic abuse, or political violence personally ordered by Alter.

## Myths, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “Alter and Erlich were murdered together”

- **Story:** The two inseparable Bund leaders were jointly tried and shot after their December 1941 arrest.
- **Appearance and circulation:** Soviet notices in 1943, Bund protest literature, socialist press, memorial books, reference works, and the Warsaw cenotaph paired them.
- **Evidence:** They were arrested together and treated as one international case. Opened Soviet records report Erlich's suicide in May 1942 and Alter's shooting in February 1943.
- **Assessment:** True as a political and commemorative pairing; false if understood as the same immediate death event.
- **Effect:** The dyad “Erlich and Alter” became a compact symbol of Stalin's destruction of independent Jewish socialism.

### 2. The December 1941 execution date

- **Story:** Both were shot almost immediately after re-arrest.
- **Origin:** A later Soviet “correction” to Litvinov's first date and early memorial sources working without access to files.
- **Evidence against:** Internal prison records place Erlich's death in May 1942 and Alter's execution on 17 February 1943.
- **Effect:** The false date entered catalogs, memorial books, and older biographies; some library records still list 1941 death dates.

### 3. The December 1942 trial and execution

- **Story:** A Soviet supreme tribunal retried them in December 1942 and executed both.
- **Origin:** Litvinov's 23 February 1943 letter to William Green.
- **Evidence against:** Erlich was already dead by May 1942; Alter's execution report is dated February 1943; later file research found no second valid sentence.
- **Effect:** It allowed the USSR to present an extrajudicial disappearance as regular wartime justice.

### 4. “Hitler spies” who urged surrender

- **Story:** After Soviet leniency, Alter and Erlich betrayed the USSR by calling on soldiers to cease resistance and make peace with Hitler.
- **Circulators:** Soviet diplomats and aligned press/organizations in 1943.
- **Evidence against:** Their documented activity was to organize Jewish support against Hitler and recruit Jews for armed service; no incriminating appeal has surfaced. The 1991 rehabilitation declared the repression unlawful.
- **Effect:** The accusation divided wartime left-wing opinion, enabling Soviet allies to defend or avoid the killings while labor and Bund organizations protested.

### 5. Katyn as the decisive cause

- **Story:** Stalin ordered their destruction because surveillance caught them discussing Soviet murder of Polish officers at Katyn.
- **Origin:** Later interpretation of the monitored Kuibyshev conversations and the parallel Polish diplomatic crisis.
- **Evidence:** Discussion of rumors is reported; a direct order tying it to the arrest or execution is not available.
- **Assessment:** Possible contributing factor, not an established motive.
- **Effect:** It connects the Erlich–Alter affair to Soviet suppression of information about Katyn and has strengthened the Polish national reading of the case.

### 6. Beria's “mistake” and Stalin's personal rage

- **Story:** Beria proposed Erlich and Alter for anti-fascist work without realizing that they had criticized Stalin; Stalin saw their names, became enraged, and ordered arrest.
- **Origin:** Memoirs of Beria's son Sergo, quoted in later historical work.
- **Evidence:** Retrospective familial testimony, compatible with the political sequence but not independently decisive. The documentary record gives no explicit rationale for the reversal.
- **Effect:** Personalizes a decision that may also have reflected institutional preference for Soviet-controlled, Soviet-citizen propagandists and hostility to independent foreign socialists.

### 7. Sikorski abandoned them

- **Story:** Polish prime minister Władysław Sikorski failed to intervene personally and decisively.
- **Origin:** Postwar historian Władysław Pobóg-Malinowski and related controversy.
- **Evidence:** Kot's embassy sent repeated notes and made personal requests; the question is whether Sikorski could or should have elevated the case further amid larger Polish-Soviet disputes.
- **Assessment:** A judgment about adequacy of diplomacy, not proof of collusion in their deaths.

### 8. Zygielbojm was killed because he investigated their case

- **Story:** Szmul Zygielbojm's May 1943 suicide in London was staged by the NKVD, possibly by poisoning, because he was collecting Erlich–Alter material and planned American lectures.
- **Origin:** Wartime/postwar rumor noted in Polish discussion of the case.
- **Evidence against:** Zygielbojm left a documented suicide letter explaining his act as protest against Allied inaction during the annihilation of Polish Jewry. No credible forensic or operational evidence for an NKVD killing was found.
- **Effect:** The rumor joined two Bundist martyr narratives and expressed distrust created by Soviet concealment. It remains unverified and should not displace the documented suicide.

## Rehabilitation and Posthumous Reputation

### International protest, 1943

The announcement of the deaths produced protests among the American Federation of Labor, Jewish labor organizations, non-Communist socialists, the Polish government-in-exile, and Bund branches. Soviet-aligned organizations either defended the espionage story, minimized the affair, or treated protest as harmful to the wartime alliance. This political division shaped the initial memory: Alter was either a socialist martyr or an alleged traitor, depending on institutional allegiance.

### Suppression in Communist Poland

Postwar Polish Communist censorship largely blocked public examination of the affair. Alter's Bundist anti-Bolshevism, Polish civic identification, and killing by the NKVD were all inconvenient to the official account of Polish-Soviet fraternity. The Bund itself was absorbed or dissolved in Communist Poland in 1948–1949, weakening institutional transmission of his work.

### Warsaw cenotaph, 1988

On **17 April 1988**, a symbolic gravestone for Erlich and Alter was unveiled at Warsaw's Jewish cemetery on Okopowa Street. **Marek Edelman**, surviving Bundist commander of the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising and a Solidarity-associated dissident, was central to the project. More than three thousand people reportedly attended. The monument identifies them as leaders of the Bund executed in the Soviet Union. It is a cenotaph, not a grave.

The monument's pairing consolidates the historically powerful “Erlich and Alter” memory while necessarily omitting the distinct mechanisms and dates of death later established from Soviet records.

### Rehabilitation, 1991

On **8 February 1991**, Soviet/Russian prosecutorial authorities informed Henryk Erlich's son Victor that Erlich and Alter had been rehabilitated and that the repression against them was unlawful. The timing is sometimes loosely described as “under Boris Yeltsin”; in February 1991 the Soviet Union still existed and Yeltsin was chairman of the Russian Supreme Soviet, not yet president of an independent Russian Federation. The legal action nonetheless belonged to the late-Soviet/early-Russian reassessment of Stalinist cases.

Rehabilitation is not by itself proof of every biographical claim made by defenders. Its precise significance is that the Soviet criminal case and repression were officially nullified; combined with the opened file, it removes any evidentiary basis for treating the Nazi-agent charge as established.

### Current legacy

Alter survives in several overlapping historical fields:

- as a central leader of the largest Jewish socialist movement in interwar Poland;
- as a theorist of democratic socialism, labor organization, cooperation, economic planning, and Jewish national-cultural autonomy;
- as an important anti-Zionist observer of Mandatory Palestine;
- as an anti-colonial socialist whose language also records contemporary European racial hierarchies;
- as a Warsaw municipal and Jewish communal official;
- as an early architect of the idea later embodied, under different leadership and Soviet control, in the Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee;
- as a victim of successive Tsarist and Soviet political repression;
- as half of the commemorative pair “Erlich and Alter,” used to represent Stalin's destruction of independent Jewish socialism.

Recent digitization and translation have begun to shift attention from the manner of his death back toward his own texts. Most of those texts remain unavailable in English. His intellectual corpus is therefore cited more often than it is closely read, and any definitive account of his political thought awaits a full multilingual edition.

## Atlas Connections

### Henryk Erlich

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Alter and Erlich were longtime colleagues in the Polish Bund’s leadership from 1919, jointly represented it internationally, prepared and signed political documents together, and collaborated in Moscow and Kuibyshev during 1941 on plans for an international Jewish anti-fascist committee. They shared accommodation in Kuibyshev before being summoned and rearrested together on 3–4 December 1941. This was a sustained personal and working relationship, not merely organizational proximity. [Contemporary Bund case documentation](https://www.bibliotecaginobianco.it/fliplibri/ERLALT/); [Polish diplomatic note, 8 December 1941](https://digit.pism.pl/Content/1542/Confronting%20the%20Holocaust.pdf)

### Karl Marx

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Alter was the later writer in this pair. His Yiddish essay **“Marks un di visnshaft fun geshikhte”** (*Marx and the Science of History*), published in Moscow in 1918 and subsequently as a pamphlet, explicitly interpreted Marx’s ideas and historical method. This is a direct authored engagement with the earlier figure, rather than a connection inferred merely from Alter’s Marxism. [Victor Alter biographical entry](https://congressforjewishculture.org/people/6923/)

### Albert Einstein

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Einstein participated in a one-sided written intervention concerning Alter while Alter was imprisoned. He joined William Green, Philip Murray, and others in a telegram dated 29 January 1943 urging Soviet authorities to release Alter and Erlich. In June 1943 Einstein also answered an inquiry about the case in writing, stating that he had never accepted their alleged guilt. No personal meeting or correspondence directly between Alter and Einstein is documented. [Contemporary account of Einstein’s statement](https://www.marxists.org/subject/jewish/ghetto-speaks/ghetto-speaks-15-sept-1-43.pdf); [1943 Congressional Record summary](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1943-pt5/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1943-pt5-3-1.pdf)

### Vladimir Lenin

- **[T4] Documented fact:** Alter, traveling under the name **M. Lorman**, was present in Moscow in June–July 1921 for negotiations associated with the Third Congress of the Communist International; Lenin participated in and addressed that congress between 22 June and 12 July. This establishes a narrowly defined shared political venue and period, but the available evidence does not establish a personal meeting, conversation, or correspondence between them. [Lenin’s Third Congress materials](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1921/jun/12.htm)

### Leon Trotsky

- **[T4] Documented fact:** Alter’s 1921 Comintern mission placed him at the Third Congress in Moscow while Trotsky was delivering congress reports and speeches, including his 23 June address on the world economic crisis. The record supports shared presence in the same congress milieu, but no direct Alter–Trotsky exchange or personal meeting has been located. [Third Congress proceedings](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/comintern/3rd-congress/); [congress chronology and Trotsky speech listing](https://www.marxists.org/francais/inter_com/1921/ic3.htm)

No additional roster connection was included merely because another person was a contemporary socialist, Bundist, Zionist, scientist, or participant in the same broad historical setting; such affiliations alone do not document a crossing.
