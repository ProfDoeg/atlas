# Johannes Itten: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

Johannes Itten left a large artistic, pedagogical, and documentary record, but no single freely accessible source reconstructs every part of his life. The principal documentary bases for this dossier are:

- the Johannes-Itten archive at the Zentralbibliothek Zürich;
- the Johannes-Itten-Stiftung and Kunstmuseum Bern;
- the Bauhaus Kooperation and Bauhaus-Archiv;
- the modern three-volume catalogue raisonné project directed by Christoph Wagner;
- contemporary and later museum catalogues;
- German records concerning “degenerate art” confiscations;
- bibliographic and authority databases.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact**: supported by institutional records, archival descriptions, contemporary documents, or multiple strong sources.
- **Reported fact**: published by a credible secondary source but not independently verified here in a primary document.
- **Disputed interpretation**: the underlying events are documented, but their significance or motivation is contested.
- **Allegation or theory**: a claim circulated by identified people or publications without sufficient proof.
- **Rumor or myth**: a reputational story, simplification, or symbolic construction not demonstrated as literal fact.
- **Unknown**: the consulted record does not establish the point.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Johannes Itten.

**Recorded alternative forms:** Johann Itten; Giochannes Itten; Iohannes/Iochannes Itten; Japanese bibliographic form イッテン, ヨハネス. These are cataloguing or transliteration variants rather than pseudonyms.

**Religious or community name:** No securely documented formal religious name has been found. Itten sometimes adopted the appearance and authority of a Mazdaznan teacher, leading contemporaries and later writers to describe him as a “master,” “monk,” or “guru”; these were reputational descriptions, not proven legal titles.

**Nationality:** Swiss.

**Home municipality/place of origin:** Spiez, canton of Bern, according to the Swiss rural-history authority record.

**Occupations:** Primary- and secondary-school teacher; painter; draughtsman; printmaker; sculptor; designer; textile designer; interior designer; art theorist; color theorist; art educator; private-school proprietor/director; museum director; exhibition organizer.

**Birth:** 11 November 1888, Süderen-Linden—also rendered Südern-Linden, Süderenlinden, or Südern-Lindern—near Thun in the Bernese Oberland, Switzerland.

**Death:** 25 March 1967, Zürich, Switzerland.

**Burial:** Friedhof Hönggerberg, Zürich.

**Cause of death:** The consulted institutional biographies state the date and place but do not supply a medically documented cause. It should therefore be recorded as **unknown in the sources consulted**, not inferred.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and social background

**Documented fact:** Itten was the son of Johannes or Johann Itten, a schoolteacher who also worked a small farm, and Elise Itten. The German National Library authority file records the father as Johann Itten and names several siblings.

The family occupied a rural Bernese setting. Later biographies commonly describe Itten as the son of a teacher and mountain farmer or peasant farmer. This dual description is not necessarily contradictory: village teachers in the region could combine teaching with agricultural work.

**Reported fact:** His father died when Johannes was approximately four years old. A brother died about three years later. His mother remarried, and Itten later recalled or was reported to have experienced an exacting rural childhood under a strict stepfather. At about ten, he went to live with an uncle in Thun.

These early losses are repeatedly invoked by later interpreters to explain his discipline, austerity, spiritual searching, and concern with education. The losses themselves are reported biographical facts; the psychological conclusions are retrospective interpretations.

### Siblings

The German National Library authority record names:

- Rudolf Itten, brother;
- Gottfried Itten, brother;
- Maria Elisa Burgener-Itten, sister.

The consulted open records do not establish their complete dates or biographies. Because one brother reportedly died during Johannes’s childhood, it is unclear from these records which named brother that statement concerns.

### First marriage

**Wife:** Hildegard Itten, born Hildegard Anbelang, known as “Daniela” (1894–1952).

**Marriage:** 10 September 1919.

**End of marriage:** 18 March 1938, apparently by divorce or formal dissolution; the concise Bauhaus family record supplies the date but not the legal circumstances.

**Child:**

- Johannes Matthias Itten, generally called Matthias or Matthis, born 12 June 1920.

Itten painted the well-known *Kinderbild* in connection with Matthias’s birth. Correspondence also reportedly referred to the boy affectionately as “Büali.”

Hildegard became deeply involved in Mazdaznan. A specialist chronology reports her formal reception into the third degree of the Mazdaznan-Bund on 3 January 1922. Her religious commitment appears to have been part of the family’s move to Herrliberg.

### Second marriage

**Wife:** Anneliese Itten, born Anneliese Schlösser (1913–2002).

**Marriage:** 22 April 1939.

**Children:**

- Marion Itten, later Marion Lichardus-Itten, born June 1941 in Zürich; prehistoric archaeologist and later president of the Johannes-Itten-Stiftung.
- Nikolaus Immanuel “Klaus” Itten, born 7 February 1944; died 26 February 2023; geographer and remote-sensing scholar at the University of Zürich; also served as president of the foundation.
- Thomas Itten, born May 1946.

Some short biographies say Itten had “three children,” omitting Matthias from the first marriage. The Bauhaus family record documents four children in total.

### Estate and descendants

Anneliese Itten founded the Johannes-Itten-Stiftung in 1992. The foundation is associated with the Kunstmuseum Bern and preserves, studies, and publishes Itten’s work. Marion Lichardus-Itten and Klaus Itten both served as presidents of its governing board. Thomas Itten has supplied information to researchers, including material concerning Matthias and the *Kinderbild*.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1888–1904

Itten was born into a rural, German-speaking Swiss environment. The combination of village schooling, farm labor, death in the immediate family, and relocation to an uncle’s household formed the backdrop to his later educational career.

He began drawing and painting seriously in adolescence, reportedly around age sixteen. His early decision, however, was not to enter a purely artistic profession. Teaching offered a recognized career and remained central to his identity even after he became a professional artist.

No reliable evidence found in the consulted record supports later romanticized descriptions of him as a naturally ordained mystic or artistic prodigy. Those are retrospective constructions based on his later appearance, pedagogy, and religious commitments.

---

## Education and Formation, 1904–1916

### Hofwil teacher training, 1904–1906

From 1904 to 1906 Itten attended the teacher-training seminary at Hofwil near Bern.

A significant influence there was the young director **Ernst Schneider**, associated with progressive or reform pedagogy. Hofwil exposed Itten to child-centered and activity-based education. Later accounts also connect his early teaching with methods descended from Friedrich Fröbel’s educational system.

### Early schoolteaching, 1908–1909

Itten worked briefly as a primary-school teacher, commonly identified as teaching in Schwarzenburg or another Bernese village. The record is consistent on the period, approximately 1908–1909, although short biographies vary on the precise locality and length of service.

His later Bauhaus teaching retained features of elementary education: tactile exploration, play with basic forms, staged exercises, observation, repetition, and the conviction that latent capacities could be awakened rather than merely filled with academic information.

### Geneva, 1909–1910

Itten enrolled at the École des Beaux-Arts in Geneva. He found the conventional academic curriculum unsatisfactory and returned to Bern.

### Bern scientific training, approximately 1910–1912

He undertook further study in mathematics and the natural sciences—physics, mathematics, and chemistry—and qualified to teach at secondary level.

The scientific education mattered to his later color teaching. His theories were not modern experimental color science in a strict sense, but they consistently attempted to combine systematic diagrams, perceptual observation, measurement, and subjective experience.

### Return to Geneva, winter 1912–1913

Itten again attended the Geneva art school and took a course with **Eugène Gilliard**. Gilliard’s teaching emphasized elementary geometric forms, compositional structure, and contrasts. Itten used Gilliard’s exercises when organizing his first private art school.

### Stuttgart Academy and Adolf Hölzel, 1913–1916

In 1913 Itten moved to Stuttgart to study with **Adolf Hölzel**, entering Hölzel’s advanced or master-student circle.

Hölzel was decisive. Itten absorbed and transformed:

- a general theory of contrasts;
- systematic color contrasts;
- formal analysis of old-master paintings;
- abstraction from observed objects;
- collage and material montage;
- automatic or spontaneous drawing;
- rhythmic and bodily preparation for artistic work.

Itten’s Stuttgart associates included **Ida Kerkovius**, **Oskar Schlemmer**, **Willi Baumeister**, and **Hermann Stenner**.

In 1914, a work by Itten appeared in the “Expressionists’ Room” organized by Hölzel for the Stuttgart exhibition of the Verband der Kunstfreunde in den Ländern am Rhein.

The First World War made Itten’s Swiss nationality practically important: he was not a German combatant. His wartime art passed through Cubist, Futurist, Orphist, and expressionist languages while becoming increasingly abstract.

### First major exhibition

In 1916 Herwarth Walden presented Itten’s first one-person exhibition at the Galerie Der Sturm in Berlin. This placed him within one of the principal European networks for expressionist and avant-garde art.

---

## Vienna: Private School and Cultural Networks, 1916–1919

Itten moved to Vienna in 1916 and established a private art school, partly as a means of livelihood.

The school developed methods later formalized at the Bauhaus:

- preparatory breathing, relaxation, and gymnastic exercises;
- studies of rhythm and movement;
- elementary contrasts of line, form, texture, light, and color;
- material studies;
- analysis of canonical works;
- encouragement of individual expressive character.

The Vienna circle reportedly included or brought him into contact with **Alma Mahler**, **Adolf Loos**, and other modernist cultural figures. Through this environment he met **Walter Gropius**. Several Vienna pupils subsequently followed Itten to Weimar, giving him a ready-made circle at the early Bauhaus.

Itten married Hildegard Anbelang in September 1919, shortly before or around the beginning of the Bauhaus period.

---

## The Bauhaus, Weimar, 1919–1923

### Appointment

Walter Gropius appointed Itten in 1919 as one of the first masters of the Staatliches Bauhaus Weimar, alongside Lyonel Feininger and Gerhard Marcks.

Itten served as:

- a teaching master;
- form master for several workshops;
- organizer and principal teacher of the preliminary course;
- temporarily an instructor connected with mural and glass painting;
- deputy director, according to the Bauhaus Kooperation’s biographical record.

### Creation of the Vorkurs

Itten’s most consequential institutional achievement was the preliminary course, or **Vorkurs**. It became compulsory preparation before students entered specialized workshops.

Its purposes included:

1. freeing students from inherited academic habits;
2. identifying individual strengths;
3. testing responses to materials;
4. teaching basic relationships of form, proportion, rhythm, texture, light, dark, and color;
5. preparing students for workshop decisions;
6. integrating physical, sensory, emotional, and intellectual training.

Exercises included:

- charcoal and ink studies;
- light-dark scales;
- texture inventories;
- assemblages of contrasting materials;
- rhythmic drawing;
- abstract composition;
- old-master analyses;
- color scales and complementary relationships;
- studies in warm/cool, light/dark, saturation, extension, and simultaneous contrast.

Itten worked with **Gertrud Grunow**, whose “harmonization theory” linked sound, color, bodily movement, and psychological state. He invited her to teach at the Bauhaus.

### Workshop influence

Although remembered chiefly for the Vorkurs, Itten was involved with the school’s workshops and early expressionist program. His exercises influenced weaving, stained glass, metalwork, wall painting, book design, and stage experimentation.

His students or people who passed through his course included figures later central to modern art and design, among them Josef Albers and Anni Albers. **Gunta Stölzl**, who had studied with Itten in Vienna and at the Bauhaus, became a major collaborator in textile work and later helped establish the Ontos workshops.

### Artistic and theoretical work

Important Bauhaus-period works include:

- *Turm des Feuers* (“Tower of Fire”), 1920, an assemblage later reconstructed;
- *Haus des weißen Mannes* (“House of the White Man”), 1920, published in the first Bauhaus masters’ print portfolio in 1921;
- *Spruch* (“Saying”), 1921;
- *Farbenkugel in 7 Lichtstufen und 12 Tönen* (“Color Sphere in Seven Light Values and Twelve Tones”), 1921;
- the essay or teaching material *Analysen alter Meister*, published in *Utopia: Dokumente der Wirklichkeit* in 1921;
- *Kinderbild*, made in connection with Matthias’s birth in 1920.

### Mazdaznan at the Bauhaus

Itten had encountered Mazdaznan circles in Bern by about 1912. Mazdaznan was a modern syncretic religious and life-reform movement associated with Otoman Zar-Adusht Ha’nish. It combined purported Zoroastrian teachings with Christian, Hindu, yogic, dietary, breathing, eugenic, and hygienic elements.

By the Bauhaus years Itten practiced:

- vegetarianism;
- fasting or dietary regulation;
- breathing exercises;
- meditation;
- bodily purification;
- shaved or closely cropped hair;
- a high-collared robe or specially designed clothing.

He incorporated some of these practices into teaching. Students might undertake breathing, relaxation, gymnastics, or concentration before drawing. Some students adopted Mazdaznan practices; **Georg Muche** was among the best-known adherents.

Itten’s print *Haus des weißen Mannes* carried a saying attributed to Ha’nish greeting hearts illuminated by love and undirected by hope of heaven or fear of hell.

### Authority and conflict

**Documented fact:** Itten inspired intense loyalty among part of the student body.

**Documented fact:** His religious-pedagogical authority and the presence of a Mazdaznan circle caused friction within the school.

**Disputed interpretation:** Some accounts call his group a “cult within the Bauhaus” and characterize Itten as an authoritarian sect leader. Other accounts emphasize voluntary life-reform experimentation and the pedagogical use of concentration and bodily preparation. The evidence supports a strong charismatic hierarchy and religious recruitment or influence, but not every student participated, and “cult” is an interpretive label rather than a contemporaneous legal finding.

### Conflict with Gropius and departure

The larger dispute concerned the Bauhaus’s future.

Itten stressed:

- personal development;
- intuition;
- spiritual and artistic self-discovery;
- autonomous work;
- craft as a site of inner formation.

Gropius increasingly stressed:

- commissions;
- economically viable products;
- standardization;
- cooperation with industry;
- a new unity of art and technology.

The conflict intensified around production and the institution’s practical direction. Itten left at the end of 1922 or formally in 1923. The apparent date discrepancy reflects the difference between cessation of work and formal institutional chronology.

He was succeeded in the preliminary course by **László Moholy-Nagy**, working alongside **Josef Albers**. Itten’s departure is conventionally treated as marking the end of the Bauhaus’s most expressionist and mystical phase.

It is reductive, however, to describe the rupture as religion alone. Curriculum, institutional power, production policy, economics, and Gropius’s strategy for the school all contributed.

## Herrliberg and the Mazdaznan Community, 1923–1925

By 9 February 1923 Itten and his family were officially resident in the international Mazdaznan temple community at Herrliberg on Lake Zürich.

### Community structure

The European community had been led by **David Ammann**, described as an ambassador or senior representative of the movement. Ammann died on 20 February 1923. Itten led his funeral ceremony on 27 February, indicating a recognized religious standing within the community.

The settlement later operated economically under the name **Aryana-Gesellschaft** or Aryana association. The name derived from the movement’s mythic conception of an ancient Aryan spiritual homeland.

### Ontos school and workshops

Itten founded:

- the **Ontos-Kunstschule**, teaching nature study, composition, form and color, and graphic art;
- the **Ontos-Werkstätten**, producing handwoven goods, Smyrna-style knotted carpets, and tapestries.

Gunta Stölzl assisted the textile undertaking. The operation represented both an educational project and a small productive enterprise.

The workshops appear to have closed by April 1925. Sources report increasing conflict over the community’s direction. Itten left Herrliberg for Berlin in October 1925.

### Financial record

No reliable account books, ownership shares, investment amounts, property valuations, or personal income figures were located in the consulted online sources. It cannot be established from this evidence whether Itten personally owned workshop capital, held shares in Aryana, or primarily served as teacher and artistic director.

---

## Berlin: The Itten School, 1925/1926–1934

Itten established a private school in Berlin, variously called the **Moderne Kunstschule Berlin** and later the **Itten-Schule**.

Recorded locations include:

- Nollendorfplatz 1 from September 1926;
- Potsdamer Straße 75 from October 1927;
- later Konstanzer Straße 14, Berlin-Wilmersdorf.

The school reportedly became successful and attracted students preparing for art, design, architecture, photography, and textile careers.

### Faculty and collaborators

Teachers associated with the school included:

- Max Debus;
- Fred Forbát;
- Friedrich Köhn;
- Lucia Moholy;
- Georg Muche;
- Julius Pap;
- Max Bronstein;
- Umbo, the photographer Otto Umbehr;
- Ernst Neufert, approximately 1932–1934.

The faculty linked the Itten school to Bauhaus, architectural, photographic, and modern-design networks without making it a formal continuation of the Bauhaus.

### Curriculum

The school continued the Vorkurs tradition while offering more vocational instruction. Subjects included:

- elementary form and color;
- drawing and painting;
- architecture;
- photography;
- advertising and graphic design;
- textile design;
- material and spatial studies.

### Commercial design

In 1928 Itten designed the shop and interior for the **Erich Hamann chocolate factory** at Brandenburgische Straße 17 in Berlin-Wilmersdorf. The surviving interior is regularly cited as an important example of modern retail design.

This is a documented commercial commission, but the amount paid and contractual structure are not supplied by the consulted sources.

### Stettin and Das Neue Pommern

Itten also taught as a guest at the Kunstgewerbeschule or Werkschule für gestaltende Arbeit in Stettin. With its director, **Gregor Rosenbauer**, he helped initiate the artists’ association **Das Neue Pommern**, active approximately 1930–1933.

### Closure under National Socialism

The Nazi authorities closed the Berlin school in 1934. The exact bureaucratic sequence and stated grounds should be reconstructed from school and government files; the accessible summaries agree that the closure was compelled, not voluntary.

---

## Krefeld Textile School, 1931/1932–1937/1938

In 1931 the Krefeld velvet and silk industry appointed Itten to direct a new institution for textile surface design, the **Höhere Fachschule für textile Flächenkunst**. Classes began in January 1932 in premises connected with the weaving school.

Itten continued operating his Berlin school during the first part of this appointment.

### Program

The Krefeld school sought to connect:

- elementary visual training;
- color theory;
- weaving and textile structure;
- pattern and surface design;
- modern industrial production;
- the needs of the local silk and velvet industries.

The appointment demonstrates that industrial patrons considered Itten’s pedagogy practically useful despite its earlier association with expressionism and spirituality.

### Nazi-period position

Itten was Swiss, a modernist, a former Bauhaus master, and not known to have been a member of the Nazi Party.

**Documented fact:** His Berlin school was forcibly closed in 1934.

**Documented fact:** He remained in the Krefeld post for several more years, then was dismissed or compelled to resign in 1937; some institutional chronologies carry the directorship through 1938, probably reflecting the formal end of the school year or administrative tenure.

**Documented fact:** In 1937 Nazi authorities confiscated works by Itten from German public collections as “degenerate art.” The standard history of the exhibition identifies nine confiscated works and reports that two lithographs appeared in the traveling or Munich exhibition.

**Unresolved question:** Why he retained his Krefeld post until 1937 despite being a foreign modernist is not fully explained by the available sources. Industrial sponsorship, administrative delay, his specialized expertise, and efforts at accommodation are possibilities, but should not be presented as proven without personnel files.

### Attempts at accommodation

Research by Ulrich Linse reports that Itten attempted in 1934 to defend or reframe the Mazdaznan teaching he represented in terms that might be acceptable under the new regime.

This evidence is relevant to later accusations that Itten or Mazdaznan was ideologically close to National Socialism. It establishes attempted self-preservation or accommodation; it does **not by itself establish Nazi Party membership or systematic political collaboration**.

---

## Netherlands and Return to Switzerland, 1937–1938

After losing the Krefeld position, Itten went to the Netherlands. Some accounts state that he contemplated or prepared emigration to the United States.

Before that move occurred, he received an appointment in Zürich. In December 1938 he became director of the **Kunstgewerbeschule Zürich** and the associated **Kunstgewerbemuseum**.

He thereby returned permanently to Switzerland.

---

## Zürich Institutional Career, 1938–1956

### Kunstgewerbeschule and Kunstgewerbemuseum

Itten directed the Zürich School and Museum of Applied Arts from December 1938 until April 1954, though abbreviated biographies sometimes give 1938–1953.

His work included:

- curriculum reform;
- teacher appointments;
- exhibitions;
- international exchange;
- integration of fine art, craft, design, photography, and textiles;
- preservation and reinterpretation of modernist pedagogical methods after the Bauhaus’s closure.

The Zürich appointment gave Itten greater institutional power than he had held since Weimar.

### Zürich Textile School

From 1943 he also directed the **Textilfachschule Zürich**. Sources differ on the terminal date, with some giving 1960. The consulted institutional biography does not settle every administrative detail.

### Exhibition activity and non-European art

Itten became increasingly involved with Asian, African, Islamic, and other non-European art as an administrator and exhibition organizer.

In 1949 he was commissioned to help establish Zürich’s museum for non-European art. The resulting **Museum Rietberg** opened in the early 1950s, and Itten served as director from 1952 to 1956.

His role included transforming the collection in the Villa Wesendonck into a public museum and framing non-European objects as art rather than solely ethnographic specimens.

Current research at the Zürich University of the Arts and Zentralbibliothek Zürich examines Itten’s exhibition practice within colonial and national constructions of “self” and “other.” That is a contemporary research program, not a completed finding that every exhibition can be reduced to a single colonial ideology.

### Teaching at Ulm

In August 1953 Itten taught the first group of twenty-one students at the newly founded **Hochschule für Gestaltung Ulm**. Other summaries date his color courses to 1955. Both may be correct if he participated in the opening phase and returned for later teaching.

The Ulm school inherited parts of Bauhaus pedagogy but increasingly emphasized scientific, systems-based, and industrial design. Itten’s presence linked the new institution directly to the Weimar preliminary-course tradition.

### Retirement

Itten left the Zürich School of Applied Arts in 1954. From approximately 1955 he concentrated on independent painting, writing, lectures, exhibitions, and color teaching.

## Later Artistic and Intellectual Work, 1954–1967

Itten’s later paintings combined geometric construction with a continued interest in subjective experience, cosmic order, rhythm, and color.

He moved repeatedly between:

- figurative and nonfigurative painting;
- expressionist gesture and geometric organization;
- empirical observation and symbolic cosmology;
- individual color preference and claims about objective color relations.

His late compositions sometimes resemble or anticipate aspects of concrete art and Op art, although he should not simply be classified as a member of either movement.

### *Kunst der Farbe*, 1961

Itten’s principal theoretical book, *Kunst der Farbe: Subjektives Erleben und objektives Erkennen als Wege zur Kunst*, appeared through Otto Maier in Ravensburg in 1961.

Its central teaching structure was the theory of seven color contrasts:

1. contrast of hue;
2. light-dark contrast;
3. cold-warm contrast;
4. complementary contrast;
5. simultaneous contrast;
6. contrast of saturation or quality;
7. contrast of extension or quantity.

The system developed Hölzel’s contrast teaching and incorporated earlier color traditions, including Goethe and simultaneous-contrast theory.

Itten also argued that people possessed individual “subjective color” dispositions. Exercises could reveal a student’s habitual palette, temperament, and expressive orientation.

### Criticism of the color theory

Later color scientists and educators have criticized Itten for:

- mixing perceptual, material, symbolic, and psychological categories;
- treating painterly observations as general laws;
- presenting historically inherited ideas without always distinguishing their sources;
- insufficiently accounting for modern colorimetry, additive color, and context-dependent perception;
- encouraging rigid classroom formulas that Itten himself may have intended as exploratory exercises.

The criticisms do not erase the historical reach of the system. They distinguish a powerful pedagogical model from a comprehensive scientific theory.

### *Mein Vorkurs am Bauhaus*, 1963

In 1963 Itten published *Mein Vorkurs am Bauhaus: Gestaltungs- und Formenlehre*. It reconstructed and codified exercises developed at Vienna, Weimar, Berlin, Krefeld, and Zürich.

Because it was written four decades after the early Bauhaus, it is both a primary statement and a retrospective self-presentation. It should not be treated as a stenographic record of what every Bauhaus student experienced between 1919 and 1923.

### Honorary doctorate

The Technische Hochschule Darmstadt awarded Itten an honorary doctorate in architecture on 22 February 1965. The university’s register supplies the date and field.

### Final years and death

Itten continued painting, lecturing, and revising his pedagogical ideas. He died in Zürich on 25 March 1967 and was buried in Höngg Cemetery.

No reliable medical cause of death was found in the consulted records.

### Inventions, technologies, and patents

No patents have been identified.

Itten’s color sphere, color star, contrast exercises, and preliminary-course system were pedagogical and diagrammatic innovations, not patented technologies. His most durable “technology” was an instructional system transferable across media and institutions.

### Wealth, property, and business structure

No comprehensive public accounting of Itten’s wealth, salary, property, art-market income, school tuition, investment holdings, or estate valuation was located.

Known financial structures include:

- income from teaching and directing schools;
- tuition or school revenue from private institutions;
- workshop production at Ontos;
- design commissions;
- museum and public-school salaries;
- book royalties;
- sales of art;
- later foundation stewardship of the estate.

It would be speculative to characterize him as wealthy or impoverished for his entire life. The Vienna school was partly created for subsistence; the Berlin school reportedly became successful; and the Zürich directorships provided stable institutional employment.

## Political, Religious, Philosophical, and Artistic Development

### Politics

No evidence found establishes Itten as a member of a political party, elected official, intelligence operative, revolutionary organization, or violent movement.

His career nevertheless intersected directly with politics:

- postwar German cultural reconstruction at the Bauhaus;
- Weimar debates over craft, industry, and social function;
- Nazi closure of his school and dismissal;
- Nazi confiscation and exhibition of his works;
- Swiss wartime cultural institutions;
- postwar international design education;
- museum representations of non-European cultures.

### Reform pedagogy

His educational development combined:

- Bernese teacher training;
- reform pedagogy;
- Fröbelian activity and play;
- Hölzel’s formal exercises;
- gymnastic training;
- expressionist ideas of inner necessity;
- life-reform practices;
- individual psychological differentiation.

### Mazdaznan

Mazdaznan was the most important organized religious influence in his adult life from the 1910s through at least the Herrliberg period.

Its elements included:

- bodily purification;
- vegetarianism;
- regulated breathing;
- meditation;
- sexual and reproductive doctrines;
- syncretic readings of Zoroastrianism, Christianity, Hinduism, and yoga;
- racial and eugenic concepts expressed through the movement’s “Aryan” mythology.

Itten’s exact degree of continuing formal adherence after leaving Herrliberg is difficult to establish. He retained breathing, meditation, diet, and holistic training practices even when explicit organizational commitment receded from public view.

### Goethe and color

Itten drew upon Goethe’s color theory, especially polarity, qualitative observation, and the relation between color and experience. His quantitative contrast of extension also used proportions associated with Goethean color values.

This was an intellectual influence, not a personal connection.

### Asian and non-European art

Itten’s interest in Asian thought and visual culture predated his Museum Rietberg tenure. It informed his composition, calligraphic exercises, spiritual language, and museum work.

His engagement combined serious study and institutional advocacy with categories characteristic of European modernism, including broad generalizations about “Eastern” wisdom and “primitive” art. Present-day scholarship investigates both the preservation and aesthetic elevation of non-European art and the colonial structures through which it was collected and displayed.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Adolf Hölzel

Teacher and primary formative artistic mentor. Itten developed Hölzel’s contrast theory, masterwork analysis, abstraction, and pedagogical exercises into his own system.

### Walter Gropius

Patron, employer, collaborator, and later institutional opponent. Gropius brought Itten to the Bauhaus; their conflict over spiritual-individual education versus industrial production led to Itten’s departure.

### Gertrud Grunow

Collaborator at the Bauhaus. Her harmonization theory connected sound, color, bodily sensation, and movement with Itten’s preliminary course.

### Gunta Stölzl

Student, Bauhaus colleague, and textile collaborator. She helped create or operate the Ontos weaving workshops at Herrliberg.

### Georg Muche

Bauhaus colleague, Mazdaznan associate, and later teacher at the Berlin school. Muche belonged to Itten’s spiritually oriented Bauhaus circle.

### Paul Klee

Bauhaus colleague. Itten helped encourage Klee’s recruitment to the school. Their teaching both investigated form and color, though through distinct systems.

### Wassily Kandinsky

Bauhaus colleague from 1922. Kandinsky’s color-form theories intersected with Itten’s, but neither can be reduced to a disciple of the other.

### Oskar Schlemmer

Associate from Hölzel’s Stuttgart circle and later Bauhaus colleague. Both connected visual form with the disciplined human body.

### Josef Albers

Student in Itten’s preliminary course and later successor/co-developer of Bauhaus foundation teaching. Albers’s later color pedagogy both continued and departed from Itten’s methods.

### Anni Albers

Bauhaus student who passed through the preliminary educational structure associated with Itten before developing as a major textile artist.

### Ida Kerkovius, Willi Baumeister, Hermann Stenner

Fellow members of the Hölzel circle in Stuttgart.

### Alma Mahler and Adolf Loos

Members of Itten’s reported Vienna network. Alma Mahler’s circle helped connect cultural figures; Loos belonged to the architectural environment in which Itten met Gropius.

### Herwarth Walden

Dealer and publisher who gave Itten his first solo exhibition at Galerie Der Sturm in 1916 and another exhibition in 1923.

### Hildegard “Daniela” Itten

First wife, mother of Matthias, and active Mazdaznan member. The surviving chronology suggests that religious commitment was shared within the household rather than imposed by Itten alone.

### Anneliese Itten

Second wife, mother of Marion, Klaus, and Thomas; later steward of the legacy and founder of the Johannes-Itten-Stiftung.

### David and Frieda Ammann

Leaders of the Herrliberg Mazdaznan community. Itten conducted David Ammann’s funeral and participated in the community leadership before later tensions led to his departure.

### László Moholy-Nagy

Successor after Itten’s departure from the Bauhaus. Their contrasting reputations—mystical expressionist versus technological constructivist—became a standard narrative of Bauhaus history, although the actual institutional transition was more complex.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Charismatic authority and “cult” behavior

**Documented fact:** Itten held exceptional influence over a group of Bauhaus students, integrated Mazdaznan practices into teaching, wore distinctive clothing, and encouraged holistic discipline.

**Reported fact:** Some students and colleagues perceived the circle as sectarian.

**Disputed interpretation:** Later descriptions of Itten as a cult leader or guru capture his charisma but can exaggerate his control over the entire Bauhaus. There is no evidence of criminal prosecution, unlawful confinement, or a judicial finding against him.

### Racial and eugenic elements in Mazdaznan

**Documented fact:** Mazdaznan literature used “Aryan” mythology and contained racial, reproductive, and eugenic doctrines.

**Documented fact:** Itten was an important adherent and institutional participant.

**Unresolved question:** The available record does not establish that he accepted every doctrine published under the Mazdaznan name, nor does affiliation alone determine the content of every classroom exercise.

**Reported fact:** Researchers have identified attempts by Itten during the Nazi period to present his teachings in terms compatible with the authorities.

**Not established:** Nazi Party membership, participation in state violence, denunciation of victims, or a formal role in Nazi racial policy.

### Simultaneous persecution and accommodation

Itten’s Nazi-period record resists a simple category.

- His Berlin school was closed.
- He lost his Krefeld position.
- His art was confiscated and displayed as “degenerate.”
- He nevertheless remained employed in Krefeld until 1937.
- He apparently attempted to protect his work by rhetorically accommodating parts of the regime’s vocabulary.

These are not mutually exclusive. Persecuted modernists could attempt accommodation; attempted accommodation does not erase persecution, and persecution does not prove political resistance.

### Munich street-name controversy

**Documented fact:** Munich planned to name a street after Itten in 2011.

**Documented fact:** In 2012 the proposal was reversed following objections concerning Mazdaznan’s racial ideas and alleged ideological proximity to National Socialism.

**Disputed interpretation:** The controversy sometimes collapsed Itten, Mazdaznan, and Nazism into a single identity. The historical record instead shows religious affiliation, racialized doctrine within the movement, attempted accommodation, and simultaneous Nazi suppression of Itten’s institutions and art.

### Colonial museum questions

Current scholarship examines Itten’s direction of the Kunstgewerbemuseum and Museum Rietberg for the ways exhibitions framed Europe and non-European cultures.

No consulted source establishes that Itten personally stole objects or knowingly handled a specifically identified looted collection. Provenance questions must be assessed object by object. The documented issue is institutional participation in twentieth-century European systems for collecting and interpreting non-European art.

### Criminality, lawsuits, and investigations

No substantiated evidence was found of:

- criminal conviction;
- imprisonment;
- fraud;
- embezzlement;
- money laundering;
- corruption prosecution;
- intelligence activity;
- political violence;
- patent litigation;
- major personal lawsuit.

Absence from the consulted record is not proof that no civil dispute ever occurred, but no significant case emerged from the archival and institutional sources used here.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The Bauhaus monk”

**Story:** Itten was a monk-like spiritual master who ruled early Bauhaus students through ritual, diet, shaved hair, robes, and mystical doctrine.

**Origins and circulation:** Contemporary photographs show his distinctive high-collared clothing and cropped head. Bauhaus memoirs, histories, exhibitions, and popular accounts amplified the image.

**Evidence for:** Clothing, breathing exercises, vegetarianism, religious teaching, and a loyal student circle are documented.

**Evidence against literal reading:** He was a married professional artist and school administrator, not a monk in a recognized monastic order.

**Reputational effect:** The image made him the emblem of the Bauhaus’s expressionist and spiritual phase.

### “Itten left the Bauhaus solely because of Mazdaznan”

**Story:** Gropius expelled Itten because he had turned the school into a religious cult.

**Evidence:** Mazdaznan contributed substantially to tension.

**Counterevidence:** The documented conflict also involved production, commissions, industry, curriculum, finances, and institutional authority.

**Assessment:** **Simplifying myth based on a real conflict.**

### “From mysticism to technology overnight”

**Story:** Itten’s departure in 1923 instantly transformed the Bauhaus from irrational mysticism to rational industrial modernism.

**Evidence:** Moholy-Nagy’s arrival and Gropius’s “art and technology” orientation did represent a major change.

**Against:** Institutional transitions were gradual; material experimentation already existed under Itten, and later Bauhaus teachers retained psychological, artistic, and subjective concerns.

**Assessment:** **Useful periodization, not a complete factual description.**

### “Inventor of the color wheel”

**Story:** Itten invented the color wheel.

**Evidence:** He produced influential twelve-part color diagrams, a color sphere, and a color star.

**Against:** Circular color systems long predated him; his work developed Hölzel, Goethe, and other traditions.

**Assessment:** **False if taken literally.** He popularized a particular pedagogical system.

### “Scientifically proven seven contrasts”

**Story:** The seven contrasts are universal laws of color science.

**Evidence:** They describe many useful painterly and perceptual relationships.

**Against:** They combine physical, physiological, psychological, symbolic, and compositional categories and do not constitute a complete modern colorimetric theory.

**Assessment:** **Pedagogical doctrine sometimes misrepresented as settled science.**

### “Nazi Itten”

**Story:** Because Mazdaznan used Aryan and eugenic language, Itten was essentially a Nazi ideologue.

**Evidence:** His religious movement contained racial doctrine, and he made at least some attempt to accommodate his teaching to the regime.

**Against:** No Nazi Party membership has been established; the regime closed his school, removed him from office, confiscated nine works, and displayed two as “degenerate.”

**Assessment:** **Unsupported as a categorical claim.** The underlying ideological overlaps warrant investigation without erasing contrary evidence.

### “Pure victim and opponent of Nazism”

**Story:** Because his work was condemned, Itten was necessarily a consistent anti-Nazi resister.

**Evidence:** He suffered real professional and artistic repression.

**Against:** Persecution does not itself demonstrate organized resistance, and researchers document accommodation efforts.

**Assessment:** **Hagiographic overstatement.**

### Mazdaznan antiquity claims

Mazdaznan presented itself as recovering ancient Zoroastrian or Aryan wisdom. Modern historical scholarship treats it as a modern syncretic movement. Its ancient lineage is therefore part of the movement’s self-created sacred mythology, not established institutional continuity with ancient Zoroastrianism.

## Works by Johannes Itten

### Authored or substantially authored during his lifetime

#### 1921 — “Analysen alter Meister”

Published in Bruno Maria Adler, editor, *Utopia: Dokumente der Wirklichkeit*, Weimar.

A compact statement of formal analysis emerging from Itten’s Bauhaus teaching. Genuine contemporary authorship.

#### 1921 — *Farbenkugel in 7 Lichtstufen und 12 Tönen*

A diagrammatic color sphere published in the Bauhaus-era context of *Utopia*. Genuine work by Itten; both artwork and teaching instrument.

#### 1961 — *Kunst der Farbe: Subjektives Erleben und objektives Erkennen als Wege zur Kunst*

Ravensburg: Otto Maier.

Itten’s principal color treatise. Genuine lifetime publication. It has appeared in full, abridged, study, and translated editions.

Major translated titles include:

- *The Art of Color*;
- *The Elements of Color*;
- *L’Art de la couleur*;
- *Arte del colore*;
- *Kunst en kleur*;
- *Iskusstvo tsveta*.

*The Elements of Color* is an abridged or adapted presentation based on the larger book and should not be confused with the complete original.

#### 1963 — *Mein Vorkurs am Bauhaus: Gestaltungs- und Formenlehre*

Ravensburg: Otto Maier.

A retrospective reconstruction of the Bauhaus preliminary course and its later development. Genuine lifetime authorship, but edited publication and retrospective testimony rather than a verbatim 1919–1923 course record.

English titles include:

- *Design and Form: The Basic Course at the Bauhaus*;
- *Design and Form: The Basic Course at the Bauhaus and Later*.

### Print portfolios and graphic works

#### 1920/1921 — *Haus des weißen Mannes*

Lithograph made in 1920 and published in 1921 in *Neue europäische Graphik, 1. Mappe: Meister des Staatlichen Bauhauses in Weimar*.

#### 1921 — *Spruch*

Print in the same Bauhaus masters’ portfolio, incorporating a Mazdaznan saying.

### Important artworks and projects

A complete list is impossible within a textual dossier because the modern catalogue raisonné contains more than 3,000 works. Representative documented works or groups include:

- early landscapes and student drawings, from 1907 onward;
- Hölzel-period abstractions;
- expressionist and geometric paintings from Stuttgart and Vienna;
- *Turm des Feuers*, 1920;
- *Kinderbild*, 1920;
- the color sphere and color-star diagrams;
- Bauhaus prints;
- textile designs and Ontos textiles;
- the Erich Hamann chocolate-shop interior, 1928;
- abstract, figurative, and geometric paintings from the Zürich decades;
- *Raumkomposition I* and *Raumkomposition II*, 1944;
- *Variationen* series, including *Variationen III*, 1957.

### Posthumous editions and compilations

#### 1970 — *The Elements of Color*

An abridged presentation based on *Kunst der Farbe*. Often catalogued under Itten’s name, but editorially derived from the larger work.

#### 1985 — *Der Farbstern*

Posthumous teaching publication associated with Itten’s color-star system; editions credit or involve Anneliese Itten. It should be classified as a posthumous editorial realization, not a wholly new lifetime book.

#### 1988 — *Bildanalysen*

Posthumously edited collection of Itten’s analyses of artworks. Genuine underlying materials, later editorial compilation.

Translations include:

- *Beeldende kunst in beeld: Analyses van vorm en inhoud*, 1990;
- *L’étude des œuvres d’art: De l’art antique à l’art moderne*, 1990.

#### 2002 — *Elemente der Bildenden Kunst: Studienausgabe des Tagebuchs*

Edited study edition drawn from Itten’s diaries. Genuine archival writings, selected and edited posthumously.

#### *Johannes Itten: Kunst als Leben*

Publication or exhibition-book title assembling art, diaries, and biographical material. Posthumous editorial presentation, not a single lifetime manuscript of that title.

#### *Jeder Mensch ist bildnerisch begabt*

Zentralbibliothek Zürich publication based on the archive and Itten’s pedagogical record. Posthumous documentary/exhibition compilation.

### Diaries and notebooks

Itten kept extensive diaries and teaching notebooks, including the **Tempelherrenhaus-Tagebuch** from the Bauhaus period. These contain:

- compositional analyses;
- color studies;
- religious reflections;
- teaching notes;
- bodily and breathing exercises;
- records of Mazdaznan practice.

They are primary sources, but most have been published only selectively or in facsimile excerpts.

### Correspondence

The Zürich archive preserves correspondence with students, artists, museum figures, family members, and institutions. Individual letters have appeared in exhibition catalogues and documentary editions. There is no complete critical edition of all correspondence identified in the consulted open sources.

### Catalogue raisonné

Christoph Wagner, with collaborators including Felix Billeter, Celina Berchtold, Gerald Dagit, and Marion Lichardus-Itten, directs the modern catalogue raisonné:

- Volume I: paintings, watercolors, and drawings, 1907–1938;
- Volume II: paintings, watercolors, and drawings, 1939–1967;
- Volume III: textiles, sculpture, furniture, and art-theoretical work.

The project reports approximately 3,000 illustrated works and more than 1,000 additions beyond Willy Rotzler’s 1972 catalogue. It is the principal current reference for authenticity, dating, provenance, exhibition history, and the extent of Itten’s oeuvre.

## Death, Burial, and Estate

Johannes Itten died in Zürich on 25 March 1967, aged seventy-eight.

- descendants participated in scholarship and foundation governance;

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Bauhaus education

Itten’s preliminary course became one of the most widely imitated parts of Bauhaus pedagogy. Foundation programs in art, architecture, and design worldwide adopted variants of:

- elementary form exercises;
- material studies;
- texture contrasts;
- color scales;
- compositional analysis;
- experimental work before specialization.

His direct line of influence runs through Bauhaus successors, schools in Zürich and Ulm, émigré teachers, and postwar design education.

### Color theory

The seven contrasts remain widely taught in art schools, graphic design, fashion, interiors, cosmetics, and introductory color courses.

The system’s reach exceeds its scientific authority. Its continued usefulness lies in naming operational visual relationships, while its limitations become apparent when treated as a universal physics or psychology of color.

### Seasonal color analysis

Modern personal-color or “seasonal color” systems often claim descent from Itten’s ideas about subjective color harmony. The connection is real at the level of color typing and individual palettes, but commercial four-season systems were developed later and should not be attributed to Itten as a finished invention.

### Artistic reputation

Itten’s fame as a teacher long overshadowed his art. The modern catalogue raisonné seeks to correct this imbalance by presenting thousands of paintings, drawings, watercolors, textiles, sculptures, furniture pieces, and designs.

His work is now interpreted across:

- expressionism;
- geometric abstraction;
- concrete art;
- spiritual modernism;
- textile modernism;
- color theory;
- life reform;
- museum and exhibition history.

### Reassessment of spirituality

Earlier modernist histories sometimes treated Mazdaznan as an embarrassing eccentricity. Later scholarship has made it central to understanding:

- bodily preparation;
- vegetarianism;
- breathing exercises;
- charisma;
- Bauhaus communal life;
- modernism’s engagement with esotericism.

The reassessment also exposes racial and eugenic material previously minimized in celebratory histories.

### Political reassessment

Itten is simultaneously remembered as:

- a victim of Nazi cultural repression;
- a Swiss modernist who remained in Germany until 1937;
- an adherent of a movement containing Aryan and eugenic ideas;
- a figure who attempted some accommodation;
- an artist whose work was officially condemned.

Current scholarship increasingly preserves all of those facts rather than forcing him into a simple category of resister, collaborator, or apolitical mystic.

### Museum legacy

His role in founding and directing Museum Rietberg helped establish non-European objects as major works of art in a European municipal museum. The collecting and display structures involved are now being reexamined through provenance and colonial-history research.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Anni Albers — **Documented fact**

Anni Albers entered the Bauhaus in 1922 and encountered the preliminary educational structure created by Itten. His material, form, and color teaching formed part of the environment from which Bauhaus weaving developed. This was principally a teacher-student/institutional connection in Weimar.

### Oskar Alfred Victor Schlemmer — **Documented fact**

Itten met Schlemmer in Adolf Hölzel’s Stuttgart circle after 1913. Both later taught at the Bauhaus in Weimar. Their relationship joined shared training, collegial work, and an interest in disciplined relations between body, space, and form.

### Paul Klee — **Documented fact**

Itten and Klee were colleagues at the Bauhaus. Itten encouraged or participated in Klee’s recruitment around 1920–1921. Both developed influential theories of color and form, although their systems remained distinct.

### Wassily Kandinsky — **Documented fact**

Kandinsky joined the Bauhaus in 1922 while Itten was still associated with it. They shared the institution and an interest in spiritual and psychological relations among colors and forms. No evidence found establishes a simple teacher-student relationship.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe — **Documented influence; no personal meeting**

Itten drew on Goethe’s *Zur Farbenlehre*, especially qualitative color experience, polarity, and proportional relations. Goethe died decades before Itten’s birth; this was an intellectual connection only.

### Friedrich Fröbel — **Reported/documented pedagogical influence; no personal meeting**

Itten’s early teacher training and elementary-school work exposed him to methods descended from Fröbel, including learning through activity and elementary forms. Fröbel died before Itten’s birth.

### Johann Heinrich Pestalozzi — **Reported intellectual-pedagogical connection; no personal meeting**

Itten’s Swiss teacher-training environment belonged to a reform-pedagogical tradition strongly shaped by Pestalozzi. The evidence supports an inherited educational influence, not a direct relationship.

### Rudolf Steiner — **Reported intellectual proximity; direct relationship unproven**

Itten’s spiritual modernism, holistic education, color symbolism, and life-reform setting overlapped with anthroposophical currents. The sources consulted do not establish a documented meeting or correspondence with Steiner. This should therefore not be upgraded to a personal connection.

### Émile Jaques-Dalcroze — **Reported indirect influence; no meeting established**

Itten’s rhythmic bodily exercises emerged in a Central European educational environment influenced by Dalcroze eurhythmics. No direct teacher-student relationship was established in the consulted sources.

### Paul Klee, Wassily Kandinsky, Oskar Schlemmer, and Anni Albers as a shared Bauhaus network — **Documented fact**

All intersected Itten through the Bauhaus rather than merely through contemporaneity. The specific relations differed: colleagues in the case of Klee, Kandinsky, and Schlemmer; institutional teacher-student influence in the case of Anni Albers.

### Other roster names

No sufficiently documented personal crossing was found with the remaining atlas names. Some—such as Rudolf Laban, Mary Wigman, Maria Montessori, Carl Jung, Friedrich Nietzsche, and Le Corbusier—belonged to overlapping intellectual or modernist environments, but shared themes or geography alone are not evidence of meeting, correspondence, or acknowledged influence.
