# Muhammad ibn Abdullah: Research Dossier

## Scope and Identification

This dossier concerns **Muhammad ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib**, the Arabian religious preacher, political leader, legislator, military commander, and founder of the community from which Islam developed.

The evidence is uneven. The Qurʾan is the earliest substantial source associated with Muhammad’s lifetime, but it is not a continuous biography. Detailed narratives survive chiefly in Islamic texts compiled generations later. These include:

- Ibn Isḥāq’s *Sīrat Rasūl Allāh*, composed approximately 120–140 years after Muhammad’s death and surviving mainly through Ibn Hishām and al-Ṭabarī.
- Al-Wāqidī’s *Kitāb al-Maghāzī*.
- Ibn Saʿd’s *Kitāb al-Ṭabaqāt al-kabīr*.
- Canonical and noncanonical hadith collections.
- Al-Balādhurī’s historical works.
- Al-Ṭabarī’s *Taʾrīkh al-rusul wa-l-mulūk*.

Some material in these works probably preserves early memories or documents; other material reflects later theological, legal, political, genealogical, and narrative development. Precise dates, ages, conversations, casualty numbers, motives, miracle accounts, and private episodes generally carry more uncertainty than the broad public chronology.

Contemporary or near-contemporary non-Muslim notices confirm that an Arabian prophetic leader named Muhammad was remembered near the beginning of the conquests. Their interpretation remains disputed because they are brief, polemical, geographically distant, and sometimes difficult to date.

## Source Base and Evidentiary Problems

### The Qurʾan

The Qurʾan names Muhammad four times and Aḥmad once. It addresses him as messenger, prophet, war leader, adjudicator, husband, and head of a household. It preserves material connected with:

- Conflict with Meccan opponents.
- The Hijra and expulsion from Mecca.
- Badr, Uḥud, and the Confederates.
- Relations with Jews, Christians, hypocrites, and Arabian polytheists.
- Muhammad’s wives and household.
- His marriage to Zaynab after Zayd’s divorce.
- Treaty-breaking, spoils, captives, and warfare.
- The “slander” against a wife traditionally identified as ʿĀʾisha.
- The assertion that Muhammad was mortal and would die.

The Qurʾan does not give Muhammad’s parents’ names, date of birth, continuous childhood narrative, detailed list of wives and children, or a conventional biography.

### Sīra and maghāzī literature

Ibn Isḥāq died around 767, more than a century after Muhammad. His biography survives in Ibn Hishām’s edited recension, quotations in al-Ṭabarī, and fragments elsewhere. Ibn Hishām stated that he omitted material he considered objectionable, irrelevant, unverified, or offensive.

Al-Wāqidī, Ibn Saʿd, and al-Ṭabarī preserved extensive expedition lists, genealogies, letters, speeches, and conflicting reports. Medieval hadith critics often distrusted al-Wāqidī, but historians continue to use his material cautiously.

### Hadith

Hadith collections preserve attributed words and deeds through transmission chains. They are indispensable for Muhammad’s reception and for reconstructing early Islamic law, but individual reports require source criticism. Their canonical status in later Sunni or Shiʿi communities does not itself prove that every report is verbatim historical testimony.

### Non-Muslim sources

The *Doctrina Jacobi*, a Syriac notice sometimes called the “Fragment on the Arab Conquests,” Thomas the Presbyter, and the Armenian history attributed to Sebeos are important early external witnesses. They describe a prophet or the “Arabs of Muhammad,” though they do not reproduce the full later Muslim biography.

The *Doctrina Jacobi* speaks polemically of a prophet appearing “with the Saracens.” Because its chronology and dramatic setting are uncertain, it cannot simply be treated as an eyewitness biography. Sebeos, writing later in the seventh century, associates Muhammad with an Abrahamic program and an alliance or common cause between Arabs and Jews. These witnesses support Muhammad’s historical existence but complicate attempts to reconstruct the earliest movement’s boundaries.

### Broad historical minimum

The following outline has comparatively strong support:

- Muhammad was an Arabian monotheistic preacher connected with Mecca.
- He proclaimed revelations that form the core of the Qurʾan.
- He gathered followers and encountered opposition.
- He migrated to Medina.
- He became the leader of a community combining religious, legal, diplomatic, and military functions.
- He fought the Meccan Quraysh and other opponents.
- He acquired control of Mecca and much of western Arabia.
- He died near the beginning of the Arab conquests, traditionally in 632.

## Family and Ancestry

### Clan and paternal ancestry

Muhammad belonged to the **Banū Hāshim**, a clan of Quraysh. Traditional genealogies trace him through:

Muḥammad → ʿAbd Allāh → ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib/Shayba → Hāshim/ʿAmr → ʿAbd Manāf → Quṣayy → Kilāb → Murra → Kaʿb → Luʾayy → Ghālib → Fihr/Quraysh.

Later genealogists extended the line through ʿAdnān to Ishmael and Abraham. The genealogy from Muhammad to ʿAdnān belongs to Arabian genealogical tradition; extensions beyond ʿAdnān are substantially more legendary.

### Father

**ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib** reportedly died before Muhammad’s birth or shortly afterward. Accounts place his death at Yathrib after returning from a trading journey. These particulars are traditional rather than contemporary documentation.

### Mother

**Āmina bint Wahb**, of Banū Zuhra, reportedly cared for Muhammad until her death when he was about six. Tradition says she died at al-Abwāʾ while returning from a visit to Yathrib.

### Grandfather and guardian

**ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib** reportedly assumed responsibility for the orphaned Muhammad but died when the child was about eight.

### Uncle and protector

**Abū Ṭālib ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib** became Muhammad’s guardian and later his clan protector. He did not, according to the dominant Sunni tradition, publicly embrace Islam. Some Shiʿi traditions affirm that he was a believer who concealed his faith. His protection was politically decisive during Muhammad’s Meccan preaching.

### Other uncles and aunts

Important traditional relatives included:

- **Ḥamza ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib**, supporter and prominent warrior, killed at Uḥud.
- **Al-ʿAbbās ibn ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib**, ancestor of the Abbasid dynasty.
- **Abū Lahab/ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā**, an opponent condemned in Qurʾan 111.
- **Ṣafiyya bint ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib**, mother of al-Zubayr ibn al-ʿAwwām.

### Foster family

Tradition states that Muhammad was nursed by **Ḥalīma al-Saʿdiyya** among Banū Saʿd, following a reputed Meccan practice of placing infants with Bedouin foster families. Miracle narratives describing angels opening and cleansing his chest are hagiographic traditions, not independently documented events.

## Childhood and Early Life

The traditional biography depicts Muhammad as an orphan protected by relatives but without inherited wealth sufficient to make him independently powerful.

Qurʾan 93 asks, “Did He not find you an orphan and shelter you?” Muslim exegesis connected this verse directly to Muhammad’s childhood. It is the closest early textual reference to his orphanhood.

Stories of his early life include:

- Tending sheep.
- Accompanying Abū Ṭālib on a caravan journey toward Syria.
- Meeting the Christian monk **Baḥīrā**, who recognized marks of prophethood.
- Participating in or witnessing the **Ḥarb al-Fijār**, the “Sacrilegious War.”
- Joining **Ḥilf al-Fuḍūl**, an alliance intended to protect merchants and victims of injustice.
- Arbitrating the placement of the Black Stone when the Kaʿba was rebuilt.

The Baḥīrā narrative appears in later Christian–Muslim polemics in radically altered forms. In Muslim hagiography, Baḥīrā confirms Muhammad’s future mission. In some Christian polemics, a monk supposedly authored or supplied Muhammad’s teachings. No contemporary evidence verifies either version.

The Black Stone arbitration story says that competing Quraysh clans accepted Muhammad’s proposal to place the stone on a cloth carried jointly by clan representatives. It functions as an account of pre-prophetic trustworthiness and political skill but lacks contemporary corroboration.

## Education, Literacy, and Formation

No formal education or named human teacher is securely documented. Islamic tradition generally portrays Muhammad as **ummī**. Interpretations include:

- Unable to read and write.
- Not formally educated in scripture.
- Belonging to a people without an earlier revealed book.
- “Gentile” in contrast to Jews and Christians.

Traditional accounts of the first revelation emphasize his response, “I do not read” or “What shall I recite?” Some reports of the Treaty of Hudaybiyya and Muhammad’s final illness have been interpreted as suggesting limited writing ability, but the texts are ambiguous and late.

Potential formative environments included:

- Meccan pilgrimage and trade.
- Arabian oral poetry and genealogy.
- Contact with Jewish and Christian traditions circulating in Arabia.
- Caravan connections with Syria.
- The monotheistic vocabulary of late antiquity.
- Arabian figures later called **ḥanīfs**, monotheists outside organized Judaism and Christianity.

Named figures such as Waraqa ibn Nawfal, Zayd ibn ʿAmr, Baḥīrā, and Salmān al-Fārisī appear in traditions about Muhammad’s religious environment. Claims that any one of them secretly authored the Qurʾan are later polemical theories without supporting documentary evidence.

## Marriage to Khadīja and Household Formation

Around **595**, according to traditional chronology, Muhammad married **Khadīja bint Khuwaylid**, a wealthy Qurayshi widow or divorcee engaged in trade. He reportedly managed a caravan or commercial venture for her before the marriage.

Traditional sources commonly give Muhammad’s age as twenty-five and Khadīja’s as forty. Alternative traditions make Khadīja younger, sometimes in her late twenties or thirties. Her exact age cannot be established.

Khadīja is presented as:

- Muhammad’s first wife.
- His only wife while she lived.
- The first person to believe his prophetic claim.
- A source of emotional and financial support.
- The person who took him to her relative Waraqa after the first revelation.

### Children attributed to Muhammad and Khadīja

The conventional list is:

- **Al-Qāsim**, died young.
- **Zaynab**, married Abū al-ʿĀṣ ibn al-Rabīʿ.
- **Ruqayya**, married ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān after an earlier engagement or marriage to a son of Abū Lahab.
- **Umm Kulthūm**, later married ʿUthmān after Ruqayya’s death.
- **Fāṭima**, married ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib.
- **ʿAbd Allāh**, also associated in tradition with the names al-Ṭayyib and al-Ṭāhir; sources disagree whether these were one child or several.

Some early Shiʿi and later sectarian narratives claimed that Zaynab, Ruqayya, and Umm Kulthūm were Khadīja’s daughters or nieces from an earlier relationship rather than Muhammad’s biological daughters. The majority early biographical tradition treats all four daughters as Muhammad’s.

## Beginning of the Prophetic Career

### First revelation

Traditional chronology places Muhammad’s first revelation around **610**, when he was approximately forty. He reportedly withdrew for contemplation to the cave of **Ḥirāʾ** outside Mecca.

The standard narrative says the angel Gabriel commanded him to recite. The opening of Qurʾan 96 is traditionally identified as the first revelation. Muhammad returned distressed; Khadīja reassured him, and Waraqa ibn Nawfal identified the visitor with the angelic messenger of earlier prophets.

Competing traditional sequences name portions of Qurʾan 74 or the opening chapter as the first revelation. The exact order cannot be historically established.

### Early followers

Tradition identifies among the earliest adherents:

- Khadīja.
- ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib.
- Zayd ibn Ḥāritha.
- Abū Bakr.
- ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān.
- Al-Zubayr ibn al-ʿAwwām.
- Ṭalḥa ibn ʿUbayd Allāh.
- Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ.
- ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf.
- Abū ʿUbayda ibn al-Jarrāḥ.

The exact conversion order became politically significant because later factions used priority in Islam to support claims to authority.

### Early message

The early Qurʾanic proclamations emphasize:

- One sovereign God.
- Resurrection and judgment.
- Accountability for wealth and treatment of the vulnerable.
- Continuity with Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and other prophets.
- Condemnation of idolatry.
- Muhammad’s status as warner and messenger.
- The Qurʾan as an Arabic revelation.

## Meccan Opposition and Persecution

Tradition dates public preaching to approximately 613. Opposition reportedly intensified as Muhammad condemned the gods, ancestral customs, and social authority of the Quraysh.

The sources describe:

- Mockery and accusations that Muhammad was a poet, sorcerer, possessed man, or fabricator.
- Pressure on Abū Ṭālib to withdraw protection.
- Abuse of socially vulnerable followers.
- Manumission of persecuted slaves by wealthy believers, especially Abū Bakr.
- Migration by some followers to the Aksumite kingdom in Africa.
- A boycott against Banū Hāshim and Banū al-Muṭṭalib.
- Negotiations offering Muhammad status or wealth if he abandoned his preaching.

The severity and chronology of persecution differ across sources. Accounts of individual tortures may preserve genuine memories but also developed into martyr narratives.

### Migration to Aksum

Groups of believers reportedly crossed the Red Sea to the Christian kingdom of Aksum around 615. The ruler, called **al-Najāshī** in Arabic sources, allegedly rejected a Qurayshi extradition request after hearing Qurʾanic material about Mary and Jesus.

The episode is plausible within Red Sea political geography. The detailed courtroom speeches are literary transmissions, not contemporary transcripts.

### “Satanic verses”

Some early Muslim historical and exegetical sources report that Muhammad once recited words acknowledging pagan intercessory beings associated with al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, and Manāt, then retracted them as satanic interference. The episode became known in modern scholarship as the “Satanic verses.”

Evidentiary status: **early but disputed report**.

- It appears in multiple early transmission lines, including material preserved by al-Ṭabarī.
- It is absent from canonical Sunni hadith collections in its developed narrative form.
- Later Muslim scholars commonly rejected it on theological or transmission-critical grounds.
- Some modern historians considered its potentially embarrassing character evidence of antiquity.
- Others argue that it arose as exegesis of Qurʾan 22:52 or from attempts to explain an apparent temporary reconciliation with Quraysh.

No consensus establishes the event as fact.

### “Year of Sorrow”

Khadīja and Abū Ṭālib reportedly died around **619**, depriving Muhammad of domestic support and clan protection. Precise dating is traditional.

### Journey to Ṭāʾif

Muhammad reportedly traveled to Ṭāʾif seeking protection and converts. Later accounts describe rejection, stoning, bleeding, and angelic offers to destroy the town. The journey is embedded in hagiographic narrative, although a failed search for an alternative protector is historically plausible.

### Night Journey and Ascension

Islamic tradition says Muhammad was transported by night from the “Sacred Mosque” to the “Farthest Mosque,” then ascended through the heavens, met earlier prophets, and received the command of daily prayer.

Evidentiary status: **Qurʾanic allusion plus developed religious tradition**.

- Qurʾan 17:1 refers to a night journey but does not name Jerusalem explicitly.
- Qurʾan 53 contains a vision narrative whose relationship to the ascension is debated.
- Hadith and sīra supply Burāq, Jerusalem, the heavenly levels, meetings with Adam, Jesus, Moses, Abraham, and others, and negotiation over the number of prayers.
- Some early interpreters treated it as a bodily journey; others as a vision.
- The story became central to Islamic sacred geography, devotional literature, and Persianate manuscript painting.

## The Road to Medina

Yathrib, later Medina, contained the Arab groupings of Aws and Khazraj and several Jewish communities. Local feuding created demand for an outside arbitrator.

At pilgrimage meetings near **al-ʿAqaba**, Yathrib converts reportedly pledged allegiance and protection. Muhammad sent **Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr** as a teacher.

The Quraysh allegedly plotted to kill Muhammad. He escaped with Abū Bakr and hid in the cave of Thawr before traveling north. Qurʾan 9:40 refers to “the two” in the cave, forming the principal early textual basis for Abū Bakr’s presence.

The **Hijra**, conventionally dated to September 622, later became the epoch of the Islamic calendar. The calendar was instituted administratively under Caliph ʿUmar, not by Muhammad in its later numbered form.

---

## Medina: Community and Government

### Settlement

Muhammad settled at Qubāʾ and then in central Yathrib. The city came to be called **Madīnat al-Nabī**, “City of the Prophet,” or simply al-Madīna.

Traditional measures included:

- Construction of a mosque and adjoining domestic chambers.
- Pairing Meccan emigrants with Medinan supporters.
- Arbitration among local groups.
- Regulation of worship, charity, marriage, inheritance, warfare, and communal discipline.
- Establishment of a market independent of certain existing market arrangements.

### Constitution or Compact of Medina

A document preserved in Ibn Isḥāq and Abū ʿUbayd is commonly called the **Constitution of Medina**. It defines relationships among believers from Quraysh and Yathrib, their allies, and specified Jewish groups.

Many historians accept that the text contains an early core, possibly several agreements from Muhammad’s lifetime. Its provisions include:

- Mutual defense.
- Collective responsibility for blood-money and ransoming captives.
- Muhammad’s role in referring major disputes to God and his messenger.
- Protection of participating Jewish groups’ religion.
- Prohibition on assisting Quraysh.
- Identification of the parties as an **umma**, though the term’s exact scope varies by clause.

Calling it a modern constitution is anachronistic; “compact,” “agreement,” or “community document” is more precise.

### Change of prayer direction

Tradition states that Muslims initially prayed toward Jerusalem and later toward the Kaʿba in Mecca. Qurʾan 2 discusses the change of qibla and the controversy around it.

### Relations with Jewish groups

Muhammad’s relations with Medinan Jews cannot be reduced to a single policy. Sources preserve religious argument, alliances, legal interaction, marriage, trade, political conflict, expulsion, and killing.

Three major Jewish-associated groups in later narrative are:

- Banū Qaynuqāʿ.
- Banū al-Naḍīr.
- Banū Qurayẓa.

The exact tribal composition, treaty terms, and chronology are reconstructed chiefly from Muslim narratives written later.

## Warfare and Expansion

### Caravan raids

After the Hijra, Muhammad authorized or led expeditions against Meccan caravans. These operations served economic, strategic, retaliatory, and political purposes. Qurʾanic texts connect fighting with expulsion and persecution while also regulating spoils.

### Nakhla expedition

A Muslim raiding party killed ʿAmr ibn al-Ḥaḍramī and captured goods during or near a sacred month. Qurʾan 2:217 acknowledges the gravity of fighting in a sacred month but characterizes persecution or obstruction from God’s path as graver.

### Battle of Badr, 624

Muhammad’s forces intercepted or attempted to intercept a major caravan led by Abū Sufyān. A Qurayshi relief force confronted them near Badr.

Traditional figures give roughly 300 Muslims against about 1,000 Meccans. The Muslims won, killing several Qurayshi leaders and capturing prisoners.

Consequences included:

- Enhanced Muhammad’s prestige.
- Redistribution of booty under emerging rules.
- Execution of at least some captives, notably al-Naḍr ibn al-Ḥārith and ʿUqba ibn Abī Muʿayṭ in traditional accounts.
- Intensified conflict with Mecca.
- Political realignment in Medina.

Exact troop and casualty figures are traditional approximations.

### Banū Qaynuqāʿ

After Badr, Muhammad besieged and expelled Banū Qaynuqāʿ. Later narratives blame a market assault, treaty violation, or political defiance. ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy reportedly intervened against their execution. The triggering incident cannot be independently verified.

### Kaʿb ibn al-Ashraf

Early sources report that Muhammad authorized or approved the killing of **Kaʿb ibn al-Ashraf**, a poet and political opponent associated with Banū al-Naḍīr, after Badr. Kaʿb was lured from his residence and assassinated by Muhammad’s followers.

Evidentiary status: **reported in early Islamic narrative and canonical hadith; broadly accepted as a historical political assassination, though the speeches and motives are transmitted later**.

Sources variously accuse Kaʿb of inciting Quraysh, composing erotic verses about Muslim women, and violating political commitments.

### Battle of Uḥud, 625

A Qurayshi army led by Abū Sufyān attacked near Mount Uḥud. Muslim archers reportedly abandoned a designated position, allowing a counterattack.

Muhammad was injured. His uncle Ḥamza was killed; tradition identifies **Waḥshī**, an enslaved spearman, as the killer. Hind bint ʿUtba was later said to have mutilated Ḥamza and chewed his liver. The mutilation is widely transmitted, but lurid details may have developed in retelling.

The battle was a serious military setback but did not remove Muhammad from power.

### Banū al-Naḍīr

Muhammad expelled Banū al-Naḍīr after accusing them of plotting to kill him. Their lands and property were distributed or administered as *fayʾ*. Qurʾan 59 is traditionally associated with the episode.

The assassination plot is reported through Islamic narrative; no independent source adjudicates it. The expulsion and transfer of property fit the subsequent political and economic transformation of Medina.

### Battle of the Trench/Confederates, 627

A coalition of Quraysh and allied tribes besieged Medina. Tradition credits **Salmān al-Fārisī** with proposing a defensive trench.

The siege failed because of the fortifications, weather, supply problems, distrust, and coalition fragmentation. Qurʾan 33 describes the fear and internal tensions.

### Banū Qurayẓa

After the confederate withdrawal, Muhammad’s forces besieged Banū Qurayẓa, accused in Islamic sources of aiding or negotiating with the besiegers. They surrendered.

The dominant sīra account says **Saʿd ibn Muʿādh**, an Aws leader and former ally, judged that the adult males should be killed, property divided, and women and children enslaved. Muhammad approved the judgment. Traditional totals for executed men range from several hundred to approximately 900.

Evidentiary assessment:

- **Execution and enslavement:** strongly embedded in early Muslim tradition and generally accepted in broad outline by many historians.
- **Exact number:** uncertain; large round figures may be exaggerated.
- **Universal execution of every adult male:** depends chiefly on later narrative.
- **Reason:** traditional sources say treason during an existential siege; modern writers dispute the precise treaty and evidentiary procedure.
- **Revisionist denial:** some modern Muslim authors argue that only a small group of active traitors was executed. This is possible only by rejecting or radically reinterpreting the dominant early narrative; direct contemporary documentation is absent.

The episode later became a major site of apologetic, polemical, legal, and political contestation.

## Hudaybiyya, Khaybar, and Regional Diplomacy

### Treaty of Hudaybiyya, 628

Muhammad and followers approached Mecca intending pilgrimage but were stopped at Hudaybiyya. A ten-year truce was negotiated with Quraysh.

Terms reportedly included:

- Postponed pilgrimage.
- Return of certain Meccan defectors.
- Freedom for tribes to ally with either side.
- Suspension of hostilities.

Some followers considered the terms humiliating. Qurʾan 48 called the outcome a “clear victory.” Strategically, the treaty recognized Muhammad as a negotiating power and allowed wider alliance-building.

The famous account that ʿAlī hesitated to erase “Messenger of God” from the treaty formula and Muhammad altered or ordered alteration of it has figured in literacy debates.

### Khaybar

Muhammad attacked the wealthy oasis of **Khaybar**, inhabited substantially by Jews. The settlements surrendered after fighting.

Reported arrangements allowed cultivators to remain and work land in return for a portion, often stated as half, of the produce. Captives included **Ṣafiyya bint Ḥuyayy**, daughter of a prominent Jewish leader. Muhammad freed and married her according to dominant tradition, though the context of conquest and captivity has generated enduring controversy about meaningful consent.

### Poisoned meat

Canonical hadith and sīra report that **Zaynab bint al-Ḥārith**, a Jewish woman whose relatives had been killed, served Muhammad poisoned lamb. A companion, Bishr ibn al-Barāʾ, reportedly died; Muhammad survived but suffered effects.

Accounts disagree whether Zaynab was pardoned or executed after Bishr’s death. Reports from Muhammad’s final illness say he still felt pain from the food.

Evidentiary status: **well-established within Islamic tradition but without external confirmation**. Whether poisoning contributed medically to Muhammad’s death four years later cannot be determined.

### Fadak

The oasis of Fadak reportedly submitted without battle and became property administered directly by Muhammad. After his death, Fāṭima claimed inheritance or ownership. Abū Bakr rejected the inheritance claim on the basis of a report that prophets leave no inheritable estate, making Fadak a lasting Sunni–Shiʿi controversy.

### Diplomatic letters

Tradition reports letters inviting rulers to Islam, including:

- Byzantine emperor Heraclius.
- Sasanian ruler Khusrau II.
- The ruler called al-Muqawqis in Egypt.
- The Negus of Aksum.
- Arabian and Ghassanid rulers.

Later copies and purported originals survive. Their authenticity must be assessed individually. Several physical “original letters” and seal impressions displayed in later periods have been judged late or forged. The general practice of sending emissaries is plausible; the exact texts and universal diplomatic program may have been standardized retrospectively.

## Marriages, Partners, and Domestic Politics

Sources differ over whether Muhammad contracted eleven, twelve, or thirteen consummated marriages and whether Rayḥāna and Māriya were wives or enslaved concubines.

### Khadīja bint Khuwaylid

First wife and mother of most or all traditionally recognized children except Ibrāhīm. No other marriage is reported during her lifetime.

### Sawda bint Zamʿa

An early convert and widow, married after Khadīja’s death. Later traditions say she ceded her allotted night to ʿĀʾisha to avoid divorce; the exact circumstances are uncertain.

### ʿĀʾisha bint Abī Bakr

Daughter of Abū Bakr and a major transmitter of hadith. Canonical Sunni reports state that the marriage contract occurred when she was six or seven and consummation when she was nine. Other chronological reconstructions place her in adolescence or older.

Evidentiary status:

- **Traditional Sunni report:** contract at six or seven, consummation at nine.
- **Modern historical dispute:** Joshua Little’s isnād-cum-matn study argues that the specific age report developed in eighth-century Iraq and is not reliable evidence for her historical age.
- **Alternative age calculations:** based on Asmāʾ’s age, participation in battles, or conversion chronology, but these depend on other uncertain reports.
- **Conclusion:** her exact age cannot be established independently; the age-nine tradition became canonical and enormously influential in later law and controversy.

#### The “slander” incident

Qurʾan 24 responds to an accusation of sexual misconduct against an unnamed woman. Sīra and hadith identify her as ʿĀʾisha, accidentally left behind after an expedition and returned by Ṣafwān ibn al-Muʿaṭṭal. The revelation rejects the accusation and establishes evidentiary rules for adultery allegations.

### Ḥafṣa bint ʿUmar

Daughter of ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb and widow of Khunays ibn Ḥudhāfa. Later tradition associates her with sheets of Qurʾanic material used in ʿUthmān’s recension.

### Zaynab bint Khuzayma

Remembered as “Mother of the Poor.” She died shortly after marrying Muhammad.

### Umm Salama/Hind bint Abī Umayya

Widow, early migrant, and influential counselor. At Hudaybiyya she reportedly advised Muhammad to begin the sacrificial rites personally when followers hesitated.

### Zaynab bint Jaḥsh

Muhammad’s cousin and former wife of his freedman and adopted son **Zayd ibn Ḥāritha**. Qurʾan 33:37 states that after Zayd ended the marriage, God married her to Muhammad so believers would not consider the former wives of adopted sons forbidden.

The Qurʾanic passage openly acknowledges that Muhammad concealed something God would disclose and feared public reaction. Later hostile narratives claim he saw Zaynab and desired her, prompting Zayd’s divorce. Other traditions say Muhammad already knew by revelation that he would marry her and nevertheless told Zayd to retain her.

Documented minimum: Zayd divorced Zaynab; Muhammad married her; the marriage was publicly controversial; Qurʾanic revelation linked it to reform of adoptive kinship.

### Juwayriyya bint al-Ḥārith

Captured in the campaign against Banū al-Muṣṭaliq. Muhammad married her after settling or offering to settle her manumission. Tradition says followers then freed many captives related to her.

The narrative combines marriage, manumission, alliance, and conquest. Her freedom of choice cannot be reconstructed from independent testimony.

### Umm Ḥabība/Ramla bint Abī Sufyān

Daughter of Muhammad’s leading Meccan opponent Abū Sufyān. She had migrated to Aksum. The Negus reportedly arranged her marriage to Muhammad.

### Ṣafiyya bint Ḥuyayy

Captured at Khaybar after the deaths of her husband and male relatives. Muhammad selected her, freed her, and married her. Muslim tradition treats manumission as her bride-price. Modern discussion emphasizes the coercive setting of captivity; no personal testimony from Ṣafiyya survives to establish her subjective consent.

### Maymūna bint al-Ḥārith

Widow and relative of several politically connected women. Usually considered Muhammad’s last wife.

### Rayḥāna bint Zayd

A woman of Jewish association captured after Banū Qurayẓa. Sources disagree whether Muhammad manumitted and married her or retained her as an enslaved concubine.

### Māriya al-Qibṭiyya

Reportedly sent from Egypt with her sister Sīrīn and other gifts. Most early sources classify Māriya as an enslaved concubine or *umm walad*, though some later presentations call her a wife.

She bore Muhammad a son, **Ibrāhīm**, who died in infancy. Tradition connects a solar eclipse on the day of his death with Muhammad’s rejection of the claim that celestial events occur for human deaths.

### Household privilege and conflict

Qurʾan 33 and 66 address Muhammad’s household, including:

- Special marital rules.
- The title “Mothers of the Believers.”
- Restrictions on remarriage after Muhammad’s death.
- Privacy and veiling regulations.
- Domestic jealousy or disclosure of confidences.
- A choice between worldly provision and continued marriage.

Interpretations of Qurʾan 66 differ. Proposed occasions include Muhammad’s relations with Māriya in Ḥafṣa’s quarters or an oath concerning honey. Neither occasion is specified by the Qurʾanic text itself.

---

## Adopted, Freed, and Enslaved Dependents

### Zayd ibn Ḥāritha

Zayd was enslaved, acquired by or for Khadīja, freed by Muhammad, and adopted as “Zayd ibn Muhammad” according to tradition. Qurʾan 33 required adopted persons to be called by their biological fathers and referred to Zayd by name.

Zayd became a close military associate and died commanding the expedition to Muʾta in 629.

### Usāma ibn Zayd

Son of Zayd and Umm Ayman. Muhammad appointed the young Usāma to command an expedition shortly before his death, provoking reported objections because of his age.

### Umm Ayman/Baraka

An enslaved or dependent woman inherited from Muhammad’s family and later freed. Tradition describes her as a maternal figure in his life. She married Zayd and bore Usāma.

### Slavery

Muhammad lived in and governed a slaveholding society. Early sources list enslaved persons, freed persons, captives, and concubines connected with his household.

His reported practices included:

- Owning enslaved people.
- Receiving, distributing, ransoming, selling, and freeing captives.
- Encouraging manumission as charity or expiation.
- Prohibiting or discouraging certain abuses.
- Recognizing sexual access to female captives under the category “those whom your right hands possess.”
- Incorporating slavery and concubinage into emerging Islamic law rather than abolishing them.

Lists of Muhammad’s slaves vary and often include later legendary accumulation. Individual status, dates, and routes of acquisition frequently cannot be verified.

---

## Conquest of Mecca and Final Ascendancy

### Muʾta, 629

A Muslim force fought Byzantine-associated Arab troops near Muʾta. Zayd ibn Ḥāritha, Jaʿfar ibn Abī Ṭālib, and ʿAbd Allāh ibn Rawāḥa were killed. Khālid ibn al-Walīd assumed command and withdrew or preserved the remaining force.

Later Muslim accounts sometimes magnify opposing numbers beyond plausibility.

### Conquest of Mecca, 630

After a breach involving allies of the Hudaybiyya treaty, Muhammad advanced on Mecca with a large force. The city surrendered with limited fighting.

Reported actions included:

- General amnesty for much of Quraysh.
- Destruction of idols in or around the Kaʿba.
- Execution orders against a small list of enemies, some later pardoned.
- Confirmation of Kaʿba custodial and pilgrimage roles.
- Pledges of allegiance from Meccans.

The scale of the amnesty and exact execution list vary. Traditional sources identify people accused of murder, apostasy, political propaganda, or insulting poetry. The conquest ended the Quraysh’s organized opposition.

### Ḥunayn and Ṭāʾif

Coalitions including Hawāzin and Thaqīf fought Muhammad at Ḥunayn. Muslim forces were initially thrown into disorder but recovered. Large numbers of captives and livestock were taken.

Muhammad besieged Ṭāʾif without immediately capturing it. Hawāzin representatives later sought return of captives; traditions describe partial or extensive restoration.

Distribution of booty to recent Meccan converts caused resentment among some Medinan supporters. Muhammad reportedly addressed them emotionally, emphasizing that they would return home with him rather than livestock.

### Tabūk, 630

Muhammad led a large northern expedition to Tabūk, traditionally in response to a possible Byzantine threat. No major battle occurred. Agreements were reportedly concluded with northern communities.

The immediate Byzantine invasion threat may have been exaggerated or based on rumor. The expedition demonstrated Muhammad’s ability to mobilize and project power.

### Submission of Arabian delegations

The years 630–632 are called the “Year” or “Years of Delegations.” Tribes entered alliances, submitted, paid alms or tribute, or adopted Islam. The depth of conversion varied, as shown by rebellions after Muhammad’s death.

---

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### From warner to community ruler

The Qurʾanic corpus reflects a development from eschatological preaching in Mecca to community regulation in Medina. Muhammad’s roles expanded to include:

- Prophet and liturgical leader.
- Judge and arbitrator.
- Military commander.
- Diplomat.
- Distributor of booty.
- Regulator of marriage, inheritance, and charity.
- Leader of an intertribal polity.

Whether this constitutes “theocracy,” “state,” “confederation,” or prophetic commonwealth depends on modern definitions.

### Theology

Central teachings included:

- Radical monotheism.
- Prophecy as a recurring historical institution.
- Muhammad as messenger and final or sealing prophet.
- Resurrection, judgment, heaven, and hell.
- Angels, revelation, and scripture.
- Abraham as model monotheist.
- Continuity with and correction of Jewish and Christian traditions.
- Moral obligations involving alms, kin, orphans, debtors, and enslaved people.
- Ritual prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, and dietary rules.

### Religious identity

Scholars disagree over when a distinct “Islam” separated fully from a broader community of Abrahamic believers.

Fred Donner argues that Muhammad initially led an ecumenical “Believers’ movement” potentially including righteous Jews and Christians. Other scholars contend that distinct Qurʾanic identity and polemic were already sufficiently developed during Muhammad’s lifetime. The Medina document’s terminology and the Qurʾan’s changing rhetoric support a movement whose boundaries were developing rather than static.

### Law

Muhammad’s attributed judgments became a foundational source of Islamic law. The Qurʾan and later sunna address:

- Marriage and divorce.
- Inheritance.
- Trade and debt.
- Retaliation and compensation.
- Theft, adultery, and false accusation.
- War and peace.
- Captives and slavery.
- Charity and taxation.
- Ritual practice.

Much later jurisprudence cannot automatically be projected back into Muhammad’s lifetime; legal schools systematized and sometimes disputed the attributed precedents.

---

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Abū Bakr

Early follower, migration companion, father-in-law, donor, and eventual first caliph. Sunni memory emphasizes his priority and cave companionship. Shiʿi narratives challenge the political implications of these traditions.

### ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib

Cousin, ward, early follower, son-in-law, warrior, scribe, and later fourth caliph and first Shiʿi Imam. Traditions concerning Ghadīr Khumm, succession, and ʿAlī’s status became central to Sunni–Shiʿi division.

### ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb

Initially an opponent, later a major adviser and second caliph. Reports sometimes depict Qurʾanic rulings as confirming positions previously urged by ʿUmar.

### ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān

Early convert, twice Muhammad’s son-in-law, envoy at Hudaybiyya, later third caliph, and sponsor of the standard Qurʾanic codex.

### Ḥamza

Uncle and military supporter. His death at Uḥud generated martyr and vengeance narratives.

### Abū Sufyān and family

Principal Qurayshi leader during much of the war. Submitted around the conquest of Mecca. His daughter Umm Ḥabība was already married to Muhammad. His son Muʿāwiya later founded the Umayyad caliphate.

### Khālid ibn al-Walīd

Former Meccan commander who converted and became an important Muslim general. His conduct in the Banū Jadhīma affair reportedly caused Muhammad to disavow the killings and pay compensation.

### ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ubayy

Medinan leader characterized in Muslim sources as head of the “hypocrites.” He opposed or complicated Muhammad’s policies but remained within the community until his death.

### Poets and propagandists

Poetry was a political weapon. Important figures included:

- Ḥassān ibn Thābit, defender of Muhammad.
- Kaʿb ibn Mālik.
- ʿAbd Allāh ibn Rawāḥa.
- Kaʿb ibn Zuhayr, whose praise poem became famous.
- Kaʿb ibn al-Ashraf, assassinated opponent.
- Abū ʿAfak and ʿAṣmāʾ bint Marwān, whose alleged assassinations appear in weaker or contested traditions.

---

## Controversies, Violence, and Disputed Conduct

## Political assassinations

Early sources attribute to Muhammad authorization or approval of killings outside pitched battle, especially Kaʿb ibn al-Ashraf. Other alleged cases include:

- Abū ʿAfak.
- ʿAṣmāʾ bint Marwān.
- Ibn Sunayna.
- Sallām ibn Abī al-Ḥuqayq.

The Kaʿb and Sallām cases have comparatively strong traditional attestation. The Abū ʿAfak and ʿAṣmāʾ reports rely heavily on al-Wāqidī or Ibn Saʿd and have weak hadith transmission; their historicity is unresolved.

## Treatment of captives

Muhammad’s forces ransomed, released, exchanged, enslaved, incorporated, or executed captives depending on the episode. Claims that his practice was uniformly merciful or uniformly exterminatory are inconsistent with the varied traditional record.

## Banū Qurayẓa

The execution and enslavement narrative remains one of the principal controversies in Muhammad’s biography. The broad event is early and multiply transmitted, but the exact numbers, scope, legal reasoning, and treaty background remain uncertain.

## Marriage to Zaynab

Documented by the Qurʾan itself. The allegation that Muhammad engineered Zayd’s divorce because of sexual desire appears in later narrative elaboration. The Qurʾan says Muhammad told Zayd to retain his wife, while acknowledging concealed knowledge or intention and fear of public opinion. Motive beyond this cannot be proved.

## ʿĀʾisha’s age

The canonical age-nine report is a documented part of Sunni hadith tradition but not an independently established birth record. It influenced premodern marriage law and became a major modern polemical issue.

## Captive wives and concubines

Juwayriyya, Ṣafiyya, Rayḥāna, and Māriya connect Muhammad’s household to war captivity or slavery. Manumission and marriage altered the legal status of some women, but later accounts do not supply evidence sufficient to reconstruct subjective consent under modern criteria.

## Raiding and material gain

Muhammad’s community obtained caravan goods, land, tribute, agricultural shares, ransom, captives, and booty. The Qurʾan regulated Muhammad’s share and the distribution of spoils. These resources supported emigrants, fighters, dependents, diplomacy, and the leadership household.

Sources also depict Muhammad as living without sustained personal luxury, pawning armor, accepting gifts, and distributing wealth. Both accumulation and redistribution were elements of his government.

## Property

Important property or revenue associations included:

- Land taken from Banū al-Naḍīr.
- Khaybar agricultural shares.
- Fadak.
- A statutory share of booty.
- Gifts from followers and delegations.
- Household chambers beside the mosque.

After his death, whether these were public, prophetic, familial, or inheritable assets became politically disputed.

## Treatment of apostasy and blasphemy

Later Islamic law attributed severe rules to Muhammad, but the historical episodes combine apostasy with desertion, murder, treason, or wartime allegiance. Projecting fully developed medieval blasphemy and apostasy doctrine directly into every seventh-century case is methodologically unsafe.

## The ʿUrayna or ʿUkl punishment

Canonical hadith reports that men who killed a shepherd and stole camels were captured and subjected to mutilation, blinding, and exposure. The narrative became a precedent in discussions of banditry and retaliation. Its canonical transmission is documented; the precise historical circumstances cannot be independently verified.

---

## Finances, Administration, and Material Interests

Muhammad did not create companies, patents, or investments in the modern sense. His economic activity was embedded in kinship, trade, tribute, booty, agricultural revenue, taxation, and household property.

### Early commerce

Traditional sources associate him with:

- Caravan agency for Khadīja.
- Travel toward Syria.
- Reputation for reliability.
- Commercial partnership and brokerage.

Detailed profit figures and contracts are not preserved.

### Medinan revenue

Revenue categories included:

- Voluntary charity.
- Obligatory alms or *zakāt*.
- Booty from fighting.
- *Fayʾ*, property obtained without conventional battle.
- Ransom.
- Agricultural tribute.
- Gifts.
- Agreements with subordinate communities.

### Administrative personnel

Muhammad used:

- Scribes.
- Envoys.
- Tax and alms collectors.
- Military commanders.
- Governors or representatives.
- Market and treaty officials.

Later sources provide numerous names, but some lists may retroject later administrative institutions.

### Personal estate

Traditions depict a modest domestic material life alongside command over expanding public resources. His armor was reportedly pledged to a Jewish creditor at death. These reports became models of prophetic simplicity but should not obscure his authority over land, tribute, captives, and distribution.

---

## Final Pilgrimage

In 632 Muhammad performed the pilgrimage later called the **Farewell Pilgrimage**.

The “Farewell Sermon” survives in multiple recensions. Themes include:

- Sanctity of life and property.
- Abolition of pre-Islamic blood claims and usury.
- Duties involving women.
- Muslim solidarity.
- Transmission of the message.
- Adherence to scripture, and in some versions scripture and Muhammad’s family or sunna.

There is no single stenographic text. Later compilations combine statements delivered at different points in the pilgrimage.

### Ghadīr Khumm

On the return journey, Muhammad reportedly declared concerning ʿAlī: “For whomever I am *mawlā*, ʿAlī is his *mawlā*.”

The declaration has substantial transmission. Its interpretation is disputed:

- Shiʿi interpretation: designation of ʿAlī as successor and leader.
- Sunni interpretation: affirmation of friendship, loyalty, or status, often in response to complaints about ʿAlī.
- Historical minimum: a public statement concerning ʿAlī probably occurred; the intended political scope cannot be established apart from later sectarian interpretation.

---

## Final Illness and Death

Muhammad became ill in Medina in 632, reportedly with fever, headache, and weakness. He stayed in ʿĀʾisha’s chamber and delegated prayer leadership to Abū Bakr according to Sunni reports.

### The “pen and paper” incident

Canonical reports say Muhammad requested writing materials so he could leave a statement preventing the community from going astray. A dispute arose in the room; ʿUmar reportedly said illness had overcome him and that the Qurʾan was sufficient. Muhammad then told those present to leave or abandoned the request.

Evidentiary status: **canonical transmitted report; content of the unwritten statement unknowable**.

Sunni and Shiʿi traditions assign different significance to it, especially regarding succession.

### Cause of death

The immediate traditional description is a short febrile illness. Proposed possibilities include infection, pneumonia, pleurisy, meningitis, malaria, or complications of earlier poisoning. Retrospective medical diagnosis is impossible.

Hadith says Muhammad recalled pain from the poisoned Khaybar meat and felt as though his aorta was being cut. This establishes a traditional association, not a medically demonstrable cause after several years.

### Date

The dominant traditional date is Monday, 12 Rabīʿ I 11 AH, commonly converted to 8 June 632. Calendar conversion and reports about the weekday, pilgrimage dates, and lunar visibility create unresolved discrepancies.

### Reaction

Tradition says ʿUmar initially denied Muhammad had died. Abū Bakr entered, confirmed the death, and recited Qurʾan 3:144: Muhammad was only a messenger and messengers had passed away before him.

### Burial delay

Muhammad was washed and buried in ʿĀʾisha’s chamber. Reports place burial Tuesday night or Wednesday, after political deliberations at the Saqīfa had begun. The delay later attracted sectarian and polemical speculation.

---

## Succession Crisis

Muhammad left no universally acknowledged written succession instrument.

While his household prepared the body, Anṣār leaders met at the **Saqīfa of Banū Sāʿida**. Abū Bakr, ʿUmar, and Abū ʿUbayda attended. Abū Bakr emerged as caliph.

Competing interpretations:

- **Sunni account:** consultation produced a legitimate selection of Muhammad’s close companion.
- **Shiʿi account:** Muhammad had designated ʿAlī, particularly at Ghadīr Khumm; Saqīfa displaced his rightful succession.
- **Historical assessment:** Muhammad’s death created an immediate political crisis; no procedure recognized by every faction existed.

Several Arabian groups refused taxes, rejected Medinan authority, or followed other prophets. Abū Bakr’s Ridda wars consolidated the polity Muhammad had created.

---

## Physical Description, Habits, and Personal Character

Hadith and *shamāʾil* literature describe Muhammad as:

- Of medium height.
- Broad-shouldered.
- Thick-haired, with hair sometimes reaching the ears or shoulders.
- Full-bearded.
- Reddish-white or brown-complexioned, depending on translation.
- Marked by a “seal of prophethood” between the shoulders.
- Walking rapidly.
- Speaking distinctly.
- Using perfume and tooth-cleaning sticks.
- Wearing simple Arabian garments.
- Enjoying honey, sweets, meat, and certain vegetables while disliking others.

These reports contributed to devotional emulation. They are not contemporary portraits and sometimes conflict.

Tradition attributes to him grief, fear, anger, humor, affection, jealousy, tactical deception in war, forgiveness, punishment, generosity, and severity. Hagiography and hostile polemic each select different portions of this corpus.

---

## Illnesses, Injuries, and Psychological Claims

Documented within tradition:

- Distress and trembling after first revelation.
- Injury at Uḥud, including facial wounds and a damaged tooth.
- Fever and headaches during final illness.
- Pain attributed to the Khaybar poisoning.
- Grief following deaths of family and followers.

Hostile Meccans reportedly called him possessed, mad, bewitched, or a poet. The Qurʾan rejects these charges. Medieval Christian polemic sometimes alleged epilepsy. Modern retrospective diagnoses—including epilepsy, temporal-lobe disorders, schizophrenia, or psychosis—lack clinical evidence and cannot be established from literary revelation accounts.

A canonical hadith reports that Muhammad was temporarily affected by magic performed by Labīd ibn al-Aʿṣam and imagined doing things he had not done. Some Muslim theologians accepted the report while limiting its effect to ordinary perception; others rejected interpretations that might compromise revelation. It is a religious tradition, not medical documentation.

---

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Birth miracles

Stories include:

- A light shining from Āmina.
- The palaces of Syria becoming visible.
- Collapse of parts of the Persian palace.
- Extinguishing of Zoroastrian sacred fire.
- Idols falling.
- Astrologers and monks predicting his birth.

These appear in later sīra, *dalāʾil al-nubuwwa*, and mawlid literature. They are hagiographic motifs paralleling legendary births of rulers and prophets.

### Splitting of the moon

Qurʾan 54:1 says the hour approached and the moon split. Hadith interprets this as a miracle witnessed near Mecca.

Alternative interpretations treat the verse as:

- A literal historical miracle.
- An eschatological future event described prophetically.
- A visionary sign.
- Apocalyptic imagery.

No independent astronomical record establishes a global lunar division.

### Trees, stones, and animals

Later traditions report:

- Trees greeting Muhammad.
- Stones praising God in his hand.
- A palm trunk crying when he stopped leaning on it.
- Animals complaining to him.
- Food and water multiplying.

These belong to miracle and devotional literature rather than independently verified biography.

### The spider web and doves at the cave

Popular Hijra folklore says a spider spun a web and doves nested at the cave entrance, misleading pursuers. The story is not in the Qurʾan and has weaker transmission than the cave episode itself.

### Baḥīrā as teacher or conspirator

Muslim story: Baḥīrā recognized signs of prophethood in the young Muhammad.

Christian polemical transformation: a heretical monk instructed Muhammad or helped fabricate Islam.

Evidence: no contemporary documentation for either claim; narrative served rival theological explanations of Muhammad’s scriptural knowledge.

### Muhammad as “Mahound”

Medieval Latin and European literature transformed Muhammad into an idol, demon, heresiarch, magician, or false god called “Mahound.” Since Muhammad preached against idols, the portrayal reflects hostile mythology rather than biography.

### Epilepsy theory

Byzantine and later European polemics sometimes explained revelation through epilepsy or fraudulent staging. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century writers medicalized the theory. No clinical record permits diagnosis.

### Poisoning conspiracy

Documented tradition accuses Zaynab bint al-Ḥārith of poisoning Muhammad at Khaybar. Later sectarian theories accused members of Muhammad’s household or political rivals of causing his death. These expansions lack early evidence sufficient to establish a conspiracy.

### Survival beyond 632

A modern revisionist argument, associated especially with Stephen Shoemaker, proposes that Muhammad may have lived into the first campaigns in Palestine, relying partly on early non-Muslim texts that speak of a prophet active with the Arabs.

Counterarguments note:

- External witnesses may use imprecise or retrospective language.
- The traditional death date is deeply embedded in Muslim chronology.
- Early conquest movements could continue under Muhammad’s name after his death.

Mainstream scholarship continues to accept death in 632, while recognizing chronological tensions in external sources.

### Illiteracy as miracle

Theological tradition made Muhammad’s inability to read or write evidence that he could not have authored the Qurʾan from earlier books. Historical interpretation of *ummī* remains unsettled, and practical literacy in late antique Arabia was not an all-or-nothing condition.

### Perfect preservation of every saying

Later devotional culture sometimes treats authenticated hadith as if they were verbatim recordings. Historical transmission involved oral recollection, paraphrase, selection, legal controversy, collection, and redaction. Canonization was a later process.

### Relics

Hair, footprints, sandals, swords, cloaks, letters, seals, and other objects have been attributed to Muhammad. Some became major Ottoman and local devotional relics. Continuous provenance is rarely demonstrable. Their historical importance lies also in patronage, ritual, dynastic legitimacy, and devotional culture.

### Visual representation

Islam has never had one universal historical rule prohibiting all images of Muhammad. Medieval Persian, Ottoman, Central Asian, and other manuscripts depicted him, sometimes unveiled and later with a flaming halo or veiled face. Modern controversy has made aniconism appear more uniform than the art-historical record supports.

---

## Works by or Attributed to Muhammad

Muhammad is not known to have authored a conventional book in his own hand. The following categories must be distinguished.

### The Qurʾan — القرآن

**Status:** Recited or proclaimed by Muhammad as divine revelation; collected and standardized after his death.

Traditional Islamic position: God is the author; Muhammad received and transmitted the text rather than composing it.

Historical-critical position: the Qurʾan is the earliest corpus of proclamations associated with Muhammad and his community. Its consonantal text was substantially stabilized in the seventh century, conventionally under Caliph ʿUthmān.

Original proclamation: approximately 610–632.

Compilation history:

- Individual written and memorized units during Muhammad’s life.
- Traditional first collection under Abū Bakr.
- Standardization under ʿUthmān.
- Later development of vowel marks, reading conventions, verse numbering, and orthography.

### The Medina Compact — صحيفة المدينة / كتاب المدينة

**Date:** Conventionally 622–627.

**Status:** Document issued or agreed under Muhammad’s authority; preserved only in later literary sources. It may combine several agreements.

### Treaty of Hudaybiyya

**Date:** 628.

**Status:** Historical treaty with Quraysh. Wording survives in variant hadith and sīra recensions, not as an uncontested original manuscript.

### Diplomatic letters

Attributed recipients include Heraclius, Khusrau II, al-Muqawqis, the Negus, al-Mundhir ibn Sāwā, and other rulers.

**Status:** Mixed. Some textual cores may preserve authentic diplomatic practice; surviving decorated manuscripts and supposed originals often have late provenance or paleographic problems.

### Covenants with Christian communities

Attributed covenants include agreements with:

- Christians of Najrān.
- Monks of Mount Sinai.
- Other Christian groups.

**Status:** Disputed and recension-dependent. Some scholars argue that later manuscripts preserve early documentary cores; others date major forms to later Christian–Muslim legal negotiation. The famous Ashtiname at Saint Catherine’s does not survive as a securely authenticated seventh-century original.

### Farewell Sermon — خطبة الوداع

**Date:** 632.

**Status:** A collection of statements transmitted in multiple versions. No single continuous text can be authenticated as a verbatim transcript.

### Hadith and sunna

Major Sunni collections include:

- Mālik ibn Anas, *al-Muwaṭṭaʾ*.
- Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal, *al-Musnad*.
- Al-Bukhārī, *al-Jāmiʿ al-ṣaḥīḥ*.
- Muslim ibn al-Ḥajjāj, *al-Ṣaḥīḥ*.
- Abū Dāwūd, *al-Sunan*.
- Al-Tirmidhī, *al-Jāmiʿ*.
- Al-Nasāʾī, *al-Sunan*.
- Ibn Māja, *al-Sunan*.

Important Shiʿi collections include:

- Al-Kulaynī, *al-Kāfī*.
- Ibn Bābawayh/al-Ṣadūq, *Man lā yaḥḍuruhu al-faqīh*.
- Al-Ṭūsī, *Tahdhīb al-aḥkām*.
- Al-Ṭūsī, *al-Istibṣār*.

**Status:** Posthumous collections of attributed speech and conduct, not works written or edited by Muhammad.

### Supplications, sermons, and correspondence

Thousands of prayers, aphorisms, letters, sermons, medical prescriptions, predictions, and legal decisions circulate under Muhammad’s name.

**Status:** Individually variable. Attribution must be evaluated report by report; no comprehensive collection can be assumed entirely authentic.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Religious authority

For Muslims, Muhammad became:

- The final prophet.
- Transmitter of the Qurʾan.
- Model of conduct.
- Intercessor in many theological traditions.
- Focus of blessings, praise poetry, pilgrimage, relic devotion, and imitation.
- Source, through his sunna, of law and ritual.

### Caliphal and dynastic legitimacy

Successive caliphates claimed to continue his community. Political legitimacy drew on:

- Companionship.
- Qurayshi lineage.
- Kinship with Muhammad.
- Descent through Fāṭima and ʿAlī.
- Custodianship of holy cities or relics.
- Defense and enforcement of prophetic law.

Abbasids claimed descent from his uncle al-ʿAbbās. Numerous dynasties claimed descent through Fāṭima and ʿAlī.

### Sunni–Shiʿi division

Disputes over ʿAlī, Fāṭima, Fadak, Ghadīr Khumm, Saqīfa, and Muhammad’s final illness became foundational to competing memories of authority.

### Sufi devotion

Sufi traditions developed doctrines of:

- Muhammadan light.
- Pre-existent Muhammadan reality.
- Spiritual inheritance.
- Visionary encounters.
- Devotional poetry and blessings.
- Muhammad as perfect human being.

These metaphysical systems are posthumous developments rather than recoverable statements of Muhammad’s own lifetime.

### Christian and European reception

Muhammad was successively represented as:

- False prophet.
- Heretic or schismatic.
- Idol or demon.
- Political legislator.
- Impostor.
- Enthusiast.
- Religious genius.
- Social reformer.
- Revolutionary.
- Statesman.
- Imperial conqueror.

Enlightenment writers sometimes used Muhammad to criticize Christianity or praise rational monotheism; others used him as an emblem of fanaticism or despotism. Nineteenth-century Orientalism combined philology with colonial assumptions.

### Modern Muslim reform and nationalism

Modern writers have presented Muhammad as:

- Constitutional founder.
- Social egalitarian.
- Anti-racist leader.
- Feminist reformer.
- Military strategist.
- Entrepreneur.
- Socialist or anti-capitalist.
- Defender of property and markets.
- Democratic leader.
- Authoritarian lawgiver.

These portraits generally select genuine elements of the source tradition but often translate them into modern ideological categories.

### Polemical afterlives

Modern controversies concentrate on:

- ʿĀʾisha’s age.
- Slavery and concubinage.
- Banū Qurayẓa.
- Assassinations.
- Warfare and jihad.
- Blasphemy and apostasy.
- Muhammad’s marriages.
- Qurʾanic authorship.
- Medical explanations for revelation.
- Cartoons and visual depictions.

The historical questions and the later uses of those questions should be distinguished.

## Atlas Connections

Only source-supported intersections with the supplied atlas roster are included. The subject’s own duplicated appearance in the roster is not treated as a connection.

### Abu Bakr as-Siddiq

**Evidentiary label: documented or strongly reported historical relationship.**

Early follower, close political associate, migration companion, father of ʿĀʾisha, and Muhammad’s father-in-law. Qurʾan 9:40 is traditionally and very early identified with Muhammad and Abū Bakr in the cave during the Hijra. Abū Bakr became caliph immediately after Muhammad’s death.

Dates and places: Mecca and Medina, principally c. 610–632; Hijra route, 622.

### Aishah bint Abi Bakr

**Evidentiary label: documented or strongly reported family relationship.**

Wife of Muhammad and daughter of Abū Bakr. Central transmitter of reports about his household, ritual, illness, and death. The marriage is historical; her exact age at contract and consummation is disputed.

Dates and places: Medina, from the early post-Hijra period until Muhammad’s death in 632.

### Abraham

**Evidentiary label: documented textual and claimed religious connection, not a personal meeting.**

The Qurʾan explicitly presents Muhammad’s proclamation as restoring the monotheistic religion of Abraham. Abraham and Ishmael are associated with the Kaʿba in Qurʾan 2. Traditional genealogy traced Muhammad through Ishmael to Abraham, but the long genealogy is not independently documentable.

### Moses

**Evidentiary label: documented textual influence and religious typology; visionary meeting is religious tradition.**

Moses is the most frequently discussed named prophet in the Qurʾan and functions as a major model for revelation, confrontation with rulers, migration, law, and community formation. Hadith about the ascension says Muhammad met Moses and consulted him about the number of daily prayers. The textual connection is documented; the meeting is a faith claim and sacred narrative.

### Jesus of Nazareth

**Evidentiary label: documented textual connection; visionary or eschatological meetings are religious tradition.**

The Qurʾan presents Jesus as Messiah, prophet, and messenger while rejecting his divinity and crucifixion in conventional Christian form. Ascension narratives say Muhammad met Jesus in heaven. Hadith predicts Jesus’s future return. No earthly meeting is historically claimed.

### David ben Jesse

**Evidentiary label: documented textual connection, not personal contact.**

The Qurʾan names David as prophet, king, recipient of the Psalms, judge, and model of repentance. Muhammad’s movement incorporated David into its prophetic history.

### King Solomon

**Evidentiary label: documented textual connection, not personal contact.**

The Qurʾan portrays Solomon as prophet-king with authority over humans, spirits, winds, and animals. Later Islamic rulership, occult, and wisdom traditions connected Muhammad’s prophetic authority with Solomonic precedents.

### The Queen of Sheba

**Evidentiary label: documented Qurʾanic narrative connection, not personal contact.**

Although unnamed in the Qurʾan, she appears in the story of Solomon in Qurʾan 27. Later Islamic tradition calls her Bilqīs. Her connection to Muhammad is textual and religious, not biographical.

### Elizabeth

**Evidentiary label: indirect scriptural-traditional connection.**

The Qurʾan recounts Zechariah and the miraculous birth of John but does not name John’s mother as Elizabeth. Islamic exegetical traditions inherited or adapted details known from Christian narratives. No personal connection is alleged.

### Mary Magdalene and Mary, mother of Jesus

**Evidentiary finding: no supported personal connection.**

Mary, mother of Jesus, is prominent in the Qurʾan but was not separately and unambiguously named in the supplied roster except possibly through generic “Mary” entries. Mary Magdalene is not a significant named figure in the Qurʾan and no documented biographical connection exists.

### Zoroaster

**Evidentiary label: possible contextual influence only; no documented personal or explicitly acknowledged connection.**

Muhammad’s Arabia bordered the Sasanian world, and later Islamic law classified Zoroastrians in relation to protected religious communities. No reliable source documents Muhammad meeting Zoroastrians or explicitly identifying Zoroaster.

### Alexander the Great

**Evidentiary label: disputed literary association, not a personal connection.**

The Qurʾanic Dhū al-Qarnayn narrative has often been compared with late antique Alexander legends. Identification of Dhū al-Qarnayn with Alexander was common in premodern exegesis but is disputed. The Qurʾan does not name Alexander.

### Aristotle

**Evidentiary finding: no documented connection.**

Greek philosophical works entered Arabic intellectual life substantially after Muhammad’s death. No reliable source documents Muhammad reading, citing, or encountering Aristotle.

### Later atlas figures

No direct personal relationship is possible with later roster members such as Ibn ʿArabī, Rūmī, al-Junayd, al-Qushayrī, ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Jīlānī, Maimonides, Ibn Baṭṭūṭa, Dante, Gandhi, or modern political leaders. Many were deeply influenced by Muhammad or constructed interpretations of him, but this is posthumous reception rather than a crossing during his life.
