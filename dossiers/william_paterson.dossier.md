# William Paterson: Research Dossier

## Scope and Identification

This dossier concerns **William Paterson (1658–1719)**, the Scottish merchant, financial projector, founding director of the Bank of England, promoter of the Company of Scotland and the Darien colony, advocate of Anglo-Scottish union, and writer on trade and public credit.

He is not to be confused with William Paterson (1745–1806), the New Jersey statesman and United States Supreme Court justice, or William Paterson (1755–1810), the Scottish-born Australian soldier and naturalist.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented fact**: supported by contemporary records, legislation, correspondence, accounts, or reliable archival catalogues.
- **Reported fact**: asserted by later institutional or historical sources but not independently established here from a surviving primary record.
- **Allegation or disputed claim**: contemporary or later accusation for which the evidence is conflicting or incomplete.
- **Rumor, legend, or myth**: a story circulated without adequate documentary proof.
- **Inference**: a conclusion suggested by evidence but not expressly established by it.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and origin

**Documented or strongly reported facts**

- Paterson was the son of **John Paterson of Skipmyre** and **Elizabeth, also called Bethia**.
- Skipmyre was a farm in Dumfriesshire. The farmhouse traditionally identified as his birthplace was demolished in 1864.
- The family belonged to the rural Scottish population rather than the titled aristocracy. Nothing found establishes noble descent.
- Nineteenth-century antiquarian investigation encountered serious gaps in Scottish parish and local records. Even Paterson’s birth year was formerly given as 1655 or 1660 before 1658 became the accepted date.

### Siblings

His 1718 will named:

- **Janet Mounsey**, his sister, and her children.
- **Elizabeth Paterson**, another sister, who married **John Paterson the younger of Kinharvey**.

The exact number and birth order of his siblings have not been securely reconstructed.

### Marriages and stepchildren

Paterson married twice.

1. **Elizabeth Turner**, widow of **Thomas Bridge**, described as a minister of the gospel in Boston, New England.

   - **Reported fact:** Paterson’s marriage to a New England minister’s widow confirms meaningful personal as well as commercial connections with New England.
   - She died before Paterson’s permanent return to England.
   - She had children from her first marriage. Paterson remembered stepchildren in his will, but the sources consulted do not provide a complete, securely identified list.

2. **Hannah Kemp**, widow of **Samuel South**.

   - She accompanied Paterson on the first Darien expedition.
   - She and the couple’s child died in Darien, apparently from the epidemic disease that afflicted the settlement.
   - Some later retellings call the child a son, others a daughter. The older *Dictionary of National Biography* says “one son”; because the surviving accounts are not uniform, the child’s sex should not be treated as entirely certain without examination of the original death list.

Paterson had no known surviving biological children. His testamentary beneficiaries included stepchildren, nieces, nephews, and other relations.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1658–1681

The surviving record of Paterson’s first two decades is exceptionally thin.

### Birth and upbringing

He was born at Skipmyre in April 1658. Later writers described him as having lived there until approximately age seventeen, but this has not been established from a contemporary household record.

### Education

No school or university enrolment has been found.

A modern banking history states that he is believed to have attended a local parish school in Dumfries. His adult writings and career demonstrate:

- advanced commercial numeracy;
- knowledge of bookkeeping, revenue, credit, navigation, geography, and joint-stock organization;
- ability to work with English, Scottish, Dutch, German, and Atlantic commercial networks;
- familiarity with travel literature and manuscript geographical intelligence.

Whether this learning came from formal schooling, apprenticeship, self-education, commercial employment, or some combination is unresolved.

### Bristol tradition

**Reported fact with uncertain details:** Paterson reportedly went as a young man to Bristol and lived with an older female relative of his mother. One version says she died around 1674 and left him a small legacy or property, which he sold to finance travel to the Caribbean.

This story appears in later biographies and is compatible with his subsequent Atlantic career, but the relative has not been securely identified in the sources consulted.

### Covenanter-exile story

**Rumor or legend:** An old account, later used by Eliot Warburton and other biographers, says the adolescent Paterson left Scotland because he was suspected of communicating with persecuted Covenanters.

No direct judicial, parish, or government record establishing his flight for religious reasons has been located. The tale became part of a sympathetic mythology presenting him as a principled dissenter forced into exile.

### Missionary and buccaneer stories

**Rumor or myth:** Early hostile and romantic literature represented Paterson as, successively, a pack-pedlar, religious missionary, and buccaneer in the West Indies.

The older *Dictionary of National Biography* traced versions of the story to *A Defence of the Scots Abdicating Darien* (1700) and the satirical poem *Caledonia, or the Pedlar Turn’d Merchant*. It concluded that the claims lacked evidence “of any value.”

What can be said more securely is:

- Paterson did spend substantial time in the West Indies.
- He acquired unusually detailed knowledge of Caribbean and Central American commerce.
- He knew or used information supplied by men connected with buccaneering, notably **Lionel Wafer**; some accounts also connect him with **William Dampier**.
- His commercial library included published buccaneer narratives.
- None of this proves that Paterson personally sailed as a pirate.

## Atlantic and European Formation, approximately 1681–1691

### Merchant Taylors’ Company

The first firm chronological record of Paterson’s adult career is his admission to the **Merchant Taylors’ Company of London by redemption on 16 November 1681**. “By redemption” meant that he purchased entry rather than completing the company’s conventional apprenticeship route.

He was admitted to the company’s livery on **21 October 1689**.

The Merchant Taylors’ Company provided access to London’s organized mercantile community. Membership did not mean that Paterson necessarily worked primarily as a tailor; by the late seventeenth century the company included merchants and financiers operating well beyond the craft.

### West Indies and New England

Paterson later said that before the Revolution of 1688 he had gained experience “abroad and at home in matters of general trade and revenues.” Older accounts place him in the Caribbean for seven or eight years, including Jamaica and possibly the Bahamas.

**Documented general fact:** he possessed Caribbean trading experience and New England connections.

**Unresolved details:** no complete itinerary, employer, partnership ledger, cargo list, or surviving account book has been located. Claims that he worked specifically in Jamaica or the Bahamas remain plausible but incompletely documented.

### Slavery and the Atlantic economy

Two distinct claims require separation.

1. **Personal slave-trading claim — unverified or insufficiently documented:** Some modern summaries state that Paterson made his fortune “primarily through the slave trade with the West Indies.” The sources consulted did not supply cargo manifests, partnership books, insurance policies, bills of sale, or port records directly demonstrating that Paterson personally financed or transported enslaved people. The statement should not be repeated as established fact without such evidence.

2. **Darien Company’s planned participation in slave-based commerce — documented institutional context:** The Company of Scotland was authorized to trade in Africa and the Indies, and present-day museum research identifies the intended colonial-commercial system as including the trafficking of enslaved Africans and their forced labor, including projected labor in mines. The expedition also encountered Atlantic traders whose cargoes included enslaved people. Paterson’s project therefore belonged to a colonial economy structurally connected to slavery, even though the precise extent of his personal earlier participation remains unresolved.

### Amsterdam and the Revolution of 1688

Paterson was reportedly “much in the coffee-houses of Amsterdam” during the preparations for the Revolution of 1688. Later accounts state that he associated with supporters of William of Orange.

**Reported fact:** Paterson supported the political-financial order that followed the overthrow of James II and the accession of William III and Mary II.

**Unresolved question:** His exact operational role in revolutionary planning is not established. No evidence located shows that he held an official intelligence or military post.

### Early Darien conception

Paterson later dated his Darien conception to approximately **1684**. Before securing Scottish backing, he reportedly made approaches to:

- the government of James II;
- Frederick William, Elector of Brandenburg;
- Emden;
- Bremen;
- Dutch or Amsterdam interests.

These proposals aimed to place a commercially open entrepôt on the Isthmus of Panama, linking Atlantic and Pacific trade and shortening the European route to Asia.

No government adopted the plan.

### Influences

Paterson’s formation combined:

- Dutch and English public-credit experiments;
- the experience of chartered trading companies;
- Amsterdam’s commercial institutions and coffee-house networks;
- Caribbean and New England trade;
- geographical intelligence from voyagers and buccaneers;
- opposition to closed commercial monopolies;
- belief that predictable public credit and wider trade would enlarge state revenue.

His later maxim, often paraphrased as “trade will increase trade, and money will beget money,” expressed a cumulative view of commerce and credit.

## Bank of England and London Projects, 1691–1695

### National-bank proposal

In July–October 1691 Paterson, **Michael Godfrey**, and other London merchants promoted a national bank.

The Bank of England archive preserves records relating to:

- a society meeting on 14 October 1691;
- Paterson’s letter of 21 October 1691 to **William Blathwayt**, secretary-at-war;
- another letter dated 23 October referring to New York;
- transcriptions of further Paterson correspondence.

In January 1692 Paterson was the leading witness before a House of Commons committee receiving proposals for raising supplies. He represented that he and associates could advance £500,000.

### Political and fiscal setting

The English state needed dependable war finance during the Nine Years’ War against France. Parliamentary appropriation of taxes gave lenders greater confidence that government debts would be honored.

The adopted scheme offered:

- a £1.2 million loan to the government;
- interest at 8 percent;
- incorporation of the subscribers as the Governor and Company of the Bank of England;
- banking operations and transferable stock.

The enabling legislation passed in 1694, and the royal charter is dated **27 July 1694**. The £1.2 million subscription was filled rapidly; the Bank of England states that 1,268 subscribers completed it in eleven days.

### Paterson’s actual position

“Founder of the Bank of England” is traditional shorthand, but it can obscure the collective nature of the institution’s creation.

**Documented fact:** Paterson’s proposal was the foundation scheme ultimately selected, and he became one of the first directors.

**Documented collaborators:**

- Michael Godfrey, merchant and first deputy governor;
- Charles Montagu, later Earl of Halifax, who assisted the scheme through Parliament;
- Sir John Houblon, the Bank’s first governor;
- a broad body of parliamentary, Treasury, City, and mercantile supporters.

Paterson held the £2,000 stock qualification required of a director. He was not the Bank’s first governor and did not own or control it personally.

### *A Brief Account of the Intended Bank of England*

An anonymous pamphlet published in London in 1694 explained the proposed institution. It has long been attributed to Paterson, but catalogues also identify Michael Godfrey as a possible author or collaborator. Authorship should therefore be described as **probable or traditional, not conclusively demonstrated**.

### Break with the Bank

In February 1695 Paterson withdrew from the Bank after conflict over his plan for reorganizing the **City of London Orphans’ Fund**.

The fund represented debts owed by the City to money administered for orphans and other creditors. Paterson proposed consolidation of the obligations into an interest-bearing institution with wider functions involving land security, trusts, settlements, and credit.

His critics regarded the proposal as:

- prejudicial to the Bank;
- a competing land-bank project;
- a conflict with his duties as a director;
- in contemporary terms, possibly a breach of trust.

Paterson argued that it did not conflict with the Bank. A sympathetic 1695 pamphlet, *Some Account of the Transactions of Mr. William Paterson in Relation to the Bank of England, and the Orphans Fund*, claimed he was effectively judged in his absence without a fair reply.

**Evidentiary conclusion:** There was a genuine corporate dispute and Paterson was outvoted. No criminal prosecution or judicial finding of fraud against him was found. He sold his Bank stock and voluntarily left the directorate, but “voluntary” does not exclude intense institutional pressure.

Paterson had approximately **£4,000** connected with the Orphans’ Fund, making the issue financially important to him.

### Hampstead and Southwark water projects

Paterson was involved in the **Hampstead Water Company**, intended to bring water from reservoirs south of Hampstead and Highgate to north London.

In December 1693 the City of London granted him authority to lay pipes to supply Southwark. He retained a financial interest in the undertaking into later life.

This was a commercial infrastructure project rather than a patented invention. No patent personally issued to Paterson has been identified.

### London residence and wealth

By the early 1690s he had a house in **St Giles-in-the-Fields** and was described as possessing a substantial and growing London business.

No complete personal balance sheet survives in the sources consulted. His apparent wealth later became tied up in the Orphans’ Fund, the Company of Scotland, and Darien-related obligations.

## Scotland, the Company of Scotland, and the Darien Project, 1695–1698

### Bank of Scotland

Modern banking histories sometimes credit Paterson with helping promote a Scottish national bank. The Bank of Scotland was founded by Scottish legislation in 1695.

**Qualification:** Paterson’s precise role is less securely established than his role in the Bank of England. **John Holland** was the principal promoter and first governor of the Bank of Scotland. Claims that Paterson “founded” both banks overstate the evidence.

### Andrew Fletcher and access to Scottish government

In 1695 **Andrew Fletcher of Saltoun** introduced Paterson to Scottish political figures. Paterson helped prepare the scheme embodied in the Act of 26 June 1695.

### Company charter

The **Act for a Company Trading to Africa and the Indies**, passed by the Parliament of Scotland on 26 June 1695, named Paterson among the London incorporators.

The Company received extensive powers:

- perpetual corporate succession;
- monopoly privileges in Scottish trade to broad overseas regions;
- authority to establish colonies, towns, forts, and factories;
- governmental and admiralty powers in its settlements;
- tax privileges;
- military and defensive powers.

This was not merely a benign free-port association. It was a sovereign-style chartered colonial company.

Paterson became a director and subscribed **£3,000** of stock.

### Capital raising and English opposition

Paterson and associates raised or obtained commitments for large sums in London. English chartered companies—especially interests threatened by Scottish competition—petitioned against the venture.

The English Parliament investigated the promoters. Paterson and twenty-two associates faced threatened impeachment on a technical allegation that their conduct infringed the Scottish Act or English commercial privilege. The proceeding was ultimately dropped.

English pressure caused London subscribers to withdraw. A later effort in Hamburg, reportedly attracting about £200,000 in promises, was frustrated when English diplomatic representatives warned Hamburg authorities that participation would offend William III.

Dutch and other European subscriptions likewise failed.

These events became central to Scottish accusations that England deliberately sabotaged a legally chartered Scottish enterprise.

### Paterson’s compensation contract

On 6 November 1695 Paterson reportedly contracted for:

- £12,000 in cash plus 3 percent of profits for twenty-one years; or
- an alternative additional payment arrangement later represented as producing a total claim near £30,000.

He abandoned his London business to work for the Company.

The directors later offered or resolved to provide only a fraction of this amount. The validity and enforceability of the original arrangement subsequently became the basis of his long Equivalent claim.

### Edinburgh

Paterson was made a burgess of Edinburgh in **1696**. He was initially popular and influential among the Company’s supporters.

### Strategic division inside the Company

Company factions differed over whether to emphasize:

- direct Asian and African trade;
- opposition to the English East India Company;
- Paterson’s free-port project in Darien.

A 5 September 1696 letter from merchant **Robert Douglas** accused Paterson of obstructing the Scottish East India trade and introducing men connected with the English East India Company into the Scottish directorship.

**Allegation:** Paterson secretly weakened the East India option in order to force adoption of his Darien scheme.

**Evidence and qualification:** His long-standing commitment to Darien is clear, and he did prefer it. No evidence located proves that he acted as a covert English agent or deliberately betrayed the Company.

### Colonial ideology

Paterson described Darien as the “door of the seas” and “key of the universe.” His plan was to establish an entrepôt that would:

- reduce the cost and duration of trade between Europe and Asia;
- permit goods to cross the isthmus rather than sail around Cape Horn;
- open Spanish-American trade to other nations;
- attract merchants through religious and commercial toleration;
- use alliances with Indigenous peoples against Spanish authority.

His texts sometimes represented the project as freeing Indigenous inhabitants from Spanish tyranny.

That emancipatory language coexisted with Company demands for rights over land, minerals, fisheries, government, and admiralty. Modern scholarship accordingly treats Darien as a project of colonial expansion, even where its advocates used anti-monopoly and anti-Spanish arguments.

### The James Smyth loss

In 1697 Paterson and other agents were commissioned to buy ships and supplies abroad with approximately **£25,000**. London merchant **James Smyth**, engaged to handle the Company’s funds and retire bills, absconded or defaulted with a large portion.

A substantial amount was recovered, but more than **£8,000** remained missing. Paterson was blamed because of his role in employing or trusting Smyth.

A Company committee headed by **William Dunlop**, principal of Glasgow College, and **Robert Blackwood** investigated.

**Reported finding:** The committee exonerated Paterson of personal dishonesty. Paterson reportedly contributed about **£6,000 of his own resources** toward the loss.

Despite the exoneration, the affair destroyed much of his internal authority. He offered either to leave the Company or to serve it on reduced terms. He was not restored to effective command.

No evidence found supports the claim that Paterson personally embezzled the missing funds.

## The First Darien Expedition, 1698–1699

### Departure

Five vessels comprised the first expedition:

- *Caledonia*
- *St Andrew*
- *Unicorn*
- *Dolphin*
- a smaller vessel generally called the *Endeavour* or “Snow” in institutional summaries

Approximately 1,200 people sailed from Leith in July 1698. Sources give 14, 16, or 26 July depending on whether they refer to embarkation, movement within the roads, or final departure. The fleet took the northern route around Scotland.

Paterson traveled with Hannah and their child.

### Lack of formal authority

Although he was the central projector, Paterson was not one of the seven governing councillors. Management was vested in a rotating council whose members repeatedly disagreed.

Paterson’s own later report said that before departure he tried to have the council inspect the expedition’s readiness and was rebuffed by Captain Robert Pennecuik.

### Atlantic route

The expedition called at or passed Madeira and the Caribbean, including waters around Puerto Rico. It dealt with traders and privateers for supplies and information.

A privateer named **William Alliston or Allison**, who knew the region, was associated with guiding or advising the expedition. Paterson’s access to buccaneer geography was practically important, even though it does not establish that he himself had been a buccaneer.

### Landing

The fleet reached the Darien coast on **2 November 1698** and established:

- the colony of **Caledonia or New Caledonia**;
- the settlement of **New Edinburgh**;
- **Fort St Andrew**;
- a harbor and projected free-trading station.

The location is in the Guna Yala/Darién region of present-day Panama.

### Indigenous relations

The Scots negotiated with local Indigenous leaders, ancestors of or associated with the modern Guna.

The Company’s papers represented the Indigenous parties as voluntarily receiving the Scots. Agreements purported to give the colonists extensive rights over land, resources, government, and trade.

Important qualifications:

- The parties may not have shared the same understanding of permanent land alienation and sovereignty.
- Indigenous communities were pursuing their own strategy against Spanish pressure and other European actors.
- Cooperation was neither uniform nor permanent.
- Later promotional literature exaggerated uncomplicated Indigenous enthusiasm for the colony.

### Material conditions

The settlers constructed huts and fortifications, but the site presented severe difficulties:

- torrential rain and tropical humidity;
- malaria, yellow fever, dysentery, or other poorly differentiated “fevers”;
- inadequate and spoiled provisions;
- unsuitable European trade goods;
- poor agricultural results;
- internal factionalism;
- absence of reliable resupply;
- lack of an established trans-isthmian transport system;
- Spanish military hostility.

Popular accounts emphasize cargoes such as wigs, woolen clothing, shoes, Bibles, and clay pipes. These examples symbolize mismanagement but should not be treated as the colony’s entire inventory.

### English embargo

English colonial governors were instructed not to assist the Scottish settlement. Jamaica and other English colonies refused or restricted supplies.

**Documented political context:** William III was also king of Spain’s wartime ally and wished to avoid provoking Spain. English trading companies feared Scottish competition.

The embargo materially worsened the colony’s position but was not the sole cause of failure. Disease, inadequate planning, governance failures, geography, and Spanish opposition were independently severe.

### Family deaths and illness

Hannah Paterson died of fever shortly after landing. Their child also died.

Paterson became seriously ill. During the evacuation and voyage north he suffered what contemporaries or later biographers called “trouble of mind,” temporarily depriving him of reason.

This may describe delirium caused by tropical disease, grief, exhaustion, psychological collapse, or a combination. No modern clinical diagnosis is possible.

### Abandonment

The first colony was abandoned in June 1699. Survivors sailed in desperate conditions toward Jamaica, New York, and other ports.

The *Caledonia* and surviving colonists reached New York in August 1699. Paterson remained extremely ill.

He returned aboard the *Caledonia* and arrived in Edinburgh on **5 December 1699**. On 19 December he submitted a detailed report to the Company’s directors.

### Paterson’s responsibility

The evidentiary record permits several distinct findings:

- He was the most persistent promoter of the Darien location.
- He helped induce the Company to make Darien its central project.
- He did not hold supreme operational command during the expedition.
- He objected to aspects of the preparation and government.
- He joined the settlers and personally shared their disease, bereavement, and danger.
- Afterward he defended the underlying project rather than repudiating it.

Thus, neither “sole commander personally responsible for every failure” nor “powerless bystander” accurately describes his role.

## The Second Darien Expedition and Final Collapse, 1699–1700

A second expedition left Scotland before reliable news of the first abandonment arrived. It reoccupied the site in November 1699.

Paterson was not present.

The second settlement faced:

- continuing disease and scarcity;
- divided leadership;
- Spanish attack;
- siege of Fort St Andrew.

After a defensive success at Toubacanti but worsening strategic conditions, the colonists capitulated to Spanish forces in 1700 and evacuated.

Across both expeditions approximately 2,500 settlers were dispatched. Modern estimates commonly state that more than 2,000 died, though exact counts differ because some survivors dispersed through the Caribbean and North America rather than returning to Scotland.

The subscribed Company capital was roughly **£400,000 sterling**. Estimates identifying this as one-fifth, one-quarter, or nearly half of Scotland’s available capital depend on different definitions of national wealth and liquidity. “A very large proportion of Scotland’s investible capital” is secure; a precise national percentage is not.

## Return, Political Economy, and Union, 1700–1707

### Continued Darien advocacy

Paterson did not conclude that the isthmus idea itself was impossible. He tried to revive Darien as a wider British or internationally supported project.

His 1701 memorial proposed a colony that would open South American commerce and oppose Spanish monopoly. The combination of free-trade language, geopolitical strategy, and colonial appropriation remained intact.

### William III

Paterson returned to London and was received by William III in April 1701. He reportedly held repeated private conferences with the king on:

- public credit;
- national debt;
- revenue administration;
- the West Indies;
- union with Scotland.

No evidence indicates a formal ministerial office. He operated as an adviser and projector.

### Fiscal proposals

Paterson urged:

- dependable interest payments on existing public debts;
- clearer Treasury and Exchequer administration;
- regular investigation of revenue officials;
- a commission to examine the national debt;
- consolidation and conversion of debt;
- a sinking mechanism for debt redemption;
- commercial union between England and Scotland.

Later writers credited these plans as antecedents of Walpole’s sinking fund and the 1717 debt-conversion scheme. Paterson did not personally administer Walpole’s later fund, and calling him its sole “inventor” exaggerates a broader evolution in public finance.

### Public library of trade

In 1703 Paterson proposed or attempted to establish a public library devoted to trade and finance. His catalogue called for:

- books and pamphlets on commerce;
- schemes of public finance;
- histories;
- voyages;
- accounts of laws and customs;
- materials showing how war, conquest, government, scarcity, and abundance affected national industry.

His collection contained works in English, French, German, and Dutch. A catalogue survives in British Library Harley Manuscript 4684.

### Political and social views

Paterson’s later writings supported:

- wide commercial exchange;
- religious toleration as an inducement to settlement and trade;
- systematic public accounting;
- protection of public creditors;
- universal or broadly available education;
- relief for honest debtors from imprisonment;
- productive employment rather than merely punitive confinement for offenders;
- political union accompanied by equalized commercial rights.

His “free trade” was not identical to nineteenth-century laissez-faire. He also favored chartered corporations, public commissions, strategic colonies, and active state organization.

### Dispute with John Law

In 1705 Paterson answered proposals by **John Law** for expanding Scottish currency through paper money not immediately convertible into specie.

Paterson’s *The Occasion of Scotland’s Decay in Trade* and *An Essay Concerning Inland and Foreign, Publick and Private Trade* opposed the plan. The Scottish Parliament did not adopt Law’s proposal.

The disagreement was intellectual and political, not a lawsuit or personal feud. Both men sought to solve Scotland’s scarcity of money but proposed different credit architectures.

### Advocacy of union

Paterson advocated an incorporating union between Scotland and England, arguing that Scotland required:

- access to English colonial and overseas markets;
- stable public credit;
- proportionate representation;
- adjusted taxation;
- compensation for financial burdens and the Darien loss.

He wrote pro-union material and worked with ministers, including interests associated with **Sidney Godolphin** and later **Robert Harley**.

He assisted **David Gregory** and **William Bower** in calculating the “Equivalent,” the sum payable to Scotland to compensate for assuming a share of English public liabilities and for other fiscal adjustments. Paterson received £200 for this work.

### Treaty participation

Contemporary and later accounts credit Paterson with a “great share” in framing commercial and financial provisions, although he was not one of the formally appointed commissioners who signed the Treaty of Union.

He went to Scotland in 1706, followed the parliamentary negotiations, explained the terms, and promoted ratification.

On 25 March 1707 the final Scottish Parliament recommended him to Queen Anne “for his good service.”

## Parliamentary Candidacy and Later Political Position, 1707–1719

### Dumfries Burghs election

Paterson stood for the newly created Westminster constituency of Dumfries Burghs after union.

A double return named Paterson and **William Johnstone**. The House of Commons ultimately declared Paterson not duly elected, and he did not retain the seat.

It is therefore misleading to describe him simply as a serving MP. He was returned in a disputed election but unseated before taking an effective parliamentary role.

### The Equivalent and personal compensation

The Union settlement provided **£398,085 10s** as the Equivalent. Part was allocated to reimburse Company of Scotland proprietors and creditors.

Paterson’s contractual claim against the Company had been omitted from the original schedule.

He repeatedly petitioned:

- In 1713 the Commons reported favorably and passed a bill awarding approximately £18,000.
- The House of Lords rejected or failed to pass that bill.
- In 1715, under George I, legislation granted him **£18,241** in Equivalent Company debentures.

The parliamentary report on his petition survives.

### Was compensation corrupt payment for supporting union?

**Allegation or political interpretation:** Paterson’s opponents and later nationalist narratives have treated his compensation as a reward for helping “sell” Scotland.

**Documented facts:**

- He had a substantial pre-existing contractual and loss claim against the Company.
- The claim predated the Treaty of Union.
- Its omission forced years of petitioning.
- The Lords initially blocked payment.
- He was not paid until 1715, eight years after union.

**Unresolved interpretive issue:** His support for union aligned with his financial interest in a settlement that reimbursed Company creditors. That conflict of interest is real. The record located does not establish a secret bribe or an agreement conditioning payment on his political advocacy.

### South Sea Company and government advice

Paterson received small payments for services associated with the **South Sea Company** and continued advising ministers on public credit.

No evidence was found that he held a dominant management position in the Company or participated in the speculative “South Sea Bubble,” which peaked after his death.

### Reduced circumstances

From approximately 1703 he lived in **Queen Square, Westminster**, where rate records placed him among the higher-rated residents.

Nevertheless, accounts describe him as financially constrained before the 1715 award. He is said to have taught mathematics and navigation for income.

The combination is not necessarily contradictory: social status, property assessment, illiquid claims, debts, and ready cash could diverge sharply.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Merchant Taylors’ Company

- Member by redemption, 16 November 1681.
- Liveryman, 21 October 1689.
- Principal institutional gateway into London commerce.

### Bank of England

- Chief or leading projector of the selected 1691–1694 plan.
- Original director, 1694–1695.
- Held the £2,000 director’s stock qualification.
- Left after the Orphans’ Fund dispute.
- Had no permanent ownership or dynastic control over the Bank.

### Orphans’ Fund

- Proposed consolidation in February 1695.
- Personally exposed for approximately £4,000.
- Conflict helped end his Bank directorship.
- No criminal finding of misconduct located.

### Hampstead Water Company and Southwark water supply

- Participated in a company developing London water infrastructure.
- Received a 1693 license concerning pipes in Southwark.
- Retained or sold interests later in life.
- No personal engineering patent identified.

### Bank of Scotland

- Associated by later banking histories with the climate of promotion.
- John Holland, not Paterson, was the principal formal founder and first governor.
- Paterson’s exact institutional contribution requires further archival verification.

### Company of Scotland Trading to Africa and the Indies

- Named incorporator and London director in the 1695 Act.
- £3,000 subscriber.
- Promoter, international fundraiser, purchasing agent, and Darien advocate.
- Lost influence after the Smyth affair.
- Accompanied the expedition without a seat on the colonial council.

### Darien/New Caledonia

- Projected free port, colony, fortress, and trans-isthmian commercial hub.
- No canal technology was proposed in an engineering sense comparable to the later Panama Canal; movement was expected through overland and river transport.
- The project did not establish durable trade or colonial occupation.

### South Sea Company

- Later compensated for limited advisory or financial work.
- Precise role was small and incompletely documented.

### Library of Trade and Finance

- An information infrastructure project intended to collect commercial knowledge.
- Proposed in 1703.
- Surviving catalogue demonstrates the breadth of Paterson’s reading.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Michael Godfrey

Friend and commercial collaborator in the national-bank proposal. Godfrey became the Bank of England’s first deputy governor and died in 1695 while accompanying William III near Namur.

### Charles Montagu

Treasury politician whose parliamentary work was important in converting the bank proposal into legislation. His involvement limits any account that treats the Bank as Paterson’s solo creation.

### Sir John Houblon

First governor of the Bank of England and a major figure among the institution’s merchant founders.

### William Blathwayt

Government official to whom Paterson addressed his October 1691 national-bank proposal. The correspondence is preserved in the Bank of England archive.

### Andrew Fletcher of Saltoun

Introduced Paterson to Scottish political circles in 1695. Fletcher later opposed incorporating union, placing the two men on different sides of Scotland’s constitutional settlement.

### Robert Blackwood and William Dunlop

Company of Scotland figures who investigated the Smyth loss and reportedly cleared Paterson of personal dishonesty.

### James Smyth

London merchant and Company financial agent whose disappearance or default with Company funds caused a loss exceeding £8,000 and severely damaged Paterson’s career.

### Lionel Wafer

Buccaneer, traveler, and author of *A New Voyage and Description of the Isthmus of America* (1695). His manuscript and published geographical intelligence informed the Company’s assessment of Darien.

Accounts differ on whether Wafer positively encouraged the final site selection or warned that the plan was impractical. Paterson’s access to Wafer’s material is documented; the content and timing of every personal consultation are not.

### William Dampier

Navigator, author, and former buccaneer. Later biographies say Paterson acquired Darien information from him. The precise documentary evidence for direct meetings is weaker than for Wafer.

### Robert Douglas

London Scottish merchant who accused Paterson in 1696 of obstructing Scottish East India ambitions and introducing English East India interests.

### William III

Received Paterson in 1701 and consulted him about public credit, foreign strategy, and union. Earlier, the king’s English government had helped block foreign aid and subscriptions to the Scottish Company.

### Sidney Godolphin and Robert Harley

Ministers to whom Paterson submitted fiscal and political proposals. His union propaganda aligned with government policy.

### David Gregory and William Bower

Collaborators in calculating the Union Equivalent.

### John Law

Rival Scottish monetary theorist. Paterson’s 1705 publications answered Law’s paper-currency proposals.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Religion

No reliable evidence establishes ordination, missionary employment, or a sustained clerical vocation.

His writings reflected Protestant Britain’s anti-Catholic and anti-Spanish political culture. Darien propaganda portrayed Spanish rule as tyrannical and sometimes invoked protection of Protestant commerce and Indigenous peoples.

Paterson also favored religious toleration where it aided settlement and trade.

### From merchant to financial projector

His intellectual development can be reconstructed in stages:

1. Atlantic merchant experience exposed him to long-distance trade, piracy, monopoly, colonial government, and scarcity of credit.
2. Amsterdam and London demonstrated how organized credit could support states and commerce.
3. The Bank proposal joined private capital to parliamentary taxation.
4. The Orphans’ Fund and water schemes extended his interest to urban and land-based credit.
5. Darien attempted to apply commercial openness through a chartered colonial enclave.
6. After the disaster, he focused increasingly on public accounting, debt consolidation, union, education, and institutional knowledge.

### Ideological tensions

Paterson combined ideas that later traditions sometimes separate:

- free commerce and chartered monopoly;
- cosmopolitan trade and Scottish national economic development;
- Indigenous alliance rhetoric and colonial sovereignty;
- public accountability and profit-seeking corporations;
- private investment and state-backed public debt;
- opposition to some monopolies while creating a privileged Company of Scotland.

These were features of his actual program, not necessarily contradictions as understood in late-seventeenth-century political economy.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### 1. Buccaneering

**Classification:** Rumor or unverified claim.

The story says Paterson was a Caribbean buccaneer before becoming a banker. It appears in hostile pamphleteering and later romantic biography. His connections with buccaneers and their writings are real, but no ship’s roll, trial, pardon, commission, or first-person confession establishes personal piracy.

### 2. Personal slave trading

**Classification:** Unresolved claim.

Modern summaries sometimes state that his London fortune came primarily from West Indian slave trading. The present search did not recover transaction-level primary evidence proving that claim. His milieu and later colonial company were unquestionably tied to slave-based Atlantic commerce.

### 3. Conflict of interest at the Bank

**Classification:** Documented corporate accusation; no judicial finding.

His colleagues considered the Orphans’ Fund proposal prejudicial to the Bank and possibly a breach of trust. Paterson denied conflict. He lost the board dispute and left. No evidence of criminal conviction or civil judgment was found.

### 4. Manipulating the Company toward Darien

**Classification:** Documented policy struggle; allegation of improper motive unproved.

Robert Douglas and others accused him of obstructing Asian trade to privilege his private obsession with Darien. Paterson openly and persistently preferred Darien. Evidence does not establish covert sabotage on behalf of English competitors.

### 5. Loss or embezzlement of Company money

**Classification:** Smyth’s default documented; Paterson’s complicity not established.

James Smyth disappeared or defaulted with Company funds. Paterson bore responsibility for trusting him and suffered professional damage. The investigating committee reportedly exonerated Paterson, and he contributed personal money to reduce the loss.

### 6. Reckless colonial promotion

**Classification:** Historical criticism grounded in documented results.

Paterson promoted a colony in a place he had apparently never personally visited. The Company relied heavily on travel narratives and did not conduct an adequate independent survey. The expedition proved disastrously vulnerable to disease, supply failures, internal division, and Spain.

Claims that Paterson alone chose every supply, controlled the colonial council, or commanded the expedition are false or exaggerated. Nevertheless, he was the most important advocate of the location and cannot be separated from the decision.

### 7. Colonial dispossession and slavery

**Classification:** Documented institutional design; extent of implementation limited by failure.

The charter and proposed agreements asserted European corporate rights over Indigenous land, minerals, fisheries, government, and admiralty. The scheme contemplated participation in slave-based Atlantic commerce. The colony failed before a mature plantation or slave-labor system developed.

### 8. Responsibility for mass death

**Classification:** Causal and moral interpretation, not a court finding.

More than 2,000 settlers may have died across the expeditions. Paterson’s advocacy was a major causal link, but deaths resulted immediately from disease, malnutrition, maritime losses, poor administration, Spanish military pressure, and the English colonial embargo. No criminal investigation or prosecution of Paterson followed.

### 9. “Bankrupting Scotland”

**Classification:** Popular simplification.

The loss was nationally severe and disproportionately affected politically influential investors. It did not literally render every Scot or the Scottish state legally bankrupt. Estimates of the lost share of national capital range widely and depend on whether “wealth,” “cash,” or “investible capital” is measured.

### 10. Selling Scotland through the Union

**Classification:** Propaganda and contested interpretation.

The story portrays Paterson as creating the Darien disaster and then helping England absorb Scotland so that he and fellow investors could be repaid.

Supported elements:

- He promoted Darien.
- He advocated union.
- Union provided for Company reimbursement.
- He later recovered £18,241.

Unsupported or unproved elements:

- a prearranged plan to make Darien fail;
- secret English direction of Paterson;
- a covert bargain exchanging his political support for payment;
- his receipt of compensation before ratification.

### 11. Central-banking conspiracy quotations

**Classification:** Modern conspiracy mythology.

Internet posts repeatedly attribute to Paterson the statement that the Bank profited from “interest on all moneys which it creates out of nothing.”

No contemporary source located in this research established that exact wording as Paterson’s. It appears widely in modern anti-central-bank quotation collections without manuscript, pamphlet page, or reliable edition reference.

The underlying historical fact is less sensational: the 1694 Bank joined a funded government loan, transferable stock, deposit banking, and note issue. It was a privately owned chartered corporation lending to the state, not a secret Paterson family institution.

### 12. “Inventor of national debt”

**Classification:** Myth or exaggerated shorthand.

English monarchs and governments incurred public obligations before Paterson. The financial revolution involved Parliament, tax assignment, long-term funded debt, multiple companies, bankers, and officials. Paterson was an important institutional innovator, not the sole inventor of state borrowing.

### 13. Recent adversarial narratives, 2024–2026

The recent search produced renewed criticism presenting Paterson as:

- a technically brilliant financier whose reputation improperly transferred to colonial planning;
- a promoter who relied on romanticized pirate geography;
- a man who solved, through union, a crisis he helped create;
- an emblem of Scotland’s entanglement in colonialism and projected slave trading.

The strongest factual elements of that critique—his central promotion, lack of firsthand Darien knowledge, catastrophic results, and later Equivalent claim—are documented.

More expansive suggestions that he deliberately engineered failure to force union are speculative and unsupported.

No credible recent evidence was found of an undiscovered criminal conviction, secret intelligence service, money-laundering network, or personal ownership scheme behind the Bank of England.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The packman who became a banker

**Story:** Paterson walked out of Scotland with a pack on his back, became a pedlar, and rose by genius to found the Bank.

**Appearance and circulation:** Hostile and satirical pamphlets around the Darien crisis; later popular biographies transformed insult into a self-made-man legend.

**Evidence:** His humble rural origin is credible. No contemporary apprenticeship or itinerary proves a pedlar career.

**Reputational effect:** The story served both hostile ridicule and later hagiography.

### Missionary turned pirate

**Story:** He first evangelized Caribbean inhabitants, then joined buccaneers.

**Evidence:** No contemporary missionary appointment or piracy record located.

**Probable source of myth:** His religious language, Caribbean residence, and use of Wafer, Dampier, and other buccaneer intelligence.

### The solitary founder of the Bank

**Story:** Paterson conceived and personally founded the Bank of England alone.

**Evidence against:** He was the leading projector of the adopted scheme, but Godfrey, Montagu, Houblon, Parliament, Treasury officials, and 1,268 initial subscribers were indispensable.

**Effect:** This mythology increased his later authority as a universal financial genius.

### “The Bank hath benefit of interest on all moneys it creates out of nothing”

**Story:** Paterson openly confessed that the Bank created money from nothing and enslaved society through interest.

**Evidence:** Exact contemporary source not found. The wording should be treated as apocryphal unless a verifiable seventeenth-century text is produced.

**Effect:** It is frequently used in anti-banking, sovereign-citizen, hard-money, and monetary-conspiracy discourse.

### Darien as an inevitable triumph destroyed solely by England

**Story:** Darien was sound and would have succeeded had England not embargoed it.

**Evidence:** English obstruction was real and consequential. However, severe disease, deficient supply, geographic difficulty, divided government, unrealistic trade assumptions, and Spanish sovereignty claims independently endangered the colony.

### Darien as only foolish Scottish incompetence

**Story:** The enterprise failed purely because ignorant Scots shipped wigs to a jungle.

**Evidence:** This suppresses English and Spanish pressure, European charter-company politics, global climatic and disease conditions, and the sophistication of parts of Paterson’s commercial design.

### Darien directly and automatically caused Union

**Story:** Scotland lost its money and immediately had no choice but to unite.

**Evidence:** Darien intensified fiscal pressure, elite interest in English market access, and the demand for compensation. Union also arose from dynastic succession, war, security, religion, parliamentary bargaining, and Anglo-Scottish trade conflict. The link was strong but not mechanical or inevitable.

### Grave at Sweetheart Abbey

**Story:** Paterson returned to Dumfriesshire after death and was buried in Sweetheart Abbey.

**Evidence:** Repeated by modern institutional sources but questioned in other accounts. Paterson died in London, and the chain of evidence for transporting and interring the body at New Abbey has not been securely demonstrated here.

**Status:** Tradition, plausible but unresolved.

## Health, Psychological Crisis, and Death

### Darien illness

Paterson suffered serious tropical illness during the first colony and evacuation.

Symptoms are not recorded with enough precision for diagnosis. “Fever” could encompass malaria, yellow fever, typhoid, dengue, or other infections.

### Mental crisis

His post-Darien “trouble of mind” temporarily deprived him of reason.

This is a contemporarily framed report, not a modern psychiatric diagnosis. Relevant conditions included:

- severe infectious illness;
- malnutrition and dehydration;
- loss of wife and child;
- collapse of the project;
- prolonged maritime hardship.

He recovered sufficiently to write his December 1699 report and resume political-economic work.

### Death

Paterson signed his will in Westminster on **1 July 1718**. It was certified at the Ship Tavern outside Temple Bar on 3 July.

He died in January 1719. Probate was granted at Doctors’ Commons on 22 January 1719 Old Style.

No reliable source located specifies a cause of death.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Banking

Paterson remains conventionally described as founder or co-founder of the Bank of England. The more precise formulation is that he was the leading projector of the scheme selected in 1694 and one of the first directors.

His later break with the institution meant that he did not shape its long-term administration.

### Public credit

His work belongs to the British financial revolution that made possible:

- funded national debt;
- parliamentary taxation pledged to creditors;
- liquid government securities;
- joint-stock banking;
- closer integration between private capital and state war finance.

Claims of singular invention should be avoided, but his institutional contribution was substantive.

### Scottish history

Paterson occupies several conflicting roles:

- commercial visionary;
- national economic modernizer;
- reckless promoter;
- victim of English commercial obstruction;
- architect of a colonial catastrophe;
- advocate of British union;
- beneficiary of the Equivalent.

Each image derives from genuine episodes, but political traditions emphasize different parts.

### Colonial history

Recent scholarship and museums have moved beyond treating Darien solely as a financial adventure. They place it within:

- Scottish imperial ambition;
- European competition for Indigenous territory;
- Atlantic slavery;
- Spanish imperial warfare;
- Guna resistance and diplomacy;
- the production of promotional colonial knowledge.

### Reputation in economic thought

His advocacy of commercial openness predates Adam Smith but did not amount to Smith’s later systematic political economy. His program was more directly concerned with chartered projects, public credit, and geopolitical trade corridors.

### Portrait and commemoration

The Bank of England holds or displays a portrait tradition associated with Paterson. The pelican beneath the portrait symbolizes self-sacrifice, reinforced by *sic vos non vobis*.

This iconography contributed to a hagiographic reading of Paterson as a man who created institutions for others but personally suffered or received little benefit.

### Modern political symbolism

In debates about Scottish independence and the Union, Darien is repeatedly used as:

- an example of English coercion;
- evidence of Scottish elite incompetence;
- a story of bought political union;
- an illustration of early globalization;
- a warning about overconfidence and national investment bubbles.

These applications are politically consequential but should not substitute for the documentary chronology.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are listed. Mere contemporaneity or thematic similarity has been excluded.

### John Law

**Evidentiary label: Documented fact**

- **Nature:** Intellectual and policy opponent.
- **Date and place:** Scotland, principally 1705.
- **Connection:** Law offered proposals for expanding Scottish currency through paper credit. Paterson answered them in *The Occasion of Scotland’s Decay in Trade* and *An Essay Concerning Inland and Foreign, Publick and Private Trade*. Paterson argued against an inconvertible paper-money system, and Law’s proposal was not adopted by the Scottish Parliament.
- **Qualification:** The evidence supports a published monetary controversy, not necessarily a sustained personal feud or close relationship.

### Adam Smith

**Evidentiary label: Reported intellectual afterlife; no personal connection**

- **Nature:** Later Scottish political economist whose work belongs to a tradition sometimes compared with Paterson’s arguments for enlarged commerce.
- **Dates:** Smith was born in 1723, four years after Paterson’s death.
- **Connection:** There was no meeting or correspondence. Scholars of Scottish political economy have discussed the lingering memory of Darien and broader Scottish free-trade arguments in the intellectual environment preceding Smith.
- **Qualification:** No source consulted establishes that Smith expressly acknowledged Paterson as a direct influence. This is contextual reception, not a personal Atlas crossing.

### William Paterson

**Evidentiary label: Identity entry**
