# Viktor Sulčič: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Viktor Sulčič left a substantial architectural, artistic, literary, and organizational record, but no full critical biography or comprehensive catalogue raisonné appears to be available online. The strongest accessible synthesis is Irene Mislej’s archival biography for the *Primorski slovenski biografski leksikon*, now incorporated into *Slovenska biografija*. Mislej also prepared the principal 1989 and 2010 exhibition catalogues.

Sulčič’s surviving papers include personal documents, professional-authorization records, family correspondence, correspondence with individuals and institutions, exhibition material, photographs, and architectural documentation. Much is held by the National and Study Library in Trieste; drawings and other material are held by the Pilon Gallery in Ajdovščina. Consequently, several intimate or financial aspects of his life cannot be reconstructed securely from digitized sources.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records, archival correspondence, institutional documentation, or multiple strong sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a credible secondary or retrospective source but not independently confirmed here from an underlying document.
- **Allegation or theory:** a contested interpretation or accusation.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story transmitted without adequate contemporary documentation.
- **Unresolved:** surviving accessible evidence is contradictory or insufficient.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and childhood household

**Documented fact:** Viktor was born in the Slovene village of Križ, Italian Santa Croce, on the Karst above Trieste. His parents were:

- **Jožef/Josef Sulčič**, described in one established biographical source as a farmer and in a later heritage profile as a stonemason.
- **Marija, née Bogatec**.

The descriptions “farmer” and “stonemason” need not be mutually exclusive in a Karst village household, where agricultural work and stone-related trades could coexist, but the precise occupation recorded in civil or parish documents has not been established here.

A regional biographical profile identifies a brother, **Josip/Josip Sulčič**, but the publicly indexed excerpt truncates his birth information. No dependable complete sibling list was found.

His aunt **Marjana** reportedly worked at the Italian-language Lega Nazionale primary school he attended in Križ.

### Marriage

**Reported fact, strongly supported by archival-family accounts:** In Buenos Aires in **1929**, Sulčič married **Ana/Anna Kiselicki**, a piano teacher from Vranjevo, near Novi Bečej in Vojvodina, Serbia.

Ana helped preserve his papers and artistic estate after his death. The 2010 exhibition catalogue expressly credits Sulčič’s wife and surviving son Fedor with safeguarding the material that later entered Slovenian collections.

### Children

Sources agree on two sons but conflict over Fedor’s birth year:

- **Fedor Sulcic** — one heritage profile says born in **1930**; another says **1940**, almost certainly a transcription or sequencing error because it simultaneously calls him the first son while placing Igor’s birth in 1939. The 1930 date is the stronger reading.
- **Héctor Igor Sulcic**, born **1939**; died tragically in **1951**, aged approximately twelve or thirteen.

Igor’s death profoundly affected his father. Later biographical accounts connect it with Sulčič’s gradual withdrawal from professional architectural work and increasing concentration on painting, travel, and literature.

Fedor became a businessman, according to later family notices. He married **Carmen Teresa Guaycochea** and had two children:

- **María Victoria Sulcic**
- **Nicolás Fedor Sulcic**

Fedor died in Buenos Aires in February 2010, shortly before or during preparations for the major Buenos Aires exhibition of his father’s work.

No reliable evidence of another spouse, partner, or children was found.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1895–1914

Sulčič grew up in Križ/Santa Croce, an ethnically Slovene coastal village within the Austrian Littoral. The landscape combined the hard limestone terrain of the Karst with views toward the Adriatic. Later architectural interpreters repeatedly connected his handling of mass, structure, horizon, light, and stone to this early environment. That is an art-historical interpretation, not a proposition demonstrable from his childhood papers.

He attended the local Italian primary school associated with the **Lega Nazionale**. In **1914**, he completed the industrial or vocational secondary school in Trieste. The curriculum emphasized drawing and preparation for applied professions connected with construction, architectural decoration, shipbuilding, theater, and other technical-artistic work.

Surviving student drawings demonstrate unusually strong draftsmanship. They help explain both his initial choice of sculpture and the high level of architectural presentation drawing that later made him valuable in collaborative offices.

Information about childhood illnesses, household finances, adolescent friendships, or early religious formation was not found.

---

## First World War, 1914–1918

**Documented fact:** Sulčič was called into military service shortly after finishing school in 1914.

The available online biographies do not establish:

- his unit;
- rank;
- fronts or postings;
- whether he experienced combat;
- any wounds, decorations, captivity, disciplinary action, or desertion;
- his precise demobilization date.

Any more detailed military narrative would therefore be speculative.

The war transformed his legal and political setting. The Trieste region passed from Austria-Hungary to Italy, and Sulčič consequently became an Italian citizen. Italian rule, followed by Fascist pressure on Slovenes and Croats in the annexed territories, became a central factor in his later national and political identity.

## Education and Formation, 1919–1922

### Florence

In autumn **1919**, Sulčič entered the Academy of Fine Arts in Florence. He first studied sculpture under **Domenico Trentacoste**, a respected but academically conservative sculptor.

A heritage account states that he finished the year with **27 of 30 points and an honorable mention**. After his first year he transferred from sculpture to architecture. In a September 1920 letter to painter **Veno Pilon**, Sulčič explained this change; the letter survives in archival documentation cited by Mislej.

His architectural instruction remained rooted in the academy tradition:

- measured and observational drawing;
- historical architecture;
- decorative detail;
- study of Egyptian, classical, medieval, and Renaissance monuments;
- large compositional exercises;
- design more than structural engineering.

The Pilon Gallery holds groups of these student drawings, including studies of the Palazzo dell’Arte della Lana entrance in Florence and architectural monuments in Pienza.

### Veno Pilon

**Documented fact:** Sulčič and Slovene painter Veno Pilon were friends and housemates in Florence in **1921**, though they followed different courses of study. Pilon produced at least two portraits of Sulčič, now associated with the Pilon Gallery collection.

Their friendship is one of the most clearly documented relationships of Sulčič’s formative years.

### Bologna and degree

In **1922**, Sulčič transferred to the **Reale Istituto di Belle Arti** in Bologna and graduated that year.

The exact legal character of his diploma later became consequential. Italian educational reform was separating architecture from the fine-arts academies and placing it in technically oriented university faculties. Sulčič’s credential represented the older academy-based formation—described in the 2010 catalogue as a qualification in architectural design—and did not satisfy Argentine revalidation requirements.

This did not mean that he lacked architectural training. It meant that he could not independently sign or formally submit certain projects in Argentina as a locally recognized architect.

### Intellectual and artistic formation

His mature work combined several strands:

- classical academic composition;
- sculptural massing learned through sculpture;
- late historicism and Beaux-Arts planning;
- Gothic and neo-Gothic spatial effects;
- expressionism;
- Art Deco and Streamline Moderne;
- exposed reinforced concrete;
- modular and additive structural design.

He was not a doctrinaire functionalist. Later commentators accordingly disagree over whether to classify him principally as an Art Deco architect, a modern classicist, a transitional modernist, or an early structural expressionist.

## Early Career in Italy and Yugoslavia, 1922–1924

### Riva del Garda

After graduating, Sulčič worked briefly as an architect in **Riva del Garda**. No securely attributed completed building from this period was located.

### Zagreb

In **1923**, he moved to Zagreb. Sources variously describe him as working for architect **Rudolf Lubinski** or collaborating with architect **Harold Bilinić**. Mislej’s fuller account identifies Bilinić in connection with the renovation of sculptor **Ivan Meštrović’s** house or studio on Mletačka Street.

**Documented fact:** Sulčič participated in the renovation of Meštrović’s Zagreb property.

Meštrović became both an artistic and national model for him. The 2010 catalogue says that Sulčič admired the sculptor’s monumental, rounded, expressive forms and his Yugoslav national commitment. Meštrović reportedly gave Sulčič a sculpted head of Bishop **Josip Juraj Strossmayer** when Sulčič left. The object carried political-symbolic importance for Sulčič as an emblem of South Slav cultural solidarity.

### Why he left Europe

The immediate professional stimulus came from **Luis V. Migone/Migoni**, an engineer and former acquaintance already in Buenos Aires, who wrote to Sulčič describing opportunities in Argentina.

Several retrospective sources state that Sulčič emigrated because Italian Fascism had blocked employment and cultural freedom for Slovenes in the annexed Littoral. The chronology supports a political motive: Mussolini had come to power, Slovene public life was under increasing pressure, and Sulčič later became an explicit anti-Fascist and relinquished Italian nationality in favor of Yugoslav documentation.

The evidence best supports a combination of:

- poor professional prospects;
- anti-Slovene discrimination and Fascist consolidation;
- encouragement from Migone;
- the attraction of Buenos Aires’s building economy.

A narrative of a specific flight from arrest or imminent personal persecution was not found.

---

## Migration to Argentina, 1924–1926

Sulčič left for Argentina near the end of **1924**, aged twenty-nine. Passenger vessel, departure port, arrival date, and first Buenos Aires address were not established from the accessible sources.

He arrived as an Italian citizen but identified culturally as Slovene and politically with Yugoslav national aspirations.

For approximately eight months he worked as a **draftsman in an unidentified design organization**. There he met surveyor or geometer **Raúl Bes**.

With engineer **Luis Migone**, he entered a competition for the municipal building at **Bragado**, Buenos Aires Province, and won **second prize**. Sources disagree whether this occurred in 1926 or 1928; the fuller chronological biography places it before the decisive Banco Hipotecario competition and therefore favors 1926.

---

## Formation of Delpini–Sulcic–Bes

In March **1926**, Sulčič persuaded Bes to prepare an entry for the competition to design the new central palace of the **Banco Hipotecario Nacional** on Buenos Aires’s Diagonal Sur.

After the proposal was substantially prepared, they recognized that neither could validly submit it under Argentine professional rules because their qualifications were not locally revalidated. They approached the young engineer **José Luis Delpini**.

According to Mislej’s reconstruction:

1. Delpini agreed to sign the design.
2. He required the group to prepare a second alternative.
3. The two entries were submitted under the pseudonyms **ICATU** and **IMPROBUS**.
4. On **31 December 1926**, the jury awarded first prize to ICATU, against approximately forty-five other entrants.
5. The three men then formed **Delpini, Sulcic, Bes—Ingenieros, Arquitectos**.

The project’s authorship is therefore genuinely collaborative. Sulčič supplied architectural and spatial design; Delpini contributed engineering authority and expertise in reinforced-concrete structures; Bes contributed surveying and technical work. Claims that any single man independently “designed” all of the partnership’s buildings oversimplify the documentary record.

## Chronological Life History

### 1926–1928: Banco Hipotecario success and cancellation

The Banco Hipotecario win propelled the young firm into Argentina’s senior professional circles. The proposed building was never constructed. A change of government in **1928**, associated with Hipólito Yrigoyen’s return to the presidency, stopped the undertaking after the architects had completed extensive plans.

**Documented legal dispute:** The partners pursued payment through court proceedings and eventually received what Mislej describes as substantial compensation.

No accessible case citation, pleadings, judgment, or exact amount was found. This was a contractual fee-recovery action, not a criminal or corruption case.

### Late 1920s: Smaller works

The partnership undertook residential, industrial, funerary, civic, and commercial projects. Attributed works include:

- monumental entrance to the municipal cemetery at **Luján**;
- **Florentino Ameghino Library**, Luján;
- **Mercado Vélez Sársfield**, Buenos Aires;
- several monumental tombs;
- apartment buildings, including one at **Venezuela and Piedras**;
- an apartment and commercial building on **Avenida Jujuy between Hipólito Yrigoyen and Alsina**;
- buildings of four, six, and seven stories whose exact addresses remain incompletely catalogued.

The cemetery entrance uses a severe, classically proportioned Doric vocabulary. The Luján library is more explicitly historicist.

### 1929–1935: Mercado de Abasto Proveedor

In **1929**, the firm received a private commission from **Mercado de Abasto Proveedor S.A.** to replace or expand Buenos Aires’s central produce market on Avenida Corrientes. A previous competition had failed to produce an accepted solution.

Mislej credits Sulčič with the initial conceptual sketch accepted by the client. Construction began in **1931**—some sources say planning or preliminary works began in 1930 or 1932.

The complex was conceived in three stages. Only two were built:

- five principal longitudinal naves, dominated by a central barrel-vaulted nave;
- transverse naves added in the second stage;
- two basements and two above-ground levels;
- lower rail access tied to the Lacroze system;
- meat-auction, wholesale, and retail levels;
- freight lifts, conveyors, and mechanical distribution systems;
- escalators reportedly capable of moving 6,000 people per hour;
- vast reinforced-concrete ribs and coffered vaults;
- glass structural blocks or glass infill providing diffuse illumination;
- exposed reinforced-concrete façades.

Dimensions reported in the biographical literature include:

- central nave: approximately **20.60 metres wide**;
- four side naves: approximately **14.50 metres each**;
- façade: approximately **115 metres wide**;
- central nave height: approximately **30 metres**;
- total site: **21,259 square metres**;
- completed phases: approximately **12,161 square metres**;
- materials: roughly **22,800 cubic metres of reinforced concrete** and **four million kilograms of iron**.

The first completed phase opened ceremonially on **26 May 1934**; President **Agustín P. Justo** visited the previous day. A second phase opened the following year. The façade received a **first municipal prize in 1937**.

The third stage was never completed. Sources attribute this to the deteriorating economy and the approach or effects of the Second World War on the operating company.

The building later experienced a major basement fire; municipal material gives **1952**, while the 2010 exhibition catalogue gives **1958**. The date remains unresolved without consultation of contemporary fire and newspaper records.

The market closed in **1984**, stood largely vacant, and became associated with the decline and alternative cultural life of the Abasto district. It was converted into the Abasto shopping center in **1998–2000**. The conversion retained the monumental nave system but was criticized for altering surfaces and elements of the original fabric.

### 1929 onward: Painter

Sulčič participated in Argentina’s national art salon in **1929** and exhibited with Argentine painters. Landscape watercolor became his principal medium.

Subjects included:

- Buenos Aires and the Riachuelo;
- the Argentine interior;
- mountains and Patagonia;
- southern Chile;
- the Karst and Adriatic memory-landscapes.

An *El Magallanes* review of his 1955 Punta Arenas exhibition praised his forceful brushwork, clear color, and treatment of changing light. That is a contemporary critical judgment rather than neutral fact.

### 1930s: Community organizer and educator

Sulčič became a central participant in Buenos Aires’s Slovene and wider Yugoslav immigrant organizations.

Documented or strongly reported offices include:

- deputy head, or **podstarosta**, of the Sokol association, around **1931**;
- president of **Izseljeniška zaščita/Jugoslovanska narodna zaščita**, a mutual-protection or welfare body helping immigrants with illness and employment;
- repeated leadership roles in the Yugoslav Home at Dock Sud;
- member of an educational committee reorganizing Slovene schooling;
- organizer or teacher of Spanish courses for newly arrived compatriots;
- wartime secretary-general of **Yugoslav National Defence in Argentina**.

In **1933**, Sulčič chaired the consortium that founded the weekly **Novi list**, published from 1933 to 1936 and edited by **Viktor Kjuder/Kluder**. He also illustrated material for the paper.

### Political orientation of *Novi list*

An academic history of Slovene immigrant organizations reports that *Novi list* reflected Yugoslav patriotism and defended King Alexander’s dictatorship as a “necessary evil” that had ended parliamentary disorder.

This is evidence about the paper’s editorial line under a consortium chaired by Sulčič. It does not by itself establish that he personally wrote every political position. No signed political manifesto by Sulčič was found.

### “Priseljenčeva hiša”

In 1933–34, Sulčič published a technical article series titled **“Priseljenčeva hiša”** (“The Immigrant’s House”) in *Novi list*. It offered plans and practical guidance so immigrant families could build simple modern houses without the expense of employing a professional designer.

The articles demonstrate a social-housing interest that later reappeared in his prefabricated, fire-resistant, and earthquake-resistant house proposals.

### Isidor Cankar and Yugoslav institutions

When art historian and diplomat **Izidor Cankar** arrived as Yugoslav minister in late **1936**, he helped coordinate Slovene schooling and commissioned Sulčič to design a **Yugoslav Home at Dock Sud**.

Sulčič presented the design in the newspaper *Naša sloga* in **1939**. The full design was not realized; a reduced version of the home opened in **1943**.

For his work among Yugoslav immigrants, Cankar presented Sulčič with the **Order of Saint Sava, fifth class**, in **1939**.

### Boca Juniors and La Bombonera, 1937–1941

The firm won the commission for a new stadium for **Club Atlético Boca Juniors** in **1937**. Earlier attempts had struggled with the extremely constrained site.

The project addressed the site by concentrating steep, vertically stacked stands on three sides and reserving a narrow fourth edge for later accommodation. The scheme was initially described as capable of serving up to 100,000 spectators, although the first built capacity was smaller.

The foundation ceremony occurred on **18 February 1938**. Construction was undertaken rapidly, reportedly by the German construction company **GEOPÉ/GAOPE**.

The stadium opened on **25 May 1940** with Boca defeating San Lorenzo 2–0 in a friendly. A second tier was completed in **1941**. The third tier and artificial lighting were completed in **1953**, after Sulčič had begun withdrawing from active design.

Authorship is recorded institutionally as:

- architect **Viktor/Victorio Sulčič**;
- engineer **José Luis Delpini**;
- geometer **Raúl Bes**.

The stadium’s official name eventually became Estadio Alberto J. Armando, but its dominant popular name remains **La Bombonera**.

### San Justo church and Italian Hospital complex

The partnership designed buildings for the Italian Hospital’s old-age home at **San Justo**, Buenos Aires Province. Sulčič had particular responsibility for the **Church of the Sacred Heart of Jesus**, built approximately **1941–42**.

Its features include:

- reinforced-concrete construction;
- vertical, neo-Gothic spatial emphasis translated into modern form;
- subdued bluish light;
- gray marble and white vaults;
- a cross silhouetted against a cross-shaped window;
- an integrated interior designed by Sulčič;
- sculptural furnishings that he also sketched.

Some brief profiles date the design to 1939, probably referring to planning rather than completion.

A second Sacred Heart church in **San Juan** is attributed to him in later journalism, but this attribution was less fully documented than the San Justo church.

### Formosa school and memorial commissions

In **1940**, Sulčič designed a secondary-school building in Formosa for Slovene School Sisters.

In **1942**, he designed the tomb or memorial for **Jože/José Kastelic**, the first Slovene immigrant priest in Argentina, who died in a mountaineering accident on Aconcagua.

He also produced, after the war, a conceptual design for the headquarters of the newer postwar Slovene community in Buenos Aires.

### 1943: Meeting Juan/Ivan Benigar

In **1943**, Sulčič traveled to **Aluminé, Neuquén**, near the Chilean border, to visit Slovene-Croatian engineer, linguist, and ethnographer **Ivan/Juan Benigar**. Salesian priest **Ludvik Pernišek** had told him about Benigar during an earlier journey.

The two became friends. Sulčič later drew on personal conversations and anecdotes for his biography of Benigar. This journey was important both intellectually and geographically: it deepened his engagement with Patagonia, Indigenous questions, landscape, and the experience of Central European emigrants in Argentina.

### Second World War politics

Sulčič was a consistent opponent of Italian Fascism. A later Trieste account states that during the interwar period he renounced Italian citizenship and obtained a Yugoslav passport.

During the Second World War, he supported the Yugoslav liberation movement led by **Josip Broz Tito**. The 2010 catalogue reports that Italian clients in Argentina abandoned him for ideological reasons once he took this position.

This combination complicates simple party labels:

- he had participated in royal-Yugoslav institutions;
- received a royal Yugoslav decoration;
- belonged to Sokol and immigrant mutual-aid networks;
- opposed Fascism;
- later supported Tito’s Partisan-led liberation movement.

No evidence was found that he joined the Communist Party, worked for an intelligence service, participated in political violence, or held office under the postwar Yugoslav government.

### 1944 San Juan earthquake and housing projects

After the catastrophic **1944 San Juan earthquake**, Sulčič designed prefabricated houses intended to be:

- earthquake-resistant;
- fire-resistant;
- demountable or rapidly assembled;
- affordable to poorer families.

One account says he presented the design to Minister **Joaquín Sauri** and donated it to the Ministry of Public Works; another says it was donated to the municipality of Buenos Aires and published in an Asociación Sarmientina bulletin. These may represent more than one design or successive presentations.

No evidence was found that the system entered mass production. It should therefore be described as a socially motivated experimental project, not a successfully commercialized construction technology.

No patents in Sulčič’s name were identified.

### 1951–1953: Death of Igor and withdrawal from architecture

Héctor Igor died in **1951**. The cause and circumstances are described in accessible sources only as “tragic.” No reliable account of an accident, illness, suicide, or other cause was found.

Later sources say the death changed the family and drove Sulčič inward. The strongest chronology is:

- after 1951, he progressively reduced architectural work;
- in **1953**, he formally or effectively left active project design;
- thereafter he concentrated on watercolor, travel, and writing.

Thus, claims that he stopped architecture immediately in 1951 and claims that he retired in 1953 are compatible if understood as a gradual transition.

### 1954–1959: Exhibitions and travel

Known exhibitions include:

- **1929:** participation in the Argentine national salon or a Buenos Aires group exhibition;
- **1954:** solo exhibition, Galería Witcomb, Buenos Aires;
- **1955:** Punta Arenas, Chile;
- **1956 or 1957:** Belgrade; sources disagree by one year;
- **1959:** second solo exhibition at Galería Witcomb;
- other group exhibitions within the Slovene community.

He traveled extensively in southern Argentina and Chile, painting mountain and regional landscapes.

In mid-**1956**, he returned to Europe and visited:

- Trieste;
- Ljubljana;
- Opatija;
- Dalmatia;
- Belgrade, where an exhibition was held either during this trip or the following year.

No complete travel diary or itinerary was found.

### 1960s–1973: Writer and elder community figure

Sulčič continued participating in emigrant cultural organizations until his death. He self-published poetry, stories, and his Benigar biography. His literary work was written mainly in Spanish, although *Luces y sombras* included three Slovene poems.

His later writings combined:

- immigrant memory;
- landscape;
- religious sensibility;
- patriotic attachment to Slovene origins;
- gratitude toward Argentina;
- biographical and ethnographic interest;
- the grief and inwardness associated by later writers with his son’s death.

No evidence was found of dementia, addiction, prolonged psychiatric hospitalization, or major late-life legal trouble.

### Technical significance

The Abasto and Boca projects are important for their use of reinforced concrete as the architectural generator rather than merely a concealed engineering frame.

At Abasto, structure, daylight, circulation, and monumental form were integrated through repeatable vault modules. At Boca, steep tiers transferred enormous crowds and loads through a narrow footprint. Sulčič’s sculptural and historical training combined with Delpini’s structural calculation.

Statements that Abasto was definitively the “first” exposed-concrete building in all South America should be treated cautiously. Slovene and Argentine heritage sources repeat the claim, but no continent-wide comparative construction survey was found.

### Wealth and property

No reliable figures were found for Sulčič’s income, personal wealth, real estate, investments, or inheritance.

The Banco Hipotecario fee judgment reportedly produced substantial compensation. Major private commissions suggest professional success during the late 1920s and 1930s, but his later reduced practice and self-publication do not establish great personal wealth.

No evidence was found that he owned the Mercado de Abasto company, Boca Juniors, construction contractors, or a building-material enterprise. His relationship to these entities was professional, not proprietary.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Slovene and Yugoslav identity

Sulčič’s identity developed across changing sovereignties:

1. Slovene upbringing in Austria-Hungary.
2. Italian citizenship after annexation.
3. resistance to Fascist Italianization.
4. Yugoslav cultural and political identification.
5. Argentine civic and professional integration.

He did not abandon his Slovene language or networks after migration. He nevertheless wrote major late works in Spanish and treated Argentina as a second homeland.

### Anti-Fascism

**Documented/reported fact:** His anti-Fascism was consistent enough that he gave up Italian nationality, acquired Yugoslav documentation, and supported the wartime Yugoslav liberation movement.

### Yugoslav monarchism and Tito

His political record crossed institutional regimes. The *Novi list* consortium he chaired published a qualified defense of King Alexander’s dictatorship. In 1939 he accepted the Order of Saint Sava from the royal Yugoslav representative. During the war he supported Tito’s liberation movement.

The available evidence does not resolve whether this reflected ideological evolution, broad Yugoslav patriotism, tactical anti-Fascism, or some combination.

### Religion

Mislej’s biography describes him as **deeply religious**. His Sacred Heart church and several memorial commissions support sustained Catholic cultural engagement. His cooperation with priests and Slovene School Sisters is well documented.

No source identified a conversion, clerical vocation, conflict with the Church, occult affiliation, or formal theological writing.

### Architectural ideology

His work resisted clean stylistic categorization. Scholars have identified:

- classicism in proportion and monumentality;
- expressionism in mass and structure;
- neo-Gothic verticality;
- Art Deco and Streamline elements;
- Italian Futurist echoes;
- early structuralism;
- constructional expressionism.

His designs generally did not pursue the ornament-free International Style. Instead, he simplified historic spatial types until their structure itself became expressive.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### José Luis Delpini

Professional partner and structural collaborator from 1926 onward. Their complementary expertise made the office’s largest works possible. Later Argentine attribution often foregrounded Delpini because he was the authorized signatory and university professor, contributing to Sulčič’s comparative obscurity.

No evidence of a personal rupture or hostile rivalry was found.

### Raúl Bes

Geometer, colleague from Sulčič’s first Buenos Aires drafting employment, and third partner in the firm. Bes participated in the Banco Hipotecario, Abasto, and Boca projects.

### Luis V. Migone/Migoni

Friend and engineer whose letters encouraged Sulčič to migrate. Collaborated with him on the Bragado municipal competition.

### Veno Pilon

Friend, Florence housemate, correspondent, and portraitist. Their surviving correspondence and portraits are key evidence for Sulčič’s student years.

### Ivan Meštrović

Employer or senior artistic associate during the Zagreb period. Meštrović influenced Sulčič’s sculptural vocabulary and Yugoslav national consciousness and reportedly gave him a Strossmayer portrait head.

### Domenico Trentacoste

His sculpture teacher at the Florence Academy.

### Ivan/Juan Benigar

Friend, intellectual subject, and fellow Central European emigrant. Sulčič visited him in Aluminé in 1943 and later wrote the principal personal biography that fixed Benigar’s epithet “the sage who died sitting.”

### Izidor Cankar

Yugoslav diplomat, art historian, institutional patron, and community collaborator. He commissioned the Yugoslav Home, involved Sulčič in educational work, and awarded him the Order of Saint Sava.

### Viktor Kjuder/Kluder

Editor of *Novi list*. Kjuder’s contemporary account emphasized Sulčič’s reluctance to publicize his own role in the Abasto project.

### Ludvik Pernišek

Salesian priest who first told Sulčič about Benigar.

### Jože Kastelic and Janez Hladnik

Slovene immigrant priests in Argentina. Sulčič designed Kastelic’s memorial and participated in institutional networks that overlapped with Hladnik’s educational and welfare work.

### Italian clients in Argentina

The Italian community initially provided important commissions, including the San Justo complex. According to the 2010 catalogue, some clients severed relations when Sulčič publicly supported Tito’s anti-Axis liberation movement.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Dedicated adversarial-search finding

Searches were conducted in Spanish, Slovenian, Italian, and English using combinations of Sulčič’s name with terms corresponding to controversy, scandal, allegation, lawsuit, fraud, corruption, criminal accusation, conspiracy, criticism, Fascism, Communism, and political dispute, including material indexed in 2024–26.

No credible evidence was found that Viktor Sulčič was:

- criminally charged or convicted;
- investigated for fraud, corruption, embezzlement, or money laundering;
- accused of professional malpractice causing deaths;
- involved in organized crime;
- an intelligence operative;
- involved in political violence;
- formally disciplined by an architectural body;
- accused of plagiarism in a documented proceeding.

### Banco Hipotecario litigation

**Documented fact:** Sulčič and his partners sued or otherwise judicially pursued unpaid professional fees after the Banco Hipotecario project was cancelled. They reportedly obtained substantial compensation.

This was a successful contractual claim by the architects. It is not evidence of wrongdoing by Sulčič.

### Argentine recognition of his degree

**Documented administrative fact:** His Italian academy qualification was not revalidated in Argentina.

This created a structural authorship problem: Delpini signed submissions and official records sometimes credited only Delpini. Later advocates have described Sulčič as “forgotten” or unfairly erased.

No evidence was found that Sulčič falsely represented himself to Argentine authorities, forged credentials, or was prosecuted for unauthorized practice. He worked through a recognized professional partnership.

### Authorship disputes and unequal credit

**Unresolved historiographical question:** Exactly how design responsibility was divided within Delpini–Sulcic–Bes cannot be reconstructed for every project.

Evidence favoring a major Sulčič role includes:

- archival sketches;
- testimony from later architectural researchers;
- the partnership name on period documentation;
- the conceptual role attributed to him at Abasto;
- Boca Juniors’ current institutional acknowledgment;
- his distinctive sculptural and spatial training.

Evidence favoring prominent Delpini credit includes:

- official signatures;
- his engineering role;
- municipal documentation naming him alone in some records;
- his established Argentine academic status.

The fairest description is collaborative authorship with differentiated roles, not exclusive authorship by either Sulčič or Delpini.

### Political controversy

The politics of immigrant organizations were divided among royal Yugoslav, Slovene nationalist, Catholic, socialist, Communist, and later anti-Communist constituencies.

Sulčič’s record includes:

- leadership of a paper that defended Alexander’s dictatorship as a necessary response to disorder;
- a royal Yugoslav decoration;
- explicit anti-Fascism;
- wartime support for Tito.

Later communities with sharply different postwar views nevertheless collaborated in commemorating him. No evidence was found of his imprisonment or prosecution for these activities.

### Same-name court result

A 2010 Río Negro civil proceeding titled *Domínguez Roberto c/ Sulcic Victorio y otros* appears in search results. Viktor Sulčič had been dead for thirty-seven years, and the case references a 2009 succession. No evidence connects that defendant to the architect. It has therefore not been treated as part of his record.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The chocolate box and the name “La Bombonera”

**Story:** A woman friend gave Sulčič a chocolate box for his birthday. Its shape closely resembled the constrained, steep-sided stadium design. He carried it to project meetings and began calling the project *la bombonera*, after which colleagues, builders, club officials, and supporters adopted the name.

**Earliest source located here:** A retrospective account by engineer and Sulčič friend **José Antonic**, quoted in a 2010 *Página/12* feature. Antonic said Sulčič proudly recalled the term being used in speeches on opening day.

**Evidence for:**

- attributed testimony from someone who knew him;
- newspaper evidence that “Bombonera” was already current at the 1940 opening;
- widespread institutional repetition;
- the physical resemblance between the building form and a tiered chocolate box.

**Evidence against or limitations:**

- no contemporary diary entry, letter, photograph of the box, or 1930s project-minute record was located;
- versions differ over whether the giver was a female friend, an unspecified friend, or Sulčič’s wife;
- one embellished account assigns the gift to his birthday on 2 August 1938 without producing a contemporary source.

**Classification:** **Reported oral history approaching legend**, plausible but not conclusively documented.

### Alternative Bombonera-origin stories

Other versions credit:

- Delpini, because steep stands resembled carts called *bomboneras*;
- journalist **Hugo Marini**;
- radio commentator **Fioravanti**;
- the building’s resemblance to a chocolate box without any actual gift.

These alternatives are less strongly tied to identified testimony in the sources consulted. Their circulation demonstrates how the stadium’s nickname became part of football folklore.

### “Sulčič alone designed the Bombonera”

**Mythologized simplification:** Popular profiles frequently call him the sole designer.

The documentary record names Delpini, Sulčič, and Bes. Sulčič appears to have been the central architectural-form designer, but the built stadium depended on Delpini’s structural engineering and Bes’s technical contribution.

### “Delpini alone designed the Abasto”

Some Buenos Aires municipal records call the Abasto a work of engineer José Luis Delpini alone. Other municipal heritage documentation and the professional plaque identify **Delpini / architects V. Sulcic and R. Bes**.

This is best understood as a documentary consequence of signing authority and professional status, not proof that Sulčič was uninvolved.

### Exile versus opportunity migration

Commemorative accounts sometimes portray Sulčič as simply “fleeing Fascism”; others describe a move for professional opportunity.

Both factors are documented in retrospective archival scholarship. No evidence was found of a formal expulsion order or sudden clandestine escape. “Politically conditioned emigration” is more precise than “forced exile.”

### “Forgotten genius”

Exhibitions and journalism since 1989 have promoted a recovery narrative in which Sulčič was a genius erased from Argentine architectural history.

The underlying obscurity is real: his inability to sign plans, Delpini’s public position, collaborative attribution, and political rupture with Italian clients reduced his visibility. “Genius,” however, is a commemorative critical judgment, not a documentary category.

### Numerical and dating legends

Published accounts contain recurrent inaccuracies:

- birth on 14 January rather than 2 August;
- death at age 79 rather than 78;
- Fedor born in 1940 rather than apparently 1930;
- Abasto opening in 1934, 1935, or 1936;
- Boca designed in 1932, commissioned in 1937, or built beginning in 1938;
- Belgrade exhibition in 1956 or 1957;
- *Luces y sombras* in 1965 or 1968;
- Abasto fire in 1952 or 1958.

These are source conflicts, not separate biographical events.

## Illnesses, Accidents, Psychological Crises, and Death

No documented chronic illness, disability, addiction, psychiatric diagnosis, or serious personal accident was located.

The death of Héctor Igor in 1951 was the central documented family crisis of Sulčič’s later life. Sources describe his subsequent inward turn, reduced architectural activity, and increased devotion to painting and writing. This should not be converted into a clinical diagnosis without evidence.

Sulčič died in Buenos Aires on **9 September 1973**. The medical cause of death was not found in the sources consulted.

His remains reportedly rest in the Slavic Association vault in Chacarita Cemetery. Confirmation from Chacarita burial records remains desirable.

## Later Life, Death, and Immediate Aftermath

Sulčič remained in Buenos Aires for the rest of his life after his 1956 European journey. He continued painting, writing, traveling, corresponding, and participating in immigrant cultural affairs.

He died on **9 September 1973**. The cause was not established in the sources consulted.

A large retrospective of his visual art was organized in Buenos Aires after his death, reportedly in 1974. His wife Ana and son Fedor preserved his documentary and artistic estate.

Because major projects had been officially associated with Delpini or the collective firm, Sulčič did not immediately enter the Argentine architectural canon as an individual figure. His rehabilitation began through family preservation and Irene Mislej’s research.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Argentina

Sulčič’s reputation rests primarily on two buildings that became urban institutions:

- the Abasto, whose reinforced-concrete naves remain a landmark despite conversion to retail use;
- La Bombonera, whose extremely steep, asymmetrical form became one of world football’s most recognizable stadium environments.

Current Boca Juniors institutional publications explicitly credit him. The club’s 2026 anniversary account described the stadium’s vertical structure as his response to the restricted site.

### Slovenia and the Slovene diaspora

Sulčič became an emblem of Slovene emigrant achievement, especially after the 1989 retrospective. His life joins:

- the Austro-Hungarian Littoral;
- Fascist-era emigration;
- South Slav cultural politics;
- Argentina’s interwar building boom;
- postwar diaspora memory.

The recovery has sometimes taken a national-hero form that foregrounds Sulčič at the expense of Delpini and Bes. Archival scholarship, however, generally recognizes both his major design role and the collaborative nature of the firm.

### Architectural reinterpretation

Later scholars have moved beyond the simple label “Art Deco.” They identify in his work an unusual transition from academic historicism to modular modern structures:

- Abasto as an expandable generative system;
- Boca as architecture created by load, crowd movement, and site constraint;
- San Justo as neo-Gothic spiritual space remade through concrete and controlled light.

Student projects in Ljubljana have explored hypothetical completion of Abasto and Boca, treating their unfinished edges as part of Sulčič’s architectural legacy.

### Cultural afterlife of the Abasto

After the market closed, the building became associated with urban abandonment and alternative culture. It appears in the cultural geography surrounding tango, Carlos Gardel, Luca Prodan, and the film *Mercado de Abasto*. These are associations of the building, not documented personal relationships with Sulčič.

### Cultural afterlife of La Bombonera

The stadium’s “chocolate box” origin story made Sulčič a character in Boca folklore. Later proposals to complete or enlarge the ground have used the name **Proyecto Esloveno** in tribute to him.

### Unresolved research needs

A truly complete biography would require:

- birth and parish registers from Križ;
- Austro-Hungarian military files;
- Florence and Bologna student files;
- passenger and Argentine naturalization records;
- complete partnership records;
- the Banco Hipotecario lawsuit file;
- Boca and Mercado de Abasto corporate minutes;
- a catalogue of all buildings and tombs;
- Ana and Fedor Sulcic’s correspondence;
- Héctor Igor’s death record;
- Sulčič’s death and Chacarita burial certificates;
- full bibliographic examination of his three books and newspaper writings.

## Atlas Connections

The roster was checked against documented people in Sulčič’s life and against people culturally associated with his buildings.

### Diego Armando Maradona

- **Nature:** Posthumous institutional connection through La Bombonera. Maradona played for Boca Juniors in the stadium designed by Sulčič, Delpini, and Bes.
- **Dates and places:** Buenos Aires, principally during Maradona’s Boca periods beginning in 1981, eight years after Sulčič’s death.
- **Label:** **Documented fact, posthumous architectural connection**.
- **Qualification:** No meeting or personal relationship was possible. This is a documented relationship between Sulčič’s work and Maradona’s career, not between the two men.

### Carlos Gardel

- **Nature:** The Abasto district and Sulčič’s market became strongly associated with Gardel in Buenos Aires cultural memory.
- **Dates and places:** Gardel lived in the Abasto area before his death in 1935; the new market opened in 1934.
- **Label:** **Documented shared urban-cultural setting; no documented personal connection**.
- **Qualification:** No source consulted establishes that Sulčič met, corresponded with, designed for, or was influenced by Gardel. Mere proximity is not counted as a biographical crossing.

### Antoni Gaudí i Cornet

- **Nature:** Later architectural comparison. Critics have compared the corridor-like vaults and controlled light of Sulčič’s San Justo church to Gaudí’s Colegio de las Teresianas.
- **Label:** **Reported critical comparison, not a documented personal connection or acknowledged influence**.
- **Qualification:** Gaudí died in 1926, and no source establishes a meeting, correspondence, or explicit statement by Sulčič acknowledging Gaudí.

### Other roster findings

No documented direct meeting, correspondence, collaboration, family tie, patronage relation, rivalry, or explicitly acknowledged formative influence was found between Sulčič and any other person in the supplied atlas roster.

In particular, his important documented relations—Veno Pilon, Ivan Meštrović, Domenico Trentacoste, José Luis Delpini, Raúl Bes, Luis Migone, Izidor Cankar, Viktor Kjuder, Ludvik Pernišek, Jože Kastelic, Janez Hladnik, and Ivan Benigar—are not included in the roster.

Absence of further atlas connections is therefore a finding, not an assumption.
