# Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Limits

ʿAbd al-Qādir al-Jīlānī lived in the fifth–sixth Islamic centuries, when biographical writing rarely recorded private life, finances, physical appearance, or daily chronology in modern detail. The surviving record has several layers:

1. **Relatively restrained biographical notices:** medieval chronicles, Hanbali biographical dictionaries, and notices preserved by al-Dhahabī, Ibn Rajab, Ibn al-Jawzī, Ibn Kathīr, al-Samʿānī, and related writers.
2. **Texts assembled from sermons:** especially *al-Fatḥ al-rabbānī* and *Futūḥ al-ghayb*.
3. **Later hagiography:** principally al-Shaṭṭanawfī’s *Bahjat al-asrār*, compiled approximately 140–150 years after al-Jīlānī’s death.
4. **Qādirī institutional and family traditions:** genealogies, initiation chains, shrine records, devotional literature, and claims transmitted by descendants and affiliated communities.
5. **Modern scholarship:** notably Jacqueline Chabbi, Walther Braune, Bruce Lawrence, George Makdisi, J. Spencer Trimingham, Annemarie Schimmel, Hamza Malik, Jonathan Allen, and Ahmet Karamustafa.

Accordingly, statements below are labeled where necessary as **documented fact**, **reported fact**, **later tradition**, **hagiography**, **disputed attribution**, **unsupported claim**, or **myth/legend**.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Principal name:** Abū Muḥammad Muḥyī al-Dīn ʿAbd al-Qādir ibn Abī Ṣāliḥ Mūsā al-Jīlānī.
- **Arabic:** محيي الدين أبو محمد عبد القادر بن أبي صالح موسى الجيلاني.
- **Variants:** al-Jīlī, al-Jīlānī, al-Gīlānī, al-Kīlānī; Gilani, Geylani, Jilani, Jeelani, Gailani, Qadiri.
- **Father’s name in different sources:** Abū Ṣāliḥ Mūsā; Abū Ṣāliḥ ʿAbd Allāh; Mūsā Jangī-dōst/Zangī-dost. The genealogy is unstable in its earliest generations.
- **Kunya:** Abū Muḥammad.
- **Honorifics and devotional titles:**
  - *Muḥyī al-Dīn*, “Reviver of Religion.”
  - *Shaykh al-Islām*.
  - *Sulṭān al-awliyāʾ*, “Sultan of the Saints.”
  - *Quṭb Baghdad*, “Pole of Baghdad.”
  - *al-Ghawth al-Aʿẓam*, “the Greatest Succor/Helper.”
  - *Pīr-i Dastgīr*, “the helping spiritual master.”
  - *Bāz Allāh* and *al-Bāz al-Ashhab*, “God’s falcon” or “the Grey Falcon.”
  - *Tāj al-ʿārifīn*, “Crown of the Gnostics.”
- **Reported poetic pen name:** “Muḥyī.” The poems carrying it are not securely established as contemporary compositions.
- **Birth:** conventionally 470 AH/1077–78 CE.
- **Specific devotional date:** 1 Ramadan 470 AH is frequently given, but it is not securely recoverable from contemporary documentation.
- **Birthplace:** most scholarly accounts favor the province of Gīlān, southwest of the Caspian Sea, in present-day Iran. Neyf/Nīf is named in later sources.
- **Alternative birthplace:** Jīl near Baghdad or another locality in Iraq, advanced in some later medieval accounts. Jacqueline Chabbi and most modern specialists favor Caspian Gīlān.
- **Ethnolinguistic origin:** probably Persian-speaking or otherwise Iranian. Modern claims that he was specifically Arab, Kurdish, Persian, or Gilaki often rest on later genealogy or retrospective ethnic classification.
- **Death:** 561 AH/1166 CE, Baghdad.
- **Reported day:** 8, 10, or 11 Rabīʿ II; commemorative observance usually falls on 11 Rabīʿ II. The exact Gregorian equivalent varies with calendar conversion.
- **Burial:** his school in Baghdad, the nucleus of the present al-Jīlānī shrine complex in the Bāb al-Shaykh quarter.
- **Primary historical occupations:** Hanbali jurist, legal teacher, preacher, traditionist, ascetic, spiritual instructor, and administrator of a madrasa/ribāṭ.
- **Later institutional identity:** eponym and retrospectively designated founder of the Qādiriyya.

The fundamental scholarly problem is the transition from a historically visible Hanbali preacher to a universal intercessory saint. Oxford’s reference survey describes him as both historically elusive and exceptionally widespread in later religious memory. [Oxford Bibliographies](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62361/reference-article-abstract/554575952)

## Family and Ancestry

### Father and paternal descent

His father is usually called Abū Ṣāliḥ Mūsā Jangī-dōst. Little dependable biographical information survives about him beyond later descriptions of piety.

The name **Jangī-dōst/Zangī-dost** is Persianate. Its interpretation is uncertain: translations include “friend of battle,” while Turkish transmission sometimes understands *Zengī-dost* differently. It is evidence of an Iranian cultural setting, but it does not by itself settle ethnicity.

### The Hasanid genealogy

Qādirī tradition supplies a paternal line running approximately:

ʿAbd al-Qādir → Mūsā → ʿAbd Allāh → Yaḥyā → Muḥammad → Dāwūd → Mūsā → ʿAbd Allāh → Mūsā al-Jawn → ʿAbd Allāh al-Maḥḍ/al-Kāmil → al-Ḥasan al-Muthannā → al-Ḥasan ibn ʿAlī → ʿAlī and Fāṭima, daughter of Muhammad.

**Evidentiary status: later genealogical tradition, disputed.** The genealogy made al-Jīlānī a *sharīf* or *sayyid* through al-Ḥasan. It is foundational to later Qādirī family identity but is not securely demonstrated by near-contemporary documents. Bruce Lawrence characterizes the line as the work of overzealous hagiographers; the TDV encyclopedia notes that his being called *aʿjamī*—a non-Arab—and the Persianate family name prompted doubts that the genealogy was early. Neither point alone disproves biological descent. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/abd-al-qader-jilani/) [TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/abdulkadir-i-geylani)

### Mother and maternal family

His mother is named in later tradition as **Umm al-Khayr Amat al-Jabbār Fāṭima**, daughter of the ascetic **Abū ʿAbd Allāh al-Ṣawmaʿī**. She and her father are represented as saints.

Some Qādirī genealogies give her a Husaynid descent, producing a double lineage through both al-Ḥasan and al-Ḥusayn. **This is later tradition and cannot be independently established.**

His father reportedly died while he was young, after which his mother and maternal grandfather raised him. No securely documented siblings have been identified.

### Marriages

Shihāb al-Dīn al-Suhrawardī reportedly said that al-Jīlānī had **four wives**. Their names, marriage dates, property, and legal circumstances are not securely preserved. A tradition also makes him the son-in-law of the ascetic Ḥammād al-Dabbās, but the sources are inconsistent about whether this relationship was literal.

### Children

Late family tradition, especially *Qalāʾid al-jawāhir*, assigns him **forty-nine children: twenty-seven sons and twenty-two daughters**. This total cannot be confirmed from contemporary records.

Better-attested or institutionally important sons include:

- **ʿAbd Allāh** (d. 589/1193), described as the eldest.
- **ʿAbd al-Wahhāb** (d. 593/1196), reportedly led his father’s funeral prayer and later served as a judge.
- **ʿAbd al-Razzāq** (d. 603/1206–07), traditionist and teacher; credited with collecting *Futūḥ al-ghayb* and succeeding within the Baghdad institution.
- **ʿĪsā** (d. 573/1177), associated with Egypt and credited with *Jawāhir al-asrār*.
- **Ibrāhīm** (d. 592/1196 at Wāsiṭ), retrospectively made ancestor of several western Qādirī families.
- **ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz** (d. 602/1205–06), connected with Sinjar and Baghdad.
- **Mūsā** (d. 618/1221), associated with Syrian lines.
- Also named in varying lists: ʿAbd al-Jabbār, Muḥammad, Yaḥyā, and others.

Al-Wāsiṭī regarded only ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz and ʿAbd al-Razzāq as inheriting spiritual aspirations comparable to their father’s; al-Shaṭṭanawfī counted eleven spiritually active sons. The disagreement suggests later expansion of the family’s sacred genealogy.

No dependable complete list of daughters survives. Claims by later dynasties, political families, and shrine custodians to descend from al-Jīlānī require verification line by line; the honorifics Qādirī, Gīlānī, Jīlānī, or Kaylānī do not alone prove descent.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1077–1095

Al-Jīlānī was reportedly born in rural Gīlān during Seljuk rule. Almost nothing about his childhood is preserved outside hagiography.

Later stories say that:

- he refused milk during daylight in Ramadan as an infant;
- angels accompanied him to school;
- at ten he heard invisible beings declare, “Make room for the friend of God”;
- he saw spiritual signs from childhood;
- his mother was sixty or older when he was born.

**Evidentiary status: hagiography.** These stories appear in developed saintly biography and establish predestined sanctity rather than supplying independently testable childhood history.

A famous departure story says that his mother sewed forty gold dinars into his coat and made him promise never to lie. On the caravan journey, bandits questioned him; he truthfully disclosed the money, causing their leader and then the whole band to repent.

**Evidentiary status: legend.** It is absent from the strongest contemporary evidence and conforms to widespread moral-tale patterns. Its historical importance lies in its enormous circulation as a lesson about truthfulness.

## Migration to Baghdad and Education, 1095–c.1127

### Arrival

At approximately eighteen, conventionally in **488 AH/1095 CE**, he left Gīlān for Baghdad. The date coincided with the opening phase of the First Crusade, but no evidence connects him personally to crusading politics or military activity.

Baghdad was the Abbasid caliphal capital and a major center of jurisprudence, hadith, preaching, book culture, Sufism, and institutional education. Seljuk and Abbasid political authority overlapped; sectarian and legal-school competition formed part of urban life.

### Legal, hadith, and literary teachers

Sources name among his teachers:

- **Abū Saʿd al-Mubārak al-Mukharrimī/al-Mukharramī**: Hanbali jurist, principal legal teacher, and later transmitter of the Sufi cloak.
- **Abū al-Wafāʾ ʿAlī ibn ʿAqīl** (d. 1119): major Hanbali jurist and legal theorist.
- **Abū al-Khaṭṭāb al-Kalwadhānī** (d. 1116/17): leading Hanbali jurist.
- **Qāḍī Abū al-Ḥusayn**: jurisprudence.
- **Abū Ghālib ibn al-Bāqillānī**, **Jaʿfar al-Sarrāj**, **Abū Bakr al-Sawsan**, and **Abū Ṭālib ibn Yūsuf**: hadith and related disciplines.
- **Zakariyyā al-Tabrīzī**: Arabic language and literature.

Some lists expand substantially in later sources. The core picture—advanced Hanbali study combined with hadith and preaching—is credible.

### Legal affiliation

Tradition says that he initially followed the Shafiʿi school and changed to Hanbalism in Baghdad, while sometimes issuing opinions according to both schools. His securely attributed or early transmitted teachings are decisively Hanbali in legal and creedal idiom.

The story that Aḥmad ibn Hanbal appeared in a dream and ordered him to revive the weakened Hanbali school is **hagiographical legitimization**, not documentary evidence.

### Sufi formation

The principal ascetic mentor named is **Abū al-Khayr Ḥammād ibn Muslim al-Dabbās** (d. 1131), a Baghdad spiritual teacher reportedly harsh toward the young scholar.

Al-Jīlānī’s formal *khirqa*, or initiatory cloak, was reportedly received around 1100 from al-Mukharrimī rather than directly from al-Dabbās. One later initiation chain runs:

al-Mukharrimī → Abū al-Ḥasan al-Hakkārī → Abū al-Faraj al-Ṭarsūsī → ʿAbd al-Wāḥid al-Tamīmī → al-Shiblī → al-Junayd.

**Evidentiary status:** the immediate relationship with al-Mukharrimī is well reported; the complete chain is an institutional Sufi genealogy whose early form is harder to establish.

## Ascetic Withdrawal, Poverty, and Travels in Iraq

Accounts say that al-Jīlānī experienced extreme poverty and spent years wandering or living in ruins and desert areas around Baghdad. Later biographies turn this into a **twenty-five-year retreat**.

Reported locations include:

- Baghdad’s abandoned quarters and ruins;
- the Tigris banks;
- the *burj al-ʿajamī*, or “Persian tower”;
- rural and desert areas of Iraq;
- possibly Wāsiṭ.

Stories include prolonged fasting, living on discarded vegetation, exposure to cold and rain, resisting temptation, and being fed only when another person placed food in his mouth.

The precise chronology is internally difficult. A literal uninterrupted twenty-five-year absence does not fit neatly with study, teaching, marriage, and his documented public emergence by 1127. The strongest reconstruction is intermittent ascetic withdrawal and poverty, later regularized into a continuous heroic period.

### The apparition of light

One of the best-known legends says that a brilliant light appeared and announced: “I am your Lord; I have made lawful for you what I forbade others.” Al-Jīlānī recognized Satan and rejected the apparition. Satan then claimed to have misled seventy ascetics in this way; al-Jīlānī answered that only divine grace saved him.

**Evidentiary status: didactic Sufi legend.** It teaches that mystical experience remains subordinate to revealed law and should not be treated as biographical proof of an apparition.

### Pilgrimage

He probably performed the pilgrimage to Mecca, possibly more than once. A late-life pilgrimage with a son is reported. Narratives that he invested numerous Sufis in Mecca cannot be dated securely.

Saʿdī’s *Gulistān* claims that the author saw al-Jīlānī praying at the Kaʿba. This is chronologically impossible: Saʿdī was born decades after al-Jīlānī’s death. The scene is literary or legendary.

## Public Career in Baghdad, 1127–1166

### Beginning of preaching

In **521 AH/1127 CE**, at approximately fifty, al-Jīlānī emerged as a public preacher. Early audiences were reportedly small. His reputation expanded rapidly, and meetings moved from the madrasa to larger spaces, including a mosque or open ground at Bāb al-Ḥalba.

Claims that **70,000 people** heard him at once, including listeners at great distances who perceived every word, are hagiographical numerical amplification. Nevertheless, independent biographical traditions agree that his preaching became exceptionally popular.

### School and institutional base

Abū Saʿd al-Mukharrimī had established a small Hanbali school in the Bāb al-Azaj/Bāb al-Araj area of eastern Baghdad. Al-Jīlānī assumed its direction or taught there after al-Mukharrimī. The complex was enlarged as attendance grew.

Instruction reportedly covered:

- Hanbali and sometimes Shafiʿi law;
- Qurʾanic interpretation and recitation;
- hadith;
- Arabic grammar;
- legal opinions;
- sermons and spiritual discipline.

The institution also served as a residence, ribāṭ, charitable center, and family base. It was neither a modern corporation nor a centralized international order.

### Livelihood and finances

No dependable balance sheet, waqf deed, salary record, or inventory of his personal property has been identified in the accessible sources.

Hagiography emphasizes early destitution and later generosity. Donations from merchants, officials, and ordinary devotees presumably supported the school, household, guests, and poor. Stories portray al-Jīlānī rejecting or redistributing rulers’ money, but specific amounts and donors are rarely verifiable.

There is no credible evidence that he accumulated commercial investments, ran trading enterprises, held patents, developed technologies, or controlled a transregional financial organization.

His large household and institution required resources. Later assertions of uninterrupted material poverty should therefore be read as claims about personal detachment, not proof that no funds or property passed through the school.

### Relations with political power

Al-Jīlānī lived under Abbasid caliphs including al-Mustarshid, al-Rāshid, al-Muqtafī, and al-Mustanjid, alongside changing Seljuk power.

Sermons attributed to him condemn hypocritical scholars, oppressive rulers, corrupt officials, attachment to wealth, and religious office sought for status. He reportedly admonished caliphs and viziers publicly.

Some biographies claim that caliphs attended him, feared his rebuke, or altered appointments after his criticism. These are plausible expressions of a preacher’s moral authority but are thinly documented as specific political interventions.

There is no evidence that he:

- held military command;
- led rebellion;
- occupied a caliphal ministry;
- was imprisoned or formally exiled;
- organized political violence;
- participated in Crusader warfare;
- founded a political party or state.

### Students and listeners

Named transmitters and associates include:

- sons ʿAbd al-Razzāq and ʿAbd al-Wahhāb;
- Abū al-Suʿūd al-Ḥuraymī;
- ʿAlī ibn al-Haytī;
- Abū Bakr ʿAbd Allāh al-Tamīmī;
- ʿUmar al-Bazzāz;
- later Hanbalis including Muwaffaq al-Dīn Ibn Qudāma and ʿAbd al-Ghanī al-Maqdisī, who encountered him near the end of his life.

Some enormous disciple lists derive from later order-building and should not be treated as attendance registers.

### Contemporary dissent

Al-Samʿānī reportedly heard him and remarked that he understood little. This is documented as a report in later scholarship but does not reveal whether the problem was acoustics, mystical language, delivery, or criticism.

Ibn al-Jawzī, a younger Hanbali preacher, criticized material about al-Jīlānī. The surviving relationship is best described as intellectual and reputational tension, not a demonstrated personal vendetta.

## Religious and Intellectual Development

### Hanbali traditionalism

Al-Jīlānī’s surviving teachings emphasize:

- Qurʾan and prophetic practice;
- literal or non-allegorical acceptance of divine attributes;
- opposition to speculative theology;
- obedience to religious law;
- ritual worship and repentance;
- distrust of innovations;
- strong criticism of Muʿtazila, Jahmiyya, and Shiʿi doctrines;
- veneration of Aḥmad ibn Hanbal.

*Al-Ghunya* includes formulations which modern readers variously classify as Athari, traditionalist, or potentially anthropomorphic. The text denies crude corporealism while resisting metaphorical reinterpretation.

This has made him usable in competing modern narratives:

- Qādirī Sufis present him as an exemplary Sunni saint.
- Salafi/Athari writers claim him as a traditionalist later misappropriated by shrine-centered Sufism.
- Ashʿari and Maturidi Sufis often emphasize his spiritual authority while interpreting or questioning polemical passages.
- Critics of Sufism use later practices at his shrine to distinguish the historical preacher from his cult.

### Spiritual teaching

Core themes include:

- annihilation of self-will before God;
- repentance and renunciation;
- sincerity;
- trust in God without neglect of lawful action;
- gradual discipline rather than antinomian ecstasy;
- service to the poor;
- purification of intention;
- submission of mystical claims to scripture and law;
- progression from attachment to created things toward exclusive dependence on God.

He criticized performative asceticism and those who used religious dress or rhetoric for income and status. His discourse remains exhortative rather than systematically philosophical.

### Was he a Sufi?

Three positions recur:

1. **Historical Sufi master:** initiation, ascetic training, terminology, *khirqa*, and sermon content place him clearly within twelfth-century Sufism.
2. **Primarily Hanbali preacher later appropriated by Sufis:** restrained contemporary sources say less about an organized mystical order than later Qādirī biographies.
3. **Both:** he was a Hanbali jurist and spiritual master, but not founder of a centralized tariqa in the later institutional sense.

Hamza Malik’s study argues from *al-Ghunya*, *Futūḥ al-ghayb*, and *al-Fatḥ al-rabbānī* that the Sufi element cannot simply be dismissed. [SOAS Research Repository](https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/output/368267)

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Abū Saʿd al-Mukharrimī

**Documented/reported fact:** principal Hanbali teacher; associated with al-Jīlānī’s madrasa and formal investiture. He was both institutional predecessor and spiritual transmitter.

### Ḥammād al-Dabbās

**Reported fact with hagiographical enlargement:** ascetic mentor who tested and sometimes humiliated him. Later tradition calls al-Jīlānī his disciple and possibly his son-in-law.

### Ibn ʿAqīl and al-Kalwadhānī

**Reported fact:** major Hanbali teachers. Their jurisprudential milieu explains al-Jīlānī’s learned legal identity.

### Abū al-Suʿūd al-Ḥuraymī

**Reported disciple and spiritual associate.** Ibn ʿArabī later ranked Abū al-Suʿūd’s spiritual non-intervention above al-Jīlānī’s miraculous activity. This was a theoretical judgment made after al-Jīlānī’s death, not evidence of hostility.

### Ibn al-Jawzī

**Documented critical relation at the level of texts and reputation.** Both were Baghdad Hanbali preachers. Ibn al-Jawzī objected to extravagant claims and reportedly composed *Durar al-jawāhir* about him, though the work is not readily available in a stable critical edition.

### Ibn Qudāma and ʿAbd al-Ghanī al-Maqdisī

**Reported fact:** the young Palestinian Hanbali scholars reached Baghdad in 1165–66 and benefited from al-Jīlānī shortly before his death. The relationship was brief, not decades-long discipleship.

### Patrons, rivals, and enemies

No securely identified enduring patron controlled his career. No named political enemy, prosecuting authority, military rival, or personal persecutor is securely documented. Later narratives tend to personify “worldly jurists,” corrupt rulers, and envious scholars rather than document individual conflicts.

## Death

Al-Jīlānī died in Baghdad in **561 AH/1166 CE**, reportedly at an advanced age near ninety lunar years. The day is variously given as 8, 10, or 11 Rabīʿ II.

His son ʿAbd al-Wahhāb reportedly led the funeral prayer. He was buried in the madrasa where he had taught.

A deathbed appendix attached to *Futūḥ al-ghayb* portrays him surrounded by sons, affirming divine unity and declaring that he feared nothing except God. This is early family transmission but remains a literary death narrative rather than a verbatim transcript.

No medically meaningful diagnosis or documented cause of death survives. There is no reliable evidence of poisoning, assassination, accident, addiction, psychiatric illness, imprisonment, or execution.

---

## Shrine, Institution, and Material Legacy

The grave became the center of a major shrine complex in Baghdad’s Bāb al-Shaykh district.

- **1258:** the complex was reportedly damaged during the Mongol sack of Baghdad.
- **Later centuries:** repeatedly rebuilt and enlarged.
- **Ottoman period:** developed into a prominent architectural and pilgrimage institution.
- **Modern period:** remained an active shrine, mosque, school, charitable institution, and hereditary custodial center.

Pilgrims from Iraq, Turkey, Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia continue to visit. Some combine the visit with travel for the Meccan pilgrimage.

The anniversary, generally called the **ʿurs** or **Gyarhwin Sharif** in South Asia, is celebrated on or around 11 Rabīʿ II with Qurʾan recitation, food distribution, invocations, sermons, and narratives of the saint.

## The Qādiriyya

### Foundation problem

Calling al-Jīlānī the “founder” is accurate as retrospective lineage language but potentially misleading institutionally. No evidence shows that he created a uniform international organization called the Qādiriyya.

The more cautious reconstruction is:

1. he directed a Baghdad teaching and devotional community;
2. sons and disciples preserved sermons, family prestige, and initiations;
3. later transmitters constructed branching *silsilas*;
4. by the fourteenth century, the name Qādiriyya denoted recognizable devotional lineages;
5. locally autonomous branches spread widely.

### Expansion

The order or family of lineages spread into:

- Iraq, Syria, Anatolia, and Iran;
- North Africa and the western Sahara;
- Egypt and Sudan;
- West Africa, including Senegal, Mauritania, Guinea, and Nigeria;
- the Horn of Africa and East Africa;
- Central and South Asia;
- India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka;
- Malaysia and Indonesia.

Its flexibility was central to expansion. Qādirī branches usually shared reverence for al-Jīlānī and an initiatic genealogy but not a single administration, political program, ritual manual, or hereditary leader.

### Political afterlives

Later Qādirī figures became involved in state formation, resistance, scholarship, trade, and colonial politics. These activities cannot automatically be attributed to al-Jīlānī’s personal program.

Examples of later Qādirī influence include West African scholarly networks and the family of Algerian Emir ʿAbd al-Qādir. Such connections are spiritual or genealogical claims across centuries, not direct associations.

## Finances, Property, and Organizational Questions

The available evidence does not permit a reconstruction of al-Jīlānī’s personal wealth.

**Documented or probable:**

- He administered or inherited direction of a madrasa.
- The school expanded.
- A large family and resident community were maintained.
- Donations and charitable endowments probably supported operations.
- His descendants later held custodial, judicial, educational, and political positions.

**Not established:**

- personal ownership of large estates;
- commercial partnerships;
- investments or banking relationships;
- concealed wealth;
- diversion of charitable property;
- hereditary control of a contemporary international Qādirī treasury.

Later institutions using his name are legally and financially distinct. Misconduct by a modern shrine custodian, claimant, or Qādirī organization would not constitute evidence about the twelfth-century individual.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Adversarial Findings

### Dedicated adversarial-search result

Searches using Arabic and English variants combined with *controversy*, *scandal*, *allegations*, *lawsuit*, *fraud*, *accusations*, *criticism*, and *conspiracy*, including results from 2024–2026, produced no credible evidence of a personal lawsuit, criminal prosecution, financial fraud, sexual scandal, political conspiracy, money laundering, or violent crime committed by the historical al-Jīlānī.

Modern search results are heavily contaminated by:

- unrelated persons named Abdul Qadir Jilani;
- the modern Pakistani “Al-Qadir Trust” case;
- contemporary hereditary pīrs claiming descent;
- online sectarian arguments;
- modern institutions trading under Qādirī or Jīlānī names.

These are not evidence about the medieval subject.

The major genuine disputes concern the record itself.

### Fabricated hadith and weak reports

Ibn Kathīr and others reported that *al-Ghunya* and *Futūḥ al-ghayb* contain weak or fabricated hadith.

**Evidentiary label: documented scholarly criticism.** This does not prove that al-Jīlānī knowingly fabricated traditions. Possibilities include reliance on circulating preaching material, weak transmission standards in exhortation, scribal interpolation, or later editorial accretion.

### Al-Shaṭṭanawfī’s hagiography

Ibn Rajab judged *Bahjat al-asrār* and comparable narratives full of absurdities or material unworthy of attribution to al-Jīlānī. Al-Dhahabī likewise respected al-Jīlānī while warning that false stories accumulated around him.

**Evidentiary label: documented medieval criticism.**

The criticism matters because *Bahjat al-asrār* became the principal reservoir for later miracle traditions. It does not follow that every report in it is false; each report requires individual source criticism.

### Shathiyyāt and cosmic claims

Statements attributed to al-Jīlānī include:

- “My foot is upon the neck of every saint of God.”
- claims of command over saints, jinn, nature, or distant events;
- guarantees of salvation for followers;
- universal posthumous assistance.

**Evidentiary label: disputed attribution/hagiography.** These claims are prominent in later Qādirī memory but poorly established as verbatim statements from his lifetime.

Al-Suhrawardī interpreted ecstatic sayings as utterances made in spiritual intoxication. Other scholars rejected them or explained them metaphorically.

### Shrine invocation and “madad”

Devotees invoke “Yā ʿAbd al-Qādir, madad” and understand the saint as an intercessor or divinely enabled helper.

- **Documented fact:** this is a widespread posthumous devotional practice.
- **Theological claim:** that al-Jīlānī actually hears or responds is not historically testable.
- **Controversy:** Salafi and other anti-shrine critics classify direct invocation as prohibited innovation or polytheistic practice; Qādirī and other Sunni-Sufi authorities distinguish independent divine power from assistance granted by God.

The practice therefore documents his reception, not his own demonstrated teaching.

### Sectarian appropriation

Modern disputes ask whether he was “really”:

- Sufi or anti-Sufi;
- Athari, Salafi, Ashʿari, or something predating those modern boundaries;
- a strict Hanbali or dual Hanbali-Shafiʿi jurist;
- Persian, Kurdish, Arab, or Gilaki.

His own milieu supports “Hanbali traditionalist and ascetic spiritual teacher.” More precise modern labels often involve retrospective identity construction.

### Anti-Shiʿi material in *al-Ghunya*

*Al-Ghunya* contains severe polemics against Shiʿi groups and identifies some opponents in language characteristic of medieval Sunni heresiography.

**Documented textual fact, subject to attribution and transmission questions.** Later stories that Shiʿi copyists altered the work to defame particular figures remain allegations without a demonstrated manuscript history sufficient to settle every passage.

### No established criminality

No reliable source supports allegations of murder, coercive conversion, embezzlement, sexual abuse, enslavement enterprises, collaboration with intelligence services, or participation in political massacres. Silence in medieval sources is not proof of every negative, but no evidentiary case currently exists.

## Myths, Legends, Folklore, and Disputed Narratives

### Infant fasting

**Story:** he would not nurse during Ramadan daylight.

**Appearance and circulation:** later Qādirī hagiography and popular *manāqib* recitations.

**Evidence:** no contemporary corroboration.

**Effect:** establishes innate sanctity and links his birth to Ramadan discipline.

### The honest child and the bandits

**Story:** he confessed to carrying forty gold coins; the robbers repented.

**Evidence:** moralizing hagiography; no independent caravan or legal record.

**Effect:** one of the most widely taught Muslim stories about absolute truthfulness.

### Twenty-five years in the wilderness

**Story:** he wandered the deserts and ruins of Iraq for twenty-five years under extreme ascetic conditions.

**Evidence:** some basis may exist in periods of poverty and retreat; the fixed duration is difficult to reconcile with his other activities.

**Effect:** supplies the ordeal through which the public saint was formed.

### Defeat of Satan as divine light

**Story:** he rejected an apparition claiming to abolish religious prohibitions.

**Evidence:** didactic legend.

**Effect:** portrays his Sufism as law-bound and resistant to antinomian revelation.

### Seventy thousand listeners

**Story:** 70,000 people heard him clearly in open-air preaching.

**Evidence:** no logistical or contemporaneous record supports the number.

**Effect:** quantifies universal charisma.

### “My foot upon the neck of every saint”

**Story:** al-Jīlānī made the declaration in Baghdad; saints everywhere bowed their necks, including Aḥmad al-Rifāʿī.

**Evidence:** developed hagiography, not securely contemporary.

**Effect:** established his rank as supreme saint and provided a ritualized hierarchy among later orders.

### Rescue of ships and travelers

**Story:** he appeared at sea or from a distance to rescue devotees, sometimes returning with wet sleeves.

**Evidence:** miracle folklore in multiple regional forms.

**Effect:** made him patron of travelers, seafarers, merchants, and people in sudden danger.

### Reviving a dead chicken

**Story:** after eating a chicken, he commanded its bones and feathers to reassemble and live.

**Evidence:** folkloric miracle patterned on saintly resurrection motifs.

**Effect:** dramatizes mastery over nature.

### Hajj every night

**Story:** he traveled miraculously from Baghdad to Mecca and returned before morning.

**Evidence:** devotional legend.

**Effect:** collapses sacred geography and makes Baghdad an extension of the pilgrimage world.

### Control of jinn

**Story:** he judged disputes among jinn, expelled possessing spirits, or compelled them to obedience.

**Evidence:** hagiographical and folkloric narrative.

**Effect:** central to his reputation as a source of protection and healing.

### Birthplace disputes

Iranian, Iraqi, Arab, and Kurdish writers have claimed him for different local or ethnic histories.

**Evidence:** the nisba al-Jīlānī and Persianate family terminology favor Gīlān; specific modern national identities cannot be projected cleanly into the eleventh century.

**Effect:** his ancestry has become an object of regional prestige.

### A thousand books

**Story:** he wrote approximately one thousand works.

**Evidence:** unsupported hagiographical claim. Roughly fifty titles have circulated under his name, and only a small core has a serious claim to early transmission.

**Effect:** transforms a preacher whose words were often collected by listeners into a universal author.

## Works by or Attributed to Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani

No surviving item can safely be treated as an author’s autograph. Several principal works are listener- or family-compiled sermon collections.

### Strongest or comparatively strong attribution

#### *al-Ghunya li-ṭālibī ṭarīq al-ḥaqq*  
الغنية لطالبي طريق الحق

Also called *Ghunyat al-ṭālibīn*.

A broad manual of ritual practice, law, creed, ethics, devotional calendars, sectarian classifications, and ascetic life. Its underlying association with al-Jīlānī is early and generally accepted, but individual passages may be interpolated. First composition date is unknown; printed Cairo editions appeared in the nineteenth century.

#### *al-Fatḥ al-rabbānī wa-al-fayḍ al-raḥmānī*  
الفتح الرباني والفيض الرحماني

Sixty-two discourses, most dated to **545–546 AH/1150–52**, reportedly transcribed at the madrasa and ribāṭ. It is among the most important sources for his mature preaching. The surviving textual form is editorial rather than autograph.

#### *Futūḥ al-ghayb*  
فتوح الغيب

Seventy-eight short discourses plus a deathbed testament, reportedly collected by his son ʿAbd al-Razzāq. Ibn Taymiyya later commented on passages under the title *Sharḥ kalimāt min Futūḥ al-ghayb*. The core is widely treated as early, though complete textual integrity is not guaranteed.

### Derived or composite works

#### *Jalāʾ al-khāṭir min kalām al-Shaykh ʿAbd al-Qādir*  
جلاء الخاطر

A collection overlapping with sections of *al-Fatḥ al-rabbānī*. It is a compilation from sermons rather than a clearly separate authored book.

#### *al-Sirāj al-wahhāj fī laylat al-miʿrāj*  
السراج الوهاج في ليلة المعراج

Extracted or rearranged from the Miʿrāj material in *al-Ghunya*; not an independent composition.

### Attributed but uncertain

- *Sirr al-asrār wa-maẓhar al-anwār* — سر الأسرار ومظهر الأنوار.
- *Maktūbāt* or *Khamsata ʿashara maktūban*, “Fifteen Letters.”
- *al-Fuyūḍāt al-rabbāniyya fī awrād al-Qādiriyya*.
- *al-Dalāʾil*, also known as *Awrād* or *al-Ṣalawāt al-kubrā*.
- *Kitāb fī uṣūl al-dīn*.
- *al-Asmāʾ al-ḥusnā*.
- *Dhikr al-maqāmāt fī ṭarīq al-ḥaqq*.
- *Yawaqīt al-ḥikam*.
- *al-Mawāhib al-raḥmāniyya*.
- *Kitāb-i Khamsa-yi Gīlānī*.

Manuscripts exist under several of these names, but a manuscript bearing his name is not proof of authorship.

### Poetry attributed to him

Collections attribute to “Muḥyī” poems called:

- *al-Khamriyya*;
- *al-Ummiyya*;
- *al-Tāʾiyya*;
- *al-Lāmiyya*;
- *al-Taṣawwufiyya*;
- *al-Qaṣīda al-Ghawthiyya*;
- *ʿAqīdat al-Bāz al-Ashhab*.

**Status:** disputed or late attribution. Poems asserting cosmic saintly supremacy are especially likely to reflect later Qādirī devotion.

### Rejected attribution

#### *al-Ghawthiyya* / *Risālat al-Ghawthiyya*

A supposed dialogue between God and al-Jīlānī.

**Status:** widely judged spurious, including by Rashīd Riḍā and modern reference scholarship. It reflects later mystical language and was attached to al-Jīlānī’s name to authorize a developed theology of sainthood.

### The “Tafsir al-Jilani”

A Qurʾanic commentary published in the twenty-first century as *al-Fawātiḥ al-ilāhiyya wa-al-mafātiḥ al-ghaybiyya* or *Tafsīr al-Jīlānī* has been promoted as a recovered work.

**Status: seriously disputed attribution.** Earlier manuscript cataloguing and scholarship commonly associate the work with Niʿmat Allāh al-Nakhjawānī, a much later author. Attribution to al-Jīlānī requires manuscript evidence predating that association, not merely a modern title page.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Al-Jīlānī became one of the most widely venerated saints in Sunni Islam.

His posthumous image combines several roles:

- Hanbali legal scholar;
- disciplinarian of the ego;
- reconciler of law and mystical practice;
- supreme saint or cosmic *quṭb*;
- posthumous helper;
- protector of travelers and the afflicted;
- ancestor of hereditary religious families;
- symbolic founder of a decentralized global order.

In South Asia his green flag was historically carried during epidemics, including cholera, while devotees invoked his aid. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/abd-al-qader-jilani/)

His shrine survived conquest, reconstruction, political change, and modern sectarian conflict. The grave’s endurance helped make Baghdad a devotional center extending beyond local Hanbalism.

The tension in his legacy remains structural:

- his authenticated preaching insists on exclusive dependence upon God;
- later devotees invoke him as God’s greatest created helper;
- reformist critics use his own legalism against the cult surrounding him;
- Qādirī interpreters answer that intercession never implies independent divine power.

Both the austere preacher and the miracle-working universal saint are historically consequential, but they belong to different evidentiary layers.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supportable from the supplied roster are included. Mere contemporaneity, shared geography, or membership in a later chain is not treated as a personal meeting.

### Abu al-Qasim al-Junayd ibn Muhammad al-Baghdadi

- **Nature:** posthumous spiritual-lineage connection.
- **Dates/places:** al-Junayd died in Baghdad in 910, long before al-Jīlānī’s birth.
- **Evidence label:** **documented later Qādirī lineage; no personal contact possible.**
- Qādirī chains connect al-Jīlānī through al-Mukharrimī, al-Hakkārī, al-Ṭarsūsī, al-Tamīmī, and al-Shiblī to al-Junayd. The historical accuracy of every link requires separate scrutiny.

### Abu al-Qasim Abd al-Karim ibn Hawazin al-Qushayri

- **Nature:** possible textual/intellectual background through the Sunni-Sufi tradition.
- **Dates:** al-Qushayrī died in 1072, before al-Jīlānī’s birth.
- **Evidence label:** **reported intellectual influence, not a documented direct relationship.**
- Al-Qushayrī’s synthesis of law-bound Sufism formed part of the wider intellectual environment. No source consulted establishes personal transmission or a specific acknowledged reading by al-Jīlānī.

### Abu Yazid Tayfur ibn Isa al-Bistami

- **Nature:** predecessor occasionally cited in the broader Sufi discourse inherited by al-Jīlānī.
- **Evidence label:** **reported intellectual background only.**
- No direct contact was possible, and no secure personal chain through al-Bisṭāmī was established.

### Mansur al-Hallaj

- **Nature:** predecessor in Baghdad’s mystical history.
- **Evidence label:** **later comparative association, not a documented direct influence.**
- Later writers contrast al-Ḥallāj’s ecstatic, politically dangerous reputation with al-Jīlānī’s law-bound public preaching. No meeting was possible.

### Ibrahim ibn Adham and Rabia al-Adawiyya

- **Nature:** earlier ascetic exemplars with whom al-Jīlānī was later compared in folklore and devotional literature.
- **Evidence label:** **posthumous symbolic association.**
- The TDV account specifically places al-Jīlānī alongside Ibrāhīm ibn Adham in Turkish folklore. No direct relationship was possible.

### Ibn Arabi

- **Nature:** posthumous interpretation and ranking.
- **Dates/places:** Ibn ʿArabī was born in 1165, approximately one year before al-Jīlānī died; no meeting is documented.
- **Evidence label:** **documented posthumous intellectual reception; no documented personal contact.**
- Ibn ʿArabī attributed abundant miracles to al-Jīlānī, categorized him among spiritually distinctive figures, and compared his spiritual station with that of his disciple Abū al-Suʿūd.

### Khwaja Mu'in al-Din Hasan Chishti

- **Nature:** alleged meeting, investiture, or spiritual connection.
- **Evidence label:** **later tradition/disputed.**
- Indo-Muslim devotional literature sometimes says Muʿīn al-Dīn Chishtī met al-Jīlānī or received blessing from him. Chronology does not make contact absolutely impossible under some proposed birth dates, but no strong near-contemporary evidence establishes it. Later Qādirī-Chishtī affiliations do not prove a personal meeting.

### Abu Abdallah Muhammad ibn Battuta

- **Nature:** later visitor to Baghdad and witness to the city’s sacred geography.
- **Dates:** Ibn Baṭṭūṭa visited Baghdad in the fourteenth century, roughly 160 years after al-Jīlānī’s death.
- **Evidence label:** **posthumous geographical/reputational connection; no personal contact.**
- His travels belong to the period when Baghdad’s saintly sites and institutions were embedded in pilgrimage geography. A precise claim that he entered al-Jīlānī’s tomb complex should be tied to a critical edition of the *Riḥla* before being stated as certain.

### Jalal al-Din Rumi, Shams al-Din Muhammad of Tabriz, Farid al-Din Attar, Baha ud-Din Walad, and Nizam ad-Din Auliya

- **Nature:** shared later Sufi canon or institutional environment.
- **Evidence label:** **no direct connection established in the sources consulted.**
- Later devotional anthologies often place these figures within a common sacred history, but no meeting or correspondence with al-Jīlānī is supported.

### Emir Abdelkader ibn Muhieddine

- **Nature:** later Qādirī affiliation and claimed family/spiritual descent.
- **Dates/places:** nineteenth-century Algeria, Morocco, Ottoman lands, and Damascus.
- **Evidence label:** **documented Qādirī institutional influence; claimed genealogy requires separate verification.**
- Emir ʿAbd al-Qādir’s religious formation drew upon the Qādiriyya named for al-Jīlānī. This is influence across seven centuries, not a personal relationship.

### Muhammad Baha al-Din Naqshband Bukhari

- **Nature:** later composite Qādirī-Naqshbandī lineages.
- **Evidence label:** **posthumous institutional combination.**
- Bahaʾ al-Dīn lived two centuries later. Orders joining Qādirī and Naqshbandī initiations document later synthesis, not contact between their eponyms.

### Muhammad ibn Abdullah, Ali ibn Abi Talib, Fatima, Hasan, and Husayn

Only Muhammad appears explicitly in the roster.

- **Nature:** prophetic model and endpoint of initiatic and genealogical traditions.
- **Evidence label:** **documented religious influence; claimed genealogical connection is later tradition.**
- Al-Jīlānī’s teachings are framed around Muhammad’s Qurʾanic and prophetic example. Qādirī genealogies make him a descendant through Fāṭima and al-Ḥasan, while initiatic chains ultimately reach Muhammad through ʿAlī. The biological genealogy is not independently proved.

No credible connection was found with the overwhelming majority of the atlas roster. Absence is expected: most lived in different centuries, regions, or intellectual worlds.
