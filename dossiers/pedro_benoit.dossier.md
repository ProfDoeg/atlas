# Pedro Benoit: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and documentary cautions

This dossier reconstructs the life and afterlife of Pedro Benoit from official heritage records, government decrees, university and municipal histories, digitized technical and scientific publications, genealogy databases, specialist architectural studies, local historical journalism, family testimony, and the published genetic study relevant to the Bourbon-survival legend attached to his father. Where an official record, a later scholarly interpretation, a family tradition, and popular retelling differ, the versions are separated rather than harmonized.

Several limitations affect the record. Benoit left no known autobiography, diary, or published memoir. Much of his surviving output consists of plans, surveys, reports, and institutional work, often produced inside collective government offices. The original 1882 project file for La Plata is incomplete, and the foundation plans are unsigned. Public genealogies do not yield a fully secure list of all ten children reported by descendants. Some frequently repeated claims—two Paris gold medals, a university professorship, personal poverty at death, and the precise symbolic program of La Plata—are not equally supported by contemporary documentation.

The description “Belgian-Argentine,” sometimes attached to this research subject, is not supported by the consulted records. Benoit was born in Buenos Aires and was Argentine. His father was a French immigrant, normally identified as a native of Calais. No evidence consulted establishes Belgian birth, nationality, or ancestry for Pedro Benoit.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father: Pierre or Pedro Benito Benoit

Pedro’s father was **Pierre Benoit**, commonly Hispanicized as **Pedro Benito Benoit**. Documentary and genealogical reconstructions usually identify him as **Pierre François Benoit**, born in Calais, France, around 1794, son of Pedro/Nicolas/François Benoit and Marie-Jeanne Daut or Dot. The exact arrangement of his baptismal names and his birth date varies among later sources; 3 August 1794 is frequently given.

Pierre arrived at Buenos Aires on **1 July 1818** aboard the French vessel *La Chiffonne*, sailing from Le Havre. He worked as an architect, engineer, and topographer in the young River Plate state. He belonged to the technical circle associated with Bernardino Rivadavia and the French architect Próspero Catelin and took part in public-works and surveying activity. This profession and network provided Pedro with both a technical apprenticeship and an entrée into government service.

Pierre died in Buenos Aires on **21 or 22 August 1852**; the date differs by one day across family and cemetery accounts. A late family tradition says that he was poisoned with arsenic. A reported 1996 forensic examination is said in press and family accounts to have found arsenic, but the underlying forensic report was not located, and the allegation cannot be treated as an established cause of death. Pierre’s much larger posthumous identity as the supposedly escaped Louis XVII is examined under “Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives.”

### Mother and maternal family

Pedro’s mother was **María Josefa de las Mercedes Leyes Espinosa**, whose surname also appears as **Reyes** in genealogical records. She was Argentine. In 1836 Pierre acquired her interest in the family property at Bolívar and Independencia; this house became an important material link between the family’s Buenos Aires period and Pedro’s later reputation.

### Sibling

The consistently identified sibling is an elder sister, **Petrona Mercedes Benoit**, called **Petra**, born in 1829. Genealogical accounts report no descendants from her. Publicly accessible sources do not establish whether there were other children who died young.

### Marriage

Pedro Benoit married **Dolores Vázquez Silva**—also spelled Vásquez—on **14 April 1860** at the parish of the Inmaculada Concepción in Buenos Aires. One later cemetery account gives 1880, but church-based genealogical records and a University of Buenos Aires historical conference support 1860. Dolores was born in 1838 or 1839 and died on **15 November 1904**. In La Plata she served as president of the directing council and later honorary president of the local Sociedad de Beneficencia, placing the family within the capital’s civic-charitable elite.

### Children and descendants

Descendant accounts state that Pedro and Dolores had **ten children**. The full ten-name list has not been securely reconstructed from the public digital record. Named children found across genealogical databases and family-oriented sources include:

- **Dolores Cándida Benoit Vázquez**, born 12 January 1861; married José Matías Zapiola Rivera or Rivero on 31 May 1882. The Zapiola line became a principal custodian and promoter of the family’s Louis XVII tradition.
- **Pedro Juan Benoit Vázquez**, baptized 8 February 1862 and died 11 May 1919; architect or architectural practitioner. He continued work on the Mar del Plata cathedral after his father’s death and is also credited with the pantheon for veterans of the Paraguayan War. He married Elina Octaviana del Carmen Medina Canedo on 29 September 1884. Their sons included Pedro Pablo and Pedro Raúl.
- **Carlos Cándido Benoit Vázquez**.
- **Ramón Segundo Benoit Vázquez**.
- **Roque Mariano Benoit Vázquez**.
- **Guillermo Benoit Vázquez**.
- **Arturo Benoit Vázquez**.

Some online genealogies list a **Pedro Raúl Benoit** born in 1887 as Pedro and Dolores’s son, while other reconstructions place Pedro Raúl in the next generation as a son of Pedro Juan. This is a genuine conflict among secondary trees and illustrates why the reported total of ten cannot be converted into a definitive roster without parish and civil-register research.

The family’s later public representatives include **Federico Zapiola**, **Lucrecia Zapiola de Saravia**, and **Alejandro Zuccarelli Benoit**, who wrote or spoke about the Benoit lineage, Pierre’s alleged royal identity, and Pedro’s legacy. Their testimony is valuable for family memory but is not independent corroboration of the dynastic claim.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1836–1850

Pedro was born into a household already centered on technical drawing, surveying, architecture, and the state’s building program. The family house stood in San Telmo, then part of the political and commercial core of Buenos Aires. His father’s work meant that maps, instruments, plans, measurements, and public commissions were likely part of his formative environment.

No detailed childhood memoir, school register, or list of private tutors was located. Later biographies emphasize instruction under Pierre. That claim is consistent with Pedro’s entry into the provincial topographical administration at an unusually young age, but the precise curriculum and identities of other teachers remain undocumented.

## Education and Formation

### Apprenticeship rather than a conventional degree

In **1850**, at about fourteen, Benoit entered the Province of Buenos Aires’s topographical service—variously named in sources as the Department of Topography and Statistics and, after institutional reorganizations, the Department of Engineers—as a **meritorious draughtsman**. This was both employment and technical formation. He learned through office practice, field surveying, copying and producing cadastral plans, and working with older engineers and surveyors.

His father was the clearest formative mentor. The broader professional milieu included Próspero Catelin and the Rivadavian public-works tradition, although direct classroom study with Catelin has not been established.

On **19 April 1861**, Benoit received the title of **public surveyor**. A University of Buenos Aires historical account says he was exempted from the ordinary examination because eleven years of service demonstrated his competence. On **14 March 1882**, the Department of Engineers granted him the title of **civil engineer**.

The frequent label “architect” describes the work he performed—designing churches, government buildings, cemeteries, schools, and urban ensembles—but should not be mistaken for evidence of a university architecture diploma. Professional categories were less rigidly separated in nineteenth-century Argentina, and experienced engineers routinely designed buildings.

### Formative currents

Benoit’s mature work combines several currents:

- the cadastral and military geometry of the surveyor;
- French and European public-works culture transmitted through his father and state institutions;
- neo-Gothic and eclectic historicism in ecclesiastical architecture;
- nineteenth-century hygienism, visible in ventilation, green space, wide avenues, separated civic functions, and concern with drainage and water;
- rational, geometric urbanism, including diagonal avenues and repeated public squares;
- the associational culture of scientific societies and Freemasonry.

No private philosophical notebook is known, so the relative weight of these influences must be inferred from projects and institutional affiliations rather than personal declarations.

## Early Career, 1850–1879

### Topographical office and field work

Benoit’s early career was spent inside the provincial technical bureaucracy. His tasks included surveys, cadastral plans, road and bridge studies, military and defensive works, flood-related infrastructure, and cartography. He contributed to the technical knowledge later synthesized in maps of Buenos Aires Province, including the 1866 provincial map.

In **1858** he studied or consolidated the **Camino Blanco** route toward Ensenada, an important connection between Buenos Aires and the port area. A surviving catalogued plan dated **20 August 1859**, *Plano de la propiedad de Domingo Iglesias frente a la Boca del Riachuelo*, bears his signature and shows his work in property surveying at the mouth of the Riachuelo.

### Merlo

In **1859**, landowner and official **Juan Dillon** commissioned Benoit to lay out the town of **Merlo**. Benoit subsequently lived in Merlo for approximately two decades. The commission began a long sequence of town plans and public buildings in western Buenos Aires Province.

He designed Merlo’s first school, opened in **1860** and associated with what became the Domingo F. Sarmiento school. In **1863** he designed the church of **Nuestra Señora de la Merced**, executed with master builder Antonio Ayerbe in a pointed neo-Gothic idiom. In **1864** he supported the creation of Merlo as an autonomous partido.

Sources agree that he participated in a local public-health relief committee during an epidemic, but they disagree about the event: some place it during the **1867 cholera epidemic**, while descendant testimony places it at the beginning of the **1871 yellow-fever crisis**. Both epidemics affected Buenos Aires and its surroundings. Without the committee record, the exact date and disease remain unresolved.

### River and transport engineering

In **1863**, Benoit worked on the rectification and canalization of the **Riachuelo**, part of the long effort to make the river more navigable and sanitary. In **1870**, he prepared or participated in studies for a tramway between **Tolosa and Ensenada**. His work was not confined to architectural façades: it extended to roads, canals, rail or tram alignments, drainage, bridges, ports, and the subdivision of territory.

### Additional town layouts

In **1872**, Benoit laid out **Santa Rosa de Ituzaingó** for Manuel Rodríguez Fragio. Secondary biographies also credit him with plans, extensions, or ejidos for Morón, Las Heras, Quilmes, Magdalena, Azul, Bahía Blanca, San Pedro, and Mercedes. The degree of responsibility varies: in some cases he drew a founding layout; in others he surveyed an existing town, prepared an extension, or acted through the Department. These attributions should not all be treated as equivalent without consulting the individual plan files in the provincial Geodesy archive.

### Scientific association

Benoit is reported as a participant or founding-era member of the **Sociedad Científica Argentina**, created in 1872. Its early *Anales* document the technical culture in which he worked: exhibitions, public works, measurements, engineering reports, and the organization of professional sections. Later Masonic biographies identify him as a founding member, but a complete membership register was not located in the consulted material.

### University and Cabildo commissions

In **1877–1878**, Benoit designed and directed the remodeling of the University of Buenos Aires Law Faculty building at Moreno 350, today the Museo Etnográfico Juan B. Ambrosetti. The University’s institutional history describes dean José María Moreno’s expectation that Benoit would direct the work, Benoit’s rapid preparation of plans, and their approval. On **24 November 1884**, he also prepared plans for a library addition.

In **1879**, the Buenos Aires **Cabildo** was remodeled for use as the Casa de Justicia. The national heritage record credits Benoit with the intervention. A technical report, *Casa de Justicia, antiguo Cabildo*, printed in the provincial government memory for 1881–1882, is often attributed to him. Some later archaeological literature instead associates Jorge Coquet with the report or intervention. Until the signed manuscript and administrative file are compared, the division of authorship should be described as disputed rather than silently assigned.

## Chronological Life History

### 1880: federalization conflict and military engineering

The armed confrontation of **1880** over the federalization of Buenos Aires placed Benoit on the provincial side. He designed or supervised fortifications and artillery batteries for the defense of the city and was afterward awarded the rank of **lieutenant colonel of engineers**. This was a formal military-technical role in a domestic political conflict, not evidence of later clandestine or intelligence activity. No record consulted links him to political imprisonment, exile, or prosecution after the defeat of the provincial forces.

The resolution of the conflict federalized the city of Buenos Aires and required Buenos Aires Province to choose a new capital. That institutional rupture created the commission that defined the rest of Benoit’s life.

### 1881–1882: selection and planning of the new capital

Governor **Dardo Rocha**, inaugurated in 1881, charged the provincial administration with locating and building a new capital. Benoit, as an experienced surveyor and senior Department of Engineers figure, helped study the territory around Ensenada, determine coordinates and levels, subdivide urban and rural lots, and organize the public-building program.

The precise authorship of the city plan is the dossier’s most important documentary dispute. The early administrative record shows:

1. The assignment was made to the **Department of Engineers of Buenos Aires Province**, not to a named private architect.
2. Department head **Jorge Coquet** transmitted a plan to the executive on **19 May 1882**.
3. The decree of **5 June 1882** approved “the plan projected by the Department of Engineers.”
4. The surviving foundation plans are unsigned, and the original project file is missing or incomplete.
5. Benoit’s name appears explicitly as project author on an updated plan prepared for the 1889 Paris exposition, several years after the foundation.

The official national heritage description now states that sole authorship has been erroneously assigned to Benoit and treats the design as a synthesis of Department proposals, including **Juan Martín Burgos’s** scheme based on a square, a central cross, and diagonals forming a rhombus. La Plata Ordinance 9159 of 2000 recognized Burgos as one inspiration for the urban trace.

This evidence does not remove Benoit from the project. He was a central technical executive: he worked on location, grading, surveys, subdivision, infrastructure, and public buildings, and he became the plan’s principal public representative. It does, however, make “Benoit alone designed La Plata” an unsustainable statement of documentary fact. The strongest formulation is that the plan was a **collective Department of Engineers project in which Benoit had a major and probably coordinating role**, followed by immense responsibility for implementation.

Other relevant professionals included Jorge Coquet, Juan Martín Burgos, Carlos Glade, Francisco Lavalle, Luis Monteverde, Ernesto Meyer, Pedro Pico, Luis Baldi, and later foreign-born architects and contractors. Historian Alberto de Paula argued that Benoit later displaced the collective authorship by placing his name on the Paris plan. This is a scholarly accusation or interpretation, not a court finding or proven act of document theft. De Paula and other revisionists also note that contemporary commendation decrees of 1883 and 1885 did not single Benoit out as sole planner.

The approved city combined an orthogonal grid with broad avenues, diagonals, a central civic axis, recurring squares, a perimeter boulevard, and major green areas. Official descriptions count **eleven perpendicular avenues and twenty plazas**, with diagonals intersecting the grid at 45 degrees. Hygienist planning, Renaissance ideal-city geometry, European precedents such as Karlsruhe, cadastral practicality, and Burgos’s proposal have all been identified as sources.

### 19 November 1882: foundation of La Plata

La Plata was founded on **19 November 1882**. Benoit directed or coordinated a large portion of the simultaneous construction campaign. The new capital was not a single-authored artwork but an administrative and logistical operation involving expropriation, rail access, brick supply, tenders, labor, drainage, water, parks, and dozens of public commissions.

His architectural section supplied the connective tissue of the city: public buildings were positioned within the plan, related to plazas and boulevards, and designed in a mixture of neo-Renaissance, neo-Gothic, and eclectic languages. Contractors and collaborators transformed the drawings into buildings under intense deadlines.

### 1882–1885: first public buildings

Projects securely associated with Benoit or the section he directed include:

- **San Ponciano church** (begun in the foundation period and opened in 1883), the city’s first Catholic church;
- the **Department of Engineers building** (1883);
- the former **Ministry of Finance** and **Ministry of Government**, both later demolished;
- police, fire-brigade, and prison-related buildings;
- an administrative building for the **Astronomical Observatory**;
- the monumental **entrance arch to the Bosque** (1884), demolished in 1915;
- the early phases and planning of the **Government Palace**;
- the residence of Dardo Rocha, popularly called the **Casa de los 60 días**, built rapidly in 1885–1886 and now a museum.

The national historic-monument decree for the Government Palace gives a more exact division of labor: the Department’s Architecture Section was led by Benoit; **Jules Dormal** developed façade and interior aspects; Benoit initially directed the work with **Luis Baldi**; the cornerstone was laid on **27 November 1882**; and the contract went to **Santiago Bertelli & Company** on 22 January 1883. This example shows why institutional and individual credits must be kept together.

### Cathedral of La Plata

The La Plata cathedral project is attributed to engineers **Pedro Benoit and Pedro Pico**, with drawings by **Ernesto Meyer** and collaboration from figures including **Pedro or Ernest Coutaret** in the wider building program. The immense neo-Gothic structure was conceived in the 1880s but remained unfinished through Benoit’s lifetime; its towers were completed only in 1999. Benoit therefore designed and initiated a project whose familiar final silhouette is partly a twentieth-century completion.

### 1886–1888: consolidation, cemetery, and institutional expansion

Benoit designed the **La Plata municipal cemetery**, opened in 1886 or 1887, with a formal layout and monumental entrance later interpreted through both hygienist and Masonic lenses. He also worked on slaughterhouse facilities, the School of Arts and Trades, infrastructure, and continued public-building adjustments.

Attributions to him for the **Asilo Marín** require qualification. The building history also involves Émile Vaudremer and Adolfo Büttner; Benoit’s participation may have been adaptation, institutional supervision, or a related design rather than sole original authorship.

### 1888–1889: the Paris plan and exposition

An updated plan of La Plata was prepared for the **Exposition Universelle of Paris in 1889**. One version bears a legend identifying it as the project of engineer Pedro Benoit; another associated with Carlos Glade refers to execution by the Department of Engineers. The exhibition made La Plata an international emblem of rapid, rational city-building.

The national heritage page says the plan received a **gold medal**. A widely repeated later story claims **two gold medals**, one for “City of the Future” and one for “Best Constructed Realization.” The official Bureau International des Expositions history confirms the 1889 exposition, and contemporary descriptions confirm Argentine displays, but the two named categories have not been substantiated in the sources consulted. The one-medal official account and the two-medal commemorative tradition should therefore remain distinct.

### 1890–1894: municipal politics and public symbolism

Benoit was elected a **municipal councilor** in 1890, associated with the minority or *rochista* wing of the **Partido Autonomista Nacional**. His relationship with Dardo Rocha joined professional collaboration, civic politics, and friendship.

He designed the **coat of arms of La Plata**, approved on **17 April 1891**. Ernesto Meyer executed the final drawing. Its composition, including the sun, city geometry, river and agricultural-industrial motifs, has generated later Masonic readings; the municipal function was to furnish the young capital with an official civic identity.

Benoit then headed the municipality as commissioner or intendente. General summaries give **18 September 1893 to September 1894**. The municipal archive’s authority record divides the service into interrupted blocks: 18 September 1893–11 January 1894, 23 January–16 March 1894, and 30 April–25 September 1894. Juan Lanusse preceded him and Adolfo Lascano followed him. Later local accounts credit his administration with confronting a deficit, attempting to collect unpaid taxes, regulating building permits, and defending the integrity of the plan. The full municipal account books and council minutes would be required to quantify the result.

### Work beyond La Plata in the 1890s

Benoit continued church and civic commissions across the province. The church of **Nuestra Señora de Luján in Uribelarrea**, inaugurated on 14 July 1890, is commonly attributed to him, though some projects from this period may include the hand of his son Pedro Juan. He is also credited in secondary sources with churches or plans in Ensenada, San Justo, Moreno, San Vicente, Benito Juárez, Azul, and other towns.

In **Mar del Plata**, he designed and directed without salary—according to descendant testimony—the church now known as the **Cathedral Basilica of Saints Peter and Cecilia**. Its cornerstone was laid on **18 January 1893**. Following Pedro’s death, his son **Pedro Juan Benoit** continued the work. Attribution to father and son must therefore be separated by phase.

### 1897: Provincial University of La Plata

The Provincial University of La Plata appointed Benoit to the constituent assembly of its Faculty of Physical-Mathematical Sciences. On **14 February 1897**, the assembly elected Julián Romero dean and Benoit **vice-dean**, with Jorge Coquet and Luis Monteverde as delegates to the Superior Council. Benoit died before the university’s public inauguration on 18 April and before he could begin sustained faculty activity.

Later biographies sometimes call him a professor or first dean. The surviving institutional chronology supports vice-dean-elect of the provincial university, not first dean, and does not demonstrate a completed teaching career.

### Property, income, and financial relationships

The record shows a salaried public professional who also accepted private and ecclesiastical commissions. No reliable balance sheet, salary series, probate inventory, investment portfolio, or corporate holdings was located.

The main documented properties associated with him are:

- the family house at **Bolívar 793 and Independencia**, Buenos Aires, acquired and developed through his parents and later remodeled by Pedro into a more elaborate residence;
- a long residence in **Merlo** during his early and middle career;
- a late residence in **Mar del Plata**, first in a Peralta Ramos property and then in a house he purchased on Avenida Colón, according to local descendant testimony.

Alejandro Zuccarelli Benoit has said that his ancestor entered public service with means and died in debt, refused gifts of land in towns he laid out, and contributed personal money to the Mar del Plata church. These are family assertions that fit the later image of incorruptible public service but were not independently verified from probate or bank records. Dardo Rocha’s funeral address emphasized Benoit’s honesty while administering large public sums. No contemporary accusation of embezzlement or illicit enrichment was found.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Provincial politics

Benoit’s career was inseparable from the Province of Buenos Aires state. The 1880 fortification work aligned him with the provincial government during resistance to federalization. His later association with Dardo Rocha and the *rochista* current of the Partido Autonomista Nacional placed him within the political network that founded La Plata and sought to make Rocha a national leader.

His municipal office was administrative rather than ideological in the surviving record. No manifesto, legislative program, or sustained partisan journalism by Benoit was found. His politics are most visible through public service, alliance with Rocha, and defense of the capital-building project.

### Freemasonry

Masonic sources record Benoit’s initiation on **26 October 1858** in **Consuelo del Infortunio No. 3**. A few later notices say 1859; the lodge date 1858 is the more specific recurring record. He was associated with the creation or early life of **Luz y Verdad No. 79** in 1884 and **La Plata No. 80**, organized in 1885. The lodges connected engineers, officials, merchants, and politicians engaged in the new capital.

This membership is documented. Claims about its consequences vary:

- It plausibly shaped networks of confidence and sociability.
- It provides context for symbolic readings of his seal, the cemetery, the coat of arms, and the city plan.
- It does **not**, by itself, prove that every geometric feature was an encoded Masonic symbol or that a secret lodge directed the provincial government.

### Catholic architecture and religious ambiguity

Benoit designed Catholic churches throughout his career, including Merlo, San Ponciano, the two cathedrals, and several provincial parish churches. These commissions required cooperation with clergy and Catholic patrons. A later story says Bishop Mariano Escalada withheld a priest from the Merlo church because of local Masonic influence; the consulted retellings do not supply the original episcopal correspondence.

There is insufficient evidence to reconstruct Benoit’s private theology. The record supports simultaneous Catholic familial practice, extensive ecclesiastical architectural work, and formal Masonic affiliation.

### Architectural and urban ideas

Benoit’s work developed from pragmatic surveying toward integrated urbanism. Recurring elements include:

- geometry used as an instrument of administration;
- wide streets, ventilation, trees, parks, and repeated plazas as public-health infrastructure;
- civic buildings placed within a legible hierarchy;
- neo-Gothic form for churches, linking modern engineering to medieval ecclesiastical imagery;
- eclectic public architecture adapted to function and ceremonial status;
- standardization that allowed a city to be surveyed and constructed at unusual speed.

Because most work was institutional, his intellectual development cannot be isolated from colleagues, foreign pattern books, competition proposals, contractors, and the political timetable imposed by Rocha.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Dardo Rocha

Governor **Dardo Rocha** was Benoit’s patron, political associate, collaborator, and friend. Rocha supplied the political mandate and access to provincial resources; Benoit supplied technical organization and designs. Benoit designed Rocha’s La Plata residence. At Benoit’s funeral, Rocha declared that Benoit’s name could not be forgotten while La Plata survived and emphasized his probity in public administration. The relationship is central to both the construction campaign and Benoit’s later heroic reputation.

### Pierre Benoit

His father was teacher, professional model, and the source of the family’s most powerful legend. Pedro inherited technical practice and possibly office contacts from Pierre. Later descendants’ focus on Pierre as Louis XVII sometimes overshadowed Pedro’s own career.

### Jorge Coquet

**Jorge Coquet**, senior Department of Engineers official, transmitted the city plan to the executive and later served with Benoit in the Provincial University. He was collaborator, institutional superior or peer, and—within later historiography—a competing candidate for credit. No evidence of a personal feud was found. The rivalry is principally posthumous, created by efforts to assign individual authorship.

### Juan Martín Burgos

**Juan Martín Burgos** submitted a proposal with elements later visible in La Plata. Later official and municipal recognition restored his place as an inspiration. There is no evidence of a documented personal enemy relationship with Benoit; the “Benoit versus Burgos” framing belongs largely to twentieth- and twenty-first-century authorship debates.

### Pedro Pico, Ernesto Meyer, Jules Dormal, Luis Baldi, and Carlos Glade

These professionals illustrate the collaborative nature of the capital project:

- **Pedro Pico** shared credit for the cathedral design.
- **Ernesto Meyer** drew cathedral material and the final coat-of-arms design.
- **Jules Dormal** contributed to the Government Palace.
- **Luis Baldi** assisted Benoit in early Government Palace direction.
- **Carlos Glade** produced or signed plan material and appears in the contested documentary chain of the city layout.

### Juan Dillon, Manuel Rodríguez Fragio, and Antonio Ayerbe

**Juan Dillon** commissioned Merlo, giving Benoit a foundational urban-planning opportunity. **Manuel Rodríguez Fragio** commissioned the Ituzaingó layout. **Antonio Ayerbe**, master builder, translated Benoit’s Merlo church design into construction.

### Pedro Juan Benoit

His son **Pedro Juan** continued the architectural profession and the Mar del Plata cathedral after Pedro’s death. Later attributions sometimes blur father and son, particularly for works around 1890–1900.

No stable record of named enemies, romantic partners outside his marriage, patrons beyond the public and ecclesiastical network, or personal rivalries apart from later authorship competition was located.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Authorship of La Plata

**Status:** documented controversy; no legal proceeding.

The popular claim that Benoit alone conceived the complete city plan conflicts with the 1882 decree crediting the Department of Engineers and with the unsigned surviving foundation plans. The later plan bearing Benoit’s name is genuine, but it is evidence of his authorship claim by 1888–1889, not proof that he alone originated every feature in 1882.

Alberto de Paula’s claim that Benoit appropriated collective credit is an influential historical interpretation. Evidence supporting it includes the early collective language, Coquet’s transmittal, Burgos’s earlier proposal, and absence of sole credit in early commendations. Evidence favoring a major Benoit role includes his responsibility for surveys and implementation, his leadership of architectural work, contemporary professional prominence, and the signed Paris-era plan. The unresolved question is not whether Benoit mattered—he plainly did—but how much conceptual authorship should be separated from institutional execution.

### Participation in the 1880 armed conflict

**Status:** documented military-political activity.

Benoit prepared defensive works for the Province of Buenos Aires. This involved him in political violence at the level of military engineering. No evidence was found that he personally commanded combat troops, ordered abuses, or was charged after the conflict.

### Public money and personal finances

**Status:** no documented corruption allegation found; later claims of probity and debt are testimonial.

Rocha praised his honesty in handling large sums. Descendants say he died indebted. Neither claim substitutes for account books or probate records. There is no located lawsuit, criminal investigation, fraud allegation, bribery case, or corruption finding involving Pedro Benoit.

### Demolition of the family house

**Status:** posthumous heritage controversy, not misconduct by Benoit.

Most of the San Telmo house disappeared during the 1978 widening of Independencia. Remaining fabric associated with the property was later demolished despite heritage concerns, and local reporting describes professional sanctions. This controversy concerns later owners, professionals, and municipal preservation, not Pedro Benoit.

### Matters for which no evidence was found

The consulted record yielded no substantiated evidence of patents fraud, money laundering, organized crime, intelligence-service activity, sexual scandal, addiction, psychiatric confinement, imprisonment, exile, or a criminal conviction. This is a statement about the searched record, not proof that every private event is recoverable.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. Pierre Benoit was Louis XVII of France

**What the story says.** The most elaborate family legend says that Pedro’s father Pierre was actually **Louis-Charles, Duke of Normandy**, son of Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette, officially considered to have died in the Temple prison in 1795. In this story he escaped, adopted the identity Pierre Benoit, traveled through Napoleonic and Atlantic networks, and eventually settled in Buenos Aires. Pedro would therefore have been a grandson of Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette and a Bourbon claimant by blood.

**When and where it appears.** The idea circulated within the Zapiola branch of the family and entered print prominently through Federico Zapiola’s *¿Luis XVII murió en Buenos Aires?* in 1941. Manuel Mujica Láinez transformed it into literary fiction in “La escalera de mármol,” included in *Misteriosa Buenos Aires* (1950). Lucrecia Zapiola de Saravia and José Matías Zapiola later advanced it in *Soy Luis XVII. Debo llamarme Pierre Benoit*. Newspaper features, genealogical websites, videos, and descendant interviews continue to revive it.

**Who circulated it.** Benoit-Zapiola descendants, sympathetic amateur historians, local cultural journalists, and literary authors. It has been especially attractive to Buenos Aires and La Plata heritage culture because it joins the French Revolution to the founding family of the provincial capital.

**Evidence cited for the story.** Proponents point to Pierre’s allegedly aristocratic education, uncertain or inconsistent birth documentation, artistic and technical accomplishments, secrecy about his past, use of fleur-de-lis motifs, initials interpreted as royal codes, supposed links to Napoleon and Simón Bolívar, resemblance in portraits, and the reported presence of arsenic in his remains. Some details come from family memory rather than contemporary records.

**Evidence against or limiting it.** Genealogical records identify Pierre’s parents and Calais origin. More decisively for the general survival claim, a peer-reviewed 2001 study in the *European Journal of Human Genetics* reported that mitochondrial DNA independently obtained by two laboratories from the preserved heart attributed to the Temple child matched the maternal lineage of Marie Antoinette’s family. That supports the conclusion that the child who died in the Temple was her son. The study did not test Pierre Benoit, so it does not genetically identify him; it undermines the premise that Louis XVII escaped. Descendants have disputed the heart’s chain of custody and called for direct DNA comparison. No peer-reviewed Benoit-to-Bourbon genetic match has been published in the sources consulted.

The alleged arsenic result, even if authenticated, would establish exposure or poisoning, not royal identity. Many cited symbols can be explained by nineteenth-century decoration, retrospective selection, or Masonic associations.

**Assessment.** A historically consequential family legend, contradicted by the strongest published genetic evidence concerning Louis XVII and unproven by direct Benoit DNA. It should not be reported as established ancestry.

**Effect on reputation.** The story gave the Benoit family a transatlantic dynastic mythology and brought Pedro attention beyond architectural history. It also displaced attention from his documented public career and encouraged readings of buildings and signatures as coded royal testimony.

### 2. Benoit as the sole, secret “genius” of La Plata

**What the story says.** A heroic version presents Benoit as the single mind who invented the entire plan and most public buildings, sometimes in a burst of inspiration.

**Origin and circulation.** Late nineteenth-century display material bearing his name, Rocha’s commemorative praise, local school histories, monuments, tourism, and later biographies consolidated the image. The loss of the original file made a personal author easier to remember than a department.

**Evidence.** Benoit’s exceptional range of work, leadership, signed Paris plan, and long association with implementation support a central role. The 1882 decree, Coquet’s transmittal, Burgos’s proposal, and anonymous foundation plans oppose sole authorship.

**Effect.** It secured Benoit’s place as “the architect of La Plata” but obscured civil servants, draughtsmen, contractors, foreign specialists, and laborers. Recent official heritage language has shifted toward collective authorship.

### 3. La Plata is a giant Masonic temple or cipher

**What the story says.** The city’s square perimeter, diagonals, compass-like geometry, cemetery iconography, and coat of arms supposedly form a giant square-and-compasses emblem, plumb line, or open-air lodge. Some versions claim that secret Masonic instructions determined the whole city.

**Origin and circulation.** Masonic lodges, local heritage tours, symbolic studies, popular journalism, and internet videos circulate variants. Benoit’s documented initiation makes the narrative plausible enough to persist.

**Evidence for and against.** Benoit and other founders were Masons, and symbols recognizable within Masonic traditions appear in the period’s civic and funerary visual culture. But no located 1882 design brief declares the city a lodge or supplies a complete esoteric key. Grid and diagonals also serve circulation, surveying, civic hierarchy, and hygiene and have non-Masonic European precedents. Burgos’s earlier geometric proposal further weakens the idea of a single secret Benoit cipher.

**Assessment.** Masonic influence and network are documented; totalizing symbolic interpretations remain interpretive and unproven.

### 4. The Casilla Benoit was the hut where he drew the 1882 plan

**What the story says.** A small prefabricated structure preserved in Parque Saavedra is described as the office or hut in which Benoit designed the city during its foundation.

**Evidence.** Municipal and heritage retellings indicate that the prefabricated wood-and-zinc buildings of this type arrived in **1884**, two years after the foundation. The surviving casilla may be associated with Benoit’s later technical office or memory, but it cannot literally be the place where the already approved 1882 plan originated. It was transferred to Parque Saavedra around 1940 and marked by a commemorative plaque in 1948.

**Effect.** The small building functions as a tangible relic in a city whose planning process was otherwise bureaucratic and dispersed. The chronological impossibility has not eliminated its symbolic power.

### 5. Two Paris gold medals and named futuristic categories

**What the story says.** La Plata won two gold medals in Paris in 1889, for “City of the Future” and “Best Constructed Realization.”

**Evidence.** Official Argentine heritage material says the city plan received a gold medal. The exposition and Argentine pavilion are well documented. The exact two-category formulation appears widely in later local sources but was not corroborated in the consulted official exposition material.

**Assessment.** A medal is officially reported; two medals with those exact category names are not securely established. The story amplified La Plata’s identity as a technologically modern planned city.

### 6. The disinterested, impoverished civic saint

**What the story says.** Benoit refused valuable land, worked without payment, spent his fortune on public projects, and died in debt.

**Source and evidence.** Descendant testimony supplies the most complete version. His unpaid work on the Mar del Plata cathedral is plausible and repeatedly reported, and Rocha praised his integrity. No probate inventory or complete payment record was located.

**Assessment.** Some pro bono service is credible; the full financial hagiography remains unverified.

### Interpretive range

The literature divides broadly into:

## Death and Final Months

Late in **1896**, Benoit withdrew from much of his Buenos Aires and La Plata activity and settled more permanently in **Mar del Plata**, where he continued to follow the cathedral construction. Local descendant testimony says that he initially stayed in a Peralta Ramos house and then bought a house on Avenida Colón.

On the morning of **4 April 1897**, according to the account transmitted by Alejandro Zuccarelli Benoit, he visited the cathedral worksite. After returning home he became unwell. The physician Ángel Garay was summoned, but Benoit allegedly declined treatment and died that night of cardiac insufficiency. The details derive from later family testimony; no contemporaneous clinical record was located.

His funeral took place in Buenos Aires on or around **6 April 1897**, and he was interred at Recoleta Cemetery. Dardo Rocha delivered the principal commemorative address. Benoit’s university appointment had come only weeks earlier, and he died before the Provincial University’s public inauguration.

No chronic illness, addiction, major accident, psychiatric crisis, or prior disabling injury is documented in the consulted sources. The sudden cardiac account is the only specific medical narrative recovered.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Burial and official honors

His Recoleta tomb was designated a **National Historic Sepulchre** by Argentine Decree 2317/1983. The official memorial language recognizes his surveying, engineering, and role in La Plata.

La Plata named **Avenida 66** for Engineer Pedro Benoit through Ordinance 41 in 1927. Schools, streets, civic sites, and the **Distribuidor Pedro Benoit** also preserve his name.

### Monuments and their losses

The sculptor **Atilio Boveri** made an early bust installed near San Ponciano; it was removed in 1951 and its whereabouts became unknown. Another bust by **Máximo Maldonado**, placed near 12 and 64 in the 1970s, was vandalized or removed. In **2021**, a new bust by **Pablo Raimondi** was installed in the garden of the La Plata Municipal Palace.

This sequence—commission, removal, disappearance, replacement—helps explain the descendant description of Benoit as “forgotten,” even while streets and official narratives continued to honor him.

### Casilla Benoit

The prefabricated Casilla Benoit in Parque Saavedra is preserved as a municipal historical object. It reached its current park setting around 1940 and received a commemorative plaque in 1948. Its popular identity as the place where Benoit created the 1882 plan is contradicted by the reported 1884 arrival date of the prefabricated structures, but it remains an important monument to the technical offices that built the city.

### Architectural survival and destruction

Some Benoit-associated buildings remain central landmarks: San Ponciano, the Dardo Rocha house, the cathedral, the Government Palace ensemble, the cemetery, the Museo Etnográfico building, and the Mar del Plata cathedral. Others were demolished, substantially altered, or completed after his death. The Bosque arch vanished in 1915; ministry buildings disappeared; the family house was mostly lost in 1978 and later demolition.

The late completion of the La Plata cathedral complicates visual attribution. The modern visitor sees a building whose initial conception belongs to Benoit, Pico, Meyer, and their period, but whose towers and technical resolutions also belong to much later architects and engineers.

### Historiographical reinterpretation

The reputation passed through several phases:

1. **Immediate commemoration:** Rocha and provincial memory presented Benoit as the indispensable artífice and honest servant of the new capital.
2. **Civic hero formation:** schools, street names, busts, and centennial histories simplified a collective process into the story of Rocha the founder and Benoit the planner.
3. **Dynastic mythology:** the Louis XVII claim connected the family to European royal history.
4. **Masonic-symbolic reading:** public interest in the city’s geometry turned Benoit into a secret-code designer.
5. **Revisionist professional history:** scholars recovered Burgos, Coquet, Glade, Pico, Meyer, Dormal, Department staff, and contractors and challenged sole authorship.
6. **Current balanced view:** official heritage language recognizes collective design while maintaining Benoit’s major technical, architectural, and administrative role.

Descendants have proposed transferring Benoit’s remains from Recoleta to La Plata, in some versions to the cathedral, arguing that the city he helped build is the appropriate resting place. The proposal belongs to commemorative politics and had not resulted in a transfer in the consulted record.

### Influence

La Plata became one of Latin America’s most cited nineteenth-century planned capitals. Benoit’s durable influence lies less in a personal theoretical text than in a built and surveyed system: a legible civic grid, diagonals, recurring green spaces, integrated infrastructure, and an ambitious public-building program. His career also exemplifies the nineteenth-century Argentine engineer-architect whose practice crossed boundaries later separated into surveying, urban planning, civil engineering, architecture, public health, and municipal management.

## Atlas Connections

### Jules Dormal

- **[T5]** **Documented fact.** Benoit and Dormal participated successively—and with overlapping design responsibility—in the Government Palace project at La Plata during the 1880s. Benoit directed the provincial Department of Engineers’ Architecture Section and the initial construction; Dormal was subsequently commissioned to redesign façades and interiors and modified portions of the existing work. This is a documented professional collaboration, although the surviving source does not establish a private friendship. [Argentine Decree 1055/2017](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/decreto-1055-2017-304920/texto)

### José Hernández

- **[T4]** **Documented fact.** Hernández and Benoit belonged to the same institutional milieu surrounding La Plata’s creation in 1882: Hernández, then a provincial senator, proposed the name “La Plata” and attended the foundation ceremony on 19 November, while Benoit directed or coordinated crucial surveying, planning, and construction work for the new capital. The evidence supports participation in the same provincial founding enterprise, but no personal meeting or correspondence between them has been located. [Buenos Aires Legislature cultural history](https://cultura.legislatura.gob.ar/cultura_posts/19-de-noviembre-1882-dardo-rocha-funda-la-ciudad-de-la-plata-como-nueva-capital-provincial971.html), [UNLP repository study](https://sedici.unlp.edu.ar/bitstream/handle/10915/135405/Documento_completo.pdf?sequence=1)

### Le Corbusier

- **[T9+]** **Documented fact.** La Plata is the shared site linking the two architects without shared occupancy. Benoit helped originate and implement the city’s 1882 plan; decades after Benoit’s death, Le Corbusier visited La Plata in 1929 and designed Casa Curutchet there beginning in 1948. The later building consciously occupies and responds to the planned urban fabric, but there is no evidence that the two men met or that Le Corbusier corresponded with Benoit. [FAPyD, “Pedro Benoit y Le Corbusier”](https://fapyd.unr.edu.ar/pedro-benoit-y-le-corbusier/), [UNESCO Le Corbusier World Heritage project](https://lecorbusier-worldheritage.org/es/la-influencia-de-la-obra-arquitectonica/argentina/)

### Daniel Schávelzon

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact.** Schávelzon, born after Benoit’s death, wrote a historical study of Benoit’s 1877–1878 remodeling of the University of Buenos Aires building at Moreno 350: “El primer edificio de la Universidad de Buenos Aires. La obra de Pedro Benoit y la arquitectura para la educación en el siglo XIX” (1975). Benoit therefore receives the positive sign as the earlier, written-about figure. [Museo Etnográfico Juan B. Ambrosetti, Pedro Benoit](https://museo.filo.uba.ar/pedro-benoit)
