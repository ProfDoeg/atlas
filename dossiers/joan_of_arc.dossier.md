# Joan of Arc: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Joan of Arc’s life is unusually well documented for a non-royal fifteenth-century woman. The principal evidence consists of:

1. the 1431 Rouen condemnation proceedings, including extensive interrogations of Joan;
2. the 1450, 1452, and 1455–1456 investigations that culminated in annulment of the conviction;
3. royal, municipal, military, and English administrative records;
4. letters dictated in Joan’s name;
5. contemporary and near-contemporary chronicles, poems, diplomatic reports, and theological opinions.

These sources are not neutral. The condemnation record was produced by a tribunal operating in English-controlled Rouen. The nullification proceedings were initiated by Joan’s family after Charles VII had recovered Normandy and were designed to overturn the earlier judgment. Witnesses in 1456 were recalling events approximately twenty-five to thirty years old, and some had political or personal reasons to rehabilitate Joan or themselves.

The surviving French minutes of parts of the condemnation trial are especially important. The official Latin record was assembled later from French notes, principally through the work of notaries Guillaume Manchon and Guillaume Colles, called Boisguillaume, with Thomas de Courcelles involved in translation and compilation. The record preserves much of Joan’s voice but remains a mediated legal text.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** established by contemporary records or mutually reinforcing evidence.
- **Reported fact:** supplied by a generally credible witness or chronicle but not independently demonstrated.
- **Allegation or theory:** a claim proposed by contemporaries or later investigators without decisive proof.
- **Rumor or myth:** folklore, propaganda, hagiography, literary invention, or a claim contradicted by stronger evidence.
- **Unresolved:** the sources permit more than one reasonable reconstruction.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Name:** Jeanne, commonly rendered in English as Joan.
- **Recorded family name:** d’Arc, Darc, Day, Tarc, or similar spellings. Fifteenth-century orthography was unstable.
- **Self-identification:** At Rouen she said that in her own country she was called **Jeannette**, and after coming into France, **Jeanne**. She identified her father as Jacques d’Arc and her mother as Isabelle.
- **Alternative names and titles:**
  - *Jeanne la Pucelle* — Joan the Maid.
  - *La Pucelle d’Orléans* — the Maid of Orléans, a later conventional title.
  - *Jeanne du Lys* — occasionally associated with the ennobled family, though not her normal personal name.
  - Saint Joan of Arc / Sainte Jeanne d’Arc — following canonization.
- **Birth:** Probably about 1412 at Domrémy, a village on the Meuse in the duchy of Bar, within the diocese of Toul. It is now Domrémy-la-Pucelle, Vosges, France.
- **Traditional birthday:** 6 January 1412.
- **Status of the traditional date:** **Reported tradition, not securely documented.** Joan told her judges in 1431 that she was about nineteen. The precise 6 January date appears in later tradition and was adopted in the 1920 canonization decree.
- **Death:** 30 May 1431, Rouen, Normandy.
- **Age at death:** Approximately nineteen.
- **Cause of death:** Judicial execution by burning after conviction as a relapsed heretic.
- **Burial:** None. Witnesses reported that the remains and ashes were disposed of in the Seine to prevent the collection of relics.
- **Nationality:** A modern nationality cannot be mapped neatly onto her circumstances. She identified herself with the “king of France,” opposed the English claim, and came from a frontier community divided among overlapping jurisdictions.
- **Religion:** Latin Christianity; baptized and raised within the Catholic Church.
- **Occupation or role:** Agricultural household member; visionary claimant; royal military and political figure; banner-bearer and participant in military planning and combat; prisoner; defendant in an ecclesiastical trial.
- **Literacy:** She could not ordinarily read or compose written texts. Her letters were dictated. Three surviving signatures show that she learned to sign **Jehanne** imperfectly.
- **Marital status:** Never married.
- **Children:** None documented.
- **Physical appearance:** No verified life portrait survives. Jean le Bâtard d’Orléans later described her as young and vigorous; other witnesses emphasized her short hair and military clothing. An often-repeated claim that she was short is unsupported by reliable measurements.

## Historical Setting

Joan entered public life during the Hundred Years’ War and the French civil conflict between Armagnac and Burgundian factions.

The 1420 Treaty of Troyes recognized Henry V of England and his heirs as successors to Charles VI of France, disinheriting Charles VI’s son, the future Charles VII. Henry V and Charles VI both died in 1422. The infant Henry VI was proclaimed king of England and France in the English-Burgundian sphere, while Charles was recognized by his supporters as Charles VII but remained uncrowned.

Northern France, Paris, and much of Normandy were controlled by the English or their Burgundian allies. Charles’s government survived principally south of the Loire. Orléans, under English siege from October 1428, was strategically and symbolically critical.

Joan’s mission united three claims:

- that her counsel came from God;
- that Charles was the rightful heir;
- that the siege of Orléans should be lifted and Charles conducted to Reims for anointing and coronation.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Jacques d’Arc**

- Joan’s father.
- A cultivator and locally substantial villager rather than an impoverished landless peasant.
- He held or worked agricultural property and livestock and performed community functions. Later testimony associates him with the office of village dean or doyen and with local fiscal or defensive responsibilities.
- He helped organize village security during wartime.
- Joan testified that after she began receiving her mission, her father dreamed she would depart with soldiers and told her brothers that he would rather they drown her than permit such a departure. The wording survives through Joan’s trial testimony and should be treated as **documented testimony**, not independent proof of Jacques’s exact statement.
- Died before the principal nullification proceedings, probably around 1431, though the exact date is uncertain.

**Isabelle Romée**

- Joan’s mother; also recorded as Isabeau, Isabelle de Vouthon, or Isabelle Romée.
- “Romée” may refer to a pilgrimage association rather than a fixed hereditary surname.
- Traditionally connected to Vouthon, near Domrémy.
- Survived Joan and became a principal petitioner for annulment of the conviction.
- Appeared publicly at Notre-Dame de Paris on 7 November 1455 and before the papally commissioned judges on 17 November.
- Lived long enough to witness the nullification judgment in 1456.
- Commonly reported to have died in 1458.

### Siblings

The 1429 letters of ennoblement name three brothers:

- **Jacquemin d’Arc**, apparently the eldest.
- **Jean d’Arc**, later called Jean du Lys.
- **Pierre d’Arc**, or Pierre Pierrelot, later Pierre du Lys.

A sister is also reported:

- **Catherine d’Arc**, probably deceased before Joan’s public career. Evidence for her is less abundant than for the brothers.

Jean and Pierre associated themselves with Joan’s public career. Pierre was captured with or near her at Compiègne. Both later became involved with Claude des Armoises, the woman who claimed to be the surviving Joan.

### Social and economic status

The family was neither noble before 1429 nor destitute. It belonged to the more secure stratum of rural society: household land, livestock, local standing, and participation in village affairs. Descriptions of Joan as a starving shepherd girl are later simplifications.

Joan probably performed normal household and agricultural labor. Rehabilitation witnesses remembered her sewing, spinning, helping her parents, attending church, and sometimes taking animals to pasture. Whether “shepherdess” accurately describes her normal occupation is **unresolved**; it became a powerful symbolic identity in later literature.

### Ennoblement and property

In December 1429 Charles VII issued letters ennobling Joan, Jacques and Isabelle, her brothers Jacquemin, Jean, and Pierre, and their legitimate descendants. The document cited Joan’s services to the king and kingdom.

The family later used the name **du Lys**. Heraldic tradition associates Joan and her family with a blue shield bearing a sword, crown, and fleurs-de-lis. The exact original grant and whether the arms were personally “awarded” to Joan in the modern sense are less secure than the ennoblement itself.

Joan personally received equipment, horses, clothing, lodging, and the maintenance appropriate to a royal military figure. No evidence shows that she accumulated private commercial wealth, investments, businesses, or an independent territorial estate.

## Childhood and Early Life, c. 1412–1428

### Domrémy and the frontier

Domrémy lay in a politically fragmented border region. Most villagers favored the Armagnac or royalist party, while nearby Maxey reportedly leaned Burgundian. Raids, cattle theft, burned settlements, and population displacement were features of Joan’s adolescence.

The parish church was dedicated to Saint Remigius. Joan was baptized there. She regularly attended Mass, confessed, received communion, prayed at churches and shrines, and visited the nearby hermitage of Notre-Dame de Bermont.

Rehabilitation witnesses consistently described her as devout, charitable, modest, hardworking, and unusually attentive to church services. These depositions are valuable recollections but were elicited in a proceeding intended to restore her reputation.

### The “Ladies’ Tree”

Near Domrémy stood a tree known as the Ladies’ Tree or Fairy Tree, beside a spring. Village children danced, sang, made garlands, and ate near it.

At Rouen the prosecution explored whether the site was associated with fairies and whether Joan had practiced superstition there. Joan admitted that children used the place and that she had made garlands but denied worshipping fairies or receiving her voices there.

- **Documented fact:** A local tree and spring existed and carried folkloric associations.
- **Allegation:** The condemnation articles linked Joan to fairy belief, magic, or pagan survivals.
- **Evidence:** No reliable evidence shows that she practiced sorcery there.
- **Reputational effect:** The tree furnished prosecutors and later writers with imagery connecting her either to witchcraft or to an enchanted rural childhood.

### Wartime displacement

A Burgundian raid forced Domrémy’s inhabitants to take refuge at Neufchâteau, probably in 1428. Joan’s family lodged at an inn kept by a woman remembered as “la Rousse.” Joan may have assisted with domestic or agricultural work.

This temporary flight is the only well-attested childhood migration.

### Proposed marriage and legal action

Joan testified that a man from Toul claimed she had promised to marry him. She appeared before the ecclesiastical court at Toul and successfully denied the alleged betrothal.

- **Documented fact through Joan’s testimony:** A marriage claim was brought and rejected.
- **Importance:** It shows her willingness to travel and litigate for herself before her military career.
- **Unresolved:** The claimant’s identity and precise date are uncertain.

## Education and Formation

Joan received no known formal schooling and said she had not learned to read or write. Her formation was oral and practical:

- parish worship and sermons;
- confession and instruction by local priests;
- saints’ stories, devotional images, prayers, and feast days;
- village traditions;
- news of war, dynastic crisis, and the siege of Orléans;
- household and agricultural labor.

No single teacher or clerical mentor is documented before her departure. Her earliest important supporters were lay soldiers and officials at Vaucouleurs.

### Prophecies and expectations

Several prophecies circulated concerning a woman who would ruin France and a virgin from the frontiers of Lorraine who would save it. Joan referred to such expectations, and supporters interpreted her through them.

- **Documented fact:** Prophetic material of this type circulated.
- **Unresolved:** Whether Joan knew a specific prophecy before formulating her mission, or whether chroniclers retrofitted it to her career.
- **Mythic effect:** The “virgin from Lorraine” became a central hagiographic motif.

## Voices, Visions, and Religious Development

Joan testified that she first heard a voice at approximately thirteen, in her father’s garden, around midday. She initially described it as a voice from God accompanied by light. She later identified her principal heavenly counselors as:

- Saint Michael the Archangel;
- Saint Catherine of Alexandria;
- Saint Margaret of Antioch.

She sometimes mentioned angels and, during interrogation, Saint Gabriel.

The initial message was to be good and attend church. The voices later ordered her to go to France, raise the siege of Orléans, and bring Charles to Reims. They directed her to Robert de Baudricourt at Vaucouleurs.

Joan maintained that she saw the saints bodily, heard them frequently, recognized their voices, embraced or honored them, and believed in them as firmly as in the Christian faith. She refused to reveal everything they had told her, calling some matters her “king’s secret.”

### Interpretive categories

**Documented fact:** Joan consistently reported auditory and visual religious experiences and organized major decisions around them.

**Theological claim:** That the voices were divine or saintly cannot be historically demonstrated or disproved.

**Condemnation position:** The tribunal eventually treated her claims as false, presumptuous, superstitious, or demonic and her refusal to submit them unconditionally to the Church Militant as disobedience.

**Nullification-era position:** Witnesses and theologians emphasized her orthodoxy, sincerity, and the procedural illegality of the condemnation.

**Medical theories:** Retrospective diagnoses have included temporal-lobe epilepsy, migraine, schizophrenia-spectrum illness, tuberculosis affecting the brain, and other neurological conditions. No diagnosis is supportable from the surviving evidence. The reports lack the clinical data needed for differential diagnosis, and Joan displayed sustained planning, memory, social functioning, and situational awareness. Medical reconstruction remains **speculative theory**.

## Departure from Home and Vaucouleurs, 1428–1429

Joan approached **Robert de Baudricourt**, captain of Vaucouleurs, probably first in May 1428. She asked him to send her to the dauphin. He dismissed her and reportedly told her escort to take her home and box her ears.

She stayed with **Henri and Catherine Le Royer** at Vaucouleurs. Local supporters included:

- **Jean de Metz**, a soldier who questioned and then supported her;
- **Bertrand de Poulengy**, another early adherent;
- **Jean de Novelompont**, sometimes identified with Jean de Metz;
- **Durand Laxart**, a relative by marriage who helped bring her to Vaucouleurs;
- **Jean Le Fumeux**, a cleric associated with her confession;
- **Robert de Baudricourt**, who eventually authorized the journey.

Her prediction or confident report concerning the French defeat at the Battle of the Herrings, 12 February 1429, was later remembered as influencing acceptance of her mission. Precise versions are retrospective.

Before leaving, she adopted male clothing and had her hair cut. A small escort included Jean de Metz, Bertrand de Poulengy, Colet de Vienne, Richard the Archer, and servants.

She left Vaucouleurs around 13 February 1429. The party traveled through hostile or uncertain territory by way of places conventionally reconstructed as Saint-Urbain, Auxerre, Gien, and Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois. At Fierbois she attended Mass and dictated a letter to Charles.

The journey covered approximately 500–600 kilometers and lasted about eleven days. She reached Chinon on or about 23 February.

## Chinon and Poitiers, February–April 1429

### Meeting Charles

Joan met Charles at Chinon, conventionally dated to 6 March 1429, though chronology and ceremonial details vary.

The famous story says Charles hid among courtiers and Joan immediately recognized him.

- **Story:** Joan identified the disguised dauphin without being told.
- **Source status:** Later chronicles and testimony, not a secure contemporary court record.
- **Assessment:** **Reported fact with legendary elaboration.** Charles may simply have been recognizable or indicated by court protocol.
- **Reputational effect:** It became proof of supernatural discernment.

Joan and Charles spoke privately. She later refused to disclose the full “sign” she had given him. Accounts proposed that she knew a secret prayer he had made or assured him of his legitimate birth and right to the throne.

- **Documented fact:** A private communication occurred or was represented as having occurred.
- **Unresolved:** Its content.
- **Later trial problem:** Under pressure Joan described an angel bringing a crown. Near death she reportedly explained that she herself was metaphorically the angel who brought the promise of coronation. The record is difficult and may reflect interrogation designed to convert symbolic speech into contradiction.

### Examination at Poitiers

Charles’s advisers sent Joan to Poitiers for examination by theologians and clerics. The original report is lost. Later references say the examiners found no evil in her, considered her a good Christian, and advised that her claim be tested by action at Orléans.

No blanket theological certification of her voices survives.

Women, reportedly under the supervision of **Yolande of Aragon**, Charles’s mother-in-law, examined Joan and declared her a virgin. In contemporary religious reasoning, virginity was relevant to her title *Pucelle*, to prophecy, and to excluding supposed sexual relations with demons.

### Tours and military household

At Tours she was equipped with armor, horses, attendants, and a banner. Jean d’Aulon became her squire or steward. Her household also included pages and heralds. Her brothers later joined her.

A sword was obtained from behind the altar at Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois after Joan requested that clergy search there. Several swords were reportedly found, one marked with crosses.

- **Documented fact:** A sword connected to Fierbois became associated with her.
- **Joan’s claim:** Her voices told her where it would be found.
- **Unresolved:** Whether prior knowledge, local tradition, ordinary inquiry, coincidence, or religious experience explains the discovery.
- **Myth:** Later versions made it Charles Martel’s sword. No contemporary evidence establishes that provenance.

Her principal banner showed religious imagery. Descriptions vary, but Jesus or the divine name, angels, fleurs-de-lis, and the words “Jhesus Maria” are repeatedly associated with it. Joan said she preferred her banner to her sword because she did not wish to kill anyone.

## The Orléans Campaign, April–May 1429

Joan dictated a confrontational letter to the English on 22 March 1429, ordering them to leave France and threatening military action if they refused. It addressed Henry VI, the duke of Bedford, and English commanders. Multiple manuscript versions differ, and Joan later denied dictating several phrases found in the trial copy.

She left Blois with a relief convoy in late April. The escort carried food, livestock, and supplies and was accompanied by clergy singing hymns.

Joan entered Orléans on the evening of 29 April 1429 and lodged in the house of Jacques Boucher, the ducal treasurer. She met **Jean, Bastard of Orléans**, later count of Dunois, the city’s principal French commander.

### Military position

Joan did not hold an independently defined office equivalent to modern commander-in-chief. Experienced captains controlled troops and operations. Nevertheless:

- she participated in councils;
- pressed for aggressive action;
- moved among soldiers under fire;
- carried or remained near her banner;
- helped restore morale;
- became the visible religious and political center of the campaign.

Claims that she devised every tactic or personally commanded the entire French army are later exaggerations. Claims that she was merely a passive mascot also understate the testimony of participants.

### Operations

- **4 May:** French forces captured Saint-Loup.
- **6 May:** Operations against the southern English positions; Saint-Jean-le-Blanc was abandoned and the Augustins taken.
- **7 May:** Assault on Les Tourelles. Joan was wounded by an arrow or crossbow bolt in the shoulder or upper chest. She returned to the fighting after the wound was dressed. The English commander William Glasdale drowned during the collapse or destruction of the bridge structure.
- **8 May:** The English abandoned the siege positions and withdrew.

Joan urged that the retreating English not be attacked on a Sunday. The lifting of the siege transformed her standing and the morale of Charles’s party.

### Conduct toward troops and prisoners

Witnesses remembered Joan:

- discouraging swearing;
- dismissing or attempting to exclude prostitutes from camp;
- urging confession and Mass;
- seeking negotiated surrender before assaults;
- mourning deaths on both sides.

These accounts coexist with the coercive realities of medieval siege warfare. Her letters threatened destruction if resistance continued, and armies in which she served killed, captured, ransomed, and plundered.

The prosecution later accused her of responsibility for prisoners’ deaths, including a captured man said to have been executed. Joan denied ordering unlawful killing and said she had attempted to protect prisoners. The evidence does not establish her personal commission of murder.

---

## The Loire Campaign and Patay, June 1429

Joan and her allies urged an immediate offensive to clear English-held Loire positions and open the route to Reims.

Principal associates included:

- John II, duke of Alençon;
- Dunois;
- Étienne de Vignolles, called La Hire;
- Jean Poton de Xaintrailles;
- Gilles de Rais;
- Jean de Brosse, marshal de Boussac;
- Raoul de Gaucourt;
- Arthur de Richemont, constable of France, whose participation created political tension.

### Campaign

- **11–12 June:** Jargeau assaulted and captured. Joan was struck on the helmet by a stone or missile from the walls but survived.
- **15 June:** The fortified bridge at Meung-sur-Loire was seized.
- **16–17 June:** Beaugency capitulated.
- **18 June:** French forces defeated the English at Patay.

At Patay, the decisive mounted attack was conducted by professional captains, particularly La Hire and Xaintrailles. Joan was present with the army but did not personally direct the battlefield breakthrough. The victory destroyed or captured much of an English field force, including John Talbot, and reversed the psychological legacy of Crécy, Poitiers, and Agincourt.

---

## The Reims Campaign and Coronation, June–July 1429

Joan pressed Charles to proceed to Reims, the traditional coronation city, through territory not securely controlled by his party.

The army departed Gien on 29 June. Important stages included:

- **Auxerre:** Negotiated neutrality or conditional submission.
- **Saint-Florentin and Saint-Paul:** Submission.
- **Troyes:** Initially resisted; after several days and preparations for an assault, the city admitted Charles.
- **Châlons-en-Champagne:** Submission.
- **Reims:** Opened its gates.

Charles VII was anointed and crowned in Reims Cathedral on 17 July 1429. Joan stood nearby with her banner. She reportedly knelt and embraced his knees, declaring that God’s will had been fulfilled in bringing him to his consecration.

Her father may have attended the coronation or visited Reims at this time; municipal accounts record support for him.

The coronation was her central strategic success. It supplied Charles with the sacramental legitimacy denied by the Treaty of Troyes and made English plans to crown Henry VI in France reactive rather than preemptive.

---

## March on Paris and Political Friction, July–September 1429

After Reims, Joan favored continuing operations against Paris. Charles and his advisers also negotiated with Philip the Good, duke of Burgundy. The resulting truces slowed military momentum.

The royal progress included Soissons, Château-Thierry, Provins, Crépy-en-Valois, and Compiègne. Joan wrote to Reims defending or explaining the truce while expressing distrust of Burgundian intentions.

### Assault on Paris

French forces approached Paris in late August. On 8 September 1429 they assaulted the Porte Saint-Honoré.

Joan was wounded in the thigh by a crossbow bolt. She remained near the ditch urging continued attack until removed. The assault failed. On royal orders the army withdrew, and a bridge constructed at Saint-Denis was dismantled.

- **Failure:** Paris remained under Anglo-Burgundian control.
- **Dispute:** Some sources blame Charles’s caution or Georges de La Trémoille’s diplomacy; others emphasize formidable defenses, logistical weakness, and lack of consensus.
- **Unresolved:** Whether a more sustained attack could have succeeded.

Joan deposited armor at the abbey of Saint-Denis, apparently as an offering. The coronation army was dissolved at Gien on 21 September.

---

## Late Campaigns, October 1429–May 1430

### Saint-Pierre-le-Moûtier

In autumn 1429 Joan served in a campaign associated with Charles d’Albret. Saint-Pierre-le-Moûtier was taken in November. Jean d’Aulon later testified that after French troops began retreating, Joan remained with only a few men, rallied the assault, and the town was captured.

### La Charité-sur-Loire

The siege of La Charité, held by forces associated with Perrinet Gressart, failed in November–December 1429.

Probable contributing factors:

- winter conditions;
- lack of artillery and money;
- inadequate resupply;
- poor political support;
- strong defenses.

Artillery had to be abandoned. This was Joan’s clearest military failure before capture.

### Ennoblement

Charles VII’s letters of December 1429 ennobled Joan and her family. This was a significant royal reward but did not give her independent political authority.

### Independent movement

During early 1430 Joan spent time at courts or garrisons including Bourges, Sully-sur-Loire, Melun, Lagny-sur-Marne, and Compiègne. Her relationship with Charles’s central government appears to have become less direct. She remained active but no longer operated at the head of a great royal army.

At Lagny she was connected with the capture of the Burgundian partisan Franquet d’Arras, who was tried and executed by local authorities. The Rouen tribunal used the case to accuse her of bloodshed. Joan said she had intended to exchange him for a French prisoner, then allowed local justice to proceed after learning the prisoner was dead. Her precise legal authority is unclear.

At Melun during Easter week 1430, she later said, her voices warned that she would be captured before Saint John’s Day.

---

## Capture at Compiègne, 23 May 1430

Joan entered Compiègne to assist its defense against Burgundian forces. On 23 May she joined a sortie toward Margny.

During the retreat, Burgundian troops cut off the rear guard. Joan was pulled or forced from her horse and captured by a soldier in the service of **John of Luxembourg**, count of Ligny.

The gates of Compiègne were closed during the retreat.

- **Documented fact:** The gates closed and Joan was captured outside.
- **Allegation:** Guillaume de Flavy, captain of Compiègne, deliberately betrayed her.
- **Evidence:** No decisive contemporary evidence proves a planned betrayal. Closing gates to prevent enemy entry was a standard defensive act.
- **Historical status:** **Unresolved allegation**, frequently repeated in hostile and romantic literature.

Pierre d’Arc and Jean d’Aulon were also captured.

---

## Captivity and Transfer to the English, May–December 1430

Joan was held successively or probably at:

- Beaulieu-les-Fontaines;
- Beaurevoir;
- Arras;
- Le Crotoy;
- Saint-Valery-sur-Somme or related transit points;
- Rouen.

At Beaulieu she attempted escape. At Beaurevoir she jumped from a tower, reportedly after hearing that Compiègne’s population might be destroyed. She survived but was injured or stunned and temporarily unable to eat.

- **Condemnation accusation:** Attempted suicide, a grave sin.
- **Joan’s account:** She acted in desperation, not with a settled wish to kill herself, and afterward confessed and asked forgiveness.
- **Assessment:** **Documented escape attempt; intent to commit suicide not established.**

The University of Paris and Pierre Cauchon, bishop of Beauvais, demanded that she be surrendered for ecclesiastical trial. English authorities financed negotiations. The sum conventionally stated is **10,000 livres tournois**, comparable to a high aristocratic ransom.

The transfer was a financial and political transaction embedded in prisoner-of-war practice. Surviving English records document payments to Cauchon and to clerics involved in the proceeding.

Charles VII’s government made no successful attempt to ransom or rescue her. Evidence for what discussions occurred is sparse. Later accusations that Charles personally ordered her abandonment are not proved, but the practical result is clear: no rescue or matching ransom secured her release.

---

## Rouen Imprisonment

Joan reached Rouen by late 1430 and was confined in the castle, the English administrative and military center in Normandy.

She was held:

- in a secular prison rather than a church prison;
- under English military guard;
- guarded primarily by men;
- chained or shackled for significant periods.

She repeatedly requested transfer to an ecclesiastical prison guarded by women. That request was refused.

This arrangement was central to the later nullification case. It exposed her to intimidation and possible sexual violence and gave English authorities control over access to her.

### Mistreatment and sexual assault claims

Nullification witnesses reported that guards mocked, threatened, and attempted to violate Joan. One account says a “great English lord” attempted to take her by force.

- **Documented fact:** She was held by male soldiers despite requests for female guards.
- **Reported fact:** Witnesses later described attempted sexual assault.
- **Unresolved:** The identity of the alleged assailant and the exact events.
- **No established evidence of completed rape:** The sources support danger and attempted assault more clearly than completed penetration.

Her tightly fastened male clothing, with hose attached to the doublet by cords, could make forced undressing more difficult. Witnesses later stated that protection from guards was one reason for resuming it after her abjuration. Joan herself also grounded the clothing in divine command and practical necessity.

## The Condemnation Trial, January–May 1431

### Institutional structure

The trial was presided over by:

- **Pierre Cauchon**, bishop of Beauvais;
- **Jean Le Maistre**, vice-inquisitor, whose participation was initially reluctant.

It involved theologians, canon lawyers, clergy, and members of or persons associated with the University of Paris. The English government financed the process and controlled Joan’s imprisonment.

The case was formally ecclesiastical but inseparable from English political needs. Joan’s claim that God supported Charles VII threatened the legitimacy of Henry VI’s French kingship. A finding that she acted through heresy or demonic deception would retrospectively discredit Charles’s coronation.

### Preliminary inquiry

Proceedings began on 9 January 1431. Public interrogations began on 21 February in the castle chapel. Joan initially objected to Cauchon’s jurisdiction and asked for assessors from the French party.

She was not supplied with an advocate. Questions covered:

- childhood and family;
- voices and apparitions;
- the sign given to Charles;
- clothing;
- church submission;
- military acts;
- the Fairy Tree;
- escape attempts;
- banners and sacred names;
- certainty of salvation;
- knowledge of whether she was in God’s grace.

Her response to the last issue became famous. She said, in substance, that if she were not in God’s grace, may God put her there, and if she were, may God keep her there. The answer avoided both presumptuous certainty and self-incrimination.

### Investigative irregularities and coercion

Documented or strongly supported problems include:

- English custody rather than church custody;
- lack of counsel;
- denial of her requested appeal to the pope or a general council;
- repeated and exhausting interrogation;
- questions designed to force theological error from an uneducated defendant;
- political pressure on assessors;
- threats against clerics who objected;
- discrepancy between the short abjuration allegedly read to Joan and the longer text incorporated into the official record;
- exclusion or compression of statements favorable to her defense.

Cauchon nevertheless attempted to create the forms of an ecclesiastical process and consulted numerous clerics. Not every participant was simply an English agent, and motives ranged from political service to genuine theological concern. The institutional imbalance remains clear.

### Charges

An initial set of approximately seventy articles was reduced to twelve principal articles. Accusations included:

- false revelation;
- idolatry toward her voices;
- sorcery or superstition;
- wearing male clothing contrary to Deuteronomy 22:5;
- refusal to submit unconditionally to the Church Militant;
- leaving home without parental permission;
- attempted suicide at Beaurevoir;
- responsibility for violence;
- use of sacred names and images in political war;
- presumptuous claims concerning salvation and divine favor.

Some charges distorted or combined her statements. The later nullification judgment specifically condemned the twelve articles as fraudulent extracts lacking proper context.

### Torture threat

On 9 May Joan was shown instruments of torture and threatened with their use. She said torture would not make her alter the truth and that if pain forced her to say otherwise she would later declare that it had been coerced. The judges decided not to proceed, though the threat itself was coercive.

### Illness

During imprisonment Joan became seriously ill, probably with fever and gastrointestinal symptoms. Physicians attended her. One English official reportedly warned that the king did not want her to die naturally because she had been bought at great cost and was to die by judgment.

No secure diagnosis survives.

### Abjuration, 24 May

At Saint-Ouen cemetery Joan was placed before a public scaffold, preached at, and threatened with immediate burning. She made a mark on an abjuration document and accepted female clothing.

Substantial uncertainty surrounds the document:

- witnesses later remembered a short text;
- the official record contains a longer Latin-form abjuration;
- Joan may not have understood the document;
- she may have expected transfer to a church prison;
- coercion by threat of immediate execution was explicit.

The death sentence was commuted to perpetual imprisonment on bread and water.

### Resumption of male clothing and “relapse”

Within days Joan again wore male clothing. Later testimony gave several possible or overlapping explanations:

- guards removed or withheld her dress;
- male clothing was placed in her cell;
- she needed it to get out of bed;
- it offered some protection against assault;
- she believed she had betrayed her voices by abjuring;
- she voluntarily resumed the identity and obligations of her mission.

She again affirmed her voices and rejected the abjuration. This enabled the court to classify her as a **relapsed heretic**, for whom execution was the standard consequence.

---

## Execution, 30 May 1431

Joan was taken from Rouen Castle to the Old Market Square. She confessed to Dominican friar Martin Ladvenu, received communion, and asked for a cross.

An English soldier or bystander fashioned a small cross from sticks; a larger processional cross was brought from a nearby church and held before her.

Witnesses reported that she repeatedly called on Jesus. She was bound to a high stake and burned. The executioner was Geoffroy Thérage.

After death, the fire was reportedly drawn back so that observers could see the body and could not claim she had escaped. The remains were burned again and the ashes and residual material cast into the Seine.

### Last words and recantation disputes

Some trial-era testimony portrayed Joan as admitting that her voices had deceived her. Nullification witnesses later emphasized her continued faith and orthodox death.

The accounts are not fully compatible. Their evidentiary setting matters:

- statements recorded under the condemnation tribunal served the relapse judgment;
- later witnesses had reasons to portray a martyr’s death;
- several final-hour reports changed across successive investigations.

Her repeated invocation of Jesus is supported by multiple witnesses. Exact speeches placed in her mouth should be treated cautiously.

### The unburned heart

A later story says the executioner found her heart or intestines unconsumed despite adding oil, sulfur, or charcoal.

- **Story:** Her heart miraculously resisted the flames.
- **Appearance:** Retrospective rehabilitation testimony, in some versions transmitted second- or third-hand from the executioner.
- **Evidence:** No contemporary physical record; hearsay.
- **Status:** **Hagiographic reported miracle, unverified.**
- **Effect:** It became evidence of sanctity in popular devotion.

---

## Investigations and Nullification, 1450–1456

### 1450 inquiry

After Rouen returned to Charles VII, the king ordered Guillaume Bouillé to investigate the earlier trial. This inquiry collected statements but did not itself annul the conviction.

Charles had a direct political interest: if Joan had been a heretic, his coronation through her agency could remain tainted.

### 1452 inquiry

Cardinal Guillaume d’Estouteville and inquisitor Jean Bréhal conducted a further inquiry. Procedural obstacles delayed a full retrial.

### Family petition and papal commission

Joan’s mother Isabelle and brothers Jean and Pierre petitioned for formal review. Pope Callixtus III authorized commissioners, including:

- Jean Juvénal des Ursins, archbishop of Reims;
- Guillaume Chartier, bishop of Paris;
- Richard Olivier de Longueil, bishop of Coutances;
- Jean Bréhal, inquisitor of France.

Proceedings opened publicly at Notre-Dame de Paris in November 1455. Investigators heard approximately 115 witnesses at Domrémy, Vaucouleurs, Orléans, Paris, Rouen, and elsewhere.

Witnesses included childhood acquaintances, soldiers, clergy, townspeople, and participants in the original trial.

### Judgment, 7 July 1456

The court declared the condemnation proceedings and resulting sentences null, invalid, without effect, and tainted by fraud, calumny, iniquity, contradiction, and manifest errors of fact and law. It ordered symbolic public correction and the erection of a cross at the place of execution.

The judgment did not conduct a modern factual adjudication of every supernatural claim. It invalidated the prosecution and restored Joan’s and her family’s reputation.

“Rehabilitation trial” is the traditional term; “nullification trial” more accurately describes the legal result.

## Military Role: Successes, Failures, and Limits

### Significant successes

- Helped transform the political and religious morale of Charles VII’s cause.
- Pressed for and participated in the relief of Orléans.
- Contributed to the aggressive operational tempo of the Loire campaign.
- Helped persuade Charles’s government to undertake the Reims expedition.
- Became the central symbolic figure at Charles VII’s coronation.
- Demonstrated personal courage under fire and returned after multiple wounds.
- Strengthened cohesion among captains and troops, at least temporarily.

### Failures and limitations

- Failed assault on Paris.
- Failed siege of La Charité.
- Could not overcome court diplomacy or compel permanent royal strategic support.
- Could not reconcile rival court factions.
- Her actions at Lagny and other independent operations did not produce lasting strategic transformation.
- Captured at Compiègne within approximately thirteen months of entering Orléans.
- Her predictions were not uniformly fulfilled; she did not expel all English forces from France during her lifetime or secure the release of the duke of Orléans.

### Command controversy

The evidence does not support the modern image of Joan as sole supreme commander of France. Armies were led by aristocratic captains with established offices and military followings. Nor does it support reducing her to a ceremonial standard-bearer. She urged objectives, shaped timing, rallied troops, negotiated, sent commands and threats in letters, and participated in councils and assaults.

Her precise authority varied from campaign to campaign.

---

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Dynastic politics

Joan’s politics centered on sacred kingship rather than a modern constitutional or ethnic nationalism. She treated Charles as the legitimate king and the English claim as wrongful. Reims mattered because anointing gave sacramental confirmation to kingship.

Her correspondence sometimes used “France” and “the kingdom of France” in ways that later nationalism could appropriate, but she operated within medieval dynastic, feudal, and religious categories.

### Attitude toward enemies

She distinguished between political enemies and souls capable of repentance. Her letters ordered English forces to depart and threatened destruction if they refused. She also called for surrender, expressed pity for deaths, and urged that captives be protected.

### Church authority

Joan did not reject the Catholic Church. She regularly sought confession, Mass, communion, and clerical support. The central conflict was over whether her claimed commands from God could be subjected to judges she regarded as hostile.

Her answers distinguished:

- the Church Triumphant in heaven;
- the Church Militant on earth;
- “our Lord first served,” as against commands she believed contradicted divine instruction.

The tribunal treated conditional submission as obstinate disobedience. Later defenders treated her appeal to God, pope, and general council as evidence that the trial court improperly equated itself with the universal Church.

### The Hussite letter

A letter dated 23 March 1430, addressed to the Hussites and threatening action against them unless they returned to Catholic unity, is attributed to Joan and transmitted through later copies.

- **Attribution:** Disputed.
- **Possible authorship:** Dictated by Joan, drafted by clerical associates, or produced in her name.
- **Significance:** If authentic, it shows militant religious conformity extending beyond the Anglo-French war.
- **Caution:** It should not be treated as unquestionably her own composition.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Charles VII

Joan’s sovereign and the political beneficiary of her mission. He authorized her military participation and was crowned through the campaign she promoted. After 1429 their strategic priorities diverged. There is no proof that he conspired to have her captured or killed, but his government did not recover her.

### Yolande of Aragon

Charles’s mother-in-law and an important political supporter of his kingship. Women associated with her examined Joan’s virginity, and her political network supported the Orléans relief effort. Later writers have sometimes made Yolande the hidden inventor or controller of Joan’s mission; direct evidence for such a conspiracy is absent.

### Robert de Baudricourt

Initially dismissed Joan, then supplied or permitted the escort that took her from Vaucouleurs to Chinon.

### Jean de Metz and Bertrand de Poulengy

Early military supporters who accompanied her through hostile territory and later testified about her conduct and mission.

### Jean d’Aulon

Her squire and household officer. He accompanied her in campaigns, was captured at Compiègne, and supplied important rehabilitation testimony.

### John II, Duke of Alençon

Major military collaborator in the Loire campaign. He praised her courage and judgment in later testimony, though that testimony was given in the nullification proceeding.

### Jean de Dunois

Commander at Orléans and later a leading military figure. His testimony is essential for reconstructing her operational role.

### La Hire and Poton de Xaintrailles

Professional captains associated with the Loire victories and later campaigning.

### Gilles de Rais

Marshal of France and participant in the same royal army at Orléans, the Loire campaign, Reims, and Paris. Later romantic literature exaggerated him into Joan’s closest companion, protector, or mystical counterpart. Contemporary sources support military association, not an intimate personal bond.

### Georges de La Trémoille

Dominant royal favorite and court political operator. Often cast as Joan’s enemy or saboteur. He favored diplomacy and guarded his influence, but evidence for a deliberate plot to destroy Joan is incomplete.

### Regnault de Chartres

Archbishop of Reims and chancellor of France. Connected to royal diplomacy and later criticized for pessimistic or politically distancing statements after Joan’s capture.

### Pierre Cauchon

Bishop of Beauvais, English political associate, and principal judge at Rouen. He negotiated for Joan’s transfer, organized the trial, and sought a formally defensible condemnation.

### Guillaume Manchon

Trial notary. His notes preserve much of Joan’s speech. He later testified about English pressure, procedural problems, and alterations or omissions.

### Jean Le Maistre

Vice-inquisitor. Initially reluctant to participate, later joined the court.

### Martin Ladvenu and Isambard de La Pierre

Dominicans associated with her final spiritual care. Later testimony portrays them as sympathetic to her.

### English political authorities

John, duke of Bedford, governed Lancastrian France for Henry VI. English officials controlled Joan’s prison, financed the trial, and required a result that neutralized her political and supernatural claims.

## Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Joan had no known companies, patents, inventions, commercial enterprises, investments, or permanent administrative office.

Institutions materially connected to her life included:

- the parish of Domrémy;
- the captaincy of Vaucouleurs;
- Charles VII’s royal household and council;
- the Orléans municipal and ducal administration;
- the French field armies;
- the University of Paris;
- the English royal administration in Normandy;
- the diocesan court of Beauvais operating at Rouen;
- the French Inquisition;
- the papally commissioned nullification court.

### Military household

Joan received:

- armor;
- horses;
- banners;
- heraldic and domestic attendants;
- lodging and food;
- funds or supplies routed through royal and municipal accounts.

These were institutional expenses, not proof of private fortune.

### Ransom and trial finance

The reported 10,000 livres tournois paid for her transfer was a state and military expenditure. English records also show payments or reimbursements to Cauchon, assessors, guards, and officials.

The financing supports the conclusion that the trial was an English state interest. It does not by itself prove that every clerical judgment was knowingly fraudulent.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Legal Findings

### Witchcraft and sorcery

- **Contemporary allegation:** English and Burgundian propaganda called Joan a witch, sorceress, or demonic agent.
- **Trial evidence:** Her voices, banners, sword, male clothing, and Fairy Tree associations were recast as superstition.
- **Court finding in 1431:** Her claims were condemned within the heresy proceeding.
- **Later legal result:** The entire judgment was annulled in 1456.
- **Historical assessment:** No independent evidence demonstrates magical practice, poisoning, spell-casting, or a pact with demons.

### Heresy

- **Formal conviction:** Relapsed heresy, 1431.
- **Legal status:** Annulled in 1456 because the process and judgment were declared invalid.
- **Later Catholic status:** Canonized saint, 1920.

### Cross-dressing

- **Documented fact:** Joan wore male military clothing from the Vaucouleurs journey and resumed it in prison.
- **Her reasons:** Divine command, military practicality, identity as the Maid, and prison necessity.
- **Defense evidence:** Later witnesses said it helped protect her against sexual assault.
- **Trial use:** Her return to male dress helped establish “relapse.”
- **Modern interpretations:** Feminist, lesbian, transgender, queer, nationalist, and religious writers have assigned different meanings to it. No surviving statement shows that Joan understood herself through modern categories of sexual orientation or gender identity.

### Suicide allegation

Her leap from Beaurevoir was prosecuted as despair or attempted suicide. Her own account described an urgent escape attempt made against the warning of her voices. Intent to die is not established.

### Violence and killing

Joan participated in war and threatened military destruction. She was accused of responsibility for executions or killings, including Franquet d’Arras. She denied personally killing anyone and said she carried her banner to avoid using her sword. No reliable evidence proves that she personally killed a combatant.

### Fraud or manipulation by Charles’s court

Theories portray Joan as:

- coached by clerics;
- created by Yolande of Aragon’s intelligence network;
- a propaganda instrument of the court;
- an illegitimate royal child whose identity was concealed.

The court certainly recognized and used her propaganda value. Clerics and officials helped formulate correspondence and public presentation. That does not establish that they invented her voices or secretly directed her from the beginning.

### Betrayal at Compiègne

The accusation against Guillaume de Flavy remains unproved. It became popular because capture was otherwise emotionally difficult to reconcile with Joan’s heroic narrative.

### Psychological illness

No documentary basis permits a reliable diagnosis. Psychiatric theories are modern retrospective models, not findings.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The shepherd girl

**Story:** Joan was an extremely poor, isolated shepherdess who left her sheep to save France.

**Evidence:** Witnesses associated her with livestock and rural work, but her family had property and local standing. She was not a social outcast.

**Effect:** The image made her a symbol of divine election of the humble, popular genius, and peasant nationalism.

### Recognition of the disguised king

**Story:** Charles hid among hundreds of courtiers; Joan supernaturally found him.

**Evidence:** Retrospective reports; no decisive contemporary record of a deliberate disguise test.

**Effect:** One of the principal signs of her supernatural mission in popular biographies.

### The secret of Charles’s birth

**Story:** Joan revealed that Charles was legitimate or repeated a prayer known only to him.

**Evidence:** Later and inconsistent accounts. Joan protected a secret during trial, but its content is unknown.

**Effect:** Used to explain why Charles trusted her.

### Charles Martel’s sword

**Story:** The Fierbois sword was the weapon of Charles Martel, left after his defeat of Muslim forces in 732.

**Evidence:** No contemporary proof. Joan’s trial testimony concerns finding a marked sword, not authenticated Carolingian provenance.

**Status:** **Legend.**

### Joan as tactical genius or supreme commander

**Story:** A seventeen-year-old peasant personally planned every victory and commanded all French forces.

**Evidence:** She influenced strategy, morale, and operational tempo, but professional commanders led formations and battles.

**Effect:** Central to military-nationalist commemoration.

### Joan as passive mascot

**Story:** She did nothing beyond carrying a banner while men made all decisions.

**Evidence against:** Eyewitness testimony places her in councils, negotiations, assaults, rallying troops, and pressing strategic objectives.

**Status:** **Reductionist counter-myth.**

### Survival and Claude des Armoises

In 1436 a woman later known as **Claude or Jeanne des Armoises** appeared near Metz claiming to be Joan. Joan’s brothers Jean and Pierre recognized or publicly accepted her. She married the knight Robert des Armoises and received gifts and civic recognition.

In 1440 she appeared before Charles VII and reportedly confessed to imposture.

- **Documented fact:** A woman claimed Joan’s identity, married Robert des Armoises, obtained support, and was accepted for a time by Joan’s brothers.
- **Evidence against survival:** Numerous people witnessed the Rouen execution; the body was displayed during burning; no contemporary escape record survives; the claimant ultimately confessed or was exposed.
- **Survivalist theory:** Joan escaped, another woman was burned, and Charles’s court concealed the exchange.
- **Assessment:** **Rumor or theory contradicted by the stronger evidence.**
- **Effect:** The episode sustained popular reluctance to accept Joan’s death and remains the foundation of modern “surviviste” literature.

### Royal-birth or “bastard” theory

**Story:** Joan was secretly the child of Queen Isabeau of Bavaria and Louis of Orléans or another prince, placed with the d’Arc family.

**Appearance:** Principally modern revisionist and conspiracy-oriented literature.

**Evidence against:**

- no contemporary document supports the maternity claim;
- Domrémy witnesses consistently knew her as Jacques and Isabelle’s daughter;
- the proposed chronologies require undocumented concealment;
- the theory often depends on mistranslated names, selective genealogy, or the survival theory.

**Status:** **Unsupported theory.**

### The unburned heart

A rehabilitation-era miracle story, discussed above. It is historically important as hagiography but not established physical fact.

### The executioner’s remorse

Later testimony says the executioner feared damnation and believed he had burned a holy woman.

- **Evidence:** Retrospective hearsay.
- **Status:** **Reported tradition.**
- **Effect:** Converts the agent of execution into a witness for martyrdom.

### Joan as Protestant precursor

Some later Protestant writers praised her resistance to corrupt clergy; others inherited the hostile English image.

Joan herself was sacramentally Catholic, invoked saints, sought Mass, and did not advocate doctrinal reform. Her conflict was with a tribunal’s jurisdiction and demands, not with the institution of the Church as such.

### Joan as nationalist symbol

Modern royalists, republicans, Catholics, secular nationalists, socialists, feminists, resistance movements, and the French far right have all claimed her. These appropriations emphasize different elements:

- sacred monarchy;
- the people saving the nation;
- resistance to foreign occupation;
- female military agency;
- martyrdom by corrupt institutions;
- ethnic or exclusionary nationalism.

None is an adequate substitute for her fifteenth-century dynastic and religious setting.

### No authentic portrait

A sketch in the margin of the register of Clément de Fauquembergue, made in May 1429, is contemporary but imaginary: the artist had not seen her and depicted her with long hair and a dress. Later paintings and statues are inventions based on changing ideals.

---

## Death, Remains, Relics, and Canonization

No authenticated bodily relic of Joan survives. Objects advertised as relics have repeatedly proved doubtful or false. A set of supposed remains examined scientifically in the twenty-first century was identified as material from an Egyptian mummy and animal remains, not Joan.

The Catholic cause developed gradually:

- Félix Dupanloup, bishop of Orléans, promoted formal recognition in the nineteenth century.
- The cause was introduced at Rome in 1869.
- Joan was declared venerable in 1894.
- Pope Pius X beatified her on 18 April 1909.
- Pope Benedict XV canonized her on 16 May 1920.
- Her feast day is 30 May.

Canonization is an ecclesiastical finding of sanctity, not a restoration of the 1431 trial; the legal annulment had occurred in 1456.

On 10 July 1920 the French Republic established the **Fête nationale de Jeanne d’Arc et du patriotisme**, observed on the second Sunday in May. It is a national commemoration rather than a statutory non-working public holiday.

---

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Fifteenth century

Joan was already a contested figure during her lifetime:

- Christine de Pizan celebrated her in *Ditié de Jehanne d’Arc* in 1429.
- French supporters described divine providence and heroic virginity.
- English and Burgundian writers portrayed sorcery, sexual disorder, presumption, or deception.
- The nullification proceedings established the official French and ecclesiastical rehabilitation narrative.

### Sixteenth and seventeenth centuries

The memory of Joan remained strongest at Orléans and in dynastic history. English literary tradition, especially Shakespeare’s *Henry VI, Part 1*, depicted her as a witch, fraud, and sexual hypocrite. French Catholic drama used her against Protestantism, despite the anachronism.

### Enlightenment

Voltaire’s mock-epic *La Pucelle d’Orléans* satirized religious and national hagiography through an explicitly sexualized fictional Joan. The poem had an enormous influence on anticlerical and erotic representations but has little biographical value.

### Revolution, empire, and nineteenth century

Joan was reconstructed as:

- a national heroine;
- a daughter of the people;
- a Catholic martyr;
- a royalist legitimist;
- a precursor of patriotic popular sovereignty.

Jules Michelet’s history made her an embodiment of France itself. The Franco-Prussian War and the loss of Alsace-Lorraine increased the political power of a heroine from Lorraine.

Competing political factions staged ceremonies and raised statues. Republican France could honor the people’s heroine; monarchists could honor the restorer of the king; Catholics could honor the visionary martyr.

### Twentieth century

Beatification, canonization, and the national feast institutionalized her dual religious and civic status.

During both world wars Joan served as an image of resistance. Vichy, Free French, Catholic, communist, and later far-right movements used her differently. The annual Paris commemoration became particularly associated with nationalist politics, though civic and religious observances remained broader.

### Gender and sexuality

Joan has been treated as:

- a model of female autonomy;
- a woman succeeding within male military structures;
- a defender of virginity;
- a challenge to binary gender presentation;
- a lesbian or transgender ancestor;
- a Catholic model whose clothing was purely vocational and protective.

The documentary record establishes prolonged male dress, short hair, virgin identity, and resistance to marriage. It does not establish sexual relationships or a modern gender identity.

### Military and political influence

Joan’s military career lasted little more than a year, but her intervention contributed to a major reversal in the war’s political psychology. France’s eventual victory in 1453 resulted from many later developments—Burgundian reconciliation with Charles VII, fiscal and military reform, artillery, diplomacy, and English weakness—not from Joan alone.

Her enduring influence has been symbolic and institutional as much as military:

- legitimacy through ritual;
- charismatic authority outside formal hierarchy;
- women in war;
- conscience versus institutional judgment;
- national mythmaking;
- the evidentiary complexity of judicial archives.

## Works by Joan of Arc

Joan wrote no book, treatise, diary, or memoir. Her documentary corpus consists principally of dictated letters, trial testimony transcribed by notaries, reported speeches, and a few signatures.

### Surviving or substantially preserved letters

The exact count varies because scholars distinguish originals, copies, extracts, duplicate recensions, and disputed texts differently.

1. **Letter to the English**, 22 March 1429, Poitiers  
   Middle French; dictated. Addressed to Henry VI, the duke of Bedford, William de la Pole, John Talbot, Thomas Scales, and English troops. Preserved in several differing copies, including the trial record. Joan denied dictating some phrases in the transmitted version.

2. **Letter or messages to the English at Orléans**, late April–5 May 1429  
   Some texts or reports are lost or preserved only through copies and chronicles. Messages demanded withdrawal and accompanied military operations.

3. **Letter to the people of Tournai**, 25 June 1429  
   Announces the victories at Orléans, Jargeau, Meung, Beaugency, and Patay and calls for loyalty to Charles.

4. **Letter to the people of Troyes**, 4 July 1429  
   Calls on the city to admit Charles VII during the Reims campaign.

5. **Letter to Philip the Good, duke of Burgundy**, probably July 1429  
   Asked him to make peace with Charles VII; surviving status and exact recensions require caution.

6. **Letter to the people of Reims**, 5 August 1429  
   Discusses the Burgundian truce and promises continued attention to them.

7. **Letter to Count Bernard VIII of Armagnac**, 22 August 1429  
   Responds to a question about rival papal claimants. Joan declined to decide until she could be at rest in Paris or elsewhere and consult her counsel from God.

8. **Letter to the people of Riom**, 9 November 1429  
   Requests military supplies for the La Charité campaign. An original survives and bears an uncertain signature, “Jehanne.”

9. **Letter to the people of Reims**, 16 March 1430  
   Warns or reassures them regarding threatened attack. An original survives with Joan’s signature.

10. **Letter to the Hussites**, 23 March 1430  
    Threatens action unless they return to Catholic obedience. Attribution and extent of Joan’s personal responsibility are disputed.

11. **Letter to the people of Reims**, 28 March 1430  
    Preserved in the documentary tradition; concerns the defense of the royal cause.

12. **Letter to the town of Orléans**, 19 January 1430, traditionally associated with gifts or civic relations  
    Documentary classifications vary; not all catalogues accept every item as a surviving Joan letter.

### Lost letters known from testimony or records

- Letter from Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois to Charles before the Chinon meeting.
- Letter to Joan’s parents after departure.
- Additional demands sent to English commanders.
- Correspondence with towns, captains, and royal officials referenced in accounts or chronicles.

### Signatures

Three original signatures are generally recognized:

- on the Riom letter, 9 November 1429;
- on the Reims letter, 16 March 1430;
- on another surviving letter from the 1429–1430 corpus.

They appear hesitant and were written separately from the scribal body text.

### Trial testimony

The 1431 interrogation is not a work “authored” by Joan, but it is the largest surviving record of her words. It must be classified as:

- orally delivered;
- recorded and edited by notaries;
- translated in part from French into Latin;
- shaped by questions, legal categories, compilation, and possible omissions.

### Attributed sayings

Many famous aphorisms appear only in later paraphrase. Statements such as “Act, and God will act” or polished modern motivational quotations should not be accepted without tracing them to the trial or nullification texts.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted evidence are listed. Mere thematic resemblance, later comparison, or shared geography has not been treated as a personal connection.

### Catherine of Alexandria

- **Nature:** Joan identified Saint Catherine as one of the heavenly figures whose voice she heard and whose counsel she followed.
- **Dates and places:** From approximately 1425 in or near Domrémy through her imprisonment at Rouen in 1431, according to Joan’s testimony.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented claim by Joan; supernatural identity not historically verifiable.**
- **Evidence:** The condemnation transcript repeatedly records her identification of the voice and apparition as Saint Catherine.

### Gilles de Montmorency-Laval, usually called Gilles de Rais

- **Nature:** Fellow commander and marshal in Charles VII’s forces; present in the Orléans, Loire, Reims, and Paris phases of Joan’s career.
- **Dates and places:** Orléans, May 1429; Loire campaign, June 1429; Reims expedition and coronation, July 1429; Paris campaign, September 1429.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented fact.**
- **Qualification:** Later claims that he was Joan’s intimate confidant, personal bodyguard, rescuer, or psychologically transformed lover are not supported by contemporary evidence.

### Yolande of Aragon

- **Nature:** Charles VII’s mother-in-law and a central patron of his political cause. Women acting under her authority reportedly supervised Joan’s examination for virginity; her network supported the relief of Orléans.
- **Dates and places:** Chinon, Poitiers, or Tours, March–April 1429; broader Angevin court network.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Reported fact with strong contextual support.**
- **Qualification:** The theory that Yolande fabricated or secretly managed Joan’s entire mission is unsupported.

### Claude des Armoises

- **Nature:** Posthumous claimant to Joan’s identity; accepted temporarily by Joan’s brothers and by several communities.
- **Dates and places:** Metz and Lorraine from 1436; later appearances through approximately 1440.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented imposture or identity claim; survival identification is rumor or theory.**
- **Importance:** Principal foundation of the legend that Joan escaped execution.

### Carl Theodor Dreyer

- **Nature:** Director of *La Passion de Jeanne d’Arc*, a major artistic reconstruction of Joan’s trial.
- **Date and place:** France, production in the late 1920s; released 1928.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented posthumous artistic influence.**

### Renée Jeanne Falconetti

- **Nature:** Played Joan in Dreyer’s *La Passion de Jeanne d’Arc*.
- **Date and place:** France, 1928.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented posthumous representation.**

### Alexandre Dumas

- **Nature:** Authored *Jehanne la Pucelle*, a nineteenth-century literary-historical treatment.
- **Date:** 1842.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented posthumous literary influence.**

### Mark Twain

- **Nature:** Authored *Personal Recollections of Joan of Arc*, a fictionalized historical novel framed as the memoir of Joan’s supposed page.
- **Dates:** Serialized 1895–1896; book publication 1896.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented posthumous literary influence.**

### Friedrich Schiller

- **Nature:** Authored the romantic tragedy *Die Jungfrau von Orleans*.
- **Date:** 1801.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented posthumous literary influence.**
- **Qualification:** Schiller substantially altered Joan’s life and death for dramatic purposes.

### Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky

- **Nature:** Composed the opera *Орлеанская дева* (*The Maid of Orleans*), drawing partly on Schiller.
- **Date:** Premiered 1881.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented posthumous artistic influence.**

### Lionel-Noël Royer

- **Nature:** Painted prominent academic-historical scenes of Joan, including imagery associated with her departure and military mission.
- **Period:** Late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented posthumous artistic representation.**

### Thomas Aquinas

- **Nature:** No personal or direct intellectual connection is possible. Later analysis of Joan’s male clothing has invoked Aquinas’s discussion of cross-dressing permitted by necessity.
- **Evidentiary label:** **Documented later doctrinal comparison, not a documented influence acknowledged by Joan.**
- **Qualification:** Joan is not known to have read or cited Aquinas.

### Joffrey de Bourlémont; Pica Bourlémont; Pierre Bourlémont

The roster contains members of the Bourlémont family associated with the region’s seigneurial history and later traditions surrounding Domrémy’s landscape. The consulted evidence did not establish a direct meeting, family tie, patronage relationship, or correspondence between Joan and the specifically named individuals.

- **Evidentiary label:** **No verified direct connection found; absence is the finding.**

### Remaining atlas roster

No adequately documented personal or expressly acknowledged connection was found between Joan and the remaining listed persons. Many—among them Abraham Lincoln, Napoleon Bonaparte, Victor Hugo, William Blake, George Orwell, Pablo Picasso, and numerous religious figures—could be compared with Joan or may have encountered her legend, but comparison, broad cultural awareness, or chronological coexistence is insufficient under the requested standard.
