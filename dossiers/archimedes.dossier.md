# Archimedes Research Dossier

**Subject:** Archimedes of Syracuse (Ἀρχιμήδης)  
**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Reconstructable biography, writings, machines, political setting, death traditions, myths, transmission, reception, and research bibliography.

> **Evidence warning.** No contemporary biography survives. Archimedes' own treatises provide the only surviving evidence written by him. [Polybius, Book 8](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/8%2A.html), written by an author born roughly a decade after Archimedes' death, is the earliest substantial narrative source for the siege; Cicero, Livy, Vitruvius, Valerius Maximus, Plutarch, Athenaeus, Pappus, late-antique authors, Byzantine writers, and Arabic manuscript traditions are progressively later. Exact birth year, education, marriage, children, wealth, appearance, and much of the familiar personality portrait cannot be established.

The often-repeated birth year 287 BCE is not a recorded ancient date. It depends on Tzetzes' late claim that Archimedes lived 75 years and the secure death year 212 BCE. Modern reference works reasonably use “c. 287,” but the precision is conventional. A lost biography is mentioned in the ancient commentary tradition and associated with a **Heracleides/Heraclides**; neither its contents nor even the biographer's exact identity is recoverable.

## Family and Ancestry

Archimedes writes in *The Sand-Reckoner* that an astronomical value had been given by “Phidias, my father.” The surviving Greek at this point is textually damaged, but the name and paternal relationship are generally accepted. Phidias is conventionally called an astronomer because the context concerns the apparent diameter of the sun. Nothing reliable survives about his ancestry, property, office, dates, spouse, or other children.

Plutarch, writing more than three centuries after Archimedes, says Archimedes was a “kinsman and friend” of **Hiero II**, ruler of Syracuse. This may preserve court knowledge; it might also be biographical inference from Archimedes' work for Hiero. The degree of kinship is never stated. Hiero's son **Gelon** is the addressee of *The Sand-Reckoner*, demonstrating access to the royal house whether or not blood kinship was real.

No ancient source securely identifies Archimedes' mother, siblings, spouse, lovers, or descendants. Assertions that he was celibate or wholly detached from family life belong to later constructions of the absorbed geometer.

## Childhood and Early Life

Archimedes grew up in third-century BCE Syracuse, one of the largest and most powerful Greek cities of the western Mediterranean. The city occupied several connected districts—Ortygia, Achradina, Tyche, Neapolis, and Epipolae—and possessed major harbors and fortifications. His lifetime spanned Syracuse's recovery under Hiero II, its alliance with Rome after the First Punic War, and the crisis that followed Hiero's death during the Second Punic War.

Nothing documentary survives about Archimedes' childhood, household, physical appearance, early illnesses, or first instruction. The portrait of a boy fascinated by mechanisms is modern retrospective storytelling.

## Education and Formation

Ancient and modern biographies commonly place Archimedes in **Alexandria**, but no surviving ancient text explicitly says that he studied there. The inference rests on strong intellectual evidence:

- His works address **Conon of Samos**, an astronomer and mathematician active at Alexandria, and after Conon's death Conon's associate or student **Dositheus of Pelusium**.
- *The Method* is addressed to **Eratosthenes of Cyrene**, librarian and mathematical scholar at Alexandria.
- Archimedes knows and extends the work of Euclid, Eudoxus, and other mathematicians associated with the Hellenistic research tradition.
- Athenaeus' ship narrative connects him with a gigantic Syracusan vessel eventually given to Ptolemaic Egypt.

This makes travel or study in Alexandria plausible, not proved. No teacher can be named securely. **Conon** was a colleague, valued critic, possible senior influence, and perhaps personal friend. Archimedes says Conon could understand and judge difficult results; after Conon's death he sent works to Dositheus. **Eratosthenes** was a respected peer and recipient rather than demonstrably a teacher.

His method combined:

1. the axiomatic and proof-centered geometry of Euclid;
2. Eudoxian exhaustion arguments for areas and volumes;
3. mechanical reasoning about balances and centers of gravity;
4. numerical bounding rather than loose approximation;
5. astronomy and very-large-number notation;
6. a deliberate separation, in *The Method*, between heuristic discovery by mechanics and formal geometrical demonstration.

## Early Career and Intellectual Network

There is no ancient curriculum vitae. The dedicatory prefaces permit a partial sequence. Archimedes circulated propositions to Conon; after Conon's death he reproached unnamed mathematicians for announcing false results that he had deliberately included among test propositions. He then sent corrected and expanded treatises to Dositheus. This reveals a competitive correspondence network in which results were sent, tested, criticized, and claimed.

The principal named nodes were:

- **Conon of Samos** — mathematician and astronomer; trusted evaluator.
- **Dositheus of Pelusium** — recipient of *On the Sphere and Cylinder*, *On Conoids and Spheroids*, *On Spirals*, and probably other works.
- **Eratosthenes of Cyrene** — recipient of *The Method* and, if authentic, the *Cattle Problem*.
- **Hiero II** — ruler, patron/client in the mechanical anecdotes, and political authority under whom defensive preparations were reportedly made.
- **Gelon** — Hiero's son and addressee of *The Sand-Reckoner*.

No academy, salary, workshop account, public office, or formal institutional appointment is recorded. Practical machines would have required artisans, foundries, carpenters, rope-makers, soldiers, and public financing, but their names and contractual arrangements are lost.

## Chronological Life History

### c. 287–260 BCE: Formation

The conventional chronology places Archimedes' birth around 287 BCE at Syracuse. He likely received elite mathematical and literary education. A period in Alexandria is possible. The sequence and dating of his treatises remain debated because internal cross-references establish only partial ordering.

### c. 260–230 BCE: Mathematical research and court-linked engineering

Archimedes developed results in plane equilibrium, centers of gravity, quadrature, spheres and cylinders, conoids and spheroids, spirals, circle measurement, hydrostatics, and numerical astronomy. Several writings are framed as letters sent eastward. Heath's collected translation and extensive introduction can be inspected through the [Online Library of Liberty](https://www.oll.libertyfund.org/titles/heath-the-works-of-archimedes) and [Internet Archive record](https://archive.org/details/worksofarchimede00arch).

[Plutarch preserves a court demonstration](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Marcellus%2A.html): after asserting that any weight could be moved by a given force, Archimedes allegedly used a compound pulley or windlass to draw a heavily laden ship smoothly by himself. The story illustrates mechanical advantage but was written centuries later. Plutarch also says Hiero induced Archimedes to turn from “pure” geometry to military machines. That opposition likely reflects Plutarch's philosophical literary agenda; Archimedes' authentic works show mechanics was integral to his mathematical practice.

[Athenaeus, quoting an earlier account attributed to Moschion](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Athenaeus/5C%2A.html), describes the enormous **Syracusia**:

- built for Hiero II;
- designed principally by the Corinthian **Archias**;
- launched or moved with a mechanism attributed to Archimedes;
- equipped with cabins, baths, gymnasium, library, gardens, towers, weapons, pumps, and an Archimedean screw;
- eventually presented to **Ptolemy III** and renamed **Alexandris** in the transmitted account.

The narrative is rich but later and partly celebratory. It supports a reputation for large-scale naval engineering, not sole authorship of the ship.

### Astronomical devices

[Cicero reports](https://www.attalus.org/cicero/republic1a.html) that Marcellus carried two Archimedean “spheres” from captured Syracuse:

- a conventional celestial globe placed in the Temple of Virtue;
- a more ingenious moving model retained by Marcellus, said to reproduce the motions of sun, moon, five planets, and eclipses.

Cicero says **Gaius Sulpicius Gallus** demonstrated the device in 166 BCE. Later authors—Ovid, Lactantius, Claudian, and others—turned it into an emblem of human imitation of the cosmos. The mechanism itself is lost. It cannot simply be identified with the Antikythera mechanism, though that later artifact demonstrates that complex geared astronomical modeling existed in the Hellenistic world.

### 215–214 BCE: Political rupture at Syracuse

Hiero II maintained a long alliance with Rome. After his death, his young grandson **Hieronymus** shifted toward Carthage. Hieronymus was assassinated; factional conflict followed; **Hippocrates** and **Epicydes** helped bring Syracuse into the Carthaginian camp. Roman commanders **Marcus Claudius Marcellus** and **Appius Claudius Pulcher** began the siege.

Archimedes' personal political beliefs are not recorded. He defended his city under its anti-Roman wartime government. There is no surviving evidence that he chose the Carthaginian alliance, held office, participated in the coups, conducted intelligence work, or directed political violence beyond designing defensive weapons.

### 214–212 BCE: Defense of Syracuse

**[Polybius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/8%2A.html)**, the earliest detailed source, says Hiero had supplied the resources and Archimedes had designed machines positioned to cover different ranges:

- long-range stone-throwers and artillery;
- successively smaller engines as ships approached;
- narrow wall openings with archers and “small scorpions” for close range;
- beams dropping stones or lead weights onto Roman siege apparatus;
- crane-like devices with an iron “hand” that gripped, lifted, tilted, or capsized vessels;
- comparable land defenses against Appius' troops.

Marcellus' fleet used paired ships carrying tall assault ladders called **sambucae**. Polybius says the countermeasures forced Rome to abandon direct assault and rely on blockade and stratagem. His witty sayings attributed to Marcellus—Archimedes using ships as cups and expelling the sambuca “band” from the banquet—are literary, but the engineering account is technically coherent and relatively early.

Livy and Plutarch expand the picture. Plutarch portrays Roman troops terrified even by the sight of a rope or timber above the wall. Neither Polybius nor Livy nor Plutarch says Archimedes burned the fleet with mirrors.

The city did not fall because Archimedes' machines were technically defeated. Romans exploited lax festival security, scaled a comparatively low wall section at night, admitted the army, and later captured remaining districts amid betrayal, disease, and factional collapse.

### 212 BCE: Death

The core fact is stable: Archimedes was killed by a Roman soldier during the capture of Syracuse, and Marcellus reportedly regretted it. Details diverge.

| Source/tradition | Account | Evidentiary classification |
|---|---|---|
| **[Livy](https://math.nyu.edu/Archimedes/Siege/Livy.html)**, *Ab urbe condita* 25.31 | Amid the sack, a soldier killed Archimedes, who was absorbed in figures drawn in dust and did not know the city had fallen; the soldier did not recognize him. Marcellus grieved and protected his relatives. | Early Roman narrative, about two centuries later |
| **Valerius Maximus**, *Memorable Deeds and Sayings* 8.7 ext. 7 | Archimedes protected his dust-drawn work and asked the intruder not to disturb it; he was beheaded, his blood falling across the diagram. | Moral exemplum, first century CE |
| **[Plutarch](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/plutarch/plutarchs-marcellus/the-death-of-archimedes/)**, *Marcellus* 19 | Version 1: he refused to follow a soldier until finishing a problem; killed in anger. Version 2: he carried instruments, which soldiers mistook for valuables. Version 3: he was taking a problem to Marcellus; killed by soldiers seeking supposed gold. | Plutarch explicitly preserves alternatives |
| Later formula | “Do not disturb my circles” / *Noli turbare circulos meos* | Famous paraphrase, not securely quoted in this exact form by the earliest sources |

“Killed mid-theorem” is therefore a defensible shorthand for the dominant ancient tradition, but the particular theorem, exact words, soldier's identity, weapon, and circumstances are unknown. No evidence suggests suicide, illness, addiction, or prior injury. Death was homicide during a sack—an act of wartime violence, not a judicial execution.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Ancient Syracuse had no companies or patent regime comparable to modern institutions. No patent, commercial partnership, investment portfolio, landholding, workshop ledger, or estate inventory survives.

### Securely grounded in surviving works

- **Law of the lever and equilibrium** — *On the Equilibrium of Planes* treats balances, centers of gravity, and equilibrium geometrically.
- **Hydrostatics and buoyancy** — *On Floating Bodies* establishes conditions of fluid equilibrium and the relation between a floating body's weight and displaced fluid.
- **Centers of gravity** — treated across mechanical works; foundational for later statics.
- **Exhaustion methods** — rigorous limiting arguments for areas and volumes.
- **Bounds for π** — *Measurement of a Circle* proves (3\frac{10}{71} < \pi < 3\frac17), using inscribed and circumscribed polygons.
- **Archimedean spiral** — mathematically analyzed in *On Spirals*.
- **Large-number system** — *The Sand-Reckoner* constructs notation capable of naming numbers vastly exceeding ordinary Greek practice and applies it to an upper bound for grains of sand filling the cosmos.

### Anciently attributed machines

- **Archimedean screw:** a helical water-raising device. Diodorus and Athenaeus connect Archimedes with it, sometimes in an Egyptian context. Whether he invented it, improved it, or merely became its canonical namesake is unresolved.
- **Compound pulleys/windlasses:** strongly consistent with his mechanics; court demonstration is anecdotal.
- **Ship-launching mechanism:** linked to the *Syracusia*.
- **Defensive artillery and cranes (“claw of Archimedes”):** well attested by Polybius and independently elaborated later, though exact reconstruction is uncertain.
- **Planetarium/celestial sphere:** attested by Cicero and later Roman writers; device lost.
- **Odometer:** Vitruvius describes one, but does not name Archimedes as inventor; later attribution is uncertain.
- **Water clock and pneumatic automata:** Arabic manuscripts transmit works attributed to Archimedes; authorship and layers of later adaptation require caution.
- **Catoptrics / mirror theory:** ancient bibliographic references associate Archimedes with optics, but the Greek treatise is lost and Arabic/Latin materials are textually complex.

### War production and responsibility

The best evidence makes Archimedes a designer of weapons that killed Roman attackers and disabled ships. Polybius assigns both design and construction to him in general terms while recognizing Hiero's financing. The labor force and operational chain are unnamed. There is no evidence of profiteering, private arms sales, atrocities against civilians, or punishment after investigation; the relevant violence occurred in an openly declared siege.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political

Archimedes lived under Hiero II's monarchy and worked closely enough with the royal household to dedicate a work to Gelon and become the subject of royal-engineering traditions. His defensive service was civic and monarchical at once. No speech, manifesto, party affiliation, diplomatic mission, vote, or theory of government survives.

### Religious

His works use conventional Greek divine and mythological references. *The Sand-Reckoner* discusses Aristarchus' heliocentric hypothesis as a mathematical cosmological model. Nothing permits a firm account of Archimedes' personal piety, skepticism, cult membership, or philosophical school.

### Mathematical and philosophical style

Archimedes' surviving persona is deliberately austere: definitions, assumptions, propositions, proofs, and compact epistolary prefaces. In *The Method*, however, he discloses discovery practices: imagined figures are weighed; indivisible slices balance around a fulcrum; a mechanically discovered relationship is then proved by accepted geometry. This is not modern integral calculus, but it is a sophisticated precursor to integral reasoning.

Plutarch claims Archimedes disdained practical engineering and considered only pure geometry worthy. That conflicts with the breadth of his mechanics and may impose a later Platonic hierarchy. It should be treated as Plutarch's character portrait, not an autobiographical statement.

## Wealth, Property, Finance, Offices, and Honors

No reliable amount of income or property is recorded. Court access and the resources needed for research and engineering suggest a socially privileged position, but “wealthy aristocrat” cannot be documented. Cicero rhetorically calls him a humble or obscure mathematician in contrast to tyrant Dionysius; this is a philosophical comparison, not a tax record or proof of poverty.

No ancient award, civic crown, priesthood, military rank, magistracy, dismissal, or formal academic chair is known. The monument described by Cicero—if his identification was correct—constituted a posthumous honor centered on the sphere-cylinder theorem.

## Controversies, Allegations, Crimes, and Unresolved Questions

### Deliberately false propositions

In the preface to *On Spirals*, Archimedes says he had sent out propositions including two false ones so that those claiming discoveries without proof could be exposed. This is documented intellectual gamesmanship, not modern scientific fraud: he later identifies the trap and supplies genuine demonstrations. It reveals priority competition and distrust within Hellenistic mathematics.

### The crown and alleged goldsmith fraud

[Vitruvius says](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Vitruvius/9%2A.html) Hiero supplied gold for a votive crown, suspected the goldsmith of substituting silver, and asked Archimedes to test it without damage. Archimedes' bath overflow supposedly supplied the method; he ran naked home crying “Eureka!” He compared displaced volumes of equal-weight gold, silver, and crown and exposed fraud.

Classification:

- **Alleged fraud inside a later anecdote**, not a preserved prosecution or judgment.
- Vitruvius is the sole ancient source for the full bath-and-crown story, writing roughly two centuries later.
- The stated displacement method is physically possible in principle but difficult at the precision required for a wreath-shaped crown; a concise modern discussion is [Scientific American's experiment-focused assessment](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/fact-or-fiction-archimede/).
- Archimedes' authentic hydrostatics could support a more precise weighing-in-water method, but no surviving work connects his buoyancy theory to the crown.
- The goldsmith is unnamed; no sentence or financial recovery is recorded.

### Palimpsest ownership litigation and modern forgery

These events concern an Archimedean manuscript, not misconduct by Archimedes:

- The thirteenth-century prayer book containing erased tenth-century Archimedean texts passed into private hands under disputed circumstances.
- The Greek Orthodox Patriarchate of Jerusalem sued Christie's in New York before the 1998 auction, alleging unlawful removal. The court allowed the sale, principally on limitations/laches grounds; that outcome did not prove a clean ancient-to-modern chain of title. The [official Palimpsest project](https://www.archimedespalimpsest.org/) and [Walters Museum account](https://thewalters.org/news/lost-and-found-the-secrets-of-archimedes/) document the later conservation history.
- Twentieth-century forgers painted gold-leaf religious images over several leaves, apparently to increase market value, obscuring undertext.
- An anonymous buyer paid **US$2 million** in 1998 and deposited the manuscript at the Walters Art Museum for conservation and imaging.

### No evidence of modern categories

No evidence exists for money laundering, organized crime, corruption in office, sexual abuse, addiction, imprisonment, or intelligence service. Applying these categories affirmatively would invent a record.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Give me a place to stand”

1. **Story:** With a long enough lever and a place to stand, Archimedes could move the earth/world.
2. **Appearance:** Plutarch gives the “another earth” form; Pappus (fourth century CE) gives the familiar imperative; Diodoran fragments preserve another form; Tzetzes later supplies Doric wording.
3. **Circulation:** Mathematicians, rhetoricians, educational texts, political oratory, art, and modern quotation anthologies.
4. **Evidence:** It accords with Archimedean lever theory, but no contemporary quotation survives. The popular wording that adds “a lever long enough and a fulcrum” is an explanatory expansion.
5. **Reputation:** It made Archimedes the archetype of reason multiplying force and generated the philosophical metaphor “Archimedean point.”

### The ship moved single-handed

Plutarch says Hiero demanded proof and Archimedes, seated at a distance, effortlessly drew a heavily laden ship using compound pulleys. It appears centuries after the event but is mechanically plausible. Its literary purpose is to turn an abstract claim into royal spectacle.

### “Eureka!” in the bath

1. **Story:** Sudden recognition that displacement could expose adulterated gold caused Archimedes to leap from the bath and run naked through Syracuse shouting “I have found it.”
2. **Appearance:** Vitruvius, *On Architecture* 9, late first century BCE.
3. **Circulation:** Roman technical literature, Renaissance natural philosophy, schoolbooks, cartoons, California's “Eureka,” and general language.
4. **Evidence:** No Archimedean writing mentions it. The detailed experimental protocol faces measurement difficulties; the underlying displacement and density logic is sound.
5. **Reputation:** It established the canonical “flash of discovery” narrative and attached bodily comedy to mathematical genius.

### Burning mirrors / “death ray”

1. **Story:** Archimedes concentrated sunlight with mirrors or polished shields and ignited Roman ships.
2. **Appearance:** Absent from Polybius, Livy, and Plutarch. Lucian (second century CE) says Archimedes burned ships but does not clearly say mirrors. Galen's wording is disputed. Anthemius of Tralles (sixth century) discusses reflector construction; Byzantine authors Zonaras and Tzetzes give developed mirror narratives.
3. **Circulation:** Byzantine historiography, Renaissance experiments, Athanasius Kircher and Buffon, nationalist retellings, textbooks, *MythBusters*, and repeated modern experiments.
4. **Evidence:** Arrays can concentrate solar heat under favorable stationary conditions. Igniting moving, wet, defended ships at useful range is operationally doubtful. Silence of the earliest detailed siege accounts weighs heavily against the canonical version. Incendiary projectiles may underlie some later notices.
5. **Reputation:** It transformed Archimedes into an ancient optical superweapon designer and continues to generate experimental folklore.

### The “claw”

The modern name **Claw of Archimedes** is not the ancient technical name. Polybius does describe an iron hand on a chain and lifting apparatus, so the tradition has a strong early core. Reconstructions differ: a grappling crane may have tilted, rolled, punctured, or destabilized vessels rather than hoisting them wholly clear of the sea.

### “Do not disturb my circles”

The exact Latin tag *Noli turbare circulos meos* is not a secure verbatim last utterance. Valerius Maximus has a plea not to disturb “this,” and Plutarch has refusal to leave before completing a problem. Later retellings compressed these into the immortal line. It symbolizes intellectual absorption confronting military power.

### Death by ignorance versus deliberate killing

Livy makes the killer unaware of Archimedes' identity. Plutarch's variants make the killing an angry response or robbery. All serve Marcellus' reputation by emphasizing his order to spare Archimedes and his grief. No source names or prosecutes the soldier. The common claim that Archimedes was specifically beheaded comes from one strand, not a unanimous record.

### Cicero “found the tomb”

1. **Story:** As quaestor in Sicily in 75 BCE, Cicero located an overgrown grave near Syracuse's Agrigentine gate by recognizing a small column bearing a sphere and cylinder; local elites had forgotten it. He had vegetation cleared and read part of the inscription.
2. **Appearance:** Cicero's own *Tusculan Disputations* 5.64–66, written about 45 BCE, roughly thirty years after the claimed discovery.
3. **Circulation:** Roman moral philosophy, antiquarian topography, neoclassical paintings, guidebooks, and Syracusan tourism.
4. **Evidence:** This is [Cicero's first-person testimony](https://www.attalus.org/cicero/tusc5A.html), although shaped as a moral exemplum contrasting mathematical wisdom with tyranny. The tomb is now lost. The rock-cut Roman columbarium promoted today as “Archimedes' tomb” is later than Archimedes and does not fit Cicero's description.
5. **Reputation:** The episode made Cicero rescuer of Greek memory and the sphere-cylinder figure Archimedes' funerary emblem.

### Obsessive geometer neglecting food and bathing

Plutarch says servants had to drag Archimedes to bathe and that he drew diagrams in ashes and oil on his skin. This belongs to an ancient “absent-minded sage” topos. It may preserve remembered habits but cannot diagnose autism, obsessive-compulsive disorder, mania, or any other modern condition.

### Probably authentic or seriously attributed

- **The Cattle Problem** (*Problema bovinum / Problema Archimedis*): a 22-distich Greek epigram addressed to Eratosthenes, setting linear and square/triangular constraints that yield enormous numbers. Found by Lessing in a manuscript and published in 1773. Most scholars accept an Archimedean origin or close ancient Archimedean context, but transmission is independent of the main corpus.
- **Book of Lemmas** (*Liber Assumptorum*): fifteen geometrical propositions surviving through Arabic and Latin transmission. The Arabic tradition attributes it to Archimedes; its present wording refers to Archimedes in the third person, showing editorial mediation. It contains the arbelos and salinon.

### Lost works known from references

Titles and contents vary by witness. Items attributed in ancient or medieval sources include:

- a work on **sphere-making** or construction of a mechanical sphere;
- **Catoptrics** (optics/mirrors);
- works on **centers of gravity**;
- **On Balances** or **On Levers**;
- a work on the **calendar**;
- **On the Construction of Water Clocks** (Arabic tradition; layered text);
- **On the Seven-Circle Figure**;
- propositions concerning **polyhedra** later called Archimedean solids;
- a work concerning a **semi-regular solid** or “fourteen-base” figure;
- **On Circle-Touchings** and other problems named by Pappus;
- material on the duplication of the cube and **neusis** constructions.

These should not be silently counted as intact books by Archimedes. Some are lost genuine titles; some may be later compilations, translations, or pseudepigrapha.

### Principal editions and translations

- **1544 Basel editio princeps**, *Archimedis Syracusani philosophi ac geometrae excellentissimi opera*, Greek and Latin, associated with Thomas Gechauff/Venatorius and Johann Herwagen.
- **Federico Commandino**, Latin translations and commentaries (1558 and later), influential in Renaissance mechanics.
- **J. L. Heiberg**, *Archimedis Opera Omnia*, critical Greek edition with Latin translation (first edition 1880–81; revised 1910–15).
- **T. L. Heath**, *The Works of Archimedes* (1897; supplement including *The Method* in later editions), English paraphrase/translation and historical introduction.
- **E. J. Dijksterhuis**, *Archimedes* (Dutch 1938; English 1956/1987), systematic reconstruction of the science.
- **Reviel Netz**, *The Works of Archimedes: Translation and Commentary*, modern text-centered translation/commentary series: Vol. I, *Sphere and Cylinder I* (2004); Vol. II, *On Spirals* (2017); further volumes form an ongoing scholarly project.
- **Reviel Netz et al.**, *The Archimedes Palimpsest*, 2 vols. (2011), images, transcription, codicology, conservation, and scholarship.

## Manuscript Transmission

Archimedes' originals are lost. Surviving knowledge depends on:

1. Late-antique editing and commentary, especially **Eutocius of Ascalon** (sixth century).
2. Byzantine manuscript families copied from earlier exemplars.
3. Arabic translations and adaptations from the ninth century onward.
4. Medieval Latin translations, notably **William of Moerbeke's** 1269 translation from Greek.
5. Renaissance Greek and Latin editions.
6. The **Archimedes Palimpsest**.

The [Palimpsest's Archimedean undertext](https://www.archimedespalimpsest.org/) was copied in Constantinople in the tenth century. Around 1229 parchment leaves were scraped and reused for a Christian prayer book. **Papadopoulos-Kerameus** noticed mathematical undertext in a Jerusalem Patriarchate catalogue (1899); **Johan Ludvig Heiberg** inspected and photographed it in Constantinople in 1906 and identified lost Archimedean texts. After disappearance into private ownership, damage, and forged illuminations, it resurfaced for the 1998 auction. Multispectral and X-ray fluorescence imaging recovered text beneath overwriting and gold leaf. The project made images and transcriptions available for study; its [specialist bibliography](https://archimedespalimpsest.org/links/bibliography.php) records the technical and textual publications.

## Death, Burial, and Tomb

Archimedes died in 212 BCE when Roman forces captured Syracuse. Marcellus' supposed order to spare him is repeated in later narratives and fits Roman presentation of the general as cultivated and merciful; it is not preserved in a contemporary military order. Livy says Marcellus arranged burial and honored Archimedes' relatives.

Cicero states that in 75 BCE he found the neglected tomb outside the Agrigentine gate. The monument bore a sphere and cylinder, commemorating the theorem that the sphere's volume and surface bear a 2:3 relation to the enclosing cylinder's corresponding measures. Cicero's first-person report is the strongest evidence, but the physical monument has not survived and his identification cannot be archaeologically verified.

The so-called Tomb of Archimedes in the Grotticelle necropolis is a Roman-period columbarium, generally dated later than Archimedes. It is a memorialized tourist site, not an authenticated burial.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Antiquity

Archimedes became two partly competing figures:

- the rigorous geometer studied by Heron, Pappus, Eutocius, and mathematical specialists;
- the marvelous engineer and distracted sage of Roman biography.

Roman authors used him to contrast Greek intellect with Roman conquest, intellectual immortality with political power, and human mechanism with divine cosmology.

### Arabic and medieval worlds

Arabic translators, mathematicians, and engineers preserved, extended, and sometimes recompiled Archimedean material. Thābit ibn Qurra, the Banū Mūsā milieu, al-Khāzinī, and later scholars transmitted statics, geometry, specific-gravity reasoning, mirror lore, and mechanical problems. Latin Europe recovered Archimedes through Arabic-Latin channels and direct Greek-Latin translation, especially William of Moerbeke.

### Renaissance and Scientific Revolution

Printed editions and Commandino's translations made Archimedes central to **Tartaglia, Guidobaldo del Monte, Galileo, Kepler, Cavalieri, Torricelli, Huygens**, and others. “Archimedean” became a methodological identity: mathematical demonstration joined to controlled mechanics. Galileo repeatedly treated Archimedes as master and model.

### Modern mathematics and science

His exhaustion proofs became part of the prehistory of calculus; his hydrostatics underlies elementary fluid mechanics; his work on equilibrium shaped statics; and his numerical bounds remain standard examples of rigorous approximation. It is anachronistic to say he “invented calculus” in the Newtonian/Leibnizian sense, but equally misleading to reduce his methods to crude approximation.

Named inheritances include:

- Archimedes' principle;
- Archimedean spiral;
- Archimedean solids;
- Archimedean property/axiom;
- Archimedean screw;
- Archimedes number and cattle problem;
- lunar crater **Archimedes** and Montes Archimedes;
- the **Fields Medal**, bearing an idealized head of Archimedes, a sphere-cylinder diagram, and a Latin inscription adapted from Manilius;
- **Eureka** as a global emblem of discovery and California's state motto;
- ships, submarines, institutions, prizes, streets, and software bearing his name.

### Historical reinterpretation

Nineteenth-century histories emphasized pure geometry. Twentieth-century work by Heiberg, Dijksterhuis, Clagett, Knorr, and others restored mechanics and transmission history. Palimpsest scholarship further weakened the simple opposition between “pure mathematician” and “practical inventor”: Archimedes used mechanical imagination as a research instrument while maintaining strict standards for proof.

Modern nationalist claims sometimes label him simply “Greek” or “Italian.” Historically he was a Greek-speaking citizen of independent Syracuse in Sicily, long before the modern Greek and Italian nation-states. Both Greek intellectual history and Sicilian/Italian local heritage legitimately preserve him, but neither modern nationality is a literal ancient citizenship.

## Research Limits

- No contemporary biography, portrait, grave, autograph manuscript, personal letter outside mathematical prefaces, financial record, or family archive survives.
- Dates assigned to individual treatises are relative and contested.
- Ancient writers often used Archimedes as a moral or literary type; their reports cannot be treated as stenographic biography.
- Technological feasibility does not by itself establish historical use.
- “Unknown” family, wealth, health, or office data must remain unknown rather than be filled with plausible fiction.
