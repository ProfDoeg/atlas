## Works by Diogenes of Sinope

### Dialogues and philosophical treatises

No work listed here survives intact. The principal ancient catalogue, preserved by Diogenes Laërtius and numbered *SSR* V B 117 in Giannantoni’s standard collection, transmits two conflicting inventories: an anonymous list of works “attributed” to Diogenes and a shorter list that Sotion regarded as genuine. Sosicrates and Satyrus, however, maintained that Diogenes wrote nothing at all. Consequently, every title below is an attribution rather than a securely extant work.

- *Cephalion*. A lost dialogue appearing in both the general catalogue and Sotion’s list of allegedly genuine works; its contents and date are unknown. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Ichthyas*. A lost dialogue known only from the general catalogue; even its attribution to Diogenes was disputed in antiquity. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Jackdaw* (*Koloios*). A lost dialogue, perhaps named for a character or the bird; nothing permits a secure decision about its subject or authorship. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Pordalus*. A lost dialogue included in both transmitted lists; the proper name or title cannot be interpreted securely. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *The Athenian People* (*Dēmos Athēnaiōn*). A lost dialogue or political text known only by title in the general catalogue. Its form, contents, and authorship remain uncertain. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Republic* (*Politeia*). A lost political-philosophical work describing a community ordered according to nature; unlike most titles in the catalogue, it is also discussed by Philodemus and other ancient witnesses. Its Diogenean authorship was challenged by some ancient Stoics and remains debated, although the surviving testimony shows that a work bearing his name circulated in antiquity. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81) [Meccariello, “Did the Hellenistic Stoics Consider Diogenes’ and Zeno’s Republics Spurious?”](https://openurl.ebsco.com/contentitem/gcd%3A184562454)
- *Art of Ethics* (*Technē Ēthikē*). A lost instructional or systematic ethical text in the general catalogue. Calling it a “book” may overstate what is known: only its title and attribution survive. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *On Wealth*. A lost philosophical dialogue or treatise known only from the general catalogue. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *On Love*. A lost philosophical work appearing in both transmitted lists, although neither its form nor its contents can now be recovered securely. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Theodorus*. A lost dialogue, presumably but not demonstrably named for an interlocutor; it occurs only in the general catalogue. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Hypsias*. A lost dialogue known only by this uncertain personal name or title in the general catalogue. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Aristarchus*. A lost dialogue included in both ancient lists and probably named for an interlocutor, although neither identity nor subject is recoverable. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *On Death*. A lost philosophical dialogue or treatise appearing only in the general catalogue. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *On Virtue*. A lost ethical work admitted by Sotion as genuine but absent from the preceding catalogue, exposing how unstable the ancient bibliography already was. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *On the Good*. A lost philosophical work found only in Sotion’s list of allegedly genuine writings. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *A Mendicant* (*Ptōchos*). A lost dialogue or character-piece named only by Sotion; whether the title designates Diogenes, another speaker, or a dramatic situation is unknown. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Tolmaeus*. A lost dialogue in Sotion’s list, probably bearing a personal name, though its interpretation and subject are uncertain. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Casandrus*. A lost dialogue known only from Sotion’s inventory; it may be named for an interlocutor, but no contents survive. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Philiscus*. A lost dialogue in Sotion’s list, possibly named for Diogenes’ pupil Philiscus of Aegina—the same figure to whom other ancient authorities assigned the tragedies attributed to Diogenes. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.73, 6.76, 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Sisyphus*. A lost dialogue or mythological-philosophical composition appearing only in Sotion’s list. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Ganymedes*. A lost dialogue or mythological-philosophical composition admitted as genuine by Sotion but absent from the general catalogue. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)

### Tragedies

All seven plays are lost, apart from doctrines or motifs that later witnesses associate with them. Their authorship was disputed already in antiquity: Satyrus assigned them to Diogenes’ pupil Philiscus of Aegina, while Favorinus reported that Pasiphon, son of Lucian, composed them after Diogenes’ death. A closely matching catalogue attached to a different figure, Diogenes of Athens, creates an additional possibility of biographical or bibliographical confusion.

- *Helen*. A lost tragedy attributed to Diogenes of Sinope in the principal catalogue, but also assigned by another ancient tradition to Diogenes of Athens; its authorship therefore cannot be settled. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Thyestes*. A lost tragedy, sometimes called *Atreus*, from which Diogenes Laërtius derives evidence for doctrines about food and human flesh; he immediately qualifies the citation because the play might instead be by Philiscus or Pasiphon. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.73, 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Heracles*. A lost tragedy attributed to Diogenes in the catalogue but inseparable from the broader ancient dispute over the authorship of the entire dramatic group. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Achilles*. A lost tragedy whose existence as an anciently catalogued title is secure, but whose attribution to Diogenes of Sinope is not. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Medea*. A lost tragedy transmitted under Diogenes’ name, though ancient authorities assigned the dramatic corpus elsewhere. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Chrysippus*. A lost tragedy, not to be confused with a philosophical treatise about the later Stoic philosopher; only its title and disputed attribution survive. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Oedipus*. A lost tragedy included in the disputed dramatic corpus attributed to Diogenes. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)

### Collections and occasional texts

- *Anecdotes* (*Chreiai*). A lost collection admitted by Sotion as genuine; the Greek category denotes memorable sayings or actions, so this may have been a collection of performative philosophical episodes rather than a continuous treatise. It must not be equated automatically with the much later anecdotes about Diogenes collected by other authors. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)
- *Letters*. A lost correspondence collection included in both ancient inventories. Diogenes Laërtius alludes to letters in which Diogenes supposedly described taking up residence in a large ceramic jar, but the collection’s extent and authenticity cannot be established. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.23, 6.80](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81)

### Embodied philosophical obra

- **Fourth century BCE, traditionally attributed:** *The Cynic life of training, poverty, and public demonstration*. Diogenes’ most consequential obra may have been an intentionally fashioned way of life: bodily training, radical material simplification, public gestures, improvised arguments, and violations of convention made philosophy manifest rather than merely described it. It resists classification as a discrete “work,” because virtually every episode is preserved by later anecdotal tradition, often in variant or transferable forms, yet ancient testimony explicitly presents his bodily and mental training as philosophical practice. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.22–38, 6.70–73](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81) [“Diogenes ‘the Cynic’: between anecdote and history”](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/CFCG/en/article/view/63588)
- **Fourth century BCE, traditionally attributed:** *Public sayings, repartees, and enacted lessons*. These are manifestations rather than securely authored texts: the surviving corpus consists of later reports of speech-acts and staged gestures, not Diogenes’ own written record. They cannot responsibly be itemized as individually authenticated works, but excluding them would erase the medium in which his philosophy was said to operate. [Diogenes Laërtius 6.24–69](https://www.lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=diogenes_of_sinope%3Adiogenes_laertius_book_6_20-81) [Giannantoni, *Socratis et Socraticorum Reliquiae*, publication description](https://www.iliesi.cnr.it/scheda.php?cl=E&id=121)
