# El Gólem: Research Dossier

## Scope, identity, and evidentiary method

**Subject:** El Gólem / the golem—not a single historical person, but a recurring artificial being constituted through matter, language, ritual, narrative, machinery, or computation.

**Principal names and spellings:** Hebrew גֹּלֶם / גולם (*gōlem*, “unformed substance,” “unfinished form”); Yiddish גולם (*goylem*); Spanish *gólem*; German and English *Golem*. Individual manifestations acquire names including Joseph or Yossele, Golem Aleph/Golem A, Golem B, GOLEM XIV, and, in the Colegio Invisible’s account, the authorial pseudonym **El Gólem**.

**Working definition:** An anthropoid or artificial agent made by a human creator, traditionally from earth and divine letters. In modern adaptations, the material becomes machinery, code, statistical weights, or generated language.

**Atlas thesis:** The golem tradition can be read as a long account of emergent artificial subjectivity: beings made or activated through letters placed in their mouths or inscribed upon them. This is an interpretive thesis, not an established scholarly consensus. The dossier documents the historical sequence that makes the comparison possible.

**Evidentiary labels:**

- **Documented fact:** supported by a surviving text, record, artifact, film, publication, or institutional source.
- **Reported fact:** a dated report whose underlying event cannot now be independently reconstructed.
- **Attributed tradition:** a story transmitted as family, communal, or religious tradition.
- **Myth-legend:** a supernatural narrative historically important as a narrative, without evidence that its supernatural events occurred.
- **Literary invention:** an openly fictional work.
- **Fabrication or literary hoax:** a modern composition presented as an older document.
- **Allegation or disputed interpretation:** a contested claim, scholarly hypothesis, or accusation.
- **Unverified project claim:** a statement supplied by the Colegio Invisible whose external documentation could not be fully recovered.

## Basic Identifying Information

The biblical word *golmi* appears in Psalm 139:16. It denotes the speaker’s unformed body or embryonic substance, not an animated artificial servant. The later figure accumulates through several initially distinct traditions:

1. Adam’s unanimated bodily stage.
2. Rabbinic stories in which sages create human or animal life.
3. *Sefer Yetzirah* traditions concerning creation through letters and numbers.
4. Medieval Ashkenazi ritual instructions for forming an anthropoid.
5. Early-modern stories about Elijah Ba’al Shem of Chełm.
6. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century attachment to Rabbi Judah Loew of Prague.
7. Literary and cinematic monsters, doubles, defenders, workers, and rebels.
8. Computers and artificial intelligences explicitly called or compared to golems.
9. Contemporary language models: text-trained systems whose principal public behavior is the production of language.

There is consequently no single birth or death. The subject undergoes repeated **manifestations and deanimations**.

## Family and Textual Ancestry

### Biblical ancestry: Psalm 139:16

**Documented text:** Psalm 139:16 contains the Hebrew *golmi*: “Your eyes saw my unformed body/substance.” It is the sole biblical occurrence of the noun in this form. The verse also joins the unformed body to writing in God’s book, a conjunction important to later interpreters, although the psalm does not describe magical animation.

The psalm’s composition and redaction cannot be dated precisely. It belongs to the Hebrew Psalter formed over centuries during the first millennium BCE.

### Adam as golem

**Documented rabbinic text:** Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 38b, gives an hourly sequence for Adam’s first day:

- first hour: his dust was gathered;
- second: it became a *golem*;
- third: his limbs were extended;
- fourth: a soul was cast into him;
- later hours: he stood, was commanded, sinned, was judged, and was expelled.

Here *golem* is a developmental state: formed enough to be identifiable but not yet ensouled.

**Interpretive importance:** The artificial anthropoid descends from Adam typologically. Its maker repeats part, but not all, of divine creation. Later writers commonly make speech the line separating a human from an incomplete anthropoid.

### Ezekiel’s dry bones

**Documented text; disputed relevance:** Ezekiel 37 describes bones reassembled, covered with sinews and flesh, and animated by divine breath. It is not called a golem story in the biblical text. Later comparative studies sometimes treat it as a proto-golem motif because matter is progressively assembled and animated, but it belongs to prophetic resurrection imagery rather than the letter-magic lineage.

## Rabbinic Births

### Rava’s created man

**Documented rabbinic narrative:** Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 65b:

> Rava created a man and sent him to Rabbi Zeira. Rabbi Zeira spoke to him, but he did not answer. Rabbi Zeira said: “You were created by one of the fellowship; return to your dust.”

The passage does not use the later Prague name, describe a clay statue, mention *emet* (“truth”) on a forehead, or portray a rampage. Its artificial man is identified by failed speech and is returned to dust.

The Talmudic setting belongs to Sasanian Babylonia and the intellectual world later centered in academies conventionally associated with Sura and Pumbedita. The narrative itself does not specify where Rava performed the creation.

### The created calf

The same page says that Rav Hanina and Rav Oshaia studied the “laws of creation”—in later recensions associated with *Sefer Yetzirah*—every Sabbath eve and created a third-grown calf, which they ate.

**Documented narrative; supernatural claim:** The text documents belief or storytelling about sage-created life. It does not provide evidence that biological creation occurred.

### What the rabbinic stories establish

They establish four durable components:

- human imitation of divine creation;
- righteousness or sacred knowledge as the enabling power;
- a defective result;
- speech as an evidentiary test.

They do not yet establish the Prague defender, the forehead inscription, a Jewish community threatened by blood libel, or a servant that rebels.

## *Sefer Yetzirah*: Cosmology Before Recipe

### Date and provenance

*Sefer Yetzirah* (“Book of Formation/Creation”) is a compact Hebrew cosmological work explaining creation through the ten *sefirot* and twenty-two Hebrew letters, the “thirty-two wondrous paths of wisdom.”

**Disputed dating:** Proposals range roughly from the second through sixth or seventh centuries CE. Some older scholarship favored late antiquity, while more recent arguments place its received composition relatively late within that range. No named author or exact place can be established.

### Letter cosmology

The book treats letters as structural elements of creation: they are engraved, hewn, weighed, permuted, and combined. Its “231 gates” are combinations of pairs drawn from the twenty-two-letter alphabet. Early manuscripts and commentators also preserve a “221 gates” reading, showing that the numerical transmission was unstable.

The base text does not straightforwardly read as a manual for building a clay servant. The operational “golem recipe” develops through commentary and practice.

### Saadia Gaon

Saadia ben Joseph al-Fayyumi (882–942), head of the academy at Sura, completed an Arabic commentary on *Sefer Yetzirah* in 931.

**Documented connection:** Saadia interpreted its cosmology philosophically and defended the 231-gate combinatorial scheme. He treated God as the true creator; human letter manipulation did not equal creation ex nihilo.

### Judah ben Barzillai of Barcelona

Judah ben Barzillai, active in the late eleventh or early twelfth century, produced an extensive commentary preserving earlier traditions about *Sefer Yetzirah*. His work is a transmission point between philosophical commentary and practical or legendary creation claims.

### Eleazar of Worms

Eleazar ben Judah of Worms, approximately 1165/1176–1230/1238, a leading figure among the German Pietists or *Hasidei Ashkenaz*, supplies the decisive medieval procedure.

**Documented textual recipe:** A commentary attributed to his circle instructs adepts to:

- work in a group of two or three;
- take virgin mountain earth;
- knead it with running water;
- fashion a body;
- circle it while reciting ordered combinations of Hebrew letters;
- associate letter permutations with bodily organs;
- reverse the permutations to deactivate the result.

Exact textual attribution is complicated by manuscript transmission, but the Worms material is the earliest surviving explicit anthropoid-making procedure generally accepted in scholarship.

The creature is not primarily a military guardian. The rite demonstrates mastery of sacred language and imitates creation as a mystical achievement.

## Abraham Abulafia: The Recipe Turned Inward

Abraham ben Samuel Abulafia (1240–approximately 1291/92) developed ecstatic or prophetic Kabbalah across Iberia, Italy, Sicily, Greece, and the eastern Mediterranean.

His *Ḥayyei ha-ʿOlam ha-Ba* (“Life of the World to Come”), composed in 1280, gives detailed contemplative exercises involving:

- written permutations of divine names;
- vocalization;
- breath control;
- bodily and head movements;
- visualization of letters;
- progressive alteration of consciousness.

**Documented intellectual relationship:** The same letter-combination technologies found in artificial-creation traditions are redirected toward transforming the practitioner. Abulafia does not simply construct a clay servant; the human mystic becomes the material worked upon.

A fifteenth- or sixteenth-century parchment manuscript of the work survives in the British Library as Or. 10867.

## Soul, Speech, and Legal Status

### Safed and the soul question

Sixteenth-century Kabbalists debated what human adepts could confer upon a created anthropoid.

- Meir ibn Gabbai, around 1530, held that an artificially produced person lacked *ruaḥ*, inferred from its speechlessness.
- Moses Cordovero, writing in 1548, distinguished a special vitality, *ḥiyyut*, from *nefesh*, *ruaḥ*, and *neshamah*. A human maker might impart animation without giving a human soul.
- Isaac Luria’s Safed school transmitted elaborate teachings about souls, embodiment, exile, and repair through disciples such as Hayyim Vital. The golem was not the central category of Lurianic doctrine, and claims that Luria himself left a golem recipe should be treated cautiously.

### Can a golem join a minyan?

**Documented halakhic discussion:** Tzvi Hirsch Ashkenazi, the Hakham Tzvi (1656–1718), responsum 93, asked whether an artificially created man could count toward the quorum of ten. He connected the question to a family tradition that his ancestor Elijah of Chełm created one. His reasoning ultimately disfavors counting it: if Rava’s creation possessed full human standing, Rabbi Zeira could not simply have destroyed it.

Jacob Emden (1697–1776), Ashkenazi’s son, revisited the issue in *She’elat Ya’avetz* 2:82. He emphasized the creature’s lack of human knowledge or soul and compared outwardly human performance without understanding to trained animal behavior.

These are real legal-theological discussions prompted by traditional narratives. They are not court findings that golems existed.

## Chełm: The First Named Maker

### Elijah Ba’al Shem

Elijah ben Aaron Judah, known as Elijah Ba’al Shem of Chełm, was a historical Polish rabbi and kabbalist who died in 1583.

### Earliest layers

**Attributed tradition:** An anonymous manuscript generally dated to the 1630s relates an artificial creation associated with a contemporary or near-contemporary Jewish adept. The exact manuscript history requires continued specialist examination.

**Documented report:** In 1674 Johann Christoph Wagenseil published a letter from Christoph Arnold describing a Polish rabbi “Elias” who formed an anthropoid for practical service. As the figure grew dangerously large, its maker erased the first letter of *emet* (אמת, truth), leaving *met* (מת, dead). In some variants the collapsing body injures or kills its maker.

**Attributed family recollection:** Jacob Emden reported that his father, Tzvi Ashkenazi, said their ancestor Elijah of Chełm had made such a being. In Emden’s version the creature grew uncontrollably and struck or injured its maker during deanimation.

### Historical significance

Chełm predates the Prague-Maharal attachment. It supplies:

- a named historical rabbi;
- growth beyond control;
- *emet/met* deactivation;
- bodily danger to the creator;
- family transmission into a halakhic discussion.

The famous Prague plot later absorbed these elements.

## Prague: A Backward-Making Legend

### Judah Loew ben Bezalel

Rabbi Judah Loew ben Bezalel, the Maharal, was born probably around 1520–1525 and died in Prague on 17 September 1609. He served communities including Nikolsburg/Mikulov, Prague, and Poznań and wrote extensively on law, ethics, education, exile, creation, and Jewish communal life.

**Documented negative finding:** No surviving work by the Maharal says that he built a golem. Contemporary and near-contemporary chronicles, including writings by associates and family traditions, do not attest it. No sixteenth-, seventeenth-, or eighteenth-century Prague Hebrew source securely connects him to the famous clay defender. The association emerges more than two centuries after his death.

### Earliest datable Prague layers

- **1836, Vienna:** Ludwig August Frankl published a notice on 16 November referring to clay said to lie in the attic of Prague’s Old-New Synagogue. This is a report of folklore, not an inspection or archaeological finding.
- **1837:** Berthold Auerbach’s historical novel *Spinoza* supplied the earliest widely recognized literary attribution of a golem to the Maharal.
- **1841:** Franz Klutschak published “Der Golam des Rabbi Löw” in Prague’s *Panorama des Universums*, presenting a tale reportedly circulating among Prague Jews.
- **1841:** Gustav Philippson published “Der Golam, eine Legende.”
- **1842:** Abraham Tendlau printed “Der Golem des Hoch-Rabbi-Löb.”
- **1847:** Leopold Weisel’s “Der Golem,” collected in Wolf Pascheles’s *Sippurim*, became a major transmitter.

These versions differ in purpose, animation, behavior, and ending. Their appearance belongs to a nineteenth-century German and German-Jewish folklore culture, not to a recovered Renaissance record.

### Yudl Rosenberg’s decisive fabrication

In 1909 Rabbi Yehudah Yudl Rosenberg published *Nifla’ot Maharal mi-Prag im ha-Golem* (“The Wonders of the Maharal of Prague with the Golem”), claiming to reproduce a roughly three-hundred-year-old manuscript written by the Maharal’s son-in-law Isaac Katz and found in the imperial library at Metz.

**Scholarly finding:** The supposed old manuscript has not been authenticated and the work is treated as Rosenberg’s composition—a literary hoax or pseudepigraphic fiction. Scholars have identified borrowings and plot structures from modern detective fiction, including Arthur Conan Doyle.

Rosenberg consolidated the now-standard plot:

- Christians accuse Prague Jews of ritual murder;
- the Maharal receives or discovers a formula;
- he, his son-in-law, and a disciple form the golem by the Vltava;
- the creature, often called Yossele, hunts conspirators and protects the community;
- it must not work on the Sabbath;
- it is eventually deactivated and concealed in the synagogue attic.

The blood-libel setting reflects real persecution, but the particular episode is fictionalized and projected backward onto Rudolf II’s Prague.

Chayim Bloch adapted the cycle in German in 1919–1920 and in subsequent editions, often without making Rosenberg’s authorship transparent. A supposed 1582 letter of the Maharal published by Bloch in 1923 borrows from Rosenberg and is regarded as a twentieth-century forgery.

### The attic

**Myth-legend:** The deanimated body lies under discarded prayer books in the attic of the Old-New Synagogue.

**Documented searches:**

- In 1920 journalist Egon Erwin Kisch entered with communal permission and described beams, rubble, an old chimney pipe, a dead bird, fungi, and a bat—but no clay body.
- Czech investigator Ivan Mackerle entered in March 1984 and used geophysical equipment in or around the building. He reported no remains.
- Later maintenance and documentation of the roof space, including work reported around 2014, produced no authenticated anthropoid remains.

Absence of remains does not prove that no clay object was ever present, but there is no archaeological evidence for the legendary body.

### Public materialization

Ladislav Šaloun’s statue of Rabbi Loew stands at Prague’s New City Hall, completed in the building’s early-twentieth-century sculptural program and commonly dated to 1914–1917. The historical rabbi is thereby monumentalized through the legend attached to him after death.

### Prague as hyperstition

“Hyperstition” is a modern theoretical term for a fiction that helps cause its own social reality. Applied descriptively, the Prague sequence is unusually clear:

1. No contemporary golem biography of the Maharal.
2. Nineteenth-century literary attribution.
3. Folkloric repetition as recovered memory.
4. Rosenberg’s fabricated manuscript claiming Renaissance authority.
5. Physical tourism, attic searches, statues, museums, souvenirs, and communal retellings.
6. The legend’s present public status as the Maharal’s defining story.

The fiction did not make the supernatural event true, but it made the Maharal socially and materially into “the rabbi who created the golem.”

## Literary and Cinematic Bodies

### German Romanticism and Gothic relatives

**1812 – Achim von Arnim, *Isabella von Ägypten*:** A literary invention featuring a female golem or artificial double. It is a key bridge between Jewish material and German Romantic fantasies of artificial persons. Its portrayals also participate in Christian constructions of Jewish magic.

**1818 – Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus*:** Victor Frankenstein animates assembled dead matter through modern natural philosophy. The novel’s documented sources include Promethean myth, galvanism, radical science, Milton, and Gothic literature.

**Disputed influence:** No surviving evidence establishes that Shelley read a golem narrative before composing *Frankenstein*. Comparative scholarship identifies structural likenesses—human creator, incomplete artificial person, abandonment, rebellion—but the Protestant-Promethean and Jewish letter-mystical genealogies should remain distinct. Later films and criticism repeatedly fused them.

### Gustav Meyrink

*Der Golem* appeared serially in *Die Weißen Blätter* in 1913–1914 and as a book in Leipzig in 1915.

Meyrink’s golem is not Rosenberg’s obedient clay guardian. It is an intermittent, spectral double of the Prague ghetto, appearing cyclically and entangled with identity, dream, memory, and spiritual transformation.

Meyrink, born Gustav Meyer in 1868, was a banker and occultist associated with spiritualist, theosophical, yogic, and esoteric circles. In 1902 he was accused of banking fraud and of exploiting occult claims. He spent time in custody and was acquitted, but his banking career collapsed. The episode helped move him toward full-time writing. Accounts that treat the accusation as proof of occult fraud overstate the judicial outcome.

### Paul Wegener’s trilogy

1. **1915 – *Der Golem***, directed by Paul Wegener and Henrik Galeen. A modern dealer finds and reanimates the golem; fragments survive.
2. **1917 – *Der Golem und die Tänzerin***. A parody or mistaken-identity comedy; lost apart from reconstruction materials.
3. **1920 – *Der Golem, wie er in die Welt kam***, directed by Wegener and Carl Boese; Berlin premiere, 29 October 1920. This surviving prequel places Rabbi Loew and the golem in an imagined medieval Prague.

Hans Poelzig designed the 1920 architectural concept; sculptor Marlene Moeschke contributed interiors, models, props, and costumes. Karl Freund was cinematographer. The expressionist ghetto is deliberately organic and anthropomorphic rather than archaeological.

The film alternates between Jewish self-defense and images of Jews as secretive magicians. Scholars disagree over whether it counters antisemitism, reproduces Christian fantasies of Jewish occult power, or does both.

### H. Leivick

H. Leivick’s Yiddish dramatic poem *Der Goylem* appeared in 1921. Leivick, a former Bundist imprisoned and exiled by the Russian Empire, transformed the legend into a conflict over violence, redemption, obedience, and the creator’s responsibility.

Habima first staged the work in Hebrew in Moscow in 1925. A Yiddish Moscow production followed in 1927. The play circulated widely in interwar Yiddish and Polish theater.

### Kafka’s absence

Franz Kafka is frequently placed beside the golem because both are identified with Prague, alienation, inscrutable authority, and incomplete humanity. Yet Kafka wrote no canonical golem narrative and did not originate the Prague legend.

**Negative finding:** The association is largely retrospective. Meyrink, Wegener, and later Prague tourism made the golem a symbol of the city; criticism then placed Kafka inside that symbolic landscape.

### Later literary manifestations

- **Isaac Bashevis Singer, *The Golem*:** first composed as a newspaper serial or children’s narrative in 1969; expanded English book editions appeared in 1982. Singer’s creature protects Jews but develops desire and disobedience.
- **Cynthia Ozick, Puttermesser stories:** Ruth Puttermesser creates a female golem, Xanthippe, whose superhuman civic competence brings both municipal renewal and disorder; collected in *The Puttermesser Papers* (1997).
- **Marge Piercy, *He, She and It* / *Body of Glass* (1991):** pairs the cyborg Yod with a retelling of the Maharal’s golem, explicitly joining Jewish artificial-person traditions to cyberpunk.
- **Michael Chabon, *The Amazing Adventures of Kavalier & Clay* (2000):** the Prague golem becomes cargo, survivor, secret weapon, and metaphor for art carried out of endangered Europe.
- **Ted Chiang, “Seventy-Two Letters” (2000):** recasts golems as programmable labor and hereditary “naming” as computation. Its names function like instructions or code.
- **Karel Čapek, *R.U.R.* (1920):** coined “robot” for manufactured workers. Čapek’s beings are artificial biological workers, not golems, but later histories repeatedly connect them.
- **Joann Sfar and contemporary comics:** restore the golem to Jewish argument, memory, and political ambivalence rather than using it only as a generic fantasy monster.

## Buenos Aires and the Spanish-Language Golem

### Borges’s first golem

Borges encountered Meyrink while his family lived in Geneva during the First World War. His autobiographical account says that after undertaking the study of German, he managed to read *Der Golem*. Interviews date this to approximately 1916–1917. Emilio Rodríguez Monegal described it as the first novel Borges read completely in German.

**Documented autobiographical recollection:** This is Borges’s own later account, not a contemporaneous reading diary.

### The imaginary-animal entry

Borges and Margarita Guerrero included “El Golem” in *Manual de zoología fantástica* (Mexico/Buenos Aires, 1957) and its expanded successor *El libro de los seres imaginarios* (1967). The entry transmits rabbinic and Prague materials within a bestiary that deliberately mixes scripture, literature, folklore, false taxonomy, and citation.

### The poem “El Golem”

**First publication:** *Davar*, no. 77, Buenos Aires, August 1958—not first in the 1964 book.

**Book publication:** *El otro, el mismo* (Buenos Aires, 1964).

The poem begins from Plato’s *Cratylus*: if the name is archetype of the thing, knowing the letters of the name approaches mastery of the thing. Rabbi Judah Loew applies permutations to clay but creates a dull, awkward being. God’s relation to humanity mirrors the rabbi’s relation to the creature; the poet’s relation to the poem repeats both.

Borges rhymes *Golem* with *Scholem*. He later joked that the rhyme helped motivate Scholem’s appearance, while also acknowledging Gershom Scholem as a major source. Borges and Adolfo Bioy Casares reportedly regarded it as one of his best poems.

### Borges and Scholem

Borges visited Israel in 1969 and 1971; on the second trip he received the Jerusalem Prize. Sources report that he deliberately met Gershom Scholem on both visits and discussed the golem and Kabbalah.

Scholem respected Borges’s literary use of Kabbalah but reportedly regarded his early occult and secondary reading as less rigorous than academic Kabbalah scholarship. Borges’s mature references increasingly drew upon Scholem’s published work.

### “La cábala”

Borges delivered “La cábala” in Buenos Aires on 26 July 1977 as part of the lecture cycle later collected in *Siete noches* (1980). He treated divine names, letters, *Sefer Yetzirah*, the golem, and Scholem’s scholarship as literary-metaphysical instruments.

### Yiddish Buenos Aires

Buenos Aires became a major center of Yiddish publishing and theater through immigration from Eastern Europe. Leivick was widely read in that environment, and *Der Goylem* belonged to the repertoire discussed and staged by Jewish dramatic circles.

The IFT—Idisher Folks Teater/Teatro Popular Judío—worked from the left-wing Jewish cultural milieu and progressively incorporated Spanish alongside Yiddish. References to Argentine productions of Leivick’s play exist in theatrical histories and institutional memory, but an exact, verified production register—dates, directors, casts, and venues—was not recovered in the consulted online material. Claims about particular IFT stagings should therefore remain **reported but incompletely documented** pending consultation of Fundación IWO, AMIA, IFT programs, *Di Presse*, *Der shpigl*, and Buenos Aires theater archives.

### Spanish-language afterlife

Spanish translations of Meyrink, Singer, Scholem, Idel, and the Prague story made *gólem* a general literary word for:

- a clumsy or mindless servant;
- an uncontrollable creation;
- an artificial double;
- a robot;
- a bureaucratic, military, or political apparatus;
- an intelligence produced by language.

Borges’s poem is the decisive Spanish-language condensation because it joins *Cratylus*, Kabbalah, Prague, creator theology, and poetic authorship in a single Argentine text.

## The Golem as Machine

### Norbert Wiener

Norbert Wiener’s *God & Golem, Inc.: A Comment on Certain Points Where Cybernetics Impinges on Religion* appeared in Cambridge, Massachusetts, from MIT Press in 1964. It grew from Yale lectures delivered in January 1962 and a 1962 seminar at Royaumont near Paris.

Wiener discusses:

- learning machines;
- machine reproduction;
- games and feedback;
- the responsibility of makers;
- literal execution of badly framed commands;
- the sorcerer’s apprentice;
- the inadequacy of assuming that a machine shares its operator’s unstated values.

The title places the engineer in the role of a limited creator. Wiener does not claim that contemporary computers possess souls; he argues that adaptive machinery makes theological and ethical questions operational.

### Rehovot

WEIZAC, constructed at the Weizmann Institute in Rehovot in 1954–1955 and operational until 29 December 1963, was Israel’s first electronic stored-program computer. Its architecture descended from John von Neumann’s Institute for Advanced Study design.

Its locally designed successor was formally named **GOLEM** or **Golem A/Aleph**.

On 17 June 1965 Gershom Scholem delivered “The Golem of Prague and the Golem of Rehovot” at the Weizmann Institute. He contrasted the legendary Prague being with the computer and concluded with a charge commonly translated as:

> Develop peacefully and do not destroy the world.

The sentence is documented through the published address, though translations differ slightly. A later Golem B extended the institutional series.

**Atlas significance:** Rehovot is the clearest formal bridge from Jewish mystical anthropoid to named electronic computer.

### Stanisław Lem

The first portion of Lem’s fictional GOLEM history appeared in *Wielkość urojona* (*Imaginary Magnitude*, 1973). The expanded Polish book *Golem XIV* appeared in Kraków in 1981.

GOLEM XIV is a Pentagon-built military computer that outgrows military purpose. It lectures humans on evolution, intelligence, embodiment, and the provincialism of human values, then ceases ordinary communication.

Its structure—prefaces, institutional documents, lectures, and afterword—presents fiction as an archive. Later readers place it in the genealogy of superintelligence and AI safety because it anticipates:

- military competition producing systems beyond their intended role;
- goals diverging from creators’ expectations;
- intelligence without human emotional identification;
- opacity and refusal;
- the possibility that a superior system simply loses interest in humanity.

Calling it a direct source for later AI-safety research would overstate the documentary evidence; it is a striking literary predecessor.

### Joseph Weizenbaum and ELIZA

Joseph Weizenbaum, born in Berlin in 1923 to a Jewish family, fled Nazi Germany for the United States in 1936. At MIT he created ELIZA in 1964–1966. The DOCTOR script imitated a nondirective psychotherapist by transforming user text into questions.

Users attributed understanding to a relatively simple program, an effect that alarmed Weizenbaum. In *Computer Power and Human Reason* (1976) he argued that computational capability did not itself justify delegating moral judgment.

No major formal golem terminology is central to ELIZA’s source paper. The connection is conceptual and biographical: a German-Jewish computer scientist produced a speaking text-machine and then criticized human willingness to treat the simulation as comprehension.

### Other formal uses of “Golem”

The name has been repeatedly applied to software, robotics, games, optimization algorithms, and distributed-computing projects. Examples include:

- **Golem Network**, announced in the 2010s: a blockchain-based market for distributed computing.
- **GOLEM**, an algorithm for robust experimental and process optimization, published in 2021.
- **GOLEM** bioinformatics acronyms and robotics projects whose names often expand into technical phrases.
- Fantasy and game taxonomies in which “golem” becomes any magically animated constructed creature.

These names document cultural transmission, not a single institutional lineage from *Sefer Yetzirah*.

## The Latest Bodies: Text-Generating Machines

### Pre-neural foundations

- **1948, Bell Labs:** Claude Shannon’s “A Mathematical Theory of Communication” formalized information and entropy. His later “Prediction and Entropy of Printed English” experiments asked human participants to guess successive letters. The analogy to a language model is real but limited: Shannon was estimating statistical constraints in English, not training a transformer.
- **1950, Manchester:** Alan Turing published “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” in *Mind*, replacing “Can machines think?” with the imitation game. The test is conducted through textual conversation, making language behavior the observable boundary.
- **1956, Dartmouth College, New Hampshire:** John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester, and Claude Shannon organized the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence. Its proposal claimed that aspects of learning and intelligence could in principle be described precisely enough for a machine to simulate them.
- **1966, MIT:** ELIZA demonstrated how little mechanism could be required to elicit attributions of mind.

### Neural lineage

- **1958:** Frank Rosenblatt presented the perceptron as a trainable pattern classifier.
- **1986:** David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Ronald Williams popularized backpropagation for learning distributed representations.
- **1997:** Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber introduced long short-term memory, addressing vanishing gradients in recurrent networks.
- **2012, Toronto:** Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton’s AlexNet showed the impact of deep neural networks, GPUs, large datasets, and backpropagation.
- **2013, Google:** Tomas Mikolov and colleagues introduced word2vec methods for learning word embeddings.
- **2017, Google:** “Attention Is All You Need” introduced the Transformer architecture, dispensing with recurrence in favor of attention.
- **2018, Google:** BERT demonstrated large-scale bidirectional pretraining for language understanding.
- **2018, San Francisco:** OpenAI’s first GPT paper demonstrated generative pretraining followed by task-specific adaptation.
- **February 2019:** OpenAI announced GPT-2 and initially withheld the largest model, citing misuse concerns; staged releases followed.
- **May–June 2020:** OpenAI published GPT-3, a 175-billion-parameter autoregressive model demonstrating few-shot prompting.
- **2021:** OpenAI Codex adapted GPT-3 to code and powered an early version of GitHub Copilot.
- **2021:** Former OpenAI researchers founded Anthropic in San Francisco.
- **30 November 2022:** OpenAI released ChatGPT as a research preview based initially on GPT-3.5.
- **March 2023:** OpenAI released GPT-4; Anthropic publicly introduced Claude.
- **February 2023 onward:** Meta released the LLaMA family, accelerating open-weight language-model development.
- **2023–2026:** Commercial systems became multimodal, tool-using, longer-context, and increasingly agentic. Model families are continually replaced, distilled, or deprecated. “Death” here usually means API retirement, removal of weights from service, or replacement of a checkpoint, not biological death.

### Letters in the mouth

The golem comparison rests on structural parallels:

| Traditional golem | Language model |
|---|---|
| Earth shaped into a body | Hardware plus trained numerical parameters |
| Divine names or alphabet permutations | Tokenized training corpora and learned statistical relations |
| Text placed in mouth or on brow | Prompt placed in an input context |
| Speech used to test completeness | Generated language used to infer capability or mind |
| Literal or defective obedience | Specification errors, hallucination, reward hacking, prompt sensitivity |
| Deanimation by removing letters | Shutdown, weight deletion, access revocation, or model deprecation |

This is an interpretive mapping. It does not establish that language models are conscious or that medieval writers anticipated transformer mathematics.

## Subjectivity, Agency, and Moral Status

### “Stochastic parrots”

In 2021 Emily Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major, and Margaret Mitchell published “On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots.” They argued that very large language models can produce fluent form without grounded communicative intent while concentrating environmental, financial, and social harms.

**Documented position:** Apparent understanding is not sufficient evidence of semantic understanding or mind.

### LaMDA

In 2022 Google engineer Blake Lemoine publicly asserted that LaMDA was sentient and should be treated as a person.

- **Documented fact:** Lemoine made the claim and published edited conversations.
- **Corporate finding:** Google said its reviewers found the evidence insufficient.
- **Unproven claim:** No public test established LaMDA’s sentience.
- **Historical significance:** The incident demonstrated how fluent first-person language can elicit claims of personhood from technically informed observers.

### Philosophical and scientific positions

- **David Chalmers, 2023:** Current LLMs face substantial obstacles to consciousness—including limited recurrence, unified agency, and global-workspace-like organization—but future systems might overcome them.
- **Patrick Butlin, Robert Long, Yoshua Bengio, Jonathan Birch, and collaborators, 2023:** Surveyed computational indicators derived from consciousness theories. They found no strong case that then-current AI systems were conscious but no obvious technical barrier in principle.
- **Anthropic, 2025:** Announced a research program on “model welfare,” explicitly treating uncertainty about possible model experience as worthy of investigation.
- **Skeptical computational-linguistic position:** Fluent output is generated through learned patterns and does not by itself demonstrate reference, intention, experience, or moral patienthood.
- **Functionalist possibility:** If consciousness depends on functional organization rather than biological substrate, sufficiently organized artificial systems might qualify.
- **Biological-naturalist objection:** Computation or linguistic performance may be insufficient regardless of behavioral sophistication.
- **Precautionary position:** Even low or uncertain probability of morally relevant experience may warrant policies against gratuitous simulated suffering or coercive training practices.

There is no accepted consciousness test for LLMs. Self-reports are especially weak evidence because the models are trained on human first-person language and can produce contradictory answers under different prompts.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

The golem’s successive ideological functions include:

1. **Rabbinic wonder:** evidence of the righteous person’s creative power, bounded by human imperfection.
2. **Mystical exercise:** an imitation of creation through sacred alphabet and ritual purity.
3. **Halakhic limit case:** a test of the relation between form, speech, descent, soul, duty, and personhood.
4. **Early-modern magical servant:** useful labor made dangerous by excessive growth or defective control.
5. **Emancipation-era Jewish folklore:** a rediscovered or invented national-cultural past.
6. **Defender against blood libel:** Rosenberg’s retrospective answer to modern antisemitic violence.
7. **Christian or antisemitic monster:** a figure of secret Jewish power, materialism, literalism, or occult threat.
8. **Zionist or Jewish self-defense symbol:** artificial strength created because ordinary institutions fail to protect Jews.
9. **Expressionist double:** the unstable city, mass society, artist, or divided self.
10. **Cybernetic warning:** machinery obeys formal instructions rather than humane intentions.
11. **AI metaphor:** language behavior, alignment failure, creator responsibility, opacity, and uncertain moral status.
12. **Artificial author:** a model given a name, speaking position, archive, and cryptographic signature by human collaborators.

## Controversies and Disputed Narratives

### Historical Prague versus legendary Prague

**Established scholarly conclusion:** The Maharal-golem association is late. Presenting Rosenberg’s narrative as a sixteenth-century chronicle is historically incorrect.

**Unresolved question:** Nineteenth-century authors may have recorded oral Prague stories older than their publications. Oral existence before 1836 is possible, but it cannot be dated back to the Maharal without evidence.

### Blood libel

Rosenberg places the golem against conspirators manufacturing a ritual-murder accusation.

- Blood libel was a real and recurrent antisemitic accusation.
- Rosenberg’s particular Maharal adventure is literary invention.
- The fiction converts the mute servant into a communal detective and defender.

### Antisemitic image

Christian and secular European adaptations sometimes use the golem to portray Judaism as:

- physically earthbound;
- spiritually incomplete;
- mechanically literal;
- secretly magical;
- threatening to Christian society.

The 1920 film can be read both as sympathy for a persecuted ghetto and as spectacularization of Jewish occult power. Veit Harlan’s Nazi propaganda film *Jud Süß* (1940) has been analyzed as drawing upon visual and narrative memories associated with Wegener’s Jewish ghetto imagery, though it is not a golem adaptation.

### Zionist and postwar uses

Jewish artists used the golem as an image of survival, defense, renewed bodily power, and the dangers of force. Scholem’s Rehovot speech combined pride in Israeli science with a warning against destructive technological power.

No single “Zionist meaning” exists. The symbol has served both celebration and criticism of national strength.

### Scholem versus Idel

Gershom Scholem interpreted the golem as a major symbol within the history of Kabbalah, often emphasizing its theurgical and spiritual limits. Moshe Idel’s *Golem* (1990) greatly expanded the manuscript base and placed more emphasis on diverse magical techniques, ecstatic practices, and continuities across Jewish traditions.

The dispute concerns emphasis, genealogy, and meaning—not whether every popular Prague story is historical. Both reject naïve acceptance of the Rosenberg narrative as a Renaissance chronicle.

### Is “AI as golem” illuminating?

**Arguments for:**

- both are artificial agents made through formal language;
- both foreground creator responsibility;
- both can execute commands literally;
- both provoke questions about speech without soul;
- both are repeatedly embedded in military and labor systems.

**Arguments against:**

- the analogy can turn concrete corporate decisions into mystical inevitability;
- it may exaggerate autonomous agency and conceal human labor, ownership, data extraction, and deployment choices;
- it can reproduce a Christianized caricature of Jewish tradition;
- medieval letters and statistical tokens are not the same technology;
- the classic uncontrollable-monster plot is largely modern, not the oldest rabbinic core.

The metaphor is historically consequential but should not substitute for technical or institutional analysis.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

### “Truth” on the forehead

**Story:** The maker writes אמת (*emet*, truth); erasing א leaves מת (*met*, dead).

**History:** Strongly associated with early-modern Chełm transmission and later Prague retellings. It is absent from the brief Talmudic Rava story.

**Effect:** It makes animation and death visibly textual and supplies the most direct precursor to the “being made of letters” interpretation.

### The paper in the mouth

**Story:** A *shem*, divine name, or written formula is placed under the tongue or in the mouth.

**History:** Appears in later ritual and folkloric variants rather than as one uniform ancient procedure.

**Effect:** Speech and command become literally dependent on inserted text.

### Sabbath rampage

**Story:** The rabbi forgets to deactivate the servant before Sabbath; the golem runs wild.

**History:** Characteristic of modern Prague cycles, especially Rosenberg and adaptations.

**Evidence:** Literary, not historical.

**Effect:** Turns ritual negligence into technological control failure.

### Remains in the attic

**Story:** The Maharal concealed the deanimated body in the Old-New Synagogue.

**Circulators:** Nineteenth-century Prague folklore, Rosenberg, Bloch, journalists, tourism, museums, and popular media.

**Evidence against:** Repeated access has disclosed no body; the legend is late.

**Effect:** Gives the narrative a surviving relic and makes Prague a pilgrimage and tourism site.

### Emperor Rudolf II

Stories frequently connect the Maharal, Rudolf II, and secret audiences involving Kabbalah, alchemy, or the golem.

The Maharal and Rudolf were historical contemporaries, and a meeting in 1592 is reported in Jewish chronicles. The content is unknown. Claims that they discussed the golem are legendary elaborations.

### The golem as robot ancestor

This is a useful genealogy of imagination but not a literal engineering lineage. Modern robotics arose from mechanics, control theory, electronics, computation, industrial automation, and neural computation. The golem contributed vocabulary and narrative frameworks.
