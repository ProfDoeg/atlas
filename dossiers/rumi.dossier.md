# Jalal al-Din Rumi: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Limits

Jalal al-Din Rumi lived in the thirteenth century. No contemporary administrative dossier, autobiography, continuous diary, court record, or independently verified eyewitness biography survives. His life must be reconstructed from:

1. His own datable or approximately datable poetry, prose discourses, sermons, and correspondence.
2. The *Maʿārif* of his father, Baha al-Din Walad.
3. The *Maqālāt* or discourses of Shams al-Din Tabrizi.
4. Sultan Walad’s *Ibtidā-nāma/Walad-nāma*, written by Rumi’s son approximately two decades after Rumi’s death.
5. Faridun Sepahsalar’s early-fourteenth-century *Risāla*, whose author claimed long personal service to Rumi.
6. Shams al-Din Ahmad Aflaki’s *Manāqib al-ʿārifīn*, assembled approximately 1318–53: indispensable but explicitly hagiographical and frequently miraculous.
7. Later Mevlevi tradition.
8. Modern philological and historical scholarship.

Statements below are therefore distinguished as:

- **Documented fact:** supported by Rumi’s writings, dated manuscripts, contemporary or near-contemporary documentary evidence.
- **Reported fact:** found in early narrative sources and considered plausible, but not independently documented.
- **Disputed reconstruction:** competing modern interpretations of incomplete evidence.
- **Hagiography, legend, or myth:** a spiritually or symbolically important narrative whose literal historicity is unproved or improbable.
- **Modern allegation or interpretation:** a recent reading not established by direct historical evidence.

A central methodological problem is that Aflaki wrote to demonstrate sanctity, not to produce a modern critical biography. His stories preserve names, locations, memories, social tensions, and early community traditions, but miracles and precisely staged encounters cannot automatically be treated as literal history. [Encyclopaedia Iranica’s biography](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/rumi-jalal-al-din/) and Franklin Lewis’s historical reassessment are the principal modern controls used here.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

Rumi’s father was **Baha al-Din Muhammad Walad**, commonly called **Baha al-Din Walad**, a Persian-speaking preacher, jurist, and religious teacher. His writings survive as the *Maʿārif*, a collection of sermons, reflections, and spiritual observations. His title **Sultan al-ʿUlamaʾ**, “Sultan of the Scholars,” appears prominently in later tradition.

Baha al-Din’s own writing indicates residence and activity in Vakhsh. He was not demonstrably the universally preeminent scholar depicted by later hagiography, but he had a following and the social authority to preach, issue legal opinions, teach, and move within learned networks. His thought combined Qurʾanic interpretation, preaching, asceticism, and intensely personal contemplation of God. It provided Rumi with both a scholarly inheritance and a vocabulary of inward religious experience. See the [Encyclopaedia Iranica biography of Baha al-Din Walad](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/baha-al-din-mohammad-walad-b/).

The paternal grandfather is usually given as **Husayn ibn Ahmad al-Khatibi**, from a family associated with preaching. Later genealogies traced Rumi’s paternal line to the first caliph, **Abu Bakr**.

- **Hagiographical or dynastic claim:** descent from Abu Bakr.
- **Evidence:** the claim occurs in early Mevlevi lineage tradition, including works associated with Sultan Walad and Aflaki.
- **Assessment:** no contemporary genealogy independently verifies the full chain. It should be recorded as a lineage tradition, not demonstrated biological fact.

### Maternal ancestry

Rumi’s mother is commonly called **Muʾmina Khatun**. She reportedly accompanied the family west and died at Laranda, later Karaman.

Later narratives connected her or Baha al-Din’s family with the Khwarazmshahs, sometimes making Rumi’s grandmother a princess or daughter of a ruler.

- **Hagiographical claim:** royal Khwarazmian maternal ancestry.
- **Evidence against:** chronological and genealogical inconsistencies; absence from the earliest secure documentation.
- **Assessment:** modern specialists generally reject or suspend judgment on the royal genealogy. It helped later authors present Rumi as combining spiritual, scholarly, caliphal, and royal legitimacy.

### Siblings

A brother is not securely documented as surviving into Rumi’s adult circle. A sister named **ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn/ʿAlāʾ Khatun** or related forms appears in some later accounts, but the evidence is much thinner than for Rumi’s wives and children. Claims about siblings should be treated cautiously.

### Marriages and children

#### Gowhar Khatun

Rumi’s first wife was **Gowhar/Gawhar Khatun**, reportedly the daughter of Khwaja Sharaf al-Din of Samarkand or a family connected with Rumi’s migrant community.

- The marriage is usually placed at **Laranda around 1225**.
- She was the mother of Rumi’s two best-documented sons:
  - **Baha al-Din Muhammad Sultan Walad**, born around 1226.
  - **ʿAlaʾ al-Din Muhammad**, born around 1228.
- Gowhar died comparatively young, at an uncertain date.

A recent study of women among the early Mevlevis emphasizes how little Rumi himself says directly about his wives and how heavily their biographies depend on Sultan Walad, Sepahsalar, and Aflaki. See [Brill’s study of Rumi and the early Mevlevis](https://brill.com/view/journals/mrr/8/1/article-p43_3.pdf).

#### Kira Khatun

Rumi subsequently married **Kira/Gira Khatun**, described in early tradition as learned and notably beautiful. Her name may reflect a Christian, Greek, or Anatolian background, but attempts to fix her original religious or ethnic identity are uncertain.

Children usually attributed to this marriage are:

- **Muzaffar al-Din Amir ʿAlim**, a son.
- **Malika Khatun**, a daughter.

Sources differ over details, chronology, and whether all named children were biological rather than household or step-relations. No reliable evidence identifies extramarital partners.

### Descendants

Sultan Walad became the principal organizer and transmitter of the family’s religious legacy. He married **Fatima Khatun**, daughter of Rumi’s close associate Salah al-Din Zarkub. Sultan Walad’s descendants furnished much of the later hereditary leadership of the Mevlevi order.

ʿAlaʾ al-Din’s later reputation was damaged by Aflaki’s story that he participated in Shams’s murder. That allegation is examined separately below.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1207–c. 1220

### Birthplace dispute

The traditional formulation makes Rumi a native of **Balkh**, one of the principal Persianate cities of Khurasan. Modern research by Fritz Meier and Franklin Lewis argues that Baha al-Din lived in **Vakhsh** between approximately 1204 and 1210, covering Rumi’s birth year. On this reconstruction, Rumi was born in or near Vakhsh, possibly around medieval Lewkand/Lavakand or Sangtuda, southeast of modern Dushanbe.

Evidence for Vakhsh includes Baha al-Din’s own *Maʿārif*, which locates him there, and early traditions describing him in conflict with the qadi of Vakhsh. A convenient presentation of the textual evidence is [Ibrahim Gamard’s Vakhsh dossier](https://www.dar-al-masnavi.org/wakhsh.html).

Competing positions are:

- **Traditional reported birthplace:** Balkh city, now Afghanistan.
- **Modern historical reconstruction:** Vakhsh, now Tajikistan.
- **Mediating formulation:** born within the broader cultural world of Balkh, to a Balkhi family, while his father was resident in Vakhsh.
- **Unresolved point:** no birth certificate or contemporary colophon gives the place.

A 2023-era Persian-language scholarly argument has renewed the case for Balkh, while other specialists retain Vakhsh. National cultural claims by Afghanistan, Tajikistan, Iran, and Turkey have made the question politically charged beyond what the surviving evidence can resolve.

### Vakhsh and Samarkand

Baha al-Din’s family appears to have left Vakhsh around **1210–12**. Rumi later recalled childhood in **Samarkand**, including warfare and displacement. The family may have moved there shortly before the Khwarazmian conquest.

The migration did not necessarily begin as one uninterrupted flight from Genghis Khan. Some popular biographies compress multiple moves into a single escape from the Mongols. Baha al-Din’s writings indicate dissatisfaction, professional mobility, and regional instability before the Mongol conquest reached its full destructive phase.

### Why the family left

Later hagiography gives a dramatic conflict between Baha al-Din and:

- the philosopher-theologian **Fakhr al-Din Razi**;
- the Khwarazmshah **Muhammad II**;
- rationalist or court scholars jealous of Baha al-Din.

In these accounts the ruler and Razi forced, or induced, Baha al-Din to leave Balkh.

- **Hagiographical narrative:** persecuted saint departs after confronting worldly intellectual and political authority.
- **Historical problems:** Fakhr al-Din Razi’s chronology, Baha al-Din’s documented residence in Vakhsh, and the absence of contemporary corroboration make the full confrontation doubtful.
- **Plausible background:** competition among preachers, jurists, theologians, and court-connected scholars; political instability; Khwarazmian expansion; later fear of Mongol invasion.
- **Reputational function:** the story portrays Rumi’s father as morally superior to rulers and rationalist theologians.

## Migration West

The itinerary is incompletely documented, and early sources transform it into a pilgrimage narrative. Places associated with the journey include:

- Vakhsh and Samarkand.
- Balkh or greater Khurasan.
- Nishapur.
- Baghdad.
- The Hijaz, including Mecca.
- Syria.
- Malatya.
- Erzincan or Akşehir.
- Laranda, now Karaman.
- Konya.

Not every stop can be independently established.

### The alleged meeting with Farid al-Din Attar

Later tradition says that at **Nishapur**, the elderly poet **Farid al-Din Attar** met the young Rumi, recognized his greatness, gave him a copy of the *Asrār-nāma*, and, seeing the boy follow his father, remarked that a sea was following an ocean.

- **Story source:** later Mevlevi hagiography.
- **Chronological possibility:** possible if the family passed Nishapur before Attar’s death, traditionally placed around 1221.
- **Evidence:** no contemporary record; Sultan Walad does not provide a securely detailed eyewitness account.
- **Assessment:** possible but unproved.
- **Historical significance:** Rumi’s own poetry explicitly reveres Attar, so literary influence is documented even if a personal meeting is not.

### Baghdad and Mecca

Aflaki and other biographers place Baha al-Din’s party in Baghdad and on pilgrimage to Mecca. Stories include recognition by famous scholars and prophetic announcements of Rumi’s future.

- **Reported journey:** Baghdad and pilgrimage are plausible for a learned migrant household.
- **Uncertain chronology:** dates and sequence differ.
- **Hagiographical additions:** instantaneous recognition of sanctity, miraculous insight, and formal deference by eminent figures.

### Anatolian settlement

The family spent several years in **Laranda**, under a local ruler’s protection. Here Rumi married Gowhar Khatun and began his adult household. His mother is traditionally buried there.

Around **1228–29**, Baha al-Din accepted an invitation to **Konya**, capital of the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum, during the reign of **ʿAlaʾ al-Din Kayqubad I**. The family entered a cosmopolitan city populated by Persian-speaking administrators and scholars, Turkish military and nomadic groups, Arabic-speaking religious scholars, Greek Christians, Armenians, Jews, merchants, refugees, and craftsmen.

## Education and Formation

### Education under Baha al-Din Walad

Rumi’s earliest education came from his father and the scholarly household around him. The curriculum almost certainly included:

- Qurʾan and Qurʾanic exegesis.
- Arabic grammar and rhetoric.
- Hadith.
- Hanafi jurisprudence.
- Theology.
- Persian ethical and mystical literature.
- Preaching and disputation.

Baha al-Din’s *Maʿārif* shaped Rumi’s religious vocabulary. Rumi later taught and interpreted his father’s work.

### Baha al-Din’s death

Baha al-Din died in Konya, conventionally dated **18 Rabiʿ II 628 AH / 12 January 1231**. Rumi, in his early twenties, inherited some combination of his father’s teaching position, congregation, students, and social obligations.

The institutional arrangement should not be imagined as a modern salaried professorship. Medieval religious teaching operated through mosques, madrasas, endowed properties, patronage, gifts, fees, and household networks.

### Burhan al-Din Muhaqqiq Tirmidhi

**Sayyid Burhan al-Din Muhaqqiq Tirmidhi**, an associate or disciple of Baha al-Din, came to Konya after the latter’s death. He became Rumi’s principal spiritual trainer before Shams.

Reported elements of this period include:

- Intensive study of Baha al-Din’s *Maʿārif*.
- Ascetic exercises.
- Periods of forty-day retreat.
- Fasting, prayer, and spiritual discipline.
- Testing by Burhan al-Din before authorization to teach independently.

Burhan al-Din is essential because the popular “before and after Shams” story can falsely suggest that Rumi had no mystical formation before 1244. In fact, years of religious scholarship and disciplined Sufi training preceded Shams.

### Aleppo and Damascus

Rumi traveled to **Aleppo** and **Damascus**, probably during the 1230s, for advanced study. Institutions and teachers named in early accounts include:

- the Halawiyya madrasa in Aleppo;
- **Kamal al-Din Ibn al-ʿAdim**, a major Aleppine scholar and historian;
- learned circles in Damascus.

The duration is disputed, with estimates ranging from several years to a longer combined period. He probably studied jurisprudence, hadith, Arabic, and theology.

### Alleged meeting with Ibn Arabi

Rumi and the Andalusian Sufi philosopher **Ibn Arabi** were both connected with Damascus. Later stories claim Ibn Arabi saw the young Rumi walking behind Baha al-Din and declared, “Glory be to God—an ocean walking behind a sea.”

- **Legend:** direct recognition of Rumi by Ibn Arabi.
- **Chronological problem:** Baha al-Din’s presence with Rumi in Damascus at the required time is uncertain.
- **No secure contemporary documentation:** neither Rumi nor Ibn Arabi records the encounter.
- **Intellectual relation:** Rumi inhabited a world increasingly influenced by Ibn Arabi, especially through **Sadr al-Din Qunawi** in Konya, but direct doctrinal dependence is debated.

### Completion of Burhan al-Din’s training

Burhan al-Din later lived at Kayseri and died around **1240–41**. Rumi visited his grave. By the early 1240s Rumi was an established scholar, preacher, and spiritual teacher in Konya.

## Early Career in Konya

Before meeting Shams, Rumi appears to have led the recognizable life of a successful religious scholar:

- delivering sermons;
- teaching students;
- issuing or interpreting religious guidance;
- maintaining relations with rulers and administrators;
- leading a household and circle of disciples;
- participating in devotional practice;
- receiving patronage or endowed institutional support.

His early public image was not that of an antinomian wandering poet. It was that of a formally educated Muslim jurist and preacher with an existing mystical formation.

The later image of Shams causing Rumi to “abandon religion” is therefore misleading. Shams transformed Rumi’s mode of expression, emotional life, teaching relationships, and use of music and poetry; he did not introduce an irreligious alternative to Islam.

## Chronological Life History

## 1244: Meeting with Shams al-Din Tabrizi

**Shams al-Din Muhammad of Tabriz** arrived in Konya in late 1244. A traditional exact date is **26 Jumada II 642 AH / 29 November 1244**.

Shams was an itinerant Persian-speaking religious seeker and teacher. His surviving *Maqālāt* portrays an intellectually forceful, provocative figure who criticized conventional piety, spiritual pretension, famous Sufis, and scholars who lacked direct experience.

### Competing meeting stories

Aflaki and other sources preserve several versions:

1. Shams stopped Rumi’s horse and asked whether Muhammad or the Sufi Bayazid Bistami was greater.
2. Shams threw Rumi’s books into water and restored them dry.
3. Shams asked what Rumi could not understand through books.
4. The two immediately recognized one another and entered prolonged seclusion.

- **Documented core:** Shams and Rumi met in Konya; the relationship profoundly changed Rumi’s writing and social conduct.
- **Hagiographical staging:** the book miracle, perfectly formulated first question, and instantaneous public recognition have multiple variants.
- **Function:** these stories dramatize the subordination of book learning to lived knowledge.

### The retreat

Rumi and Shams reportedly spent weeks or months in intense private conversation. Forty days and three months are both given. Rumi withdrew from some ordinary teaching and family obligations. Students and relatives resented Shams’s access and influence.

Shams’s own discourses indicate intimacy combined with argument, instruction, rebuke, and mutual testing. The relationship was not simply conventional teacher-to-student hierarchy. Each source frames authority differently.

### Music, dance, and poetry

Rumi increasingly practiced **samāʿ**, attentive listening to music and poetry accompanied by bodily movement. Later Mevlevi tradition systematized the whirling ceremony, but the fully codified Ottoman ritual should not be projected unchanged into Rumi’s lifetime.

The early accounts depict spontaneous turning, ecstatic movement, recitation, and response to rhythmic sounds—including, in one famous story, the hammering in Salah al-Din Zarkub’s goldsmith workshop.

## 1246–47: Shams’s First Departure and Return

Hostility from Rumi’s students or household is said to have driven Shams from Konya to **Damascus** around 1246. Rumi wrote or sent messages asking him to return. Sultan Walad reportedly traveled to Damascus and escorted Shams back respectfully on foot.

To stabilize Shams’s place in the household, Shams married **Kimya Khatun**, a young woman raised in Rumi’s household, sometimes described as Rumi’s stepdaughter or ward.

### Kimya Khatun

The evidence about Kimya is fragmentary and heavily mythologized.

- **Reported fact:** she married Shams.
- **Reported death:** she died not long afterward.
- **Later hostile or romanticized narratives:** Shams was violently jealous, confined her, abused her, or caused her death.
- **Evidence:** these claims depend on interpretive readings of Aflaki and much later fictional elaborations; no court record, medical record, or contemporary accusation survives.
- **Assessment:** the marriage and her early death are plausible; responsibility for abuse or death is unproved.

Modern novels frequently turn Kimya into either a victim silenced by male mystics or a willing spiritual initiate. Neither complete psychological portrait is recoverable.

## 1247–48: Final Disappearance of Shams

Shams disappeared permanently, probably in late **1247**, though 1248 is sometimes given. What happened remains the central unresolved event in Rumi’s biography.

### Version 1: Voluntary departure

Shams, who had repeatedly traveled and had already left Konya once, may have departed secretly after renewed hostility.

Evidence favoring this version:

- Rumi initially believed he was alive.
- Rumi searched or inquired for him in Damascus.
- Early sources closest to Rumi do not plainly report a murder.
- Shams’s itinerant habits make another departure plausible.

### Version 2: Murder by hostile disciples

Aflaki says conspirators lured Shams outside, stabbed him, and disposed of the body. Some versions identify **ʿAlaʾ al-Din**, Rumi’s son, as a participant.

Evidence problems:

- Sultan Walad does not report the murder in his extensive early account.
- Sepahsalar does not securely corroborate it.
- Aflaki offers internally inconsistent supernatural and burial versions.
- No body or securely identified grave was produced.
- Rumi’s surviving relations with ʿAlaʾ al-Din do not show an established belief that his son murdered Shams.

A useful critical summary is [Ibrahim Gamard’s discussion of common Rumi misconceptions](https://www.dar-al-masnavi.org/rumi.misconceptions.conv.pdf).

### Version 3: Death elsewhere

Later shrines in **Khoy**, **Konya**, and elsewhere have been associated with Shams. None supplies conclusive identification.

### Evidentiary judgment

- **Documented fact:** Shams vanished from Rumi’s circle.
- **Reported fact:** hostility surrounded him.
- **Allegation:** ʿAlaʾ al-Din and other disciples murdered him.
- **No indictment, trial, confession, or court finding is known.**
- **Unresolved question:** departure, murder, and later death elsewhere all remain possible; murder cannot be presented as established fact.

## Rumi’s Response to Shams’s Loss

Rumi searched for Shams physically and then increasingly represented Shams as spiritually present within himself. The lyric corpus conventionally called the **Dīvān-i Shams-i Tabrīzī** emerged from this transformation.

Rumi often placed Shams’s name in the signature position of his ghazals. This produced later confusion over authorship: the poems are principally Rumi’s, not a divan written by Shams. “Shams” functions as beloved, teacher, sun, poetic identity, and sign of divine presence.

His poems include grief, intoxication, erotic imagery, bewilderment, annihilation of self, union, separation, rebuke, theological allusion, humor, obscenity, and ecstatic wordplay. They are not a single chronological diary and cannot be read literally without regard to Persian lyric convention.

## Salah al-Din Zarkub, c. 1249–1258

After Shams, Rumi formed an especially close bond with **Salah al-Din Faridun Zarkub**, a goldsmith of Konya and an earlier associate from Burhan al-Din’s circle.

Some learned disciples reportedly resented Salah al-Din’s comparatively limited formal education and artisan status. Rumi nevertheless designated him as a principal companion or deputy.

Salah al-Din’s daughter **Fatima Khatun** married Sultan Walad, joining the families. Salah al-Din died around **December 1258**.

The famous story that Rumi began whirling when he heard goldsmiths’ hammers striking rhythmically belongs to the hagiographical presentation of this relationship. It expresses the idea that divine music can emerge from ordinary labor, but its literal details are not independently verified.

## Husam al-Din Chalabi and the Masnavi, c. 1258–1273

**Husam al-Din Hasan Chalabi**, from a Konya family associated with an *akhi* or futuwwa brotherhood, became Rumi’s principal deputy after Salah al-Din.

The [Encyclopaedia Iranica biography of Husam al-Din](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hosam-al-din-calabi/) reports that he transferred his inherited following and resources into Rumi’s circle and managed its endowed affairs. Rumi praised him as the inspiration of the *Masnavi* and called the work the **Husāmi-nāma**.

### Composition process

According to early tradition:

1. Husam al-Din observed that disciples read the didactic mystical poems of Sanaʾi and Attar.
2. He asked Rumi to compose a comparable work.
3. Rumi produced the opening eighteen lines of the reed-flute prologue.
4. Rumi thereafter dictated verses in varied circumstances.
5. Husam wrote them down, read them back, and participated in revision or organization.

The *Masnavi* therefore has both individual authorship and a collaborative material history: Rumi composed or dictated; Husam served as amanuensis, first reader, organizer, and editorial partner.

Book I was probably completed around **1261–62**. Composition paused after the death of Husam’s wife and then resumed. Six books were completed; stories of an intended seventh book are later and unsupported by the earliest textual tradition.

## Political, Institutional, and Financial Relationships

### Seljuk and Mongol Anatolia

Rumi’s adult life unfolded during the decline of independent Seljuk authority. After the Seljuk defeat at **Köse Dağ in 1243**, Anatolia came under Mongol supremacy. Court politics involved rival princes, Persian administrators, Mongol commanders, local notables, religious scholars, and urban guilds.

Rumi was not a state minister or military leader. His letters and discourses nevertheless show continuous contact with political elites.

Named associates or addressees include:

- Seljuk sultans and princes.
- **Muʿin al-Din Sulayman Parwana**, the dominant administrator of later-thirteenth-century Anatolia.
- **Fakhr al-Din ʿAli Sahib Ata**, vizier and patron of religious architecture.
- **Amir Taj al-Din Muʿtazz** and other officials.
- **Gurju Khatun**, a royal woman and supporter of Rumi’s circle.
- Physicians, judges, teachers, merchants, guild leaders, artisans, and petitioners.

A study of Mongol-era professional networks uses Rumi’s letters as evidence of close intellectual and political communication: [Bruno De Nicola, “Letters from Mongol Anatolia”](https://research.gold.ac.uk/22864/3/DeNicola_Letters%20from%20Mongol%20Anatolia_IRAN_Accepted%20version.pdf).

### Letters of intercession

Rumi’s letters asked officials for:

- appointments;
- assistance for disciples;
- tax or financial relief;
- protection;
- employment;
- access and patronage.

These are documentary evidence that his religious household operated through elite networks. They complicate the later picture of a saint detached from administration.

### Waqf and household economy

The community possessed or administered endowed resources. Husam al-Din served as a manager or deputy. Gifts and patronage flowed from wealthy disciples and officials.

No complete balance sheet survives. Claims that Rumi was either destitute or personally fabulously wealthy are not demonstrable. A more defensible reconstruction is:

- an established scholarly household;
- institutional income and gifts;
- access to wealthy patrons;
- redistribution to disciples and petitioners;
- occasional literary celebration of poverty and detachment, which should not be confused with audited personal insolvency.

### Relationship with Muʿin al-Din Parwana

Parwana was deeply involved in Mongol-Seljuk politics and was executed in 1277, after Rumi’s death. Rumi addressed or interacted with him and other members of the governing elite. A recent Turkish study examines the relationship in the context of Mongol rule: [“Mawlānā and Muin al-din Parwana in the Shadow of the Mongol Invasion”](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/usad/article/1653005).

- **Documented fact:** Rumi had relations with Mongol-era officials.
- **Modern accusation:** this makes him an ideological collaborator with Mongol domination.
- **Counterevidence and limitation:** religious scholars routinely interceded with whoever governed; Rumi held no known Mongol office and left no program advocating conquest.
- **Assessment:** accommodation and access are documented; the stronger label “agent” or “political collaborator” is an interpretation not established by an appointment, payment register, intelligence report, or explicit political program.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Sunni legal and scriptural foundation

Rumi’s writings are saturated with:

- Qurʾanic quotations and paraphrases;
- hadith;
- stories of Muhammad and earlier prophets;
- ritual prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, repentance, and remembrance;
- legal and theological vocabulary.

Popular anthologies that present him as detached from Islam omit a fundamental part of the record. The *Masnavi* describes itself as dealing with “the roots of the roots of the roots of religion.” See [Encyclopaedia Iranica on Rumi’s teachings](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/rumi-jalal-al-din-teachings/) and [its philosophy article](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/rumi-philosophy/).

### Sufism

Central concepts include:

- **tawhid:** divine unity;
- annihilation of self and subsistence in God;
- love as transformative practice;
- remembrance and attentive listening;
- the perfected spiritual guide;
- inward meaning beneath outward forms;
- disciplined imitation of Muhammad;
- death before physical death;
- creation as continual divine disclosure.

Rumi did not write a systematic philosophical summa. His positions emerge through stories, analogies, dialogue, sermons, and shifts of voice.

### Reason and knowledge

Rumi often contrasts limited discursive reason with direct spiritual understanding. This is not a rejection of all reasoning: he was highly trained in logic, law, rhetoric, and argument. His target is reason claiming independence from revelation or lived transformation.

### Sanaʾi and Attar

Rumi explicitly acknowledged **Hakim Sanaʾi** and **Farid al-Din Attar** as poetic-spiritual predecessors. Their didactic masnavis, allegorical narratives, stories of saints, and language of love materially shaped his work.

### Ibn Arabi and Sadr al-Din Qunawi

Rumi and **Sadr al-Din Qunawi**, Ibn Arabi’s stepson and major interpreter, lived in Konya. Their relationship is depicted as sometimes cordial, sometimes competitive, depending on the source.

Parallels between Rumi and Ibn Arabi include divine self-disclosure, unity, the perfected human, and imaginal interpretation. But Rumi rarely adopts Ibn Arabi’s technical vocabulary systematically. Claims that Rumi was simply a disciple of Ibn Arabi or a formal exponent of a later standardized “unity of being” doctrine overstate the evidence.

### Religious plurality

Rumi repeatedly uses Christians, Jews, Muslims, Zoroastrians, Greeks, Indians, and others in parables. He teaches that divine truth exceeds limited names and that sincere seekers may perceive one reality through different expressions.

At the same time:

- he identifies Muhammad as the normative prophetic model;
- he treats the Qurʾan as revelation;
- some stories reproduce medieval polemics, religious hierarchies, ethnic stereotypes, gender hierarchies, and slaveholding assumptions;
- universalist passages coexist with exclusivist or disciplinary ones.

Neither “modern secular pluralist” nor “uncomplicated sectarian” adequately represents the whole corpus.

## Artistic Practice and Poetics

### Forms

Rumi used:

- the **ghazal**, or lyric ode;
- the **rubaʿi**, or quatrain;
- the **masnavi**, rhyming couplets suited to extended narrative;
- prose sermons;
- letters;
- recorded conversational discourse.

### Performance

His poetry was connected with voice, music, rhythm, and communal listening. Poems may have been composed or improvised during *samāʿ*. The written corpus preserves traces of oral performance, including repeated refrains, commands to singers or musicians, rhythmic intensification, and abrupt silence formulas.

### Imagery

Frequent images include:

- wine and intoxication;
- taverns;
- the sun;
- the reed flute;
- ocean and drop;
- mirror;
- death and rebirth;
- cooking and ripening;
- erotic union;
- gambling;
- marketplaces and crafts;
- Moses, Jesus, Joseph, Abraham, Muhammad, and other sacred figures;
- animals, fools, tricksters, slaves, kings, judges, merchants, and lovers.

Wine imagery may be mystical, literary, comic, or multivalent. It cannot uniformly prove either literal drinking or total abstention.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Baha al-Din Walad

Father, first teacher, source of inherited congregation and intellectual vocabulary. Documented through both men’s works.

### Burhan al-Din Tirmidhi

Early spiritual trainer who connected Rumi’s paternal inheritance with disciplined Sufi practice.

### Shams al-Din Tabrizi

Transformative companion, teacher, beloved, critic, and poetic identity. His disappearance generated the defining grief and mythology of Rumi’s life.

### Salah al-Din Zarkub

Artisan companion and designated deputy. His elevation caused social friction with more learned disciples.

### Husam al-Din Chalabi

Deputy, administrator, amanuensis, and collaborator in the production of the *Masnavi*.

### Sultan Walad

Son, disciple, biographer, poet, successor-generation organizer, and principal architect of the hereditary Mevlevi institution.

### ʿAlaʾ al-Din Muhammad

Son whose relationship with Shams was reportedly hostile. Later accused—but never judicially charged or proved—to have participated in Shams’s disappearance.

### Sadr al-Din Qunawi

Prominent Konya intellectual associated with Ibn Arabi’s school. Early sources preserve both respect and rivalry. He reportedly participated in Rumi’s funeral rites, although details differ.

### Political patrons

Rumi’s network crossed the court, bureaucracy, religious schools, urban guilds, and elite households. His letters show patronage and intercession rather than political isolation.

### Non-Muslim communities

Aflaki depicts Greek Christians, Armenian Christians, and Jews attending Rumi’s teaching or funeral and recounts conversions attributed to him. Individual conversion totals and miracle stories are not independently verifiable, but the multi-religious social environment of Konya is historical.

## Illness, Death, and Funeral

During Rumi’s final illness in December 1273, physicians reportedly attended him. Aflaki describes fever, weakness, earthquakes, visitors, and poems welcoming death.

- **Documented date and place:** 17 December 1273, Konya.
- **Cause of death:** no medically reliable diagnosis survives.
- **Possible description:** acute febrile illness in an approximately sixty-six-year-old man.
- **Legendary framing:** earthquakes as cosmic sympathy; conscious prediction of death; final verses dictated with perfect composure.
- **No evidence:** poisoning, assassination, suicide, addiction, or criminal cause.

His death anniversary became **Şeb-i Arûs/Shab-i ʿArus**, the “Wedding Night,” representing union with God.

### Funeral

Early tradition says Muslims, Christians, Jews, rulers, scholars, artisans, and townspeople crowded the funeral. Clergy from other religions reportedly defended their presence by saying that Rumi had illuminated their scriptures.

- **Reported fact:** a large, socially diverse funeral is plausible and is embedded in early Mevlevi memory.
- **Hagiographical elaboration:** exact speeches and crowd descriptions cannot be independently verified.
- **Reputational effect:** the funeral became foundational evidence for Rumi’s universal appeal.

Rumi was buried beside Baha al-Din. The Seljuk noblewoman **Gurju Khatun** and her husband or associated patrons supported construction at the tomb. The present monumental complex incorporates later Seljuk, Karamanid, Ottoman, and modern interventions.

---

## Succession and Formation of the Mevlevi Order

After Rumi’s death, the community accepted **Husam al-Din Chalabi** as principal successor. He served until his death in 1284. Leadership then passed to **Sultan Walad**, after an intervening succession involving **Karim al-Din Bektimur** in some accounts.

Rumi himself did not leave a constitution for the mature Mevlevi order. Sultan Walad and later descendants:

- consolidated initiation and succession;
- cultivated relationships with rulers;
- developed lodges and endowments;
- preserved Rumi’s texts;
- regularized music and *samāʿ*;
- established hereditary leadership.

The famous whirling dervish ceremony is therefore a historical development from Rumi’s devotional practice, not a ritual whose final choreography he personally legislated.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Modern corporate categories do not apply, but relevant institutional structures include:

### Madrasas and mosques

Rumi taught in endowed religious settings in Konya. The exact history of the madrasa traditionally associated with his family is entangled with later reconstruction.

### The Rumi household and teaching circle

This functioned simultaneously as:

- family household;
- educational circle;
- devotional community;
- patronage network;
- recipient and distributor of gifts;
- nucleus of a later Sufi order.

### Waqf

Endowed resources supported religious teaching and the community. Husam al-Din’s administrative role is documented in early biographical tradition.

### Manuscript production

Rumi’s works depended on scribal technology and oral dictation. Husam al-Din wrote down the *Masnavi*. The earliest complete surviving *Masnavi* manuscript was copied at Konya in **677 AH/1278**, only five years after Rumi’s death, by Muhammad ibn ʿAbd Allah al-Qunawi. It is central to textual criticism and is recognized in the [UNESCO documentary heritage materials](https://media.unesco.org/sites/default/files/webform/mow001/08_135%252B.pdf).

### No patents, inventions, or companies

There are no patents, incorporated businesses, technological inventions, equity investments, or modern financial disclosures associated with Rumi. Claims treating the Mevlevi order as a “company” are anachronistic.

## Controversies, Allegations, Accusations, and Contested Material

## 1. Did ʿAlaʾ al-Din murder Shams?

- **Allegation:** Rumi’s son joined jealous disciples in killing Shams.
- **Earliest developed source:** Aflaki, written many decades later.
- **Contrary evidence:** silence of Sultan Walad and Sepahsalar; contradictions in burial stories; Rumi’s later correspondence with his son.
- **Legal status:** no known investigation, indictment, trial, or conviction.
- **Finding:** unresolved allegation, not established fact.

## 2. Was Rumi’s relationship with Shams sexual?

Rumi’s poems use erotic, bodily, and homoerotic imagery for a male beloved. Premodern Persian lyric convention frequently used a grammatically ungendered or male beloved, combining physical beauty, patronal praise, friendship, discipleship, and divine symbolism.

Competing interpretations:

- **Traditional Sufi interpretation:** spiritually transformative love articulated through established erotic symbolism.
- **Queer literary interpretation:** the poems’ male-directed desire is meaningful and should not be erased even if physical consummation cannot be proved.
- **Literal biographical claim:** Rumi and Shams were sexual partners.
- **Evidence:** no contemporaneous witness, confession, legal accusation, or unambiguous prose statement establishes a sexual relationship.
- **Finding:** homoerotic textual language is documented; a consummated sexual relationship is unproved.

The broader historical difficulty of reading conventional poetry as direct sexual autobiography is discussed by [Encyclopaedia Iranica on homosexuality in Persianate literary culture](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/homosexuality-ii/) and a recent philosophical treatment, [“Being (with) Shams”](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11841-026-01163-4).

## 3. Treatment of Kimya Khatun

- **Documented or early reported core:** Shams married Kimya; she died young.
- **Accusations:** coercion, confinement, physical abuse, or responsibility for her death.
- **Evidence:** late narrative inference and modern fictional expansion; no judicial or medical documentation.
- **Finding:** possible household conflict cannot be reconstructed; homicide or abuse must remain allegation or literary interpretation.

## 4. Relations with Mongol-aligned officials

- **Documented:** correspondence and social relations with officials serving in Mongol-dominated Anatolia.
- **Accusation:** political collaboration or ideological service to Mongol imperial rule.
- **Missing evidence:** no known Mongol office, intelligence function, military role, or payment record identifying Rumi as an agent.
- **Finding:** elite accommodation and intercession are factual; stronger collaboration narratives remain interpretations.

## 5. Obscenity and sexual narratives in the Masnavi

The *Masnavi* includes bawdy tales, adultery, prostitution, same-sex situations, scatology, and the notorious story of a maidservant, a donkey, and a gourd.

- **Documented fact:** such stories occur in the Persian text.
- **Function:** shock, satire, exposure of uncontrolled appetite, mnemonic teaching, and comic reversal.
- **Contemporary controversy:** modern critics cite them as pornography, moral hypocrisy, or evidence that sanitized quotations misrepresent the work.
- **Textual response:** acknowledging didactic intention does not remove the explicit content; calling the entire work pornography ignores narrative framing.
- **Finding:** explicit material is genuine and should neither be suppressed nor detached from its pedagogical context.

## 6. Gender hierarchy

Rumi uses feminine imagery positively for divine creativity, love, the soul, and motherhood. He also repeats medieval patriarchal assumptions and stories in which women signify appetite, deceit, or limited reason.

- **Documented:** contradictory gender imagery across the corpus.
- **Disputed interpretations:** feminist retrieval emphasizes female spiritual agency; critics emphasize misogynistic commonplaces.
- **Finding:** both bodies of evidence exist. Rumi cannot reliably be made either a modern gender egalitarian or a uniformly hostile writer about women.

## 7. Religious universalism and exclusivism

Modern collections often cite Rumi as teaching that all religions are interchangeable. Others present him as strictly exclusivist.

- **Documented:** universalizing mystical passages and narratives of shared truth.
- **Also documented:** Islamic prophetic hierarchy, Qurʾanic normativity, polemic, and calls to religious discipline.
- **Finding:** the corpus contains both mystical transcendence of labels and confessional Islamic commitments.

## 8. National ownership

Afghanistan, Iran, Tajikistan, and Turkey have each emphasized Rumi as national heritage.

- Afghanistan invokes Balkh and the name Balkhi.
- Tajikistan invokes the Vakhsh birthplace reconstruction.
- Iran invokes Persian language and the broader Iranian cultural world.
- Turkey invokes Rumi’s adult life, death, tomb, and institutional legacy in Konya.

A dispute intensified when Iran and Turkey were associated with a proposed UNESCO registration of the *Masnavi*. Afghan critics protested exclusion. See [Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty’s report](https://gandhara.rferl.org/a/afghanistan-rumi-controversy/27791966.html) and the academic study [“Heritage diplomacy and soft power competition between Iran and Turkey”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10286632.2023.2241872).

- **Finding:** all four countries preserve relevant parts of his historical geography; exclusive modern nationality is anachronistic.

## 9. De-Islamization and Coleman Barks

Coleman Barks does not translate directly from Persian. He reshaped earlier English translations into free American poems. His books helped make “Rumi” exceptionally popular in the United States.

Criticisms include:

- deletion of Qurʾanic and Islamic references;
- conversion of specific Sufi concepts into generalized spirituality;
- omission of textual provenance;
- presentation of adaptations as translations;
- creation or circulation of lines with no identifiable Persian original.

Defenses emphasize poetic accessibility and Barks’s role in creating a new English reception rather than a critical edition.

Recent criticism is represented by [“Orientalizing the East”](https://journals.upress.ufl.edu/delos/article/view/2337), [“The Americanization of Rumi”](https://researchwith.stevens.edu/en/publications/americanization-of-rumi-the-impact-of-coleman-barkss-appropriativ/), and [“In the Aftermath of Popularisation and De-Islamisation”](https://context.cns.ba/index.php/context/article/view/215).

- **Finding:** Barks’s versions are adaptations or poetic renderings, not philologically direct translations.
- **No fraud finding:** no cited court has found Barks guilty of fraud; “fraudulent” in polemic is an evaluative accusation, not a legal status.

## 10. Fabricated quotations

Large numbers of social-media “Rumi quotes” cannot be located in the *Masnavi*, *Divan*, quatrains, sermons, letters, or discourses. Some derive from Barks’s expansions; others are anonymous modern sayings assigned to Rumi.

- **Documented phenomenon:** widespread false attribution.
- **Best practice:** require Persian text, poem number, book and line number, or a traceable critical edition.
- **Historical consequence:** a modern persona—romantic, therapeutic, individualist, and minimally Islamic—can displace the medieval author.

## 11. Conservative Muslim criticism

Some Muslim critics condemn:

- musical *samāʿ*;
- whirling;
- erotic imagery;
- unity-of-being interpretations;
- veneration at Rumi’s tomb;
- explicit comic stories;
- perceived departures from literalist theology.

These are theological judgments, not discoveries of criminal conduct. Rumi’s Hanafi and Qurʾanic formation is well documented, while later Mevlevi ritual and metaphysics cannot always be assigned to him in fully developed form.

## 12. Criminal, financial, and legal allegations

No reliable source consulted documents Rumi himself as:

- indicted or convicted of a crime;
- party to a surviving lawsuit;
- investigated for fraud or corruption;
- involved in money laundering;
- operator of a criminal enterprise;
- military participant in political violence;
- intelligence agent.

The absence of surviving records is not proof that every household transaction was uncontroversial; it means such allegations cannot responsibly be asserted.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## The “ocean following a sea”

**Story:** Ibn Arabi or Attar sees young Rumi following Baha al-Din and predicts that the child will surpass the father.

**Circulation:** later Sufi biography.

**Evidence:** no contemporary account.

**Function:** validates succession while honoring father and son.

## Attar’s gift of the Asrar-nama

**Story:** Attar gives Rumi his mystical poem and recognizes his destiny.

**Evidence:** possible chronology, no independent record.

**Reputation:** creates a direct chain from Attar to Rumi.

## Books rescued dry from water

**Story:** Shams throws Rumi’s books into a pool; they emerge dry, teaching that spiritual knowledge exceeds book learning.

**Evidence:** miracle tale with variant forms.

**Reputation:** central to the modern story that Shams destroyed Rumi’s pedantry. It should not be used to claim Rumi abandoned scholarship.

## Forty days or months alone with Shams

**Story:** total seclusion produces instantaneous transformation.

**Evidence:** some retreat is plausible; numbers differ and “forty” carries symbolic significance in Islamic asceticism.

**Reputation:** supplies a clean conversion narrative.

## Shams’s body in a well

**Story:** conspirators killed Shams and threw him into a well; Sultan Walad later recovered or buried him.

**Evidence against:** inconsistent locations and chronology; silence in Sultan Walad’s own writings.

**Reputation:** enabled martyr narratives and suspicion of ʿAlaʾ al-Din.

## Shams’s many tombs

Khoy and other locations preserve Shams traditions. Shrines attest cult and memory, not necessarily possession of his remains.

## Rumi spontaneously whirling to goldsmiths’ hammers

**Story:** rhythmic hammering in Salah al-Din’s shop induces ecstatic dance.

**Evidence:** early hagiographical tradition, no independent workshop record.

**Reputation:** connects Mevlevi ritual with artisan life and spontaneous divine audition.

## “Come, come, whoever you are”

The famous quatrain beginning “Come, come, whoever you are” is widely attributed to Rumi. Its attribution has been questioned and it has also been associated with **Abu Saʿid Abu’l-Khayr** or the wider Persian quatrain tradition.

- **Finding:** attribution to Rumi is uncertain.
- **Impact:** it became perhaps the best-known summary of his supposed unconditional universalism.

## “The field beyond wrongdoing and rightdoing”

The popular English line about meeting “in a field” derives from a genuine Persian quatrain, but English adaptations vary substantially and often detach it from the technical language of belief, unbelief, duality, and soul.

## Rumi as founder of whirling

Rumi practiced *samāʿ* and turning. He did not personally codify every element of the later Mevlevi ceremony. The mature ritual developed over generations.

## Rumi as pacifist opposed to all violence

His emphasis on love supports modern peace readings, but the medieval corpus includes martial metaphors, punitive stories, religious hierarchy, and acceptance of the political world around him. There is no surviving political pacifist manifesto.

## Rumi as secret Christian, Buddhist, Hindu, atheist, or perennialist

These identities arise from universalizing selections and modern spiritual appropriation.

- **Evidence:** no historical evidence that Rumi secretly converted from Islam.
- **Documented:** creative use of Jesus and Christian imagery, admiration for spiritual truth wherever found, and a multi-religious funeral tradition.
- **Finding:** influence and analogy do not establish conversion.

## Rumi as “America’s best-selling poet”

The description circulated widely from the late twentieth century onward, usually referring to exceptional sales of Barks’s adaptations rather than an audited, permanent ranking across all poetry publishing. It accurately reflects a major American publishing phenomenon but should not be treated as a precise all-time statistical office.

## Works by Rumi

Rumi’s canonical corpus consists of five principal collections. Publication dates in the modern sense do not apply; approximate composition dates and earliest manuscripts are given.

## 1. Masnavi-yi Maʿnavi

**Original title:** مثنوی معنوی, *Mathnawī-yi maʿnawī*  
**Other titles:** *Masnavi*, *Mesnevi*, *Spiritual Couplets*  
**Language:** predominantly Persian, with Arabic  
**Composition:** approximately 1258/61–1273  
**Form:** six books of rhyming couplets, roughly 25,000–26,000 couplets depending on edition  
**Authorship:** genuine; dictated or recited by Rumi, written and read back by Husam al-Din Chalabi  
**Earliest complete manuscript:** Konya, 1278  
**Contents:** Qurʾanic interpretation, prophetic narratives, folktales, jokes, sexual and scatological stories, theology, ethics, spiritual psychology, metaphysics, and direct exhortation.

An alleged **Book VII** is spurious. The canonical work has six books.

Major scholarly editions and translations include:

- Reynold A. Nicholson, *The Mathnawí of Jalálu’ddín Rúmí*, Persian edition, translation, and commentary, 1925–40.
- Jawid Mojaddedi, *The Masnavi*, Oxford World’s Classics, Books I–VI in multiple volumes.
- Alan Williams, *The Masnavi of Rumi*, Books I–II, 2020.
- Muhammad ʿAli Muwahhid/Movahhed, critical Persian edition, 2018.
- Tofiq Sobhani, edition based substantially on the 1278 manuscript.
- A detailed working bibliography appears in the [Dar al-Masnavi bibliography](https://www.dar-al-masnavi.org/pdf/rumi.bibliography.pdf).

## 2. Divan-i Kabir / Divan-i Shams-i Tabrizi

**Original titles:** دیوان کبیر; کلیات شمس; دیوان شمس تبریزی  
**Composition:** chiefly after 1244 and especially after Shams’s disappearances  
**Form:** ghazals, tarjiʿat, and other lyrics; editions vary widely  
**Authorship:** core overwhelmingly genuine Rumi; individual poems and readings require textual criticism  
**Title problem:** the collection is Rumi’s poetry associated with or signed through Shams, not a divan authored by Shams.

The corpus includes more than 3,000 ghazals in major editions and tens of thousands of lines. Badiʿ al-Zaman Foruzanfar’s critical Persian edition remains foundational.

Major translations and selections:

- Reynold A. Nicholson, *Selected Poems from the Dīvāni Shamsi Tabrīz*.
- A. J. Arberry, *Mystical Poems of Rumi*, two volumes.
- Franklin D. Lewis, *The Rumi Collection* and translated selections.
- William C. Chittick, *The Sufi Path of Love*, thematic translations.
- Nader Khalili, versions and selections.
- Coleman Barks, numerous adaptations based on earlier translations; not direct Persian translations.

## 3. Rubaʿiyat

**Title:** رباعیات, quatrains  
**Authorship:** mixed and disputed.

Large manuscript collections assign many quatrains to Rumi, but quatrains circulated fluidly among poets. Some also appear under Abu Saʿid Abu’l-Khayr, Baba Afzal, Khayyam, or anonymous attribution. A complete bibliography must therefore distinguish:

- securely or plausibly Rumi-attributed quatrains;
- shared-attribution quatrains;
- later accretions;
- modern paraphrases.

Ibrahim Gamard and Rawan Farhadi’s *The Quatrains of Rumi* attempts a substantial direct translation while acknowledging textual issues.

## 4. Fihi ma Fih

**Original title:** فیه ما فیه, *Fīhi mā fīh*, “In It Is What Is in It”  
**Other titles:** *Discourses of Rumi*  
**Form:** prose records of conversations and teaching sessions  
**Authorship:** teachings are Rumi’s; compilation and wording were recorded or edited by disciples, possibly including Sultan Walad  
**Date:** sessions from Rumi’s later life; compiled posthumously or progressively  
**Manuscripts:** important witnesses date from 1317 and 1350.

Because it preserves social exchanges, visitors, political references, and less stylized teaching, it is a major biographical source. See [Iranica on its manuscript importance](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/rumi-jalal-al-din-teachings/).

Major English translations:

- A. J. Arberry, *Discourses of Rumi*.
- Wheeler M. Thackston, *Signs of the Unseen*.

## 5. Majalis-i Sabʿa

**Original title:** مجالس سبعه, *Majālis-i sabʿa*, “Seven Sessions/Sermons”  
**Form:** seven sermons or homiletic discourses  
**Authorship:** based on Rumi’s preaching; transmitted and edited rather than necessarily autograph prose  
**Date:** material may preserve the earlier public preacher as well as later revision.

The sermons are important evidence against portrayals of Rumi as detached from Qurʾan, hadith, and formal preaching.

## 6. Maktubat

**Original title:** مکتوبات, *Maktūbāt*, “Letters”  
**Form:** correspondence to officials, patrons, family, and associates  
**Authorship:** generally accepted, though individual scribal transmission must be assessed  
**Importance:** patronage, intercession, employment requests, political access, family relations, and institutional economy.

## Works Not Authored by Rumi but Often Confused with His Corpus

- **Maqālāt-i Shams-i Tabrizi:** discourses of Shams, compiled by listeners.
- **Maʿārif of Baha al-Din Walad:** Rumi’s father’s teachings.
- **Ibtidā-nāma/Walad-nāma:** Sultan Walad’s early Mevlevi history.
- **Risāla-yi Sepahsalar:** biography by Faridun Sepahsalar.
- **Manāqib al-ʿārifīn:** Aflaki’s hagiography.
- Modern collections by Coleman Barks: adaptations and recompositions, not newly discovered Persian works by Rumi.
- Anonymous inspirational quotations circulated under Rumi’s name: unauthenticated unless traceable to the canonical corpus.

- **Hazreti Mevlana**, Turkish screen dramatizations under this or similar titles.
- Numerous filmed Mevlevi *sema* ceremonies from Konya and international lodges.
- Documentary programs produced for the 800th anniversary of Rumi’s birth in 2007.

### Musical and spoken-word reception

Rumi has been adapted by:

- Persian classical vocalists and composers.
- Turkish Mevlevi musicians.
- Afghan and Tajik performers.
- Western spoken-word artists.
- Philip Glass and Robert Wilson in *Monsters of Grace*, using Rumi texts in adaptation.
- Popular performers citing Barks versions in concerts and recordings.

A recording that says “Rumi” may use a direct translation, indirect adaptation, or unattributed paraphrase; provenance must be checked individually.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Early Mevlevi cult

Within decades of Rumi’s death:

- Sultan Walad wrote a sacred family history.
- Aflaki collected miracle narratives.
- Rumi’s tomb attracted pilgrims.
- Manuscripts circulated through Persianate regions.
- The Mevlevi order acquired patrons and lodges.

### Ottoman period

The Mevlevi order became an influential Ottoman institution. Its lodges educated musicians, poets, calligraphers, administrators, and scholars. It never had a single unchanging political role: lodges depended on varying combinations of court patronage, urban notables, endowments, and family succession.

Ottoman Turkish translations and illustrated hagiographies spread the legend. In 1590 Sultan Murad III ordered a Turkish translation of an abridged Aflaki text, as described by the [Morgan Library](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/treasures-of-islamic-manuscript-painting/30).

### Suppression and museum transformation

The Turkish Republic closed Sufi lodges in **1925**. The Konya complex became a museum. Mevlevi ceremony later returned partly as regulated cultural performance and tourism, while living branches continued privately or abroad.

This created a division among:

- hereditary or initiated Mevlevi practice;
- Turkish national cultural heritage;
- state-sponsored staged *sema*;
- global spiritual movements.

### Persianate influence

Rumi became canonical across Iran, Afghanistan, Central Asia, the Indian subcontinent, and Ottoman lands. Persian commentaries and translations proliferated. The *Masnavi* was sometimes called “the Qurʾan in Persian”—a statement of cultural reverence, not a claim that it literally constitutes revelation.

### Muhammad Iqbal

Muhammad Iqbal repeatedly called Rumi his spiritual guide, staged dialogues with him, and made him a central figure in his philosophical poetry. This is one of the clearest modern cases of explicit intellectual discipleship.

### European reception

European Orientalists translated and analyzed Rumi from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries onward. Nicholson and Arberry established major English philological traditions. French, German, Russian, and other translations produced distinct national receptions.

### United States

From the late twentieth century, Barks’s adaptations and the New Age, interfaith, therapeutic, and poetry-reading markets made Rumi a mass-cultural figure. This increased readership while loosening textual connection to Persian originals.

### UNESCO and anniversary culture

The year **2007**, the conventional 800th anniversary of Rumi’s birth, produced international conferences, performances, publications, and a UNESCO medal. Anniversary programs also intensified disputes about nationality, birthplace, and religious identity.

### Contemporary reinterpretation

Rumi is now represented as:

- Muslim saint;
- Persian national poet;
- Afghan or Balkhi heritage figure;
- Tajik-born poet;
- Turkish Anatolian mystic;
- universal spiritual teacher;
- queer literary icon;
- interfaith pluralist;
- conservative Sunni scholar;
- New Age love poet;
- critic of ego and institutional authority;
- symbol of cultural appropriation.

Each identity selects genuine elements or later reception history, but none exhausts the archive.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by sources are included. Mere contemporaneity, shared geography, or thematic resemblance is excluded.

### Baha ud-Din Walad

- **Nature:** father, first teacher, source of scholarly position and spiritual vocabulary.
- **Dates and places:** Vakhsh/Samarkand/Anatolian migration, 1207–31; Konya until Baha al-Din’s death.
- **Label:** **documented fact**.

### Shams al-Din Muhammad of Tabriz

- **Nature:** transformative companion, teacher, beloved, interlocutor, and poetic namesake.
- **Dates and places:** Konya from 1244; Damascus-related departure and return, approximately 1246–47; final disappearance around 1247.
- **Label:** **documented fact** for relationship and disappearance; murder remains allegation.

### Farid al-Din Attar

- **Nature:** explicit poetic and spiritual influence; alleged childhood meeting.
- **Dates and places:** possible meeting at Nishapur during Rumi’s westward migration, probably before 1221.
- **Label:** **documented fact** for literary influence; **legend or unverified reported fact** for personal meeting and gift of the *Asrar-nama*.

### Ibn Arabi

- **Nature:** major contemporary Sufi thinker whose school was represented in Konya by Sadr al-Din Qunawi; alleged meeting with young Rumi.
- **Dates and places:** alleged Damascus encounter in the early thirteenth century; intellectual proximity in later Konya.
- **Label:** **legend or disputed reconstruction** for direct meeting; **reported intellectual connection** through the Konya milieu.

### Abu Bakr as-Siddiq

- **Nature:** later Mevlevi genealogies traced Rumi’s paternal ancestry to Abu Bakr.
- **Dates and places:** no personal meeting possible; lineage claim concerns seventh-century ancestry.
- **Label:** **hagiographical or unverified genealogical claim**.

### Abu Yazid Tayfur ibn Isa al-Bistami

- **Nature:** Rumi and Shams discuss Bayazid Bistami as a paradigmatic ecstatic Sufi; he appears in the famous first-meeting question contrasting his utterance with Muhammad’s.
- **Label:** **documented intellectual and symbolic influence**; the precise roadside dialogue is **hagiographical**.

### Abu al-Qasim al-Junayd ibn Muhammad al-Baghdadi

- **Nature:** part of the earlier Sufi tradition inherited and discussed in the intellectual world of Rumi and Shams.
- **Label:** **documented historical influence at the level of Sufi tradition**, not a personal connection.

### Mansur al-Hallaj

- **Nature:** Rumi invokes Hallaj and “I am the Truth” traditions in discussions of ecstatic utterance and annihilation.
- **Label:** **documented literary and intellectual influence**, not personal contact.

### Ibrahim ibn Adham

- **Nature:** appears within the ascetic and Sufi story tradition used by Rumi.
- **Label:** **documented literary influence**, not personal contact.

### Rabia al-Adawiyya

- **Nature:** part of the earlier Sufi saint tradition circulating in Rumi’s intellectual world; stories attributed to her appear in Persian Sufi literature informing his narrative repertoire.
- **Label:** **reported literary-traditional influence**; no personal connection.

### Muhammad ibn Abdullah

- **Nature:** central prophetic model, subject of extensive Qurʾanic, hadith, and narrative treatment throughout Rumi’s works.
- **Label:** **documented religious and intellectual influence**.

### Abraham

- **Nature:** Qurʾanic prophet repeatedly used in Rumi’s poetry and teaching, particularly in narratives of fire, sacrifice, faith, and iconoclasm.
- **Label:** **documented scriptural and symbolic influence**.

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **Nature:** recurring prophetic and symbolic figure; Rumi uses Jesus’s breath, healing, poverty, and spiritual life in parables.
- **Label:** **documented scriptural and literary influence**.

### Moses

- **Nature:** one of the most frequent prophetic figures in the *Masnavi*, including the story of Moses and the shepherd.
- **Label:** **documented scriptural and literary influence**.

### Plato and Aristotle

- **Nature:** Greek philosophy formed part of the intellectual field Rumi addressed, usually through Islamic philosophical transmission rather than direct reading demonstrably attributable to specific editions.
- **Label:** **reported intellectual background**, not a documented personal or direct textual relationship sufficient to claim discipleship.

### Genghis Khan

- **Nature:** Mongol expansion transformed the political geography through which Rumi’s family migrated and the Anatolia in which he lived.
- **Label:** **documented historical-context connection**, not a meeting, correspondence, or personal relationship.

### Rabindranath Tagore, Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, Carl Gustav Jung, Hermann Hesse, and other modern roster figures

Modern writers and psychologists have sometimes been compared with Rumi or participated in a wider Western reception of Asian mysticism. The sources consulted for this dossier did not establish a sufficiently specific, explicit connection to Rumi for each of these roster members. No connection is recorded merely from thematic similarity.

### Roster finding

Apart from Baha al-Din Walad, Shams, Attar, Muhammad, scriptural prophets, classical Sufi predecessors, the disputed Ibn Arabi encounter, and contextual Genghis Khan connection, no additional roster crossing was securely established. Absence of a listing is not a claim that a later person never read Rumi; it means the consulted evidence did not meet the requested threshold.
