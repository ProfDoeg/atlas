# Agustí Querol i Subirats: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier identifies the subject as the Catalan-Spanish sculptor generally known in Spanish as **Agustín Querol Subirats**, born in Tortosa in 1860 and deceased in Madrid in 1909.

The surviving record is uneven. Querol’s public commissions, prizes, parliamentary activity, studio, and posthumous estate are substantially documented. His private life—especially his parents’ full identities, his relationship with the mother of his daughter, and the circumstances under which that daughter was initially excluded from his succession—is much less securely reconstructed.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by an archival record, official catalogue, government record, court report, or contemporaneous documentation.
- **Reported fact:** stated in scholarly or institutional secondary literature but not independently verified here in the underlying record.
- **Allegation or contemporary accusation:** a claim made against Querol without a demonstrated judicial finding.
- **Disputed claim:** sources materially disagree.
- **Legend or hagiographic anecdote:** a story important to Querol’s image but not securely documented.
- **Unresolved question:** available evidence does not establish an answer.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full Catalan name:** Agustí Querol i Subirats.
- **Usual Castilian form:** Agustín Querol Subirats.
- **Other recorded forms:** Agustín Querol y Subirats; Querol Subirats, Agustín; Agustí Querol; A. Querol.
- **Expanded baptismal or civil form reported by specialist catalogues:** **Domingo Agustín Querol Subirats**.
- **Pseudonym attributed to him:** **“El Plutarco del Pueblo.”** Specialist biographies describe this as a signature or literary persona used for popular historical or biographical journalism between approximately 1894 and 1897. The exact corpus has not been satisfactorily isolated from the broader newspaper section or rubric also called *El Plutarco del pueblo*; attribution of individual unsigned articles requires archival verification.
- **Occupation:** sculptor; monumental designer; portraitist; studio proprietor; businessman associated with marble and bronze production; exhibition organizer or intermediary; journalist or occasional writer; Conservative deputy.
- **Nationality:** Spanish.
- **Primary cultural-linguistic background:** Catalan, from Tortosa in the Terres de l’Ebre; his professional public life was conducted principally in Spanish and Italian environments.
- **Birth:** **17 May 1860, Tortosa, Tarragona, Spain**, according to the date accepted by the Prado, Getty ULAN, modern specialist studies, and the most detailed sculpture catalogues.
- **Competing birth dates:** older references give **1863**; a Real Casino de Madrid publication gives **28 December 1860**. No consulted source reproduced the civil or baptismal entry itself. The strongest modern consensus is 17 May 1860.
- **Death:** **14 December 1909, Madrid**, aged forty-nine.
- **Competing death dates:** 12, 13, and 14 December appear in reference literature. A parish death certificate cited by later scholarship reportedly records the death in the register of Santa Teresa y Santa Isabel; contemporary notices and modern museum authorities predominantly support 14 December.
- **Cause of death:** reported as a cardiac condition, specifically **mitral stenosis associated with rheumatism or rheumatic disease**. This is a reported medical description rather than a modern retrospective diagnosis.
- **Burial:** Sacramental de San Justo, Madrid, Patio de San Justo, traditionally identified as grave no. 36.
- **Marital status:** contemporary and later accounts describe him as **unmarried**.
- **Known child:** **Silvia Querol**, legally recognized after posthumous litigation as his daughter and heir.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and economic background

**Documented or consistently reported facts**

Querol came from a family of bakers. His father operated a bakery or bread oven in Tortosa and is described as originating from **Ulldecona**, south of Tortosa. The family’s circumstances are consistently characterized as modest.

Querol worked in the family bakery before leaving for Barcelona at approximately eighteen. The identifying phrase “the baker’s son” became central to almost every later account of his upward social trajectory.

**Unresolved questions**

The consulted institutional sources do not securely establish:

- the full names and life dates of both parents;
- the precise location and ownership structure of the bakery;
- his siblings and their birth order;
- whether the relatives later described as his *hermanos políticos* were brothers-in-law through sisters, through a deceased or unacknowledged partner, or were being described in a looser social sense.

Newspaper reports after his death named **Federico Fernández** and **Pedro Mayor** as brothers-in-law or political brothers. Their precise connection to Querol remains to be established from civil records.

### Daughter and succession

Querol had a daughter, **Silvia Querol**. When he died, she was reportedly studying at a school in Belgium.

Her status became the central documented controversy of his private estate:

1. Querol died unmarried.
2. His relatives initially took control of the studio, works, contracts, and expected payments.
3. Silvia brought or pursued litigation to establish her filiation and inheritance rights.
4. In 1913 newspapers published her statement that a final judgment had recognized her as Querol’s daughter and therefore as the person entitled to the inheritance.
5. She warned the committees responsible for monuments in Argentina, Mexico, and elsewhere not to pay sums due under Querol’s contracts to other claimants.
6. Later documentation shows her continuing to control, offer, or attempt to dispose of works associated with her father. In 1949 she offered Madrid a bust of Quevedo. In 1963 she offered works to the Museo de Bellas Artes de Bilbao.

**Documented court outcome:** Silvia was judicially recognized as Querol’s daughter and heir.

**Unresolved questions:** The mother’s identity, the circumstances of Silvia’s birth, whether Querol privately acknowledged or financially supported her before his death, the precise parties to the inheritance case, and the complete division of the estate were not established by the consulted online sources.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1860–1878

Querol grew up in Tortosa, a commercial and ecclesiastical center on the lower Ebro. His earliest artistic teacher was **Ramon Cerveto Bestratén** (also rendered Cervetó or Cervera in imperfect secondary transcriptions), a Tortosa sculptor and founder of a local artistic family.

### The bread-dough story

**Legend or hagiographic anecdote:** From the early commemorative biography by Rodolfo Gil through modern museum writing, accounts say that the boy constantly modeled figures from bread dough in his father’s bakery, sometimes provoking paternal reprimands.

- **What the story says:** the sculptor’s talent was spontaneous and irrepressible; the raw material of the family trade became his first modeling clay.
- **Earliest circulation located:** Rodolfo Gil’s illustrated monograph, published in 1910, within months of Querol’s death, helped establish the anecdote.
- **Who circulated it:** admirers, obituary writers, museum biographies, and later popular accounts.
- **Evidence:** plausible in the setting of a bakery and repeated by near-contemporary biographers, but no surviving childhood testimony or family document was located.
- **Reputational effect:** it furnished a powerful self-made-artist narrative—poor baker’s son transformed into the most internationally commissioned Spanish monumental sculptor of his generation.

Querol reportedly studied drawing, wax, and clay with Cerveto for about eighteen months. At approximately eighteen, around 1878, he left the bakery and moved to Barcelona.

## Education and Formation, c. 1878–1884

In Barcelona, Querol worked successively in the studios of:

- **Domènec Talarn**;
- **Agapit Vallmitjana i Barbany**;
- **Venanci Vallmitjana i Barbany**.

He also attended the **Escola Provincial de Belles Arts**, commonly called **La Llotja**.

Specialist accounts state that he visited the dissection rooms of the medical faculty at the **Hospital de la Santa Creu** to study anatomy. His later sculpture—strained torsos, tightly modeled hands, twisting figures, and exposed musculature—reflects sustained anatomical study.

A modern Prado study describes his talent as exceptional but notes reports of an “indomitable” temperament and a relatively short stay in the Vallmitjana environment. That characterization belongs partly to later personality reconstruction; surviving enrollment and workshop records would be needed to establish dates precisely.

### Early works

Works attributed to the Barcelona or early formation period include:

- portraits and academic studies;
- religious figures;
- **Al·legoria de la Jove Catalunya / Alegoría de la Joven Cataluña** (1882);
- **San Juan predicando en el desierto**, the examination work with which he competed for the Rome pension.

Financial difficulty nearly ended his artistic career according to later biographies. This is a reported fact, consistent with his working-class origins, but precise debts or patrons have not been documented in the sources consulted.

## Rome and the International Exhibition Career, 1884–1890

### Spanish Academy in Rome

In 1884 Querol won, alongside sculptor **Eduardo Barrón**, a competitive state pension at the **Academia Española de Bellas Artes en Roma**.

The Prado records him as:

- **pensionado de número**, 1884–1888;
- **pensionado de mérito**, 1889–1893.

The second designation apparently extended his institutional relationship beyond the period of his continuous residence.

Rome exposed Querol to classical antiquity, Renaissance and Baroque sculpture, contemporary Italian verismo, French academic sculpture, and the international market in Carrara marble. His Roman production established the subjects and methods that remained characteristic:

- historical catastrophe;
- religious suffering;
- idealized female figures;
- densely interlocked groups;
- theatrical gesture;
- highly polished marble contrasted with rough surfaces;
- emotional narratives designed to remain legible at exhibition distance.

### Principal Roman works

- **El vencido** (1885), surviving in a patinated plaster at the Real Academia de Bellas Artes de San Fernando.
- **La Tradición** (1887): an old woman transmitting inherited memory to children. It won a first-class medal at the Exposición Nacional de Bellas Artes in Madrid and was later rewarded at Chicago.
- **Tulia pasando sobre el cadáver de su padre / Tulia** (c. 1887–1891): drawn from the Roman legend of Tullia driving over her father Servius Tullius’s corpse.
- **Sagunto / El suicidio de Sagunto** (1888): a contorted group representing the inhabitants’ legendary collective self-destruction rather than surrender to Hannibal.
- **Mater Dolorosa** (c. 1884–1888).
- **Cabeza de mujer que grita** (c. 1888).
- **San Francisco de Asís curando a los leprosos** (modeled around 1890–1891).

### Prizes and international circulation

Querol’s contemporary reputation was built through repeated exhibition awards:

- Madrid, first-class medal, 1887, for *La Tradición*;
- Barcelona Universal Exposition, first prize or gold-level distinction, 1888;
- Paris, 1889;
- Munich, 1891 and 1895;
- Chicago World’s Columbian Exposition, 1893, where *La Tradición* was rewarded;
- Vienna, 1894 or 1898 depending on the catalogue;
- Berlin, 1896 or 1898;
- Paris Universal Exposition, 1900;
- Madrid, Medal of Honor, 1906, associated with *Sagunto* in standard biographies.

Award lists vary because some sources collapse gold medals, honorary medals, diplomas, and exhibition re-showings. The individual exhibition catalogues remain the proper authority for the exact class of each distinction.

## Madrid: Official Career and the Large Studio, 1890–1901

Querol established himself in Madrid around the end of 1890. By 1893 he had created a large house-studio at **Paseo del Cisne**, at or near the corner with Calle Zurbano; the thoroughfare is now part of Paseo de Eduardo Dato.

The studio served several functions:

- personal atelier;
- school and workplace for assistants;
- modeling and enlargement facility;
- administrative office for international contracts;
- social salon visited by artists, writers, politicians, aristocrats, diplomats, and patrons;
- storage space for finished works, plaster models, casts, photographs, drawings, paintings, furniture, and antiquities.

The scale of production led contemporaries and later critics to describe it as an artistic factory. That characterization is materially credible: monumental commissions required teams of modelers, stonecutters, mold-makers, bronze founders, architects, transporters, and overseas agents.

### Recorded pupils, assistants, or studio visitors

Names preserved in the posthumous studio study include:

- Manuel González;
- Jacinto Higueras;
- José Bastida;
- Fernández de Espina;
- Rodrigo de Figueroa y Torres, Marqués de Tovar;
- Domingo Gutiérrez;
- José Martínez Banciela;
- Lorenzo Ridaura;
- Luis Pardo;
- José Vega Cruces;
- **Lorenzo Coullaut Valera**;
- Víctor Cerveto y Riva;
- José Cerveto y Riva;
- José María Larrazábal of Cuba;
- Francisca de Roda of Guatemala;
- **Pablo Gargallo**, who frequented the studio;
- **José de Creeft**, who reportedly left because he disliked its academic and quasi-industrial atmosphere.

Some spellings in the published list appear corrupted and should be checked against the original studio or exhibition documentation.

### Museo de Arte Moderno

Querol served as **vice-director of the Museo de Arte Moderno in Madrid**, usually dated 1892–1895. This appointment placed him inside the institutional machinery that selected, purchased, and displayed contemporary art.

### Monument to the Havana firefighters

One of his first major overseas commissions was the funerary monument to firefighters killed in the Havana fire of 17 May 1890. The monument was intended for Havana’s Colón Cemetery and is generally dated 1892–1897.

It established the model for Querol’s American career: design and major sculptural work in Europe, Carrara stone and/or European bronze production, maritime transport, local architectural assembly, and ceremonial inauguration by political and expatriate institutions.

### Legazpi and Urdaneta, Manila

The monument to **Miguel López de Legazpi and Andrés de Urdaneta** was inaugurated in Manila in 1893. It represented the soldier with sword and the Augustinian navigator-missionary with cross, synthesizing the military and religious dimensions of Spanish colonization.

The work later acquired conflicting political meanings:

- under Spanish rule, it celebrated imperial expansion;
- under American and postcolonial rule, it became an artifact of the former colonial capital;
- later heritage interpretation treats it as both a major work of sculpture and a monument encoding conquest.

There is no evidence that Querol traveled to Manila.

### The Biblioteca Nacional pediment controversy

In 1892 Querol obtained the commission for the sculptural pediment of the building intended for the Biblioteca y Museos Nacionales in Madrid. The ensemble, completed over the following decade and generally dated 1892–1903, contains nineteen figures centered on an allegory of Spain, with the arts, letters, and sciences.

**Documented controversy**

- The Real Academia de Bellas Artes reportedly judged competing proposals and considered designs by Jerónimo Suñol and Ponciano Ponzano’s or Trilles’s circle more congruent with the building’s classicizing architecture.
- Querol nevertheless received the commission.
- The Academy demanded modifications.
- Contemporary critics accused him of ignoring conditions, overcrowding the pediment, and filling it with wings, laurels, symbols, and decorative incident.
- His supporters defended its energy and modernity.
- Writers identified in later accounts as supporting him include **Pío Baroja, Azorín, and Gregorio Martínez Sierra**.
- Political protection from **Antonio Cánovas del Castillo** is consistently reported.

**Allegation, not proven corruption:** Opponents implied favoritism and undue political influence. No consulted source demonstrated bribery, criminal conduct, or a court finding. The controversy is best understood as an entanglement of patronage, institutional rivalry, style, and official contracting.

### Other works of the 1890s

Important commissions and exhibited works include:

- bust of Queen Regent María Cristina, 1891;
- *La Tradición* in bronze, 1889;
- *San Francisco curando a los leprosos*, marble version c. 1890–1891;
- funerary sculpture and private mausoleums;
- portraits of politicians, aristocrats, military figures, and cultural personalities;
- designs for monuments in Spain and Spanish America.

## Career at Its Height, 1901–1909

### Monument to Claudio Moyano, Madrid

The monument to educational statesman **Claudio Moyano** was commissioned around the turn of the century and inaugurated in Madrid in 1900. It joined portraiture to allegories of education and public service.

### Monument to Casilda Iturrizar, Bilbao

Querol designed the monument to philanthropist **Casilda Iturrizar**, also known as the Viuda de Epalza. Commonly dated 1901–1906, it depicts the benefactor with allegorical or grateful figures. Its public setting changed over time, and it eventually became associated with the Bilbao park bearing her name.

### Monument to Francisco de Quevedo, Madrid

Querol’s monument to **Francisco de Quevedo** was inaugurated in 1902. Its later relocation is part of Madrid’s recurring reorganization of public sculpture.

### Monument to José Elduayen, Vigo

The monument to politician and engineer **José Elduayen** belongs to the same period of official portrait monuments and was installed in Vigo.

### Monument to the Conde de Ribadedeva, Colombres

Querol completed a monument to **Manuel Ibáñez Posada, Conde de Ribadedeva**, in Colombres, Asturias, around 1903. The commission belonged to the culture of returning emigrant wealth and civic philanthropy in northern Spain.

### Monument to Colonel Francisco Bolognesi, Lima

A committee in Peru organized an international competition for a monument to **Francisco Bolognesi**, killed at Arica in 1880 during the War of the Pacific. Querol won the competition in 1902.

The monument was inaugurated in Lima in 1905 or, according to some political chronologies, ceremonially associated with events continuing into 1908. Querol represented Bolognesi wounded and collapsing while holding the flag.

**Documented later controversy**

In 1954 the principal statue was replaced by a more conventionally heroic standing figure by Artemio Ocaña. Critics had objected that Querol’s mortally wounded hero looked defeated rather than victorious. The original was moved to the Fortaleza del Real Felipe.

This was not merely an aesthetic dispute. It reflected a change in the preferred political iconography of military heroism: sacrifice and pathos gave way to triumphal firmness.

### Zaragoza: Martyrs and the Sieges

Querol created two major Zaragoza monuments:

1. **Monumento a los Mártires de la Religión y de la Patria**, commissioned earlier and inaugurated in the first decade of the century.
2. **Monumento a los Sitios de Zaragoza**, commissioned in April 1907 for the centenary of the 1808–1809 sieges.

For the latter:

- the first stone was laid on 22 March 1908;
- it was inaugurated, still not entirely finished, on 28 October 1908;
- Alfonso XIII and Queen Victoria Eugenia attended;
- the ensemble combines the allegorical figure of the patria, defenders, civilians, and ruins;
- Querol reportedly offered to perform the sculptural work without charge when questions arose about the commission’s ability to pay him adequately.

**Documented procedural criticism:** The sculptural commission was made without completion of the originally contemplated national competition. Querol’s initial proposal also failed to include the cross desired by promoters. The fee and selection attracted press discussion. No criminal or civil wrongdoing by Querol was established.

### Monument to Frederic Soler “Pitarra,” Barcelona

The monument to Catalan playwright **Frederic Soler**, known as Pitarra, was created with architect **Pere Falqués** and inaugurated in Barcelona in 1906. Later modernist and avant-garde critics treated it as an example of excessive official rhetoric. A 1935 Catalan article mockingly called it a monument “perpetrated” by Querol and Falqués, illustrating the collapse of his reputation within a generation.

### Palace of Fomento groups: *La Gloria y los Pegasos*

Querol produced the colossal rooftop groups for the former Ministry of Fomento, now Spain’s Ministry of Agriculture, opposite Atocha station:

- a central winged allegory commonly called **La Gloria**, linked to Science and Art;
- lateral Pegasus groups associated with Agriculture and Industry, and Philosophy and Letters.

The original Carrara marble groups proved structurally and materially troublesome because of weight and weathering. They were eventually replaced on the roof by bronze replicas, while parts of the marble originals were relocated to street level at Plaza de Legazpi and Glorieta de Cádiz.

This afterlife has contributed to confusion over which visible figures are original, replicated, restored, or regrouped.

### Mausoleum of Antonio Cánovas del Castillo

Cánovas had been Querol’s decisive political patron. Querol later created his monumental tomb for the Panteón de Hombres Ilustres at Atocha, generally dated 1906–1908.

The recumbent statesman, grieving female figure, reliefs, and architectural framework made the monument one of Querol’s most ambitious fusions of sculpture and architecture. The commission also embodied the reciprocal structure of Restoration-era patronage: the artist monumentalized the statesman who had helped make his official career.

### Panteón Guirao

Lawyer and politician **Luis Federico Guirao y Girada** commissioned a mausoleum after the 1907 death of his wife, **Luisa Sancho Mata**.

Querol designed and modeled the work in 1908–1909. He died before its material execution was complete; his studio finished it around 1911. It remains one of the strongest examples of Spanish modernist funerary sculpture.

### Monument to Segismundo Moret, Cádiz

Querol’s monument to statesman **Segismundo Moret** was inaugurated in Cádiz shortly before the sculptor’s death in 1909. The monument was later moved and reconfigured. Its near coincidence with Querol’s final illness made it a recurring feature of his obituaries.

## International Projects and Major Geographical Connections

### Cuba

- Monument to the Havana firefighters, Colón Cemetery.
- Cuban pupils or assistants in his Madrid studio.
- His work circulated within Spanish colonial and postcolonial commemorative networks.

Whether Querol personally traveled to Cuba is not established by the strongest consulted sources. Treccani reports frequent trips to America, but individual itineraries require confirmation.

### Philippines

- Monument to Legazpi and Urdaneta, Manila, inaugurated 1893.
- No verified personal journey located.

### Peru

- Won the Bolognesi competition in 1902.
- Monument produced in Europe and installed in Lima.
- The later replacement of the central figure became a major posthumous controversy.

### Ecuador

Querol became involved with the project for the **Columna a los Próceres del 9 de Octubre** in Guayaquil.

The chronology is complicated:

- early approaches or contacts are reported from the 1890s;
- the committee awarded him the project in 1907;
- he began work in 1908;
- he considered traveling to Ecuador;
- his death left the monument incomplete;
- José L. Soria, José Montserrat, R. Staccioli, and others completed or supplied components.

The finished monument therefore embodies Querol’s design but not exclusive execution.

### Uruguay

Querol won a competition for a monument to **Giuseppe Garibaldi** in Montevideo, reportedly defeating Italian sculptor Ezio Ceccarelli. He shipped a carved granite base, but his death halted the project. Juan Manuel Ferrari inherited the work and also died before completion; Juan D’Aniello ultimately completed the monument, inaugurated in 1934. A painted representation of Querol’s design was preserved in Montevideo.

### Argentina

Querol’s largest posthumous international presence was in Argentina.

#### *Sagunto*

A version of *Sagunto* entered Buenos Aires’s public collections and was moved several times. Its exposed bodies were reportedly denounced as indecent or “impúdicas,” reflecting conflict over nudity in civic space.

#### Monument to General Justo José de Urquiza, Paraná

Querol won the competition in 1908 and began the pedestal and portions of the sculptural program. After his death, **Mariano Benlliure** completed the monument, inaugurated in 1920. Attribution should therefore be divided between Querol’s project and initial work and Benlliure’s completion.

#### Mitre competition

Querol was invited to compete for the monument to General Bartolomé Mitre with **Jules-Félix Coutan**, Davide Calandra, and Edoardo Rubino. Querol withdrew in October of the relevant competition year; Calandra and Rubino received the commission.

#### Monument to the Carta Magna and the Four Argentine Regions

In 1908 the Spanish community in Argentina organized a monument for the centenary of the May Revolution. It is commonly called the **Monumento de los Españoles**, though its formal title is *A la Carta Magna y las Cuatro Regiones Argentinas*.

Querol designed a vast architectural-sculptural ensemble:

- a high central structure;
- the Republic and constitutional allegory;
- reliefs of Spanish-Argentine fraternity;
- figures of the Andes, Río de la Plata, Chaco, and Pampas;
- fountains and extensive bronze and marble decoration.

Querol died before execution was substantially complete. Cipriano Folgueras and Querol’s workshop continued it. Delays followed from:

- litigation over Querol’s estate;
- the complexity of fabrication;
- changes of sculptors and contractors;
- maritime shipment;
- a shipwreck involving components;
- administrative and financial complications.

Although intended for 1910, the monument was inaugurated only on **25 May 1927**. The result is a Querol design completed over many years by other hands.

### Mexico

Architect **Adamo Boari** commissioned Querol to make four bronze Pegasus groups for the Teatro Nacional, later the Palacio de Bellas Artes in Mexico City. Querol also conceived a projected fountain called *La Gloria*.

He modeled and cast or prepared the groups before his death. Shipped components reached Veracruz in 1911 and suffered handling damage. The sculptures were eventually installed outside the Palacio de Bellas Artes rather than in their originally intended roof positions.

No evidence located establishes that Querol personally visited Mexico.

## Companies, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Studio business structure

Querol’s monument-making was a business as well as an artistic practice. The studio depended on:

- competitive submissions;
- government and municipal contracts;
- private funerary commissions;
- assistants and subcontractors;
- Carrara marble suppliers;
- bronze foundries;
- architects and engineers;
- maritime transport;
- local installation committees;
- staged payment schedules.

### Carrara marble and bronze

Specialist biographies report that Querol dealt commercially in **Carrara marble** and promoted or participated in bronze-foundry enterprises. His extended Roman and Italian connections made Carrara a central material and commercial base.

The precise legal names, capitalization, ownership percentages, partners, and balance sheets of these ventures were not found. It is therefore safest to describe him as a businessman or intermediary in marble and foundry production, not as the conclusively documented sole proprietor of a particular company.

### Wealth and property

Contemporary notices describe Querol as dying famous and wealthy. His principal identifiable property was the large Paseo del Cisne house-studio and the inventory it contained.

Evidence of substantial wealth includes:

- simultaneous international commissions;
- ownership or control of a specialized studio;
- extensive collections and models;
- valuable outstanding contractual payments;
- prolonged inheritance litigation.

No verified probate valuation or complete asset schedule was located. Claims that he left “a great fortune” are plausible but remain qualitative.

### Patents and inventions

No patent attributable to Querol was located. His technical achievements lay in monumental design, enlargement, material coordination, and combining sculpture with architectural and hydraulic settings rather than patented invention.

## Political Activity

Querol aligned himself with the dynastic **Conservative Party** during the Bourbon Restoration.

### Election of 1907

He was elected deputy for **Roquetes**, Tarragona, in the general election of 21 April 1907.

A modern study of Catalan Restoration conservatism, drawing on electoral and archival material, records:

- 5,846 votes;
- approximately 69.04 percent;
- intervention by the minister of the Interior and local political organizer Canals to secure his success;
- the interpretation that the seat functioned as official recognition crowning his public career.

**Documented political patronage:** A surviving ministerial telegram reportedly requested intervention to ensure Querol’s victory.

**Interpretive conclusion:** His election belonged to the controlled, patronage-based electoral system of the Restoration. This supports descriptions of him as an officially favored or *encasillado* candidate. It does not by itself prove ballot fraud personally committed by Querol.

### Parliamentary correspondence and patronage

The catalogue of Eduardo Dato’s archive preserves a revealing series of Querol letters from 1907–1909. They show him:

- asking that a pending appeal involving doctor Jaume Ferran not be obstructed;
- requesting tickets for the congressional galleries;
- recommending names for judges and prosecutors in his district;
- recommending a candidate for procurator of the Bank of Spain;
- recommending candidates for cathedral positions in Tortosa;
- promising more regular attendance at parliamentary sessions;
- supporting free distillation of wine requested by Tarragona agricultural interests;
- seeking gallery tickets for an Argentine friend and family;
- asking to be included in the Conservative Party’s official electoral lists;
- recommending a photographer for a post in the Navigation and Fisheries administration.

These letters document routine Restoration patronage and job brokerage. They are also the strongest evidence against an overly idealized account of Querol’s parliamentary career.

### Political ideas

The full parliamentary speeches were not available in the consulted results. Guinda Pérez’s doctoral study characterizes them as reflecting contemporary **regenerationism**. His known interventions appear to have focused more on constituency administration, agriculture, professional appointments, and cultural prestige than on a systematic political doctrine.

Querol was a monarchist within the Restoration establishment and maintained access to Alfonso XIII’s court, Conservative ministers, aristocratic patrons, and official institutions.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Artistic Development

### Religion

Querol created many Catholic subjects:

- *San Juan predicando en el desierto*;
- *Mater Dolorosa*;
- *San Francisco curando a los leprosos*;
- martyrs of religion;
- funerary angels and allegories;
- the Cánovas mausoleum.

His correspondence recommending appointments in Tortosa Cathedral shows practical contact with ecclesiastical patronage. No evidence located establishes the intensity of his personal religious observance, conversion, anticlericalism, or formal religious affiliation beyond ordinary Catholic Spain.

### Political-historical imagery

Querol’s public imagery joined:

- Spanish national history;
- monarchical legitimacy;
- Catholic sacrifice;
- imperial and colonial memory;
- liberal constitutional allegory;
- military martyrdom;
- reconciliation between Spain and newly independent American republics.

This flexibility helped him serve incompatible commemorative programs: Spanish colonial expansion in Manila; Peruvian resistance to Chile; Argentine constitutionalism; Ecuadorian independence from Spain; Garibaldi’s republican-national mythology; and the Restoration monarchy.

### Artistic style

Terms applied to him include:

- academic realism;
- eclecticism;
- late Romanticism;
- naturalism;
- modernisme or art nouveau;
- symbolism;
- “impressionistic” modeling;
- official monumental rhetoric.

The “Querolian” manner identified by later scholars consists of:

- crowded compositions;
- spiraling or diagonal movement;
- drapery that dissolves into atmospheric surface;
- bodies emerging from rough stone;
- expressive heads and hands;
- allegorical abundance;
- theatrical legibility;
- integration of sculpture, architecture, fountains, inscriptions, and urban space.

He has occasionally been compared with Auguste Rodin, Jules Dalou, Medardo Rosso, and contemporary Italian verists. These are stylistic comparisons; no documented teacher-student relationship with those artists was found.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Teachers and formative figures

- **Ramon Cerveto Bestratén:** first teacher in Tortosa.
- **Domènec Talarn:** workshop master in Barcelona.
- **Agapit and Venanci Vallmitjana:** workshop training and connection to Barcelona academic sculpture.
- **Academia Española in Rome:** institutional patron and internationalizing environment.

### Political patrons

- **Antonio Cánovas del Castillo:** decisive patron, protector, and later subject of Querol’s monumental funerary work.
- **Eduardo Dato:** recipient of repeated political and patronage requests.
- **Alfonso XIII and Queen Victoria Eugenia:** royal participants in official ceremonies rather than documented intimate friends.
- Ministers, diplomats, municipal committees, colonial officials, and Spanish expatriate associations formed the wider commissioning network.

### Artistic collaborators and successors

- **Pere Falqués:** architectural collaborator on the Pitarra monument.
- **Adamo Boari:** architect who commissioned the Mexican Pegasus groups.
- **Cipriano Folgueras:** continued the Buenos Aires monument.
- **Mariano Benlliure:** completed the Urquiza monument after Querol’s death.
- **José Montserrat, José L. Soria, and R. Staccioli:** among those who completed or fabricated Guayaquil elements.
- Querol’s assistants collectively executed substantial portions of posthumous commissions.

### Pupils and younger sculptors

Lorenzo Coullaut Valera, Jacinto Higueras, Pablo Gargallo, José de Creeft, and numerous less-known Spanish and Latin American artists passed through or frequented the studio.

Not all should be called formal pupils. Gargallo and de Creeft’s presence is evidence of the studio’s centrality, not necessarily endorsement of Querol’s aesthetic.

### Critics and rivals

- Designers competing for the Biblioteca Nacional commission opposed or were preferred over him by parts of the Academy.
- **Aniceto Marinas** defeated Querol and José Alcoverro in the 1902 election for a numerical seat at the Real Academia de Bellas Artes de San Fernando.
- **Jules-Félix Coutan**, Davide Calandra, and Edoardo Rubino competed with him for the Mitre monument.
- Later critic **Juan Antonio Gaya Nuño** treated Querol as a “case,” emblematic of the excesses of official late-nineteenth-century sculpture.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### 1. Political favoritism in official commissions

**Documented fact:** Querol enjoyed the support of Cánovas and other senior political figures.

**Contemporary allegation:** Critics suggested that this influence helped him obtain commissions over artists whose designs academic juries preferred.

**Evidence:** The Biblioteca Nacional dispute and his politically managed parliamentary candidacy establish patronage and institutional intervention.

**Not established:** bribery, fraud, falsification, criminal conspiracy, or a judicial finding of corruption.

### 2. The “industrial” studio and authorship

**Documented fact:** Querol employed many assistants and subcontractors.

**Criticism:** Opponents described his production as industrial, formulaic, and dependent on an “army” of assistants.

**Assessment:** Large monuments could not be executed by a single hand. The unresolved issue is the exact division between Querol’s design and modeling and assistants’ enlargement, carving, casting, and finishing. Posthumous monuments require especially careful attribution.

### 3. Academic excess and rhetorical overcrowding

Critics objected to:

- too many figures;
- strained gestures;
- sentimentalism;
- excessive allegory;
- insufficient subordination to architecture;
- repeated formulas of wings, grief, flags, collapsing bodies, and drapery.

These are documented critical judgments, not factual misconduct.

### 4. Real Academia defeat, 1902

Querol sought the vacant academy seat following Jerónimo Suñol’s death. The candidates included Aniceto Marinas and José Alcoverro. Marinas was elected on 23 November 1902.

The defeat is significant because Querol possessed international medals and official patronage but did not command unanimous institutional confidence.

### 5. Zaragoza commission procedure

The Monument to the Sieges was initially associated with a proposed national competition but was directly entrusted to Querol.

**Documented question:** bypassing the anticipated competition drew criticism.

**Querol’s response:** he reportedly offered his sculptural labor free of charge.

**Unresolved:** the final accounting, whether “free” excluded material and workshop costs, and whether rival sculptors formally protested.

### 6. Parliamentary machine politics

The 1907 election was facilitated by ministerial intervention. His letters to Dato reveal active participation in recommendations for judicial, ecclesiastical, banking, and government posts.

**Documented fact:** patronage requests.

**Historical classification:** normal but contested Restoration clientelism.

**Not established:** a criminal conviction, indictment, or formal parliamentary sanction.

### 7. Silvia Querol’s inheritance case

**Documented litigation:** Silvia’s filial status and inheritance rights were contested after Querol’s death.

**Court outcome reported in 1913:** she was recognized as his daughter and sole or principal heir.

**Financial consequence:** overseas committees were warned not to pay Querol’s relatives or other purported successors.

This litigation probably contributed to delays in completing international commissions and dispersal of the studio.

### 8. Contractual incompletion

Querol died with major commissions unfinished in Buenos Aires, Paraná, Guayaquil, Montevideo, Mexico, Tortosa, and Madrid.

**Documented fact:** works were incomplete.

**Not evidence of fraud:** available sources attribute the situation principally to sudden death and the scale of concurrent work. However, the number of simultaneous contracts exposed commissioners and heirs to serious authorship, payment, and delivery disputes.

### 9. Adversarial-search findings

Dedicated searches in Spanish, Catalan, and English combined Querol’s name with scandal, controversy, fraud, accusations, lawsuit, plagiarism, corruption, conspiracy, criticism, and recent dates.

The search found substantial negative or contested material concerning:

- favoritism and official patronage;
- Restoration electoral management;
- studio authorship;
- bypassed competitions;
- academy criticism;
- the inheritance lawsuit;
- delays and incomplete contracts;
- later rejection or relocation of some monuments;
- nudity controversies surrounding *Sagunto*;
- posthumous criticism of his style.

It did **not** uncover reliable evidence that Querol was criminally investigated, indicted, or convicted for fraud, corruption, money laundering, violence, abuse, or another crime. No credible modern conspiracy narrative centered on him was found in material published during 2024–2026.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The baker’s son who sculpted bread”

See the childhood section. This is Querol’s foundational hagiographic story: strongly rooted in early biography but not independently documented.

### The solitary genius

**Myth:** Querol personally carved enormous international monuments through inexhaustible energy.

**Evidence against a literal reading:** he managed a large studio and supply chain. Assistants executed many stages, and other sculptors completed several works.

**Effect:** early publicity magnified the genius narrative; later critics reversed it into the hostile myth of a commercially successful operator whose assistants did the real work. The evidence supports neither extreme.

### “Ars longa, vita brevis”

Rodolfo Gil’s 1910 monograph framed the unfinished monuments as proof of almost superhuman fecundity cut short by death. The phrase helped canonize his sudden death as the end of a heroic race against time.

### The artist universally acclaimed abroad and persecuted at home

Querol and his admirers emphasized international medals against domestic academic resistance.

**Evidence for:** he was heavily awarded and commissioned abroad; the Biblioteca Nacional dispute and academy defeat were real.

**Evidence against the full myth:** he was hardly excluded in Spain. He held a museum office, received royal and governmental commissions, secured political support, accumulated wealth, and became a deputy.

### Querol as Rodin’s Spanish equivalent

Later comparisons to Rodin or French modern sculpture often seek to rescue Querol from the category of academic official art.

**Evidence:** both used expressive surfaces, bodily fragments, emotional gesture, and figures emerging from stone.

**Against direct equivalence:** Querol remained committed to narrative legibility, civic allegory, architectural ensembles, and official patronage. No personal relationship or direct acknowledged discipleship under Rodin was found.

### The disputed birth year

Older obituaries and reference works gave 1863, and at least one source gives 28 December 1860. Modern scholarship favors 17 May 1860.

The disagreement probably reflects early biographical repetition rather than self-created deception. No evidence was found that Querol intentionally falsified his age.

### The ideal heirless bachelor

Early posthumous family plans for a museum sometimes treated the estate as if it passed straightforwardly to collateral relatives. Silvia’s successful filiation suit disrupted that narrative. Her court recognition establishes that the public image of Querol as an unmarried man without direct issue was incomplete.

## Awards, Honors, Offices, and Appointments

Reported distinctions include:

- first-class medal, Exposición Nacional de Bellas Artes, Madrid, 1887;
- major award, Barcelona Universal Exposition, 1888;
- medals or diplomas at Paris, Munich, Chicago, Vienna, and Berlin;
- Medal of Honor or equivalent highest recognition at Madrid, 1906;
- **Grand Cross of the Civil Order of Alfonso XII**, 1902;

- deputy to the Cortes for Roquetes, 1907–1909.

Individual foreign decorations require verification against their respective official registers.

## Death, Funeral, and Estate

Querol died in Madrid on 14 December 1909, reportedly from mitral stenosis associated with rheumatic disease. He was forty-nine and remained professionally active.

His body lay in state in his Paseo del Cisne studio, surrounded by works and models. The funeral attracted officials, artists, political colleagues, and representatives of commissioning committees. He was buried in the Sacramental de San Justo.

At death, works still underway included:

- the Buenos Aires Monument to the Carta Magna;
- the Urquiza monument in Paraná;
- the Guayaquil independence column;
- the Garibaldi monument in Montevideo;
- Mexican Pegasus groups and fountain project;
- Panteón Guirao;
- other commissions and studio pieces.

### Failed museum projects

Immediately after his death, relatives denied reports that the works would simply be auctioned. Newspapers announced two shifting plans:

1. preserve the Madrid studio as a museum;
2. transfer the collection to Tortosa and establish a Querol museum there.

Neither succeeded.

Silvia later offered works to Tortosa and other institutions. The Madrid property disappeared as an intact ensemble, and models, sculptures, drawings, paintings, furniture, and documentary material were dispersed or lost.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate commemoration

Rodolfo Gil’s *Agustín Querol* appeared in 1910 as a lavish memorial monograph. It presented him as a prodigy of work, patriotic art, and international achievement.

His workshop and collaborators continued executing contracts, so Querol’s name remained attached to monuments inaugurated many years after his death.

### Rapid decline

His prestige declined quickly after 1910. Several factors converged:

- avant-garde rejection of academic narrative sculpture;
- political rejection of Restoration culture;
- Catalan modernist and Noucentista preference for different formal disciplines;
- the association of his work with monarchy, empire, and official competitions;
- dispersal of the studio;
- inability to distinguish his hand from workshop completion;
- relocation or alteration of monuments;
- the physical deterioration of marble exposed outdoors.

The Barcelona press could already mock the Pitarra monument by 1935.

### Mid-century rejection and alteration

The most dramatic repudiation was Peru’s replacement of Querol’s dying Bolognesi with a standing heroic figure in 1954.

Other works were:

- moved as streets and plazas changed;
- dismantled;
- recast;
- completed by others;
- stripped of their original architectural or political context.

### Scholarly recovery

Important stages in reassessment include:

- José Francés’s 1960 memorial essay;
- José Luis Melendreras Gimeno’s studies of Spanish eclectic sculpture;
- scholarship on public monuments in Spain and Latin America;
- Antonio Guinda Pérez’s 2009 doctoral thesis and 195-item catalogue;
- Leticia Azcue Brea’s study of Querol’s Roman formation;
- conservation research on the Madrid and Mexican Pegasus groups;
- municipal and museum cataloguing in Zaragoza, Madrid, Barcelona, Buenos Aires, Lima, Bilbao, Tarragona, and Tortosa.

Current scholarship generally treats him neither as a neglected modernist genius nor merely as an empty official rhetorician, but as a central organizer of the international monument industry linking Spain, Italy, Spanish America, and the Philippines around 1900.

### Tortosa

Tortosa has periodically attempted to recover Querol as a major local artist. The municipal purchase of his birthplace and discussion of restoration and exhibition projects reflect renewed civic interest, although the museum envisioned after his death was never realized in its original form.

## Works by Agustí Querol

### Authorship categories

- **Autograph or substantially designed by Querol:** works modeled or designed during his lifetime, though normal workshop assistance must be assumed.
- **Workshop-assisted:** large commissions executed through the Madrid studio under his direction.
- **Posthumously completed:** based on Querol designs or models but finished by pupils, successors, architects, or foundries after 1909.
- **Attributed or incompletely documented:** known through photographs, exhibition references, or later catalogues.
- **Literary attribution:** articles or chronicles connected with “El Plutarco del Pueblo”; individual authorship remains insufficiently reconstructed.

### Selected sculptural catalogue

This is a substantial working bibliography of principal works, not a replacement for Guinda Pérez’s 195-entry catalogue.

#### Early and Roman works

- *Al·legoria de la Jove Catalunya / Alegoría de la Joven Cataluña* (1882).
- *San Juan predicando en el desierto* (1883–1884), pension competition work.
- *El vencido* (1885), plaster.
- *La Tradición* (1887), plaster; later bronze and other versions.
- *Tulia pasando sobre el cadáver de su padre / Tulia* (c. 1887–1891), marble.
- *Sagunto / El suicidio de Sagunto* (1888), marble.
- *Mater Dolorosa* (c. 1884–1888), Carrara marble.
- *Cabeza de mujer que grita* (c. 1888), marble.
- *San Francisco de Asís curando a los leprosos* (c. 1890–1891), marble.
- *Cabeza de san Francisco* (late nineteenth or early twentieth century), bronze.
- Numerous female busts, portrait heads, and anatomical or expressive studies.

#### Monumental and architectural sculpture

- Monument to the Havana firefighters, Colón Cemetery, Havana (designed c. 1891–1892; installed during the 1890s).
- Monument to Legazpi and Urdaneta, Manila (inaugurated 1893).

- Monument to the Martyrs of Religion and the Fatherland, Zaragoza (c. 1904–1906).
- Monument to Frederic Soler “Pitarra,” Barcelona, with Pere Falqués (1906).
- Rooftop groups *La Gloria y los Pegasos*, Ministry of Fomento, Madrid (c. 1905).
- Mausoleum of Antonio Cánovas del Castillo, Madrid (c. 1906–1908).
- Monument to the Sieges of Zaragoza (commissioned 1907; inaugurated 1908).

- Project for the monument to Bartolomé Mitre, Buenos Aires; withdrawn from competition.
- Unfinished or unrealized monument for Tortosa.

#### Funerary and private works

- Monumental tombs and mausoleums for private patrons in Madrid, Bilbao, and other Spanish cities.
- Sepulchral relief for Pío Bermejillo Martínez-Negrete, Balmaseda.
- Portrait busts of royalty, politicians, military officers, writers, patrons, women, and children.
- Decorative sculpture, medallions, reliefs, drawings, paintings, and architectural studies.

### Writings and correspondence

#### “El Plutarco del Pueblo,” c. 1894–1897

Specialist biographies attribute journalistic or popular-biographical activity to Querol under this name. However:

- *El Plutarco del pueblo* also existed as a newspaper section or programmatic rubric;
- unsigned articles cannot automatically be assigned to Querol;
- no authoritative complete bibliography of his contributions was located online.

Accordingly, the corpus should be classified as **attributed but incompletely verified**.

#### Parliamentary speeches, 1907–1909

His interventions and proposals survive in the *Diario de Sesiones del Congreso*. Guinda Pérez studied them as part of Querol’s documentary record. A complete speech-by-speech bibliography requires searching the digitized parliamentary proceedings under spelling variants.

#### Correspondence

Known documentary bodies include:

- letters to Eduardo Dato, 1907–1909, preserved in the Real Academia de la Historia;
- letter to Joaquín Ferrer, 9 August 1898, catalogued by the Biblioteca Nacional de España;
- letters connected with municipal and monument contracts;
- posthumous correspondence from Silvia Querol to Tortosa, Madrid, and museums.

These are archival documents, not collected publications by Querol.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity or residence in the same city is excluded.

### Jules-Félix Coutan — documented fact

Querol and French sculptor **Jules-Félix Coutan** were invited competitors or finalists in the international process for the monument to General Bartolomé Mitre in Buenos Aires during the first decade of the twentieth century. The other principal competitors included Davide Calandra and Edoardo Rubino. Querol subsequently withdrew; Calandra and Rubino obtained the commission.

This is a documented professional rivalry within the transatlantic monument market. No personal meeting or correspondence between Querol and Coutan was located.

### Christopher Columbus — documented artistic subject, not a personal relationship

Querol prepared or was associated with an unrealized monument project to **Cristóbal Colón**. Columbus also belonged to the imperial-historical iconography surrounding Spanish commemorative sculpture.

Because Columbus died centuries before Querol, this is a subject relationship rather than a biographical crossing.

### Giuseppe Garibaldi — documented artistic subject, not a personal relationship

Querol won the competition for a monument to **Giuseppe Garibaldi** in Montevideo and supplied part of the base before his death. The project was later completed by other sculptors.

This is a posthumous commemorative relationship, not a personal meeting.

### Antoni Gaudí i Cornet — no documented connection found

Querol and Gaudí were Catalan contemporaries connected broadly with late nineteenth-century Barcelona and modernisme, but no consulted source documented a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, or acknowledged influence between them.

### Auguste Rodin — comparison only; no documented personal connection

Later writers compare Querol’s expressive surfaces and partial emergence of figures from stone with Rodin. No evidence was found that Querol studied with, corresponded with, met, or explicitly acknowledged Rodin as an influence.

### Pío Collivadino, Alejandro Christophersen, Francesco Tamburini, Jules Dormal, Víctor Meano, Mario Palanti, and other atlas figures associated with Argentine monumental or architectural culture — no direct connection established

Querol’s work entered the Buenos Aires commemorative landscape inhabited by these artists and architects, but the consulted evidence did not document a direct working or personal relationship.

### General finding

Despite Querol’s large transatlantic network, only the professional competition with Jules-Félix Coutan and the commemorative subject relationships with Columbus and Garibaldi could be established from the supplied atlas roster. No further crossings should be inferred from shared institutions, cities, styles, or historical periods.

## Chronology

- **17 May 1860:** Born in Tortosa, according to the strongest modern consensus.
- **1860s–1870s:** Raised in a baker’s family; begins modeling, traditionally said to have used bread dough.
- **c. 1876–1878:** Studies drawing and modeling with Ramon Cerveto Bestratén.
- **c. 1878:** Leaves the family bakery and moves to Barcelona.
- **1878–1882:** Works with Domènec Talarn and the Vallmitjana brothers; attends La Llotja and reportedly studies anatomy at the Hospital de la Santa Creu.
- **1882:** Produces *Alegoría de la Joven Cataluña*.
- **1883–1884:** Wins the Rome pension with *San Juan predicando en el desierto*.
- **1884:** Enters the Academia Española de Bellas Artes en Roma as pensionado de número.
- **1885:** Produces *El vencido*.
- **1887:** *La Tradición* wins a first-class medal in Madrid.
- **1888:** Receives major recognition at the Barcelona Universal Exposition; produces or exhibits *Sagunto*.
- **1889:** Pension status transitions toward pensionado de mérito; international exhibition recognition in Paris.
- **c. 1890:** Establishes his main professional base in Madrid.
- **1890–1891:** Produces *San Francisco curando a los leprosos* and major Roman-derived marbles.
- **1891:** Munich award; royal portrait work.
- **1891–1897:** Designs and executes the Havana firefighters’ monument.
- **1892:** Receives Biblioteca Nacional pediment commission amid academic controversy.
- **1892–1895:** Vice-director of Madrid’s Museo de Arte Moderno.
- **1893:** Monument to Legazpi and Urdaneta inaugurated in Manila; *La Tradición* recognized at Chicago.
- **1893:** Large Paseo del Cisne studio in operation.
- **1894–1897:** Period associated with “El Plutarco del Pueblo.”
- **1895–1900:** Further international awards at Munich, Berlin, Vienna, and Paris.
- **1900:** Monument to Claudio Moyano inaugurated in Madrid.
- **1901–1906:** Casilda Iturrizar monument developed for Bilbao.
- **1902:** Wins Bolognesi competition; monument to Quevedo; receives Grand Cross of Alfonso XII; loses Real Academia seat to Aniceto Marinas.
- **1903:** Biblioteca Nacional sculptural ensemble substantially completed; Conde de Ribadedeva monument.
- **1904–1906:** Zaragoza Martyrs monument.
- **1905:** Bolognesi monument inaugurated in Lima; *La Gloria y los Pegasos* associated with the Ministry of Fomento.
- **1906:** Pitarra monument inaugurated in Barcelona; Medal of Honor in Madrid.
- **April 1907:** Commissioned for the Monument to the Sieges of Zaragoza.
- **21 April 1907:** Elected Conservative deputy for Roquetes with 5,846 votes.
- **1907–1909:** Corresponds with Eduardo Dato regarding appointments, constituents, agricultural policy, parliamentary access, and future candidacy.
- **22 March 1908:** First stone of the Zaragoza Sieges monument laid.
- **1908:** Wins Urquiza monument competition; receives Guayaquil commission; begins Buenos Aires centenary project; designs Panteón Guirao.
- **28 October 1908:** Zaragoza Sieges monument inaugurated before full completion.
- **1908–1909:** Produces Mexican Pegasus groups for Adamo Boari’s Teatro Nacional.
- **1909:** Moret monument inaugurated in Cádiz; major American commissions remain underway.
- **14 December 1909:** Dies in Madrid from a reported rheumatic cardiac condition.
- **December 1909:** Lies in state in his studio; buried at San Justo; relatives announce museum plans.
- **1910:** Rodolfo Gil publishes the memorial monograph *Agustín Querol*.
- **c. 1911:** Querol’s workshop completes Panteón Guirao; Mexican sculptures shipped.
- **1913:** Silvia Querol announces a final judgment recognizing her as Querol’s daughter and heir.
- **1920:** Urquiza monument, completed by Mariano Benlliure, inaugurated in Paraná.
- **1927:** Buenos Aires Monument to the Carta Magna inaugurated seventeen years after the centenary for which it was intended.
- **1934:** Garibaldi monument in Montevideo, successively completed by other sculptors, inaugurated.
- **1935:** Catalan press records openly hostile judgment of the Pitarra monument.
- **1949:** Silvia offers Madrid a bust of Quevedo; municipality declines.
- **1954:** Peru replaces Querol’s dying Bolognesi with Artemio Ocaña’s standing figure.
- **1960:** José Francés publishes a memorial reassessment.
- **1963:** Silvia Querol offers works to the Museo de Bellas Artes de Bilbao.
- **1990s–2000s:** Renewed scholarship on Spanish and Latin American commemorative sculpture.
- **2009:** Antonio Guinda Pérez defends a doctoral thesis cataloguing 195 Querol works; centenary studies examine the lost studio and failed museum.
