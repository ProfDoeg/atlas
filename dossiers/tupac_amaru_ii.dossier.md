# José Gabriel Condorcanqui Noguera: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

José Gabriel Condorcanqui Noguera, better known as Túpac Amaru II, lived in the eighteenth-century Viceroyalty of Peru and died in 1781. Accordingly, modern categories such as “Peruvian national,” “independence leader,” and “abolitionist” require historical qualification.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records, correspondence, judicial papers, or independently corroborated archival evidence.
- **Reported fact:** stated in reputable scholarship but not directly verified here against the original manuscript.
- **Disputed interpretation:** historians agree on the underlying evidence but differ over its meaning.
- **Colonial allegation or court finding:** assertion made by the prosecuting viceregal authorities; not equivalent to a modern impartial adjudication.
- **Later tradition, legend, or myth:** posthumous story without adequate contemporary confirmation.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Principal name:** José Gabriel Condorcanqui Noguera.
- **Contemporary variants:** José Gabriel Túpac Amaru; Joseph/José Gabriel Tupa Amaro; José Gabriel Condorcanqui y Noguera. Eighteenth-century spelling is inconsistent.
- **Historical name:** Túpac Amaru II, adopted from or asserted through descent from Túpac Amaru I, the last ruler of Vilcabamba executed in 1572.
- **Titles and offices:** curaca or cacique and governor of Surimana, Tungasuca, and Pampamarca; noble Indian (*indio noble*); merchant and muleteer.
- **Regnal styles attributed to him:** “José I,” “Inca,” “lord,” and royal formulas employed in rebel communications. The authenticity of the most extravagant proclamation—“King of Peru, Quito, Chile, Buenos Aires … and Great Paititi”—has been challenged, and at least one English-language scholarly study classifies the document as a forgery.
- **Born:** probably March 1738 at Surimana, in the corregimiento of Tinta, Bishopric of Cuzco, Viceroyalty of Peru.
- **Date dispute:** Charles Walker and records discussed by John Rowe support **10 March 1738**; many Peruvian commemorative and reference works give **19 March 1738**. His baptism is commonly dated **1 May 1738**. Earlier literature sometimes assigned 1740 or 1742.
- **Died:** 18 May 1781, Plaza Mayor, Cuzco.
- **Cause of death:** judicial execution for rebellion and treason. An attempted dismemberment by four horses failed; he was then killed—contemporary and near-contemporary accounts generally say beheaded—and his body was cut apart and publicly dispersed.
- **Languages:** Quechua and Spanish; later biographical tradition credits him with Latin learned at the Jesuit college, although the degree of fluency is uncertain.
- **Religion:** publicly and institutionally Catholic throughout his documented life. Rebel texts invoked God, the Virgin, churches, priests, and legitimate monarchy even while the movement attacked colonial officials and eventually assumed sovereign attributes.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and sibling

José Gabriel was the son of:

- **Miguel Condorcanqui Usquionsa (or del Camino-Usquionsa) Túpac Amaru**, curaca of Surimana, Tungasuca, and Pampamarca.
- **Rosa or María Rosa Noguera Valenzuela**, of Tinta.

A brother, **Clemente**, is recorded as born in 1736. José Gabriel’s parents died while he was still young—his father in 1750—and paternal uncle Marcos Condorcanqui and maternal uncle José Noguera reportedly served successively as guardians or administrators during his minority.

### Claimed Inca descent

José Gabriel maintained that he descended from **Túpac Amaru I** through a female line, conventionally reconstructed through Juana Pilco-Huaco/Pilcohuaco. The claim underpinned his hereditary curacazgo, exemptions, social precedence, and effort to obtain recognition connected with the vacant Marquisate of Santiago de Oropesa.

This requires three distinctions:

1. **Documented fact:** Condorcanqui’s family belonged to the recognized provincial indigenous nobility and held hereditary curacal office.
2. **Documented claim under litigation:** José Gabriel asserted direct descent from Túpac Amaru I and submitted genealogical evidence.
3. **Unresolved genealogical question:** surviving records do not permit every link in the claimed descent to be treated as independently proved beyond dispute.

From 1777, **Diego Felipe Betancur** advanced a competing genealogy and denounced Condorcanqui as an impostor. Condorcanqui in turn alleged that Betancur’s agent and son-in-law Vicente José García removed, altered, or manipulated parish records. John Rowe and later historians found serious irregularities in the Betancur evidence, but this does not automatically authenticate every element of Condorcanqui’s reconstruction.

### Marriage and children

On **25 May 1760**, José Gabriel married **Micaela Bastidas Puyucahua** at Surimana. She was not merely a consort: surviving letters document her work as administrator, recruiter, logistician, intelligence coordinator, political adviser, and military strategist.

Their known sons were:

- **Hipólito Túpac Amaru Bastidas**, born about 1761; a rebel officer; executed with his parents on 18 May 1781.
- **Mariano Túpac Amaru Bastidas**, born about 1762; imprisoned and condemned to exile; died during the family’s forced journey toward Spain.
- **Fernando Túpac Amaru Bastidas**, born about 1768; compelled to witness the executions, imprisoned in Callao and Cádiz, later educated under surveillance in Spain, and died impoverished in Madrid on 30 August 1798.

Extended kin active in the rebellion included José Gabriel’s cousin and successor **Diego Cristóbal Túpac Amaru**, uncle **Francisco Túpac Amaru**, brother-in-law **Antonio Bastidas**, nephew **Andrés Mendigure**, and other Condorcanqui, Bastidas, and Noguera relatives.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1738–1750

José Gabriel grew up principally in the Vilcamayu valley and the three communities associated with his family’s curacazgo. His childhood joined two environments:

- the Quechua-speaking social and ritual world of rural Andean communities;
- the legally privileged but precarious sphere of indigenous nobles who mediated between communities, clergy, corregidores, merchants, and the Crown.

Walker reports that the boy accompanied his father on administrative and commercial journeys, observing markets, religious processions, communal affairs, tribute collection, and the transportation network. These journeys supplied practical training in the geography and personal connections later used by the rebellion.

His father’s death in 1750 left the hereditary office under guardianship until José Gabriel reached maturity.

## Education and Formation

Condorcanqui attended the **Colegio de San Francisco de Borja in Cuzco**, a Jesuit institution established for sons of indigenous nobles. Accounts differ on whether he entered at approximately ten or sixteen.

The curriculum probably included Christian doctrine, Spanish literacy, grammar, rhetoric, arithmetic, and Latin at a level appropriate to noble pupils. His surviving political papers show familiarity with Catholic, monarchical, corporate, and legal language, although clerks and secretaries drafted some documents.

### Intellectual influences

- **Catholicism:** continuously visible in his proclamations, appeals to clergy, reverence for sacred buildings, and professions of loyalty to the Church.
- **The corporate monarchy:** his pre-rebellion strategy sought relief through petitions, courts, hereditary privilege, and direct appeal to royal justice.
- **Inca dynastic memory:** his genealogy and public use of the name Túpac Amaru supplied political legitimacy beyond ordinary curacal authority.
- **Inca Garcilaso de la Vega:** Condorcanqui reportedly owned the *Comentarios reales de los incas*. In 1782 Charles III ordered the book collected because indigenous readers had learned “many harmful things” from it. Possession is documented in the historical literature; the precise extent to which Garcilaso determined the rebellion remains disputed.
- **Jesuit formation:** Jesuit schooling provided literacy, a transregional conception of Peru, and consciousness of noble status. Claims that expelled Jesuits secretly designed or directly controlled his rebellion have not been substantiated.

No reliable evidence establishes that he was a Freemason, an atheist, an Enlightenment republican, or an agent of a foreign government.

## Curaca, Merchant, and Litigant, c. 1750–1780

### Hereditary government

After reaching adulthood, Condorcanqui succeeded to the curacazgo of **Surimana, Tungasuca, and Pampamarca**. A curaca represented communities before colonial officials but also bore responsibility for tribute, labor drafts, local order, and delivery of royal demands. The office could make its holder both protector and enforcer.

### Commercial activity and property

Condorcanqui operated a substantial **mule-transport enterprise** connecting Cuzco and the southern provinces with the Upper Peruvian commercial and mining system, including Potosí. He transported agricultural goods, textiles, mercury, and other merchandise over long Andean routes.

Later biographies frequently repeat that he owned about **350 mules**. The figure is plausible and appears in historical literature, but should not be treated as a complete balance sheet. Walker emphasizes that his wealth has often been exaggerated:

- he owned livestock and interests in land;
- he had access to indigenous and commercial labor networks;
- he extended and received credit;
- he also carried mortgages, liens, and substantial debts;
- his cash position could be strained even when his visible assets appeared large.

He was affluent relative to most tributary Indians but was not a financially autonomous magnate. His livelihood depended on precisely the colonial economy he later disrupted.

### Grievances and petitions

As curaca he opposed:

- compulsory distribution of goods by corregidores, the **reparto mercantil**;
- the **mita**, particularly labor drafts associated with Potosí;
- abuses in textile workshops or **obrajes**;
- increased sales taxation or **alcabala**;
- customs houses and fiscal innovations associated with Bourbon reform;
- coercion by local officials and clergy;
- obstacles to his hereditary and genealogical claims.

He traveled to **Lima**, usually dated 1776–77, to petition viceregal and judicial authorities for relief from the mita and to pursue his lineage case. The litigation failed to deliver practical reform. His legal experience demonstrated both his command of colonial procedure and its limits.

### The Betancur litigation

Between 1777 and 1780, Condorcanqui and Diego Felipe Betancur contested descent from Túpac Amaru I and rights associated with the Oropesa succession.

The lawsuit involved prestige, office, property, and political legitimacy. Condorcanqui was not definitively awarded the marquisate before the rebellion. The royalist sentence later used the dynastic claim as evidence of premeditated ambition, alleging that he had planned rebellion for more than five years. That was a prosecutorial finding extracted from an emergency political trial, not neutral proof of the exact inception date.

## Chronological Life History: The Great Rebellion

### Background, 1778–1780

The uprising emerged during intensified Bourbon fiscal reform and widespread unrest. Rebellions, tax riots, and petitions were already occurring across the Andes. Condorcanqui did not invent these grievances, nor did every protest derive from him.

His dual position mattered: he was simultaneously a beneficiary of indigenous nobility, an economic intermediary, a debtor and taxpayer, and a representative exposed to community complaints. Interpretations that reduce the revolt either to pure altruism or solely to his commercial losses are not supported by the mixed record.

### 4–10 November 1780: Arriaga seized and executed

On **4 November 1780**, after dining at the house of **Carlos Rodríguez**, priest of Yanaoca, Condorcanqui’s supporters seized **Antonio de Arriaga**, corregidor of Tinta.

Arriaga was forced to issue orders for weapons, money, and personnel. On **10 November**, after a public proceeding organized by the rebels, he was hanged at Tungasuca. The execution was physically carried out by **Antonio Oblitas**, an enslaved Black man whom the rebels freed.

- **Documented fact:** Condorcanqui ordered and staged the execution.
- **Rebel justification:** Arriaga represented corrupt government, coercive repartos, and local abuse.
- **Viceregal classification:** murder, sedition, and high treason.
- **Modern legal description:** no regular independent tribunal tried Arriaga; the killing was a revolutionary execution.

### 16 November: Freedom proclamation

On **16 November 1780**, Condorcanqui issued a proclamation freeing enslaved people whose owners opposed his movement and summoning them to join him. It is frequently described as the first abolitionist proclamation in Hispanic America.

Its scope requires care. The surviving text links freedom to adherence and confiscation from hostile masters. It was an extraordinary anti-slavery measure, but not a modern universal emancipation statute implemented throughout a controlled state.

### 18 November: Sangarará

A royalist force commanded by **Tiburcio Landa** occupied the church at Sangarará. Condorcanqui demanded surrender; fighting followed, and the church caught fire or exploded after stored ammunition ignited. The rebels won decisively, and many royalists died.

The Bishop of Cuzco excommunicated Condorcanqui, blaming him for destruction of the church and deaths inside it. Rebel accounts said he had warned the defenders and attempted to protect clergy and noncombatants.

Sangarará was the rebellion’s greatest early military success. It also frightened potential Creole allies, gave the royalists powerful atrocity propaganda, and exposed the difficulty of controlling a mass irregular army.

### The decision not to strike Cuzco immediately

Micaela Bastidas repeatedly urged an immediate advance on Cuzco before reinforcements arrived. Her surviving letter warns that delay would disperse their assembled people and allow the Lima army to unite with Cuzco’s defenders.

Condorcanqui instead moved through southern districts, recruiting, consolidating allegiance, addressing local conflicts, and trying to broaden the coalition. Historians commonly identify the delay as a major strategic failure. It cannot be attributed simply to cowardice or incompetence: he faced uncertain supplies, divided communities, rival curacas, racial violence, and doubt over whether Cuzco would surrender. Nonetheless, the delay gave the royalists crucial time.

### Coalition and social program

The movement attempted to unite:

- indigenous communities;
- provincial curacas;
- mestizos;
- Creoles dissatisfied with peninsular officials;
- enslaved and free people of African descent;
- selected clergy.

Its demands evolved and were not fully consistent. Rebel texts called for abolition of corregidores, repartos, customs houses, mita, and oppressive taxes. Some continued to profess loyalty to **Charles III**, representing the revolt as action against “bad government.” Others used sovereign Inca language or envisaged expelling Europeans.

This produces three principal historical interpretations:

1. **Reformist-monarchical:** Condorcanqui initially sought redress inside the monarchy and claimed to act for the king.
2. **Anticolonial or proto-independence:** his acts, territorial claims, destruction of royal institutions, and assumption of Inca authority moved beyond reform.
3. **Inca-restoration or Andean revolution:** sectors of the movement expected an Inca sovereign and major reversal of the colonial social order.

The evidence supports evolution and tactical plurality more securely than a single fixed program.

### Violence within the rebellion

The rebellion cannot be reconstructed only through its proclamations. Rebel forces killed peninsular Spaniards and, in some places, Creoles, mestizos, Black people, indigenous opponents, officials, merchants, and clergy. Houses, estates, churches, obrajes, and commercial property were looted or destroyed.

Some violence was ordered by commanders; some emerged from local grievances and escaped central control. Condorcanqui sometimes protected Spaniards, priests, and churches and issued restraints against indiscriminate killing. At other moments he authorized executions, confiscation, and lethal warfare.

Royalist forces likewise carried out executions, village destruction, collective punishment, torture, and retaliatory killings. Later phases under Diego Cristóbal Túpac Amaru and Túpac Katari became still more destructive. Estimates of total deaths across the multi-regional Great Rebellion commonly reach tens of thousands, but no secure comprehensive count exists, and the total should not be assigned solely to José Gabriel’s direct command.

### December 1780–January 1781: Cuzco campaign

Condorcanqui approached Cuzco in late December and encircled or threatened it in early January 1781. The city was defended by Spanish forces, armed residents, loyal indigenous nobles, and militias.

Important royalist indigenous participants included members of Cuzco’s Inca noble corporations. **Mateo Pumacahua**, curaca of Chinchero, fought on the royal side, as did other indigenous nobles whose position and communities were threatened by the rebellion.

Condorcanqui failed to capture Cuzco and withdrew on approximately **10 January 1781**. The defeat weakened the coalition and permitted royalist forces to take the offensive.

### February–April 1781: Retreat and defeat

The Crown assembled a large counteroffensive under officials including inspector-general **José Antonio de Areche** and Marshal **José del Valle**. Authorities offered pardons, exploited factional divisions, mobilized loyal indigenous forces, and isolated rebel leaders.

Condorcanqui continued campaigning through Tinta and the southern highlands. His forces fought at Combapata, Checacupe, and associated positions. By early April the rebel army was broken.

### Capture at Langui

On or about **6 April 1781**, Condorcanqui was captured near Langui. Accounts identify **Ventura Landaeta**, **Francisco Santa Cruz**, and other former adherents or local collaborators as instrumental in detaining or delivering him.

The often-repeated claim that Mateo Pumacahua personally “betrayed” and captured him is misleading. Pumacahua was a documented royalist opponent, not a trusted member of Condorcanqui’s inner circle who suddenly turned him over. The immediate betrayal came from people within or near the rebel network at Langui.

Micaela Bastidas, Hipólito, Fernando, and numerous relatives and commanders were also captured.

## Imprisonment, Interrogation, and Trial

Prisoners were taken to Cuzco. Condorcanqui was interrogated and tortured to obtain information about collaborators and the alleged wider conspiracy. Accounts describe the strappado or *garrucha*, restraints, and other coercion.

The proceedings were conducted under emergency colonial authority. José Antonio de Areche’s sentence states that prosecutor José Zaldívar y Saavedra accused him and Miguel de Iturrizarra defended him. The record contains evidence, confessions, witness statements, seized correspondence, and political invective.

### Charges and findings

The sentence of **15 May 1781** found him responsible for:

- general uprising;
- the seizure and hanging of Arriaga;
- usurpation of royal jurisdiction;
- use of royal and Inca titles;
- seduction of Indians, mestizos, and other castes;
- attacks on royal forces and property;
- attempted seizure of Cuzco;
- conspiracy allegedly planned for more than five years.

These are **colonial court findings**. The rebellion, military command, Arriaga’s execution, and attempted capture of Cuzco are independently documented. Claims about a five-year master conspiracy or every crime attributed personally to him are less secure.

A frequently repeated exchange has Areche demanding the names of accomplices and Condorcanqui replying: “There are only two guilty parties here: you for oppressing my people, and I for trying to free them.” No securely identified contemporary transcript verifies those exact words; it should be treated as later tradition.

## Execution and Dispersal of the Body

On **18 May 1781**, José Gabriel was taken to Cuzco’s Plaza Mayor and forced to witness the executions of Micaela Bastidas, Hipólito, Francisco Túpac Amaru, Antonio Bastidas, Tomasa Tito Condemayta, Antonio Oblitas, José Berdejo, Andrés Castelo/Gastelú, and other condemned participants.

The sentence ordered:

1. removal to the public square;
2. witnessing the deaths of his family and associates;
3. cutting out or cutting off his tongue;
4. attachment of his limbs to four horses;
5. live dismemberment;
6. burning of the remains and scattering of ashes;
7. public display of separated body parts.

The horses failed to pull the body apart. The executioners then killed him and completed the dismemberment manually.

The sentence directed:

- **head:** Tinta, first displayed on the gallows and then on a stake;
- **one arm:** Tungasuca;
- **other arm:** Carabaya;
- **one leg:** Livitaca in Chumbivilcas;
- **other leg:** Santa Rosa in Lampa;
- **torso/remains:** Picchu, to be burned and the ashes scattered.

Thus the “body sent to towns” was not funerary dispersal but an official technology of deterrence. No authenticated recoverable remains of José Gabriel are known.

## Fate of the Family and Movement

The execution did not end the rebellion. **Diego Cristóbal Túpac Amaru** continued resistance, coordinated with or operated alongside other Andean insurgent currents, and accepted peace in December 1781. He was arrested and executed in 1783.

In Upper Peru, the Aymara leader **Túpac Katari** and **Bartolina Sisa** led mass sieges of La Paz. Their movement was related to the wider crisis but was not simply a subordinate branch personally controlled by José Gabriel.

The surviving Condorcanqui-Bastidas family was imprisoned, transported, or exiled. Mariano died during transportation. Fernando survived confinement but was never allowed to return to Peru. Recently recovered letters document his illness, poverty, education, petitions, and death in Madrid in 1798.

Royal authorities afterward restricted indigenous noble dress, Inca royal symbols, genealogical claims, Quechua cultural expression, and circulation of Garcilaso’s history. Administrative reforms eventually replaced corregidores with intendants and created the Audiencia of Cuzco, but it is too simple to describe these changes as direct concessions to Condorcanqui: they formed part of a wider imperial reorganization accelerated by the crisis.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### From legal petitioner to revolutionary sovereign

Condorcanqui’s surviving trajectory is:

1. hereditary noble and royal intermediary;
2. commercial operator inside the colonial economy;
3. petitioner invoking royal law;
4. insurgent claiming to correct bad government;
5. commander destroying royal administration;
6. claimant to Inca and potentially independent sovereignty.

The point at which reform became a project of separation cannot be exactly fixed.

### Monarchy and independence

His professions of loyalty to Charles III were real features of his public language, not inventions of later royalist revisionism. Equally real were his seizure of jurisdiction, appointment of officials, military taxation, abolition of institutions, territorial claims, and Inca titulature.

Therefore:

- “He was only loyal to Spain” is incomplete.
- “He declared a modern Peruvian republic” is unsupported.
- “His movement became anticolonial and sovereign in practice” is the most defensible limited formulation.

### Race, caste, and social order

Condorcanqui tried to distinguish American-born Spaniards from European officials and to attract Creole support. Simultaneously, mass participation released deep conflicts among Indians, mestizos, Creoles, peninsular Spaniards, and people of African descent.

His emancipation proclamation did not prevent anti-Black prejudice or violence within rebel society. Nor did his indigenous leadership secure support from all indigenous nobles or communities. Cuzco’s noble Inca corporations often opposed him, partly from dynastic rivalry, political interest, and fear of social upheaval.

### Religion

He did not renounce Catholicism. He sought clerical support, represented the movement as morally legitimate, invoked divine justice, and sometimes tried to prevent damage to churches. The Church hierarchy nevertheless excommunicated him after Sangarará and supported royal suppression. Individual priests appeared on both sides.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Micaela Bastidas

**Documented fact:** wife, co-organizer, adviser, logistician, and commander. Their letters combine affection, warnings, supply orders, intelligence, and strategic disagreement. Her insistence on an immediate attack on Cuzco proved prescient.

### Antonio de Arriaga

Corregidor of Tinta, commercial and political antagonist, seized and executed by rebel order. Arriaga personified the repartimiento system, although the personal history between the two men was more complex than a simple lifelong feud.

### Antonio Oblitas

Enslaved man freed by the rebels; executed Arriaga and later died with the principal prisoners.

### Tomasa Tito Condemayta

Curaca of Acos and senior rebel supporter. She supplied troops, money, and regional authority and was executed in Cuzco.

### Diego Cristóbal Túpac Amaru

Cousin and successor to command after José Gabriel’s capture. Continued the war and later entered a negotiated peace before his arrest and execution.

### Diego Felipe Betancur

Genealogical and political rival whose competing descent claim weakened Condorcanqui’s quest for formal recognition.

### Carlos Rodríguez

Priest of Yanaoca at whose house Condorcanqui and Arriaga dined immediately before the latter’s seizure.

### José Antonio de Areche

Royal inspector-general, political prosecutor, and author of the death sentence and post-rebellion program of cultural repression.

### Agustín de Jáuregui

Viceroy of Peru during the rebellion and principal representative of royal authority.

### José del Valle

Royalist military commander in the counteroffensive that defeated the central rebellion.

### Mateo Pumacahua

Curaca of Chinchero and royalist military opponent. Later joined the Cuzco rebellion of 1814, generating retrospective stories that he first “betrayed” Túpac Amaru and later redeemed himself. His royalist combat role is documented; personal betrayal is not.

## Wealth, Business Structure, and Financial Relationships

No complete audited inventory survives in the sources consulted. Reconstructable elements include:

- hereditary income and obligations attached to three curacazgos;
- land and livestock interests;
- a large mule train, conventionally given as approximately 350 animals;
- regional transport contracts and commercial partnerships;
- credit extended to and received from others;
- debts, mortgages, and liens;
- dependence on the Cuzco–Tinta–Lake Titicaca–Upper Peru route;
- financial exposure to customs duties, alcabalas, repartos, and changing administrative boundaries.

His commercial interests supplied mobility, intelligence, animals, employees, and contacts useful to rebellion. They also furnished a material grievance. The claim that the entire uprising was a fraudulent device to avoid personal taxes is a modern polemical inference, not a documented finding.

After conviction, the Crown ordered confiscation and destruction of family property and symbols. His house or houses were targeted as sites of exemplary punishment.

## Controversies, Allegations, Crimes, and Unresolved Questions

### Execution of Arriaga

- **Status:** documented rebel order and killing.
- **Controversy:** revolutionary justice versus politically motivated murder.
- **Evidence:** rebel and royal records agree on seizure, coercion, and hanging.

### Sangarará church deaths

- **Status:** documented battle and destruction of a church containing royalist troops and ammunition.
- **Dispute:** whether rebels deliberately burned people in a sanctuary or whether combat and stored gunpowder caused the catastrophe after surrender warnings were rejected.
- **Effect:** excommunication and loss of potential allies.

### Massacres and ethnic violence

- **Status:** documented within the wider rebellion.
- **Attribution problem:** acts by local crowds or later commanders cannot automatically be assigned as José Gabriel’s personal orders.
- **Counterevidence to an extermination program:** proclamations sought Creole, mestizo, clerical, and Black allies; he sometimes protected Spaniards and churches.
- **Counterevidence to a sanitized war:** he ordered executions and confiscations and led a violent insurgency whose forces killed civilians and prisoners.

### Personal financial motive

- **Claim:** he rebelled because fiscal reform harmed his mule business and because his title litigation failed.
- **Evidence for:** taxes, customs, debt, and status litigation materially affected him.
- **Evidence against reductionism:** his petitions and proclamations addressed collective mita, reparto, slavery, corregidores, obrajes, and community grievances; the scale of mobilization cannot be explained by his balance sheet alone.
- **Status:** plausible contributing motive, not sufficient explanation.

### Genealogical imposture

- **Claim:** “Túpac Amaru” was a fabricated identity.
- **Evidence:** Betancur formally contested the descent; Cuzco nobles did not unanimously accept Condorcanqui as dynastic head.
- **Counterevidence:** Condorcanqui inherited recognized curacal office, presented a developed genealogy, and used Túpac Amaru before or independently of the final rebellion. Serious irregularities attach to the rival Betancur papers.
- **Status:** lineage beyond the recognized curacal family remains disputed; wholesale “impostor” claims exceed the evidence.

### Secret Jesuit or foreign conspiracy

- **Claim:** expelled Jesuits or foreign agents orchestrated the revolt.
- **Evidence:** Jesuit schooling and anti-Bourbon rumors.
- **Finding:** no documentary chain establishing operational control.
- **Status:** colonial suspicion and later conspiracy narrative.

### Was he a “precursor of independence”?

- **Evidence for:** destruction of royal authority, continental territorial language, Inca sovereignty, mobilization across viceregal borders, and subsequent emancipatory influence.
- **Evidence against a simple label:** repeated loyalty to Charles III; no republican constitution; social and dynastic aims unlike nineteenth-century Creole independence.
- **Status:** historiographical interpretation. “Anticolonial rebellion with proto-national and restorationist components” preserves more of the evidence.

### Adversarial-search findings, 2024–2026

Explicit searches pairing his name with controversy, scandal, fraud, accusation, lawsuit, criticism, crime, and conspiracy produced no credible new criminal or archival revelation about his personal life. Recent polemics largely recycle five issues: alleged genealogical fraud, selfish commercial motive, loyalty to Spain, rebel atrocities, and the claim that Pumacahua betrayed him.

The most recent substantive archival reporting concerns newly published letters of his son Fernando, not a scandal involving José Gabriel. Social-media assertions from 2025–26 frequently cite genuine documents selectively but do not constitute independent evidence. No credible evidence of money laundering, secret intelligence service, patent fraud, sexual scandal, addiction, or undisclosed business structure emerged.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

### “I will return and I will be millions”

- **Story:** Túpac Amaru pronounced “Volveré y seré millones” before death.
- **Evidence:** the line is absent from the verified sentence and reliable contemporary execution records.
- **Competing attribution:** widely assigned to Túpac Katari; variants later became associated with Spartacus and Eva Perón.
- **Status:** later apocryphal quotation.
- **Effect:** condensed the idea that dismemberment produced collective political rebirth.

### The dialogue with Areche

- **Story:** Condorcanqui told Areche that only the two of them were guilty—Areche for oppression, Condorcanqui for liberation.
- **Evidence:** no secure contemporary verbatim transcript located.
- **Status:** plausible literary condensation but unverified quotation.
- **Effect:** casts the trial as a confrontation between empire and colonized people.

### The “last words” in Quechua

A phrase translated approximately as “Mother Earth, witness how my enemies shed my blood” is repeated in commemorative sources. Its documentary provenance is weak.

- **Status:** tradition, not securely established last speech.

### Superhuman resistance to four horses

The failed dismemberment is historically attested. Later retellings transform physical failure into miraculous or superhuman strength. The mechanics—horse positioning, animal reluctance, executioner error, or bodily strength—cannot be recovered.

- **Fact:** the horses failed and another method was used.
- **Mythic elaboration:** the land or his body supernaturally refused division.

### Inkarri

The dismembered body entered the wider Andean symbolic complex of **Inkarri**, in which the severed Inca regenerates underground and will return to restore order. The myth predates José Gabriel in forms associated with Atahualpa and Túpac Amaru I, but his execution gave it renewed political force.

- **Status:** living folklore and political mythology, not a biographical event.
- **Effect:** made bodily fragmentation a promise of reconstitution.

### The authentic portrait

No securely authenticated lifetime portrait is known. Familiar long-haired, broad-hatted images are nineteenth- and twentieth-century reconstructions. A watercolor dated broadly to 1784–1806 is among the earliest surviving representations but is posthumous.

The iconic geometric head used under Juan Velasco Alvarado was designed by **Jesús Ruiz Durand**, not copied from a verified likeness.

### Pumacahua the betrayer

- **Story:** Mateo Pumacahua personally betrayed and delivered Condorcanqui.
- **Evidence:** Pumacahua fought for the Crown, but immediate capture accounts identify Ventura Landaeta, Francisco Santa Cruz, and local collaborators at Langui.
- **Status:** conflation of military opposition with personal betrayal.
- **Effect:** supports a morality play sharpened by Pumacahua’s later participation in the 1814 rebellion.

### Polish descendants

A twentieth-century story connects a supposed Túpac Amaru descendant, Antonio, with Polish or Slovak noble families and eventually politician Andrzej Benesz.

- **Evidence:** journalistic genealogy and family lore; no demonstrated documentary bridge to José Gabriel’s immediate family.
- **Status:** unverified genealogical legend.

## Works by or Attributed to José Gabriel Condorcanqui

Condorcanqui was not an author of books in the modern sense. His corpus consists of petitions, genealogical pleadings, letters, edicts, military orders, proclamations, and interrogation statements. Many were dictated to secretaries, copied by officials, or preserved as prosecution exhibits.

### Pre-rebellion writings

- **Genealogical petition and supporting memorials in the Condorcanqui–Betancur litigation**, 1777–80. Published posthumously in collections including *Genealogía de Túpac Amaru: documento inédito del año de 1777*.
- **Petitions concerning mita, obrajes, repartos, and curacal rights**, principally 1770s.
- **Powers of attorney and legal submissions** to representatives in Cuzco and Lima.
- **Commercial and administrative records**, scattered through notarial and judicial archives.

### Rebel corpus, 1780–81

- Orders associated with the capture and compelled cooperation of Antonio de Arriaga, November 1780.
- Proclamations abolishing corregidores, repartos, mita, customs burdens, and other exactions.
- **Bando de libertad de los esclavos**, Tungasuca, 16 November 1780.
- Letters to curacas, priests, towns, military commanders, Creoles, and relatives.
- Correspondence with Micaela Bastidas.
- Appeals or exhortations to Cuzco, Arequipa, Chichas, La Paz, and other jurisdictions.
- Military appointments, requisitions, safe-conducts, tax and supply orders.
- March 1781 letters explaining grievances and terms for peace or submission.
- Statements under interrogation, April–May 1781.

### Attribution cautions

- Surviving originals, contemporary copies, hostile copies, translations, and later transcriptions must be distinguished.
- Secretaries drafted many papers from his instructions; signature does not prove sole verbal composition.
- The grandiose “José I … king of Peru, Quito, Chile, Buenos Aires …” proclamation is disputed and has been identified by some scholars as forged.
- Phrases such as “Volveré y seré millones” do not belong in the authenticated corpus.

### Principal documentary editions

- *Colección documental de la independencia del Perú: La rebelión de Túpac Amaru*, edited by Carlos Daniel Valcárcel and others.
- *Nueva colección documental de la independencia del Perú: La rebelión de Túpac Amaru II*, seven-volume re-edition and expansion, 2017 onward.
- *Documentos para la historia de la sublevación de José Gabriel de Túpac-Amaru*, Buenos Aires, 1936.
- *Genealogía de Túpac Amaru: documento inédito del año de 1777*, Lima, 1946.
- *Juicio y sentencia a Túpac Amaru II y a su familia*, modern transcription/facsimile editions.
- PARES authority and catalog records for the rebellion, criminal cases, correspondence, and administrative reports in Spanish state archives.

### Historiographical divisions

The literature has variously represented him as:

- loyal reformer;
- dynastic claimant;
- indigenous nationalist;
- precursor of independence;
- anticolonial revolutionary;
- merchant defending class interests;
- leader of a multiethnic coalition;
- failed strategist;
- restored Inca;
- symbol constructed by twentieth-century nationalism.

No one characterization accounts for every phase and constituency.

## Death, Posthumous Reputation, and Legacy

### Immediate repression

Authorities attempted to eliminate not only the rebels but the political vocabulary of Inca restoration. Measures restricted royal Inca dress, ceremonial offices, genealogical representations, theatrical works, Quechua expression associated with rebellion, and circulation of Garcilaso.

The punishment paradoxically multiplied the image it sought to destroy. Publicly dispersed remains supplied a geography of memory extending from Tinta and Tungasuca to Carabaya, Livitaca, and Lampa.

### Nineteenth century

The Creole-led independence era did not uniformly place Túpac Amaru at the center of the national story. His movement’s mass indigenous character and threat to property and caste order made it an ambivalent precedent. Later republican writers alternately described him as precursor, rebel, fanatic, patriot, or monarchist.

### Twentieth-century recovery

From the 1940s, documentary publications and nationalist scholarship strengthened his status as precursor of emancipation. Marxist, indigenist, nationalist, agrarian, and anti-imperialist movements each produced different Túpac Amarus.

Under **Juan Velasco Alvarado** (1968–75), the Peruvian military government made him a central national and agrarian-reform symbol. Jesús Ruiz Durand’s stylized head appeared on posters, cooperative signage, government publications, and public campaigns. A room in the Government Palace formerly associated with Francisco Pizarro was renamed for Túpac Amaru. His portrait appeared on Peruvian currency, including inti notes.

### Armed movements and international influence

The **Movimiento Revolucionario Túpac Amaru (MRTA)** adopted his name. The MRTA’s Marxist-Leninist insurgency, kidnappings, killings, and 1996–97 Japanese ambassador’s residence hostage crisis belong to that organization, not to Condorcanqui’s personal biography. The appropriation nevertheless changed public reception of his name during Peru’s internal armed conflict.

Uruguayan **Tupamaros** drew their name through the broader legacy of Túpac Amaru. Revolutionary, indigenous, labor, and student organizations across Latin America adopted similar names.

American activist **Afeni Shakur** named her son **Tupac Amaru Shakur** after the Andean rebel, extending the name into global popular culture.

### Present reputation

He remains:

- a statutory and civic hero in Peru;
- a symbol of indigenous resistance;
- a disputed precursor of independence;
- an emblem of agrarian reform and left-wing politics;
- a dynastic Inca figure;
- a subject of monarchist and Hispanist revision;
- a vehicle for regional memory in Cuzco, Canas, Tinta, Tungasuca, and Surimana.

Because no authenticated portrait survives, each visual representation also expresses the politics of its own period.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted evidence are included. Mere chronological overlap is excluded.

### Micaela Bastidas Puyucahua — documented fact

Wife from 25 May 1760; mother of Hipólito, Mariano, and Fernando; co-leader of the 1780–81 rebellion. Their surviving correspondence documents intimate partnership, political coordination, logistical collaboration, and disagreement over the timing of the attack on Cuzco. Both were imprisoned, tried, and executed in Cuzco on 18 May 1781.

### El Inca Garcilaso de la Vega — documented intellectual connection; degree of influence disputed

Condorcanqui reportedly possessed the *Comentarios reales*. Royal authorities considered the book politically dangerous after the rebellion and ordered copies collected in 1782. Garcilaso’s account of Inca sovereignty and the execution of Túpac Amaru I supplied an available dynastic historical framework. Direct textual causation of particular rebel decisions remains disputed.

### Atahualpa — documented symbolic connection, not a personal relationship

Atahualpa’s capture and execution formed part of Andean historical memory and the wider Inkarri complex later applied to Condorcanqui’s dismemberment. José Gabriel did not claim a documented personal or immediate genealogical relationship with Atahualpa in the evidence consulted; the connection is posthumous and symbolic.

### Francisco Pizarro González — documented posthumous symbolic opposition

They could not meet, having lived more than two centuries apart. During the Velasco government, a Government Palace space formerly named for or dominated by Pizarro was renamed for Túpac Amaru, and Pizarro’s image was displaced by that of the rebel. This is a documented twentieth-century symbolic pairing, not a lifetime connection.

### José de San Martín — documented posthumous intellectual-historical connection

No personal contact was possible. San Martín’s independence-era government promoted publication and circulation of Inca Garcilaso’s *Comentarios reales*, a work associated by royal authorities and later scholarship with Túpac Amaru’s historical imagination. The link is indirect and posthumous; the sources consulted do not establish that San Martín modeled his program personally on Condorcanqui.

### Simón Bolívar — no adequately documented direct connection found

Bolívar belonged to the next generation, but the consulted sources did not establish a specific documented acknowledgment, meeting, correspondence, or direct organizational inheritance sufficient for inclusion beyond broad continental revolutionary context.

### Remaining atlas roster

No documentary connection was found between Condorcanqui and the other listed atlas figures. The absence of a connection is especially important for later revolutionaries sometimes placed beside him rhetorically: shared anticolonial symbolism does not establish personal influence or contact.

## Compact Chronology

- **1738, 10 or 19 March:** born at Surimana.
- **1 May 1738:** commonly reported baptism date.
- **c. 1748–54:** educated at the Jesuit Colegio de San Francisco de Borja, Cuzco; exact enrollment chronology disputed.
- **1750:** father dies; relatives administer the family curacazgo during José Gabriel’s minority.
- **25 May 1760:** marries Micaela Bastidas Puyucahua.
- **c. 1761:** son Hipólito born.
- **c. 1762:** son Mariano born.
- **c. 1768:** son Fernando born.
- **1760s–70s:** curaca of Surimana, Tungasuca, and Pampamarca; develops mule-transport and commercial business.
- **1776–77:** travels to Lima to pursue relief from mita and other grievances.
- **1777–80:** litigation with Diego Felipe Betancur over Inca descent and Oropesa succession.
- **4 November 1780:** Antonio de Arriaga seized after meal at Yanaoca.
- **10 November 1780:** Arriaga hanged at Tungasuca.
- **16 November 1780:** issues emancipation proclamation for enslaved supporters.
- **18 November 1780:** rebel victory at Sangarará; church destroyed; Condorcanqui subsequently excommunicated.
- **November–December 1780:** rebellion expands through Tinta and southern provinces.
- **Late December 1780–early January 1781:** advance on and siege of Cuzco.
- **c. 10 January 1781:** withdraws after failure to capture Cuzco.
- **February–March 1781:** royalist counteroffensive; rebel coalition contracts.
- **Early April 1781:** defeats around Combapata and Checacupe.
- **c. 6 April 1781:** captured near Langui after betrayal by former or local associates.
- **April–May 1781:** imprisoned, tortured, interrogated, and tried in Cuzco.
- **15 May 1781:** Areche pronounces sentence.
- **18 May 1781:** family and principal associates executed; Condorcanqui subjected to failed four-horse dismemberment, killed, dismembered, and dispersed.
- **1781–83:** rebellion continues under Diego Cristóbal and in related Upper Peruvian movements.
- **1782:** Crown orders collection of Garcilaso’s *Comentarios reales* and suppresses Inca political symbols.
- **1783:** Diego Cristóbal executed.
- **1798, 30 August:** surviving son Fernando dies in poverty in Madrid.
- **1940s onward:** documentary scholarship expands and national-precursor interpretation strengthens.
- **1968–75:** Velasco government makes Túpac Amaru a principal symbol of the Peruvian Revolution and agrarian reform.
- **1983/84:** Federico García Hurtado’s film *Túpac Amaru* produced and released.
- **1980s–90s:** MRTA’s appropriation creates a new, contested association with armed insurgency.
- **2025:** publication and reporting on recovered letters of Fernando renew attention to the family’s post-rebellion fate.
