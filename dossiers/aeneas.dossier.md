# Aeneas: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Status

Aeneas is a figure of Greek and Roman myth, epic, cult, political genealogy, and later literature—not a person whose existence can presently be established by contemporary documentary evidence.

No independently verified birth date, death date, grave, inscription made during his supposed lifetime, personal writing, or securely identified archaeological artifact survives. A “life history” can therefore be reconstructed only by arranging ancient narratives in their internal chronology and then tracing how those narratives developed historically.

This dossier uses the following labels:

- **Narrative fact:** an event presented as true within a named ancient literary source.
- **Documented reception fact:** a securely dated text, artifact, cult site, artwork, or political use involving Aeneas.
- **Ancient report:** an ancient author’s report of a tradition, not independent confirmation that the event occurred.
- **Disputed ancient tradition:** competing versions transmitted by ancient sources.
- **Myth or legend:** a traditional story involving gods, heroic-age chronology, or otherwise historically unverifiable events.
- **Later literary invention or adaptation:** a feature introduced or substantially reshaped by a later author.
- **Modern hypothesis:** a scholarly reconstruction rather than an attested ancient event.

Dates assigned to Aeneas’s supposed lifetime depend on chronologies of the Trojan War, conventionally placed by many ancient calculations around the late thirteenth or early twelfth century BCE. They are not biographical dates established for Aeneas himself.

### Name and etymology

The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* derives **Aineias** from Greek *ainos*, in the sense of “terrible,” “dreadful,” or “awful,” explaining that Aphrodite suffered grief or shame because she had slept with a mortal. This is an ancient poetic etymology, not necessarily the historical linguistic derivation of the name. The hymn has Aphrodite declare that the child will be called Aeneas because of the grief caused by her union with Anchises.

Virgil’s characteristic epithet **pius** is broader than modern English “pious.” It signifies dutiful observance toward gods, family, dependents, community, and destiny. Virgil also calls him **pater Aeneas**, presenting him as a literal father and as a prospective father of a people.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal ancestry

The major literary genealogies make Aeneas part of the Trojan-Dardanian royal house:

1. Zeus/Jupiter and Electra  
2. Dardanus  
3. Erichthonius  
4. Tros  
5. Assaracus  
6. Capys  
7. Anchises  
8. Aeneas  

Homer’s *Iliad* gives the essential Dardanian genealogy and distinguishes the branch of Assaracus and Anchises from Priam’s branch through Ilus and Laomedon. The lineage makes Aeneas a relative of Priam and Hector but not Priam’s son.

Pseudo-Apollodorus adds women’s names to portions of the genealogy: Capys is the son of Assaracus and Hieromneme, while Anchises is the son of Capys and Themiste. Such details belong to later mythographic systematization and are not consistent in every source.

### Parents

- **Father:** **Anchises**, a mortal Trojan or Dardanian aristocrat associated with herding cattle on Mount Ida.
- **Mother:** **Aphrodite** in Greek tradition; **Venus** in Roman usage.

The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* gives the fullest surviving account of his conception. Zeus causes Aphrodite to desire Anchises, partly in retaliation for her power to make gods desire mortals. She disguises herself as a Phrygian princess, sleeps with him, reveals her divinity, predicts Aeneas’s birth, and commands that mountain nymphs rear the child until he is about five. Anchises must say only that a nymph bore him.

A later and widespread story says Anchises boasted of sleeping with Aphrodite and was struck or lamed by Zeus’s thunderbolt. Ancient traditions disagree on whether he was killed, blinded, or crippled. Virgil adopts the image of an aged, physically dependent Anchises but allows him to survive the fall of Troy and travel as far as Sicily.

### Siblings

Pseudo-Apollodorus names **Lyrus** as another son of Anchises and Aphrodite and says he died childless. Lyrus is marginal in the tradition and absent from the major epic account.

Some genealogical sources give Anchises other children, but no stable sibling group surrounds Aeneas.

### Spouses, partners, and sexual traditions

#### Creusa

**Creusa**, daughter of Priam and Hecuba, is Aeneas’s Trojan wife in the dominant Roman tradition. This makes Aeneas simultaneously Priam’s relative and son-in-law.

In Virgil, Creusa is lost during the flight from Troy. Her shade appears to Aeneas, tells him not to grieve, says that divine will has prevented her enslavement, and predicts his journey to a western land and marriage to a royal bride.

The manner of her death is not shown. Consequently, later readers have variously understood her as killed in the sack, taken by the gods, deliberately left behind by narrative necessity, or sacrificed symbolically to Aeneas’s Italian destiny. Only the disappearance and apparition are Virgilian narrative facts.

#### Dido

**Dido**, also called Elissa in non-Virgilian Carthaginian foundation tradition, becomes Aeneas’s lover in Virgil’s *Aeneid*. There is no evidence that the historical traditions behind Carthage’s founding involved Aeneas. The usual ancient dates would separate the Trojan War and Carthage’s foundation by several centuries.

Virgil’s version presents a divinely manipulated attachment. Venus and Juno intervene; Dido and Aeneas shelter together in a cave during a storm; Dido interprets the union as marriage, while Aeneas later denies that he formally entered a marriage contract. Mercury orders him to resume his mission. After his secret preparations are exposed, he leaves Carthage. Dido curses him and his descendants and kills herself with his sword.

This relationship is one of the most influential later components of Aeneas’s reputation. It generated competing portrayals of him as dutiful founder, emotionally constrained refugee, betrayer, faithless lover, instrument of empire, or victim of divine coercion.

#### Lavinia

**Lavinia**, daughter of King Latinus and Queen Amata, is Aeneas’s Italian bride. Earlier sources and Virgil agree on the dynastic importance of the marriage, but differ about the sequence of negotiations and warfare.

Virgil’s epic ends immediately after Aeneas kills Turnus, before the marriage occurs onstage. Livy and Dionysius carry the tradition forward: Aeneas marries Lavinia, establishes or rules from Lavinium, and joins Trojans and local inhabitants into a new political community.

#### Medieval homoerotic accusation

The twelfth-century *Roman d’Enéas* introduces or amplifies an accusation that Enéas prefers boys. Queen Amata uses it against his proposed marriage to Lavine. This is a **later literary accusation**, not evidence about an historical person or a stable feature of the ancient Greek and Roman Aeneas. Its importance lies in medieval adaptation, gender politics, and the romance’s reworking of Virgilian sexuality.

### Children and descendants

#### Ascanius

Aeneas’s best-attested son is **Ascanius**. Virgil also calls him **Iulus**. He is the child of Creusa in the Virgilian tradition.

Ascanius escapes Troy with Aeneas and Anchises. In Italy he participates in the war by shooting Sylvia’s pet stag, an act that precipitates fighting between Trojans and Latins. Later genealogies make him founder of Alba Longa.

#### Iulus and Julian ancestry

Roman tradition linked **Iulus** with the **gens Iulia**, the family of Julius Caesar and Augustus. Ancient authors knew uncertainty about whether Ascanius and Iulus were originally the same person. Livy explicitly reports competing versions:

- Ascanius was the son of Aeneas and Creusa and later succeeded him.
- A second Ascanius, son of Aeneas and Lavinia, may have been the founder of Alba Longa.
- The genealogical problem could not be resolved with certainty even in Livy’s account.

The Julian claim was politically potent because it connected the family through Aeneas to Venus.

#### Silvius

Later tradition often makes **Silvius** the son of Aeneas and Lavinia, born after Aeneas’s death. In some accounts Ascanius initially rules while Lavinia, fearing him, retreats into the woods and gives birth there—hence the name Silvius, associated with *silva*, “forest.” Other traditions reorganize the Alban genealogy.

#### Roman and European legendary descendants

Through Ascanius/Iulus and the kings of Alba Longa, Aeneas becomes an ancestor of Romulus and Remus and therefore of Rome. Medieval genealogists extended Trojan ancestry to Franks, Britons, Normans, and other peoples. These are political origin myths, not demonstrable biological pedigrees.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Conception on Mount Ida

**Myth:** Aphrodite, disguised as a mortal, approaches Anchises on Mount Ida. She claims royal Phrygian birth and says Hermes brought her to become Anchises’s wife. After they sleep together, she reveals herself.

The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* predicts that nymphs will rear Aeneas for approximately his first five years. He will then be delivered to Anchises, who must identify the mother as a nymph rather than Aphrodite.

### Rearing by nymphs

The nymph-rearing episode places Aeneas between divine and mortal worlds. Unlike a fully immortal god, he is subject to age and death. Unlike an ordinary prince, he is the child of a goddess and the object of a dynastic prophecy.

No ancient source supplies a detailed childhood curriculum, education, teacher, or initiation. Claims about his youthful training beyond nymphal fostering are extrapolation or later invention.

### Dynastic position

In the *Iliad*, Aeneas belongs to a junior royal line. He reportedly resents Priam because Priam does not adequately honor him, even though Aeneas is distinguished among the Trojans. This tension helps explain later hostile stories that he withdrew, negotiated separately, or betrayed Troy.

## Education and Formation

No source names a formal teacher comparable to Chiron for Achilles. Aeneas’s formation is conveyed through kinship, divine protection, warfare, prophecy, and obligation rather than schooling.

### Formative influences

- **Anchises:** genealogical knowledge, religious authority, and leadership counsel. In Virgil, Anchises interprets omens, guides migration decisions, and after death reveals Rome’s future in the underworld.
- **Aphrodite/Venus:** divine mother and recurrent protector. She rescues him from Diomedes, intervenes with Jupiter, arranges armor through Vulcan, and works to preserve his dynastic mission.
- **Hector:** in Virgil, Hector’s shade orders Aeneas to abandon the doomed city and carry Troy’s sacred things overseas.
- **The Penates:** the Trojan household or state gods repeatedly direct the journey.
- **The Sibyl of Cumae:** ritual guide into the underworld.
- **Evander:** Arcadian ally who provides military assistance and entrusts Pallas to Aeneas.
- **Pallas’s death:** becomes the immediate moral and emotional cause of Aeneas’s killing of Turnus at the end of the *Aeneid*.
- **Prophecy:** the dominant organizing influence. Aeneas is repeatedly compelled to subordinate immediate attachments to a future he will not personally live to see completed.

## Early Career: Aeneas in the Trojan War

### Command position

In *Iliad* 2, Aeneas commands the Dardanians with the sons of Antenor. Homer describes him as born to Anchises by Aphrodite on Mount Ida.

He is not Troy’s supreme commander. Hector occupies that position. Aeneas is nevertheless one of the strongest Trojan-side fighters and receives extraordinary divine protection.

### Combat with Diomedes

In *Iliad* 5:

1. Aeneas joins Pandarus against Diomedes.
2. Diomedes kills Pandarus.
3. Aeneas defends the body.
4. Diomedes strikes Aeneas with a stone and injures his hip or thigh.
5. Aphrodite enters the battle and carries Aeneas away.
6. Diomedes wounds Aphrodite.
7. Apollo then protects Aeneas and removes him to his temple.
8. Leto and Artemis heal him.
9. Apollo creates a phantom of Aeneas while the real hero recovers.

This episode establishes Aeneas as courageous but dependent on divine rescue.

### Combat with Achilles

In *Iliad* 20, Apollo encourages Aeneas to confront Achilles. Aeneas recounts his genealogy, and the two exchange threats. Achilles would probably kill him, but Poseidon intervenes.

Poseidon’s reason is dynastic: Aeneas is destined to survive and rule the Trojans because the line of Priam has incurred divine hatred. Poseidon throws mist over Achilles, removes Aeneas, and warns him not to face Achilles again until Achilles is dead.

This is the earliest surviving explicit foundation for Aeneas’s survival and future rule. It does **not** say that he will migrate to Italy.

### Other wartime episodes

Aeneas appears in multiple engagements and kills Greek fighters. Ancient epic presents him as formidable but not invincible. He is frequently associated with Apollo and Aphrodite and contrasted with Hector’s centrality.

## The Fall of Troy: Competing Traditions

There was no single ancient canonical account.

### Virgil’s escape narrative

In *Aeneid* 2, Aeneas recounts Troy’s fall to Dido:

- The Greeks leave the wooden horse.
- Laocoön warns the Trojans.
- Sinon deceives them.
- The horse enters Troy.
- Hector’s bloodied shade appears to Aeneas and orders him to flee with Troy’s sacred things.
- Aeneas initially fights in the city.
- He witnesses Priam’s killing by Pyrrhus/Neoptolemus.
- Venus reveals the gods destroying Troy and directs him home.
- Anchises initially refuses to leave.
- An omen—a harmless flame around Ascanius’s head—followed by a star persuades Anchises.
- Aeneas carries Anchises; Ascanius walks beside him; Creusa follows.
- Aeneas brings the household gods indirectly because his bloodied hands are religiously unclean; Anchises carries the sacred objects.
- Creusa disappears.
- Her shade predicts Aeneas’s western kingdom and new royal marriage.
- Refugees gather, and Aeneas leads them toward Mount Ida.

### Sophocles’ *Laocoön*

Dionysius preserves fragments of Sophocles’ lost tragedy *Laocoön*. In that version, Aeneas leaves before the city is taken, obeying Anchises, who recognizes the implications of the Laocoön omen. Aeneas carries Anchises and is followed by a large group.

This demonstrates that the famous image of Aeneas carrying his father predated Virgil.

### Greek permission or negotiated escape

Livy says Aeneas and Antenor were spared because they had favored peace and the restoration of Helen. This is neither Virgil’s fighting retreat nor straightforward betrayal.

Other ancient traditions make Aeneas leave under a truce, surrender arrangement, or Greek permission. Such versions may preserve political traditions favorable to diplomatic founders.

### The betrayal accusation

Dionysius quotes **Menecrates of Xanthus**, who alleged that Aeneas betrayed Troy to the Achaeans because Paris/Alexander had slighted him and excluded him from honors. After overthrowing Priam, he was treated as an ally of the Greeks.

Evidentiary status: **ancient hostile allegation preserved by a later author**. It is evidence that a betrayal narrative circulated, not proof that a historical Aeneas betrayed a historical Troy.

The allegation probably drew plausibility from:

- Aeneas’s resentment of Priam in Homer.
- His survival when most Trojan leaders died.
- Traditions of negotiated departure.
- The need to explain how he saved family, followers, and sacred objects.

It remained influential in medieval literature, where Aeneas could be characterized as treacherous or morally ambiguous.

### Absence from Troy

Other versions reported that Aeneas was:

- at the Trojan fleet’s station;
- away on a military expedition in Phrygia;
- already on Mount Ida;
- spared because of his reverence toward the gods and his father.

These are **disputed ancient traditions**.

## Chronological Life History: The Mediterranean Wanderings

The following itinerary is primarily Virgilian, supplemented by Dionysius and other ancient reports. It should not be treated as a verified Bronze Age travel record.

### Departure from the Troad

Aeneas and his followers construct ships below Mount Ida. Virgil gives the fleet twenty ships at departure; losses later reduce the number. The refugees carry religious objects, family members, dependents, and a political identity centered on Troy’s continuity.

### Thrace

Aeneas attempts to found a settlement called **Aeneadae** or Aeneadae-like in name. When he tears up plants for sacrificial branches, blood drips from them. The buried Trojan **Polydorus**, murdered for gold by the Thracian king, speaks from beneath the earth.

Aeneas abandons the settlement after funeral rites. The episode establishes that another people’s land cannot simply become the new Troy and that corrupted hospitality makes Thrace unsuitable.

### Delos

At Delos, King **Anius**, priest of Apollo and an old associate of Anchises, receives the Trojans. Apollo orders them to seek their “ancient mother.”

Anchises interprets this as Crete, ancestral homeland of Teucer. This interpretation proves wrong.

### Crete

The Trojans establish **Pergamea** in Crete. Disease and crop failure follow. The Penates appear to Aeneas in a dream and identify Italy—associated with Dardanus—as the destined homeland.

This episode marks an important failure of Anchises’s interpretation and a correction by direct divine revelation.

### The Strophades

The Trojans encounter the Harpies, including **Celaeno**. Fighting breaks out after the Trojans slaughter cattle and disturb the Harpies’ food. Celaeno prophesies that hunger will make the Trojans “eat their tables” before they found their city.

The prophecy is later fulfilled harmlessly when the Trojans eat bread-cakes used as platters in Latium.

### Epirus and Buthrotum

At Buthrotum, Aeneas finds **Andromache**, Hector’s widow, and **Helenus**, a Trojan prince and seer. They rule a miniature reproduction of Troy.

Helenus gives detailed navigation and ritual instructions:

- avoid the nearer Italian shore associated with hostile Greeks;
- seek the sow-and-thirty-piglets omen;
- consult the Sibyl;
- honor Juno;
- sail around Sicily rather than risk Scylla and Charybdis.

Buthrotum represents a possible but ultimately inadequate response to displacement: reproducing the lost city rather than founding a transformed future.

### Italian coast and Sicily

Virgil’s route includes:

- the southeastern Italian coast;
- the land of the Cyclopes;
- rescue of **Achaemenides**, a Greek abandoned by Odysseus;
- navigation around Sicily;
- arrival at **Drepanum**, where Anchises dies.

The rescue of Achaemenides shows a former Trojan taking responsibility for a Greek enemy’s abandoned companion.

### Anchises’s death

Anchises dies in Sicily. Aeneas later commemorates the anniversary with funeral games.

The death removes Aeneas’s paternal guide before the voyage is complete. Anchises nevertheless remains politically decisive: his shade later explains Rome’s future in the underworld.

### Storm and Carthage

Juno, still hostile to the Trojans, induces Aeolus to release the winds. Neptune calms the storm. Aeneas’s scattered ships reach the North African coast.

At Carthage:

- Venus disguises herself and informs Aeneas about Dido.
- Aeneas enters the city concealed in a cloud.
- He sees Trojan War scenes represented in Juno’s temple.
- Dido receives the refugees.
- Venus sends Cupid disguised as Ascanius to inflame Dido’s love.
- Aeneas recounts Troy’s fall and the wanderings.
- Dido and Aeneas become lovers after the cave episode.
- Jupiter sends Mercury to order Aeneas onward.
- Aeneas prepares secretly to depart.
- Dido confronts him.
- He says Italy, not personal choice, is his destined objective.
- He sails.
- Dido curses him, anticipates perpetual conflict between her people and his, and dies by suicide.

### Historical incompatibility of Aeneas and Dido

Ancient foundation chronology commonly placed Carthage centuries after the traditional Trojan War. The Aeneas–Dido relationship is therefore best classified as **Virgilian poetic synchronization or invention**, although both figures had older independent traditions.

The episode retrospectively mythologizes Roman–Carthaginian hostility, including the Punic Wars. Dido’s curse becomes a narrative origin for later interstate conflict.

### Return to Sicily

Storms drive the Trojans back to Sicily. They celebrate funeral games for Anchises. Juno, through Iris, incites the Trojan women to burn the ships because they are exhausted by wandering.

Some vessels burn; rain saves others. The aged, unwilling, and less warlike remain in Sicily, where a settlement traditionally called **Acesta** or Segesta is established under Acestes. This is both a logistical division and a political one: only a selected group continues to Latium.

### Cumae and the underworld

Aeneas reaches Cumae and consults the Sibyl. To descend alive to the underworld he must:

- find and bury his dead comrade **Misenus**;
- obtain the **golden bough**;
- perform the required sacrifices.

In the underworld he encounters:

- unburied and prematurely dead souls;
- **Palinurus**, his lost helmsman;
- Dido, who refuses to speak and returns to the shade of Sychaeus;
- Trojan and Greek war dead;
- punishments in Tartarus, described by the Sibyl;
- Anchises in Elysium.

Anchises presents a procession of future Roman figures and articulates Rome’s imperial vocation: government, peace under law, mercy to the submissive, and defeat of the proud. This is a literary projection of Roman history backward into Aeneas’s consciousness.

Aeneas and the Sibyl leave through the gate of ivory, conventionally associated with false dreams. The meaning remains disputed: poetic convention, metapoetic warning, ambiguity about revelation, or qualification of Rome’s promised future.

### Arrival in Latium

The Trojans reach the Tiber region. Ascanius jokes that hunger has caused them to eat their “tables,” fulfilling Celaeno’s prophecy through the consumption of bread platters.

Aeneas sends envoys to **Latinus**, king of the Latins. Latinus recognizes signs that his daughter should marry a foreigner and initially welcomes the alliance.

A white sow with thirty piglets identifies a destined site in prophecy. Different traditions connect this portent with Lavinium, Alba Longa, or a thirty-year dynastic sequence.

## War in Italy

### Causes

**Turnus**, leader of the Rutuli and Lavinia’s existing or intended suitor, opposes Aeneas. Queen **Amata** also resists the foreign marriage. Juno sends the Fury **Allecto** to intensify hostility.

Ascanius kills a domesticated stag beloved by Sylvia. Local anger escalates into open warfare.

### Alliances

Aeneas seeks allies:

- **Evander**, Arcadian ruler at Pallanteum, receives him.
- Evander entrusts his son **Pallas** to Aeneas.
- Etruscans hostile to their former ruler **Mezentius** join Aeneas.
- Venus obtains divine armor made by Vulcan.
- Aeneas’s shield depicts future Roman history, culminating in Augustus’s victory at Actium.

The shield makes Aeneas carry an imperial future that he cannot understand fully.

### Siege of the Trojan camp

While Aeneas is absent, Turnus attacks. Trojan ships are transformed into sea nymphs rather than burned. **Nisus and Euryalus** attempt a night expedition, kill sleeping enemies, and die.

### Death of Pallas

Turnus kills Pallas and takes his decorated sword belt as a trophy. Aeneas, enraged, conducts a destructive counterattack and takes captives intended for sacrifice at Pallas’s funeral.

The episode complicates the image of *pius Aeneas*. His devotion to Pallas and Evander produces grief, vengeance, and conduct that later readers have judged against his stated civilizing mission.

### Mezentius and Lausus

Aeneas kills **Lausus**, who dies defending his father Mezentius. Aeneas immediately recognizes the young man’s filial courage and regrets the killing. He then kills Mezentius.

The pairing mirrors Aeneas and Anchises: filial devotion exists on both sides.

### Truce, renewed war, and Camilla

Attempts at negotiation fail. The warrior maiden **Camilla**, allied with Turnus, fights successfully before being killed by Arruns. Diana’s attendant Opis avenges her.

### Duel with Turnus

A treaty provides for single combat. Juturna, Turnus’s divine sister, helps break the agreement. Fighting resumes. Aeneas is wounded by an arrow and healed with Venus’s assistance.

Aeneas threatens the Latin city. Amata, believing Turnus dead, kills herself. Turnus finally confronts Aeneas.

Turnus is defeated and asks that his body be returned to his father. Aeneas hesitates and appears ready to spare him. He then sees Pallas’s belt on Turnus and kills him in vengeance.

The poem ends at the moment Turnus’s life descends to the shades. It does not narrate Aeneas’s marriage, settlement, reign, or death.

## Marriage, Rule, and Political Settlement

### Livy’s sequence

Livy offers a compressed, partly different account:

- Aeneas reaches Laurentum.
- Trojans and local inhabitants initially prepare for battle.
- Latinus either loses and then makes peace, or meets Aeneas before battle and forms an alliance.
- Aeneas marries Lavinia.
- Lavinium is founded and named after her.
- Trojans and Aborigines are united under the name **Latins**.
- Turnus, supported by the Etruscan king Mezentius, makes war.
- Latinus dies.
- Aeneas secures the population’s unity by applying the Latin name to both groups.
- Aeneas disappears after another battle near the Numicius.

Livy openly acknowledges alternative versions, an important sign that no stable “official biography” existed.

### Dionysius’s sequence

Dionysius presents a much fuller rationalized history:

- Aeneas leads a mixed migrant force through multiple settlements.
- He negotiates with Latinus.
- Marriage and territorial arrangements create an alliance.
- War follows with Turnus and other opponents.
- Lavinium is founded.
- Aeneas disappears after battle.
- A hero-shrine marks the place associated with him.

Dionysius compares earlier writers, including Hellanicus, Cephalon, Hegesippus, Arctinus, Sophocles, Menecrates, and Roman authorities. His catalogue is invaluable evidence for diversity in the tradition but was written many centuries after the supposed events.

### Foundations attributed to Aeneas

Ancient communities across the Mediterranean claimed Aeneas as founder, visitor, or cult bearer. Places associated with him included sites in:

- the Troad;
- Thrace;
- Delos;
- Crete;
- Epirus;
- Sicily;
- southern Italy;
- Latium.

Individual claims often served local prestige, diplomatic, religious, or political purposes. The proliferation of “Aeneas routes” is evidence for the spread of the legend, not proof of a single historical itinerary.

### Government and institutions

No reliable evidence identifies actual laws, offices, administrative institutions, taxation, business enterprises, investments, property records, or financial structures belonging to Aeneas.

Within legend he is successively:

- a Dardanian prince and battlefield commander;
- leader of a refugee fleet;
- ritual head of a migrating community;
- diplomatic negotiator;
- alliance builder;
- war leader;
- founder or ruler of Lavinium;
- ancestor of the Alban and Roman dynasties.

## Death, Disappearance, and Apotheosis

### Battle at the Numicius

Livy says Aeneas disappeared after a battle against the Etruscans and Rutuli near the river **Numicius**. Whatever mortal or divine interpretation was preferred, he was afterward called **Jupiter Indiges**.

Dionysius reports that Aeneas’s body was not found. Some believed he had been translated to the gods; others believed he drowned in the river. A hero-shrine near the Numicius carried a dedication to the local father and god who presided over the river.

These are **ancient reports**, not verified cause-of-death findings.

### Ovid’s apotheosis

In *Metamorphoses* 14, Venus asks Jupiter to deify Aeneas. The river god Numicius washes away his mortal substance; Venus anoints him with divine substances, and he becomes **Indiges**.

This is a fully mythological apotheosis account.

### Length of reign

Ancient chronographers and historians vary. Aeneas is commonly said to have survived only a few years after the settlement in Latium. Exact regnal dates cannot be established.

### Lavinia after Aeneas

Some traditions say Lavinia was pregnant when Aeneas died. Fearing Ascanius, she took refuge in the woods with the shepherd or official Tyrrhus and gave birth to Silvius. Later reconciliation restored her and the child.

This story belongs to dynastic legend and helps reconcile competing lines of succession.

## Religion, Philosophy, and Ideological Development

Aeneas left no theological or philosophical writings. His religious identity is constructed by poets, historians, cult practice, and political reception.

### Homeric phase

The Homeric Aeneas is:

- the mortal son of Aphrodite;
- favored by multiple gods;
- protected because of a future dynastic destiny;
- a warrior with an independent grievance against Priam.

His future is Trojan or Dardanian, not explicitly Roman.

### Archaic hymn tradition

The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* uses his birth to explore:

- the boundary between gods and mortals;
- mortality and aging;
- divine sexuality;
- shame and unequal unions;
- dynastic continuity.

### Greek migration traditions

Post-Homeric Greek authors relocated Aeneas after Troy’s fall. Traditions sent him to Thrace, the Pallene peninsula, Arcadia, Sicily, Italy, or continued Trojan rule. These versions likely developed through colonial geography, cult diffusion, local foundation stories, and genealogical politics.

### Roman republican development

By the late Republic, Aeneas was embedded in Roman origins:

- Lavinium was connected with Rome’s sacred Penates.
- Roman families asserted Trojan ancestry.
- The Julii claimed descent from Iulus and therefore Venus.
- Julius Caesar placed Venus and Aeneas imagery on coinage.
- The story linked Roman warfare and government with religious continuity and filial duty.

### Augustan transformation

Virgil’s *Aeneid*, composed during Augustus’s rise and consolidation of power, made Aeneas the carrier of Rome’s providential future.

The poem’s political meanings remain disputed. It can sustain several readings simultaneously:

- legitimation of Augustus and the Julian dynasty;
- celebration of Roman order after civil war;
- acknowledgment of the suffering required by empire;
- exposure of violence, loss, and suppressed alternatives;
- tension between public duty and private attachment;
- uncertainty about whether promised peace overcomes recurrent vengeance.

The poem itself, rather than any recoverable personal ideology of Aeneas, is the evidence.

### Cult

Aeneas was honored as a hero or indigenous deity in Latium under the name **Indiges**. The exact relationship among Aeneas, Pater Indiges, and Jupiter Indiges developed over time and is not completely secure.

Lavinium remained central to Roman religious ideas about origins and the Penates. Roman magistrates maintained ritual connections with the site.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

No companies, patents, commercial accounts, investments, or authenticated possessions can be assigned to Aeneas.

### Ships and migration infrastructure

Virgil describes Aeneas as commanding a fleet and organizing:

- ship construction in the Troad;
- transport of refugees and sacred objects;
- provisioning and sacrifices;
- settlement attempts;
- diplomatic embassies;
- military defenses;
- burial and commemorative games.

These are literary components of refugee leadership, not administrative records.

### Sacred technologies and objects

Objects associated with Aeneas include:

- the **Penates**, carried from Troy;
- the **Palladium**, in some traditions carried by Aeneas, although other traditions assign its removal to Diomedes and Odysseus;
- the **golden bough**, ritual credential for entering the underworld;
- divine armor made by Vulcan;
- the shield depicting future Roman history.

The contradictions surrounding the Palladium are especially important. Roman iconography could show Aeneas carrying it even though influential Greek stories said it had been stolen from Troy by Greek heroes.

### Settlements and public projects

Attributed foundations include Aeneadae, Pergamea, Lavinium, and other local sites. These claims cannot be converted into verified construction projects by an historical Aeneas.

## Political Uses and Financial Relationships

### Roman state origins

The Aeneas narrative joined three stages of Roman legendary genealogy:

1. Aeneas brings Trojan people and gods to Latium.
2. Ascanius/Iulus founds Alba Longa.
3. The Alban line eventually produces Romulus and Remus.

This solved the chronological distance between the traditional fall of Troy and Rome’s traditional foundation by inserting generations of Alban kings.

### The Julian family

The Julian family asserted descent from Iulus. Because Iulus was Aeneas’s son and Aeneas was Venus’s son, the genealogy furnished divine ancestry.

Julius Caesar issued denarii showing Venus on one side and Aeneas carrying Anchises and the Palladium on the other. The coin was real circulating money and therefore made genealogy, religious legitimacy, and political identity visible in everyday exchange.

This is a **documented political and financial use of Aeneas’s image**, not proof of the genealogy.

### Augustus and monumental imagery

The Augustan cultural program repeatedly connected the Julian house, Venus, Aeneas, Romulus, and Rome. The Ara Pacis includes a sacrificial panel conventionally identified as Aeneas, though some scholars identify the figure as Numa. The official museum presentation acknowledges Aeneas as the traditional interpretation and also links Venus to Aeneas and the Julian family.

Virgil’s shield of Aeneas culminates in Augustus at Actium. The object compresses mythic ancestry and recent civil war into a divinely manufactured artifact.

### Imperial replication

Aeneas continued to appear on Roman imperial coins. An aureus of Antoninus Pius, dated approximately 140–144 CE, shows Aeneas carrying Anchises and leading Ascanius. The three generations made him a reusable emblem of continuity, preservation, and legitimate succession.

## Successes and Failures Within the Narrative

### Significant successes

- Survives repeated battlefield encounters with divine help.
- Preserves Anchises, Ascanius, followers, and sacred objects from Troy.
- Organizes a multinational migration.
- Rejects premature settlements.
- Secures alliances in Sicily, Arcadia, and Etruria.
- Reaches Latium.
- Defeats Mezentius and Turnus.
- Establishes the dynastic conditions for Lavinium, Alba Longa, and Rome.
- Becomes the object of cult and alleged apotheosis.

### Significant failures and losses

- Cannot save Troy.
- Loses Creusa.
- Misinterprets or follows Anchises’s mistaken interpretation of the Cretan oracle.
- Loses ships and companions during storms and wanderings.
- Loses Anchises before reaching Italy.
- Abandons Dido, with catastrophic consequences for her.
- Fails to protect Pallas.
- Is unable to prevent war between migrants and local inhabitants.
- Ends the epic by killing a defeated opponent at the moment mercy appears possible.
- Never sees Rome, the civilization for which his suffering is retrospectively justified.

## Controversies, Allegations, Violence, and Unresolved Questions

Because Aeneas is not historically verified, these controversies concern narrative conduct and the formation of traditions, not judicially actionable findings about a documented person.

### Alleged betrayal of Troy

- **Claim:** Aeneas helped the Greeks capture Troy because Paris had dishonored him.
- **Source:** Menecrates of Xanthus, quoted by Dionysius of Halicarnassus.
- **Classification:** Ancient allegation.
- **Evidence for circulation:** Dionysius’s quotation proves that the story existed.
- **Evidence for the underlying act:** None independent of literary tradition.
- **Counter-traditions:** Heroic resistance, divinely ordered withdrawal, absence on campaign, negotiated release, and sparing for pro-peace conduct.
- **Reputational effect:** Supported hostile and medieval portrayals of Aeneas as a traitor or opportunist.

### Treatment of Creusa

- **Claim:** Aeneas negligently or deliberately abandoned his wife during the escape.
- **Source basis:** Virgil narrates her loss after Aeneas tells the family to follow him separately.
- **Classification:** Interpretive accusation, not an explicit ancient finding.
- **Counter-evidence within the poem:** He returns to dangerous Troy searching for her; her shade says divine powers detained her and instructs him to continue.
- **Unresolved issue:** Virgil does not narrate the physical circumstances of her death.

### Dido: abandonment, deception, and responsibility

- **Documented narrative facts in Virgil:** Aeneas accepts Dido’s intimacy, prepares to leave secretly, rejects her interpretation of marriage, departs under divine command, and does not return.
- **Disputed question:** Whether the cave union is a marriage. Dido treats it as one; Aeneas denies formal marriage.
- **Mitigating narrative elements:** Gods manipulate both parties; Jupiter explicitly orders departure; Aeneas is shown suffering.
- **Aggravating narrative elements:** He conceals preparations and benefits from Dido’s hospitality before leaving.
- **Historical status:** Later literary construction, not a chronologically plausible meeting.
- **Reputational effect:** Made “false Aeneas” a durable figure in love literature.

### Human sacrifice or threatened sacrifice

After Pallas’s death, Virgil depicts Aeneas taking young captives to be sacrificed at Pallas’s funeral pyre. This is **narrative fact within the poem**. It complicates simple descriptions of him as invariably humane.

### Killing of Turnus

- **Narrative fact:** Turnus is defeated and petitions for mercy; Aeneas hesitates; seeing Pallas’s belt, he kills Turnus.
- **Legal characterization:** No historical legal forum exists.
- **Interpretations:** legitimate vengeance, execution of a treaty-breaker, ritual repayment, loss of self-control, failure of clemency, or deliberately unresolved conflict between duty and rage.
- **Textual significance:** The abrupt ending prevents narrative reconciliation.

### Captive women and forced dynastic marriage

The migration and Italian war unfold in societies where women’s marriages are instruments of dynastic settlement. Lavinia speaks little or not at all in major ancient versions; others negotiate and fight over her marriage. Modern analysis frequently treats this silence as structurally significant, but it does not supply evidence of a recoverable historical Lavinia’s wishes.

### Colonialism and indigeneity

Aeneas has been read as refugee, colonizer, conqueror, immigrant, indigenous ancestor, and imperial prototype. Ancient sources themselves disagree about whether settlement follows hospitality, battle, negotiated land grants, intermarriage, or conquest.

No single modern political category exhausts the material.

## Illnesses, Injuries, and Psychological Crises

### Physical injuries

- Diomedes injures Aeneas with a stone in *Iliad* 5; divine healers restore him.
- In the Italian war, an unidentified arrow wounds him. The physician Iapyx attempts treatment; Venus secretly supplies healing power.
- No historically documented medical record exists.

### Grief and psychological strain

Virgil repeatedly depicts Aeneas concealing distress for the sake of followers. Major crises include:

- Troy’s destruction;
- Creusa’s disappearance;
- Anchises’s death;
- conflict between affection for Dido and obligation to leave;
- Palinurus’s death;
- encounter with Dido’s silent shade;
- Pallas’s death;
- rage against Turnus.

Descriptions of depression, trauma disorders, or other modern diagnoses would be retrospective speculation. The text supports grief, fear, rage, guilt, and emotional suppression, but not a clinical diagnosis.

### Addictions

No ancient source gives Aeneas an addiction.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Divine birth

- **Story:** Aphrodite conceived Aeneas with Anchises on Mount Ida.
- **Earliest surviving context:** Homer and the archaic *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite*.
- **Classification:** Myth.
- **Function:** Explains divine protection, beauty, dynastic destiny, and Julian claims through Venus.

### Nymphal upbringing

- **Story:** Mountain nymphs raised Aeneas until early childhood.
- **Source:** *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite*.
- **Classification:** Myth.
- **Function:** Conceals Aphrodite’s union while marking Aeneas as liminally divine.

### Anchises struck by lightning

- **Story:** Zeus punished Anchises for revealing his union with Aphrodite.
- **Versions:** death, blindness, or lameness.
- **Classification:** Myth with variant outcomes.
- **Function:** Enforces secrecy around divine–mortal sexuality and explains Anchises’s disability.

### Destined survivor of Priam’s line

- **Story:** The gods preserve Aeneas so that his descendants can rule the Trojans.
- **Source:** *Iliad* 20.
- **Classification:** Mythic prophecy.
- **Later transformation:** Roman tradition converts continued Trojan rule into western migration and Roman ancestry.

### Carrying Anchises from Troy

- **Story:** Aeneas carries his elderly father while escaping.
- **Attestation:** Pre-Virgilian Greek drama, Roman art, coinage, and Virgil.
- **Classification:** Myth; documented ancient iconographic tradition.
- **Reputational effect:** Became the supreme image of filial duty.

### The Palladium

- **Story:** Aeneas saves Troy’s sacred image of Athena and brings it to Italy.
- **Contradiction:** Greek traditions often say Diomedes and Odysseus stole it.
- **Classification:** Competing sacred legends.
- **Political effect:** Roman possession of Trojan sacra represented continuity and divine protection.

### Betrayal of Troy

- **Story:** Aeneas delivered Troy to the Greeks.
- **Circulator:** Menecrates and subsequent hostile or skeptical traditions.
- **Classification:** Ancient allegation.
- **Effect:** Counter-myth to the loyal savior narrative.

### Dido’s curse and the Punic Wars

- **Story:** Dido calls for eternal conflict between her people and Aeneas’s descendants.
- **Source:** Virgil.
- **Classification:** Poetic aetiology and retrospective political myth.
- **Evidence against historicity:** incompatible traditional chronologies; lack of an earlier meeting tradition.
- **Effect:** Personalized Roman–Carthaginian conflict as the consequence of broken intimacy.

### Eating the tables

- **Story:** The Trojans will become so hungry that they eat their tables.
- **Resolution:** Bread used as a platter is eaten.
- **Classification:** Folktale-like prophecy and fulfillment motif.

### The white sow and thirty piglets

- **Story:** A white sow with thirty offspring identifies the settlement site.
- **Interpretations:** thirty years before Alba Longa; thirty Latin communities; local cult symbolism.
- **Classification:** Foundation omen.
- **Evidence:** literary reports and Roman religious/iconographic tradition, not a verified event.

### Descent into the underworld

- **Story:** Aeneas enters the land of the dead under Sibylline guidance and meets Anchises.
- **Classification:** Mythic katabasis and literary construction.
- **Function:** authorizes the Roman future through ancestral revelation.

### Apotheosis

- **Story:** Aeneas disappears in the Numicius and becomes Indiges.
- **Classification:** Hero-cult and apotheosis myth.
- **Evidence:** ancient literary testimony and cultic associations; no evidence of a bodily translation.

### Aeneas as direct founder of Rome

Popular retellings often call Aeneas the founder of Rome. In the developed ancient genealogy, he normally founds **Lavinium**, while Ascanius founds **Alba Longa** and Romulus founds **Rome** generations later. Calling Aeneas Rome’s founder is symbolically understandable but genealogically imprecise.

### Trojan ancestry of European nations

Medieval chronicles connected Trojan refugees and Aeneas’s extended lineage with Britons, Franks, Normans, and other peoples.

- **Classification:** Political and genealogical foundation myth.
- **Evidence:** documented medieval texts.
- **Evidence for biological descent:** none.
- **Effect:** Supplied prestigious antiquity, connected emerging polities with classical history, and authorized conquest or succession.

## Archaeology and Material Evidence

### The Lavinium heroon

Excavations near ancient Lavinium uncovered a tumulus originally containing a high-status burial conventionally dated around the seventh century BCE. It was later remodeled as a monumental cult place.

Ancient Dionysius described a hero-shrine near the Numicius believed to be connected with Aeneas. Modern scholarship has therefore commonly called the excavated monument the **Heroon of Aeneas**.

What the site demonstrates:

- an archaic elite burial existed;
- the monument underwent later cultic reinterpretation;
- Lavinium possessed strong ancestral and foundation cults;
- by a later period the place could be identified with Aeneas or Pater Indiges.

What it does not demonstrate:

- that the buried individual was Aeneas;
- that a Trojan prince founded Lavinium;
- that Virgil’s itinerary is historical;
- that the burial dates to the conventional Trojan War era.

A specialist archaeological study notes the possibility that a man buried around 680 BCE was transformed generations later into the focus of an Indiges cult, with explicit identification as Aeneas occurring still later.

### Sanctuary of the thirteen altars

Lavinium’s sanctuary of thirteen altars documents the city’s exceptional ritual importance. It supports the reality of major Latin cult practices but cannot by itself authenticate Aeneas.

### Iconography

Images of a man carrying an older man—often identified as Aeneas and Anchises—occur before Virgil in Greek and Etruscan contexts. This establishes the antiquity and geographical range of the escape motif.

Roman images add dynastic and sacred detail:

- Aeneas carries Anchises.
- Aeneas leads Ascanius.
- Anchises or Aeneas carries sacred objects.
- The Palladium signifies preservation of Troy’s divine guarantee.
- Venus connects the scene to Julian descent.

### Coinage

Julius Caesar’s silver denarius, conventionally dated 47–46 BCE, is among the most important securely dated political uses. An imperial aureus under Antoninus Pius shows the three-generation escape group.

These artifacts document belief, symbolism, and political messaging—not the Bronze Age existence of Aeneas.

## Works by or Attributed to Aeneas

### Authentic works

None.

No book, letter, inscription, speech, law code, memoir, diary, technical work, patent, or artwork can be authenticated as the work of Aeneas.

### Speeches placed in his mouth

Ancient poets and historians composed speeches for Aeneas. These are works **attributed within literary narrative**, not transcripts.

Major examples include:

- speeches to Achilles in Homer’s *Iliad*;
- accounts of Troy’s fall and the wanderings in *Aeneid* 2–3;
- reassurance of shipwrecked companions in *Aeneid* 1;
- explanations and defenses addressed to Dido in *Aeneid* 4;
- prayers, diplomatic statements, funeral orations, battlefield speeches, and the final words to Turnus in later books;
- speeches reconstructed by Dionysius in the conventions of ancient historiography.

### Prophecies concerning Aeneas

These are not his works but are central to the textual corpus:

- Aphrodite’s prophecy in the *Homeric Hymn*;
- Poseidon’s dynastic prophecy in the *Iliad*;
- Hector’s command in the *Aeneid*;
- oracles of Apollo and Helenus;
- the Sibyl’s predictions;
- Anchises’s revelation of Roman history.

## Principal Ancient Literary Sources

### Greek sources

- **Homer, *Iliad***, especially Books 2, 5, 13, 17, and 20. Earliest extensive surviving portrayal.
- **Hesiod, *Theogony***, lines 1008–1010. Brief genealogy: Aphrodite and Anchises produce Aeneas.
- **Homeric Hymn 5, *To Aphrodite***. Full conception and birth prophecy.
- **Arctinus, *Iliou Persis***, lost except for summaries and fragments. Relevant to Troy’s destruction.
- **Sophocles, *Laocoön***, lost; fragments preserved by Dionysius.
- **Hellanicus of Lesbos, *Troica***, fragmentary; used by Dionysius for migration traditions.
- **Menecrates of Xanthus**, fragment alleging betrayal.
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca***. Genealogical synthesis.
- **Lycophron, *Alexandra***. Obscure prophetic treatment connecting Trojan migrations and Italy.
- **Strabo, *Geography***. Local traditions involving Aeneas and Trojan movements.
- **Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities*, Book 1**. Most extensive comparative prose dossier of pre-Virgilian and Roman traditions.
- **Plutarch**, scattered references to Roman and Trojan origins.
- **Cassius Dio**, later Roman historical deployment of Julian and Trojan genealogy.

### Latin sources

- **Gnaeus Naevius, *Bellum Punicum***, fragmentary. Treated Aeneas and Roman–Carthaginian history before Virgil.
- **Quintus Ennius, *Annales***, fragmentary. Important for Roman historical epic and origins.
- **Marcus Porcius Cato, *Origines***, fragmentary. Reportedly addressed Italian foundations and peoples.
- **Marcus Terentius Varro**, surviving and fragmentary antiquarian works. Important to Roman religious and genealogical organization.
- **Virgil, *Aeneid***, composed approximately 29–19 BCE and published after Virgil’s death. The dominant surviving biography.
- **Livy, *Ab Urbe Condita*, Book 1**. Rationalizing foundation history with explicit variants.
- **Horace**, allusions to Trojan and Augustan ideology.
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses*, Books 13–14**. Later journey, war, and apotheosis.
- **Ovid, *Heroides* 7**. Dido’s fictional letter to Aeneas; intensifies the accusation of betrayal.
- **Propertius**, allusions to Aeneas and Augustan Rome.
- **Silius Italicus, *Punica***. Uses Aeneas–Dido ancestry in Punic War epic.
- **Statius**, allusive engagement with Virgilian heroism.
- **Servius, *Commentary on the Aeneid***. Preserves variant myths, antiquarian explanations, and lost-source material.
- **Macrobius, *Saturnalia***. Important for Virgil’s sources and cultural authority.
- **Historia Augusta** and later imperial literature, where Trojan descent could remain politically symbolic.

## Post-Classical Works Featuring or Reworking Aeneas

### Late antique and medieval literature

- **Dares Phrygius, *De excidio Troiae historia*** — a pseudo-eyewitness Trojan history influential in the Middle Ages; attribution and eyewitness status are fictitious.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Greek hero to Roman ancestor

The most important transformation is geographical and ideological:

- Homer: future ruler of surviving Trojans.
- Post-Homeric traditions: migrant hero with multiple possible destinations.
- Roman Republic: founder connected with Latium’s cults and Roman ancestry.
- Virgil: bearer of universal Roman destiny.
- Empire: emblem of legitimate succession and sacred continuity.
- Middle Ages: ancestor, lover, alleged traitor, moral allegory, and foundation figure.
- Modernity: refugee, colonizer, survivor, imperial instrument, traumatized veteran, absent husband, and contested ethical subject.

### Filial piety

The image of Aeneas carrying Anchises became independent of the poem. It represents:

- preservation of the past;
- filial duty;
- transfer of sacred authority;
- survival through catastrophe;
- the linking of parent, adult child, and future heir.

Bernini’s sculpture makes the three generations physically interdependent: Anchises bears sacred ancestry, Aeneas bears the present burden, and Ascanius embodies the future.

### Political genealogy

Aeneas enabled rulers and states to claim antiquity without directly identifying themselves with conquered Troy’s defeat. Survival and transfer turned loss into legitimacy.

The Julian claim connected Caesar and Augustus with Venus. Later rulers reused the image without sharing a literal Julian pedigree, because Aeneas had become a generalized emblem of Roman continuity.

### Dido’s counter-reputation

Dido-centered traditions often reverse the hierarchy of Virgil’s story. Aeneas can become:

- the deserter rather than founder;
- the cause of a woman’s destruction;
- an example of male inconstancy;
- a representative of state destiny overriding private ethics.

Ovid’s *Heroides*, medieval women’s writing, Marlowe, Purcell, and modern adaptations all strengthened this counter-tradition.

### Modern refugee symbolism

Modern writers and artists frequently interpret Aeneas as a displaced survivor carrying an elderly parent and guiding a child through a destroyed city. This reading has made the ancient image relevant to war, forced migration, exile, and cultural memory.

It is a modern reception grounded in ancient iconography, not evidence that the myth records one identifiable refugee’s life.

### Historical assessment

The fullest defensible conclusion is limited:

- Aeneas existed as a named Trojan hero in archaic Greek epic.
- Traditions of his survival were early.
- His migration destinations multiplied in post-Homeric storytelling.
- His association with Italy and Lavinium became established before Virgil.
- Republican Roman families and institutions used him genealogically and religiously.
- Virgil synthesized, altered, and monumentalized the traditions.
- Archaeology confirms an important archaic and later cult landscape at Lavinium but not Aeneas’s personal existence.
- His “biography” is a layered archive of Mediterranean migration stories, Roman state formation, dynastic propaganda, religious cult, literary invention, and later reinterpretation.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by sources or explicit literary relationships are listed. Mere coexistence in the atlas roster is excluded.

### Aeneas

- **Nature:** Subject of this dossier; self-entry in the atlas roster.
- **Dates and places:** Mythic Trojan War era; Troad, Mediterranean itinerary, and Latium.
- **Label:** Mythic personage; documented literary and cultic reception.

### Homer

- **Nature:** The *Iliad* supplies the earliest extensive surviving representation of Aeneas: his Dardanian command, genealogy, resentment of Priam, combat, divine rescues, and future dynasty.
- **Date and place:** Greek epic conventionally dated to the late eighth or seventh century BCE, drawing on older oral traditions.
- **Label:** **Documented literary connection**; not a biographical meeting.

### Publius Vergilius Maro

- **Nature:** Author of the *Aeneid*, the dominant reconstruction of Aeneas’s escape, wanderings, Dido relationship, Italian war, and Roman destiny.
- **Dates and places:** Composed approximately 29–19 BCE in the Roman world.
- **Label:** **Documented literary connection**; not a historical meeting.

### Dido

- **Nature:** Lover, host, and political counterpart in Virgil; her death and curse become a mythic origin of Roman–Carthaginian enmity.
- **Dates and places:** Carthage in the poem; the traditional chronologies of Dido and the Trojan War are incompatible.
- **Label:** **Later literary/mythic relationship**, not historical fact.

### Odysseus

- **Nature:** Aeneas and Odysseus do not have a securely narrated personal meeting in Homer. Their stories are structurally connected: Virgil models the first half of Aeneas’s journey partly on the *Odyssey*. Aeneas also rescues Achaemenides, a companion abandoned after Odysseus’s Cyclops episode. Some Palladium traditions assign its theft to Odysseus and Diomedes, conflicting with Roman traditions in which Aeneas carries it.
- **Dates and places:** Mythic Trojan War and Mediterranean wanderings.
- **Label:** **Documented literary relationship and competing myth**, not a verified encounter.

### Gaius Julius Caesar

- **Nature:** Caesar’s gens claimed descent through Iulus from Aeneas and Venus. Caesar issued denarii showing Venus and Aeneas carrying Anchises and the Palladium.
- **Dates and places:** Approximately 47–46 BCE; Roman Republican military coinage.
- **Label:** **Documented political, genealogical, and numismatic reception**; the ancestry itself is legendary.

### Dante Alighieri

- **Nature:** Dante explicitly treats Aeneas’s journey to the underworld as providential preparation for Rome. In *Inferno* 2, Dante questions whether he is worthy to follow Aeneas and Paul.
- **Dates and places:** Early fourteenth-century Italy.
- **Label:** **Documented literary influence**, not a personal meeting.

### Paul the Apostle

- **Nature:** Dante pairs Aeneas and Paul as privileged travelers to the world beyond, predecessors whom Dante fears he cannot equal.
- **Dates and places:** Connection created in *Inferno* 2, early fourteenth century.
- **Label:** **Documented later literary association**, not an ancient relationship.

### William Morris

- **Nature:** Morris published an English verse translation, *The Aeneids of Virgil*, in 1876.
- **Dates and places:** Britain, 1876.
- **Label:** **Documented translation and reception connection**.

### Julius Caesar, Homer, Virgil, Dido, Odysseus, Dante, Paul, and William Morris exhaust the clearly supported intersections identified in the supplied roster during this research. No additional connection has been inferred merely from shared geography, classical subject matter, or later cultural prominence.
