# Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa: Research Dossier

**Prepared:** 24 August 2026  
**Scope:** Reconstructable life, writings, networks, controversies, legends, and reception of Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa, approximately 1486–1535.  
**Method note:** Agrippa’s surviving correspondence documents much of 1507–1534, but the early 1500s and his final year remain fragmentary. Modern specialist reconstructions—especially Charles G. Nauert, Vittoria Perrone Compagni, Marc van der Poel, Paola Zambelli, Christopher Lehrich, and later reassessments—are used alongside digitized early editions and contemporary or near-contemporary testimony. Exact claims are qualified where the evidence does not warrant certainty.

“Agrippa” was an adopted learned name evoking Roman antiquity, not proof of descent from Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa. Likewise, the particle “von/ab Nettesheim” does not by itself establish noble ancestry. Agrippa increasingly represented his family as long associated with Habsburg service, and he styled himself knight and doctor; parts of this status presentation cannot be independently verified.

## Family and Ancestry

The Cologne matriculation record names his father as **Henricus de Nettesheym** and the student as a Cologne citizen. The father, also called Heinrich, reportedly died in 1519; his occupation and the mother’s identity are not securely documented in the sources consulted. Agrippa later claimed that his family had served the Habsburgs, a claim that may contain truth but also served a patronage strategy. No reliable reconstruction of siblings or remote ancestry is presently available.

Agrippa married three times:

1. **First wife (name unknown), from Pavia**, married during his Italian years, probably around 1515. She died before his move to Geneva, apparently about 1521. The marriage produced children, but the total and their identities are incompletely recorded.
2. **Second wife, Iana Loysa Tytia** (modernized as Jeanne Loyse Tissie; spelling uncertain), married at Geneva in spring 1521. She accompanied him to Lyon and Antwerp and died of plague at Antwerp in **1529**, shortly after childbirth. SEP’s older synthesis gives March; a reconstruction from the letters gives **17 August**. One daughter died young; five sons have been reported, three surviving: Aymon, Henry, and Jean. Agrippa’s letters express grief and financial strain.
3. **Third wife**, married at Mechelen after 1529. Johann Wier/Weyer says Agrippa repudiated or divorced her at Bonn in 1535. The grounds and legal form are obscure, and hostile accounts turned the marriage into a morality tale.

At least **three sons** survived and later appear in French court records at Saint-Antoine-de-Viennois under the surname **Corneille-Agrippa**. His first wife had borne a son by March 1517; that child’s later fate is unclear. Some children died young. Exact totals vary and should not be treated as established. No reliable evidence of extramarital partners has been found.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1486–1502

Agrippa grew up in or near Cologne, a major ecclesiastical, commercial, and university center. At about thirteen he matriculated at the **University of Cologne** in 1499. He received the **baccalaureate in arts in 1500** and **master of arts in 1502**.

Cologne’s arts faculty contained rival Thomist and Albertist currents. Agrippa later named the *Speculum* associated with **Albertus Magnus** as an early formative influence. His education included scholastic logic and natural philosophy, but humanist teachers also directed him toward **Pliny the Elder** and **Ramon Llull**. This combination—university natural philosophy, encyclopedic natural lore, combinatory arts, and claims of hidden properties—became foundational to his later magic.

Agrippa later styled himself doctor of both laws and of medicine. No surviving university record proves either doctorate. His employment as Metz’s *advocatus* and the city’s use of the title “doctor of laws” show substantial legal competence. His licensing and apparently successful medical practice in Geneva, Fribourg, Lyon, and Antwerp make medical training highly probable, but not the degree itself.

## Education and Formation, 1502–1510

### Paris and the occult circle

The years after 1502 are poorly documented. Agrippa spent time in **Paris**, where he formed a group of French friends interested in humanism, theology, alchemy, and occult philosophy. His Latinized correspondence makes identification difficult, but the network may have included **Jean Perréal**, **Germain de Ganay**, **Germain de Brie**, **Charles de Bovelles**, and **Symphorien Champier**.

A mysterious **1508 expedition to Spain**, apparently military or political and undertaken with two Paris associates, is known principally through opaque letters. Its purpose, employer, and outcome cannot be securely reconstructed. Later biographical claims of espionage or secret-society business are speculation, not documented fact.

### Dôle, 1509

In 1509 Agrippa lectured at the **University of Dôle** in Franche-Comté on Johann Reuchlin’s *De verbo mirifico* (1494). Reuchlin used Hebrew and Christian-Cabalist interpretation to argue for Christ and the wonder-working power of the name Jesus. Agrippa’s lectures linked humanist philology, Christian theology, and Kabbalah.

The Franciscan provincial **Jean Catilinet** denounced him publicly as a “Judaizing heretic.” Agrippa’s intended patronage maneuver—dedicating *De nobilitate et praecellentia foeminei sexus* to **Margaret of Austria**—failed. He answered Catilinet with an *Expostulatio*, then left Dôle.

### Johannes Trithemius, 1509–1510

Late in 1509 or early 1510 Agrippa visited **Johannes Trithemius** at Würzburg. Trithemius—former abbot of Sponheim, Benedictine scholar, bibliographer, cryptographic author, and theorist of learned magic—was not simply a classroom professor but an older adviser and crucial legitimating mentor.

Agrippa completed the first three-book draft of *De occulta philosophia* and sent or presented it to Trithemius with a dedicatory letter dated **8 April 1510**. Trithemius praised the project but advised secrecy: communicate higher matters only to trustworthy friends and “give hay to the ox and sugar to the parrot,” a counsel often paraphrased as hiding mysteries from the vulgar. Their exchange places Agrippa’s mature magical architecture substantially before his Italian sojourn, although he revised it for more than two decades.

Trithemius’s influence included the hierarchy of natural, celestial, and intellectual magic; angelic and demonic operations; names, numbers, images, and spirit communication; and the premise that magic could be restored as learned Christian wisdom. Agrippa nevertheless ranged far beyond Trithemius, incorporating Ficino, Pico, Reuchlin, medieval Arabic-Latin magic, and later Italian sources.

## Chronological Life History

### 1510–1511: England and imperial service

Agrippa probably entered the service of Emperor **Maximilian I** and accompanied an imperial diplomatic mission to **England**. In London he attended **John Colet’s** lectures on the Pauline epistles. The episode reinforced biblical humanism and supplied a setting in which Agrippa answered Catilinet’s attack. Claims that he was a formal imperial spy go beyond the evidence; diplomatic service could involve intelligence gathering, but no specific clandestine dossier has been established.

### 1511–1518: Italy—war, lectures, marriage, and philosophical expansion

Agrippa spent roughly seven years in northern Italy, principally **Pavia, Casale Monferrato, and Turin**. He claimed military service, battlefield knighthood, and attendance at the anti-papal **Council of Pisa**. The precise campaigns and unit are uncertain. Biographies sometimes present the military career as settled fact; the evidentiary base is largely his own later claims.

At **Pavia** from about 1515, he lectured publicly on the Hermetic *Pimander*. He married his first wife, a woman of Pavia. War repeatedly disrupted the city and his prospects.

The Italian environment greatly enlarged his source base:

- **Marsilio Ficino**: Platonic theology, spiritus, astral medicine, spiritual magic.
- **Giovanni Pico della Mirandola**: human dignity, Kabbalah, the legitimation of natural magic.
- **Johann Reuchlin**: Christian Kabbalah, especially after *De arte cabalistica* (1517).
- **Agostino Ricci** and the works of **Paolo Ricci**: Jewish and Cabalistic materials, including Latin translations.
- **Ludovico Lazzarelli**: Hermetic regeneration and the *Crater Hermetis*.
- Medieval and Arabic-Latin authorities: al-Kindī, Picatrix material, Pietro d’Abano, Arnold of Villanova, Roger Bacon, Avicenna, and others.

In 1516 Agrippa dedicated *Dialogus de homine* and *De triplici ratione cognoscendi Deum* to **William IX, Marquis of Montferrat**. He lectured at Turin but failed to obtain a durable Savoyard court post. He also developed relations with the **Laurencin** banking family of Lyon, whose interest may have included alchemy and astrology.

### 1518–1520: Metz—law, reform controversy, and the witch case

In 1518 Agrippa became civic orator and legal adviser (*syndic/advocatus*) at **Metz**. His office placed him in municipal politics and litigation.

In **1519** he intervened for a rural woman accused of witchcraft before Dominican inquisitor **Nicolas Savin**. Agrippa attacked procedural defects and Savin’s reasoning—especially inherited guilt and the use of an accused woman’s mother as proof—and helped prevent the immediate execution. This was a significant legal defense.

It must not be exaggerated. Recent scholarship argues that Agrippa’s action flowed from his civic office and personal conflict with Savin, not from a general rejection of witchcraft doctrine. His writings retained elements of the cumulative witch concept and accepted punishment of genuinely harmful magic. The lost or perhaps nonexistent *Adversus lamiarum inquisitores*, once used to make him a systematic anti-witch-trial reformer, is now considered doubtful.

Agrippa also supported **Jacques Lefèvre d’Étaples** in the controversy over whether Saint Anne had multiple husbands. He wrote *De beatissimae Annae monogamia* defending the scriptural basis for Anne’s monogamy. Mendicant preachers again accused him of heresy. Pressure from the friars, municipal tensions, and personal loss contributed to his resignation. He returned temporarily to Cologne.

### 1521–1524: Geneva and Fribourg—medical career

Agrippa moved to **Geneva**, obtained a license to practice medicine, and served in connection with the city hospital, apparently as physician or director. He then became **city physician of Fribourg** in March 1523. Both appointments were paid and show that, notwithstanding uncertain degree records, contemporaries accepted his medical competence.

He left Fribourg after about six months, not demonstrably because of its ban on Lutheran books but to pursue a higher-status court appointment. In this period he married his second wife and supported a growing household.

### 1524–1528: Lyon and Louise of Savoy

In May 1524 Agrippa reached **Lyon**, wartime center of the French government, as physician to **Louise of Savoy**, mother of Francis I and regent during the king’s Italian campaign and captivity after Pavia.

Initially prestigious, the post became financially disastrous. His promised pension was delayed amid wartime fiscal crisis. Agrippa petitioned courtiers repeatedly, borrowed, and threatened to shift allegiance to the imperial side. These threats damaged his credibility.

He sought the patronage of **Marguerite d’Angoulême / Marguerite of Alençon**, later queen of Navarre, by dedicating a work on marriage to her. He received a modest gift but no secure appointment.

Louise expected astrological political prognostication. In 1526 Agrippa refused to cast a personal forecast for Francis I, arguing that judicial astrology predicting an individual future was superstitious and contrary to canon law. This was not a rejection of all astrology: celestial correspondences remained central to his occult philosophy. Louise took offense, and Agrippa was removed from the pension list in October 1526.

In the summer of 1526, amid debt and disillusionment, he composed *De incertitudine et vanitate scientiarum et artium*. Its attack on learned professions, courts, clergy, and occult arts reflects the genre of paradoxical declamation, biblical skepticism toward human knowledge, and his immediate experience of patronage failure.

He left for **Antwerp** in July 1528, crossing a war-disrupted frontier.

### 1528–1532: Antwerp and Mechelen—publication, imperial office, condemnation, debt

In Antwerp he initially supported his family through medicine. His second wife died of plague in March 1529.

Agrippa sought office from Margaret of Austria and accepted appointment as **imperial archivist and historiographer**, formally dated in one archival reconstruction to **27 December 1529**, although summaries sometimes date the service broadly from his 1528 arrival. His official productions included an account of **Charles V’s Bologna coronation** (1530) and a funeral oration for Margaret (died 1530).

Publication now accelerated:

- Michael Hillenius printed a collection of short treatises and orations in 1529, including the long-delayed praise of women.
- Johannes/Cornelius Grapheus printed *De vanitate* at Antwerp in 1530.
- A printed Book I of *De occulta philosophia* appeared in 1531 while the complete work was still being prepared.

Theological opposition was immediate. The **Faculty of Theology at Louvain** condemned *De vanitate* as scandalous, impious, and heretical. The matter reached the **Great Council/Parlement of Mechelen**. Agrippa was dismissed from court employment and told to suppress the work. He issued a *Querela* and detailed *Apologia*, rejecting the tribunal’s interpretation and attacking mendicant theologians.

Debt added coercive pressure. Creditors secured at least one brief imprisonment. Fearing renewed arrest, he left suddenly for Bonn in June 1532. There is no evidence of fraud, corruption, money laundering, or enrichment schemes; the financial record is instead one of unstable salaries, unpaid pensions, patronage seeking, medical earnings, dependents, printing costs, borrowing, and debt enforcement.

### 1532–1534: Bonn and Cologne—Hermann von Wied and the battle to print

**Hermann von Wied**, archbishop-elector of Cologne, gave Agrippa refuge and court status at **Bonn**. Hermann was interested in occult learning and moderate religious reform. Agrippa lived relatively comfortably among like-minded courtiers and corresponded with **Erasmus**, who sympathized with some criticisms but disliked Agrippa’s claim to wage “war against the monks” with Erasmian weapons.

Printer **Johannes Soter** began the complete *De occulta philosophia*. A Dominican inquisitor alleged heresy; Cologne’s council stopped the press and impounded sheets. Agrippa’s protest of 11 January 1533 attacked the theologians as incompetent enemies of humanist learning. Hermann overruled the obstruction, and the complete work appeared in July 1533.

Agrippa never retracted the magical project. The 1533 book included safeguards: he reported traditions rather than guaranteeing every claim and submitted condemned propositions to the Church. These qualifications were both religious and defensive, but the extensive revision shows continued commitment.

In 1534 he published *De beatissimae Annae monogamia* and related defenses. A pseudonymous *Dialogus de vanitate scientiarum et ruina Christianae religionis*, ostensibly by a Cistercian monk defending Agrippa, has been plausibly attributed to him by Paola Zambelli but remains disputed.

His final known letter, dated 22 February 1534, addressed the Mechelen tribunal and reiterated claims of illegal and unfair procedure.

### 1534–1535: France, arrest, and death

Why Agrippa left Bonn is unknown. Later claims that Charles V formally banished him from Germany lack proof. He returned to France in 1534 or 1535.

Johann Wier, his former live-in pupil and the most valuable near-contemporary witness, says Francis I had Agrippa arrested at Lyon for insults to Louise of Savoy in *De vanitate*. Friends obtained his release. He then died at **Grenoble**, probably in 1535. Later French antiquaries confirm Grenoble. Stories placing his death in Lyon, in a disreputable inn, deserted by all, belong primarily to hostile legend.

No securely documented fatal illness is known. Poverty or illness is plausible; plague is not established as his cause of death. Burial at Grenoble’s Dominican house is a later report, not supported here by a surviving burial record.

His “technologies” were textual and ritual: combinatory tables, planetary seals, number squares, alphabets, names, images, musical proportions, medicines, fumigations, and rites meant to align the practitioner with a hierarchical cosmos. The work transmitted many earlier recipes and diagrams; it should not be confused with modern experimental validation or an inventor’s patent portfolio.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Humanism and reform

Agrippa’s recurring method was *ad fontes*: return to Scripture, classical texts, Hebrew learning, and putatively ancient wisdom. He opposed scholastic claims that unaided Aristotelian reason could reach ultimate truth. The reform program overlapped with **Erasmus**, **Lefèvre d’Étaples**, and, for a time, some Lutheran criticisms of clerical institutions.

He never became a stable partisan of the Reformation. His writings invoked Church authority, sacraments, relics, holy water, penance, Eucharist, and traditional rites. The fairest description is a combative Catholic evangelical humanist with heterodox interests, not a covert Protestant proven by documentation.

### Occult philosophy

Agrippa’s three levels were:

1. **Natural/elemental magic**—hidden properties and sympathies in stones, plants, animals, bodies, medicines, and elements.
2. **Celestial/mathematical magic**—astrology, number, proportion, music, geometry, images, planetary characters, and intermediary *spiritus*.
3. **Intellectual/divine magic**—angels, divine names, Kabbalah, prayer, purification, prophecy, and the ascent of the mind.

Magic, properly reformed, was for Agrippa “the absolute perfection of the most noble philosophy.” Corrupt magic consisted of unintelligible formulas, superstition, illicit conjuration, and dealings with evil spirits. Yet his desire to purify magic did not eliminate ritual and demonic materials from the compendium.

His psychology divided the soul into **mens** (divine intellect), **ratio** (discursive, choosing reason), and **idolum** (imagination/sensation). True power required ratio to submit to mens rather than matter. Prayer, fasting, continence, cleanliness, charity, sacrament, and divine grace prepared the soul; success remained dependent on God.

### *De vanitate* and the alleged recantation

*De vanitate* attacks every art, including astrology, magic, law, medicine, and theology, and culminates in Scripture and faith. It has been read as skeptical recantation, satire, paradoxical declamation, evangelical critique, or a product of personal crisis.

The recantation theory is weakened by chronology: Agrippa kept revising and defending *De occulta philosophia* after composing *De vanitate*, used overlapping sources in both, and fought aggressively to print the magical work. A strong modern interpretation treats the books as complementary: false sense-based learning is demolished so that wisdom grounded in faith and divine illumination can be restored. Ambivalence remains genuine; it should not be flattened into either “he abandoned magic” or “the skepticism was insincere.”

### Women and gender

*De nobilitate et praecellentia foeminei sexus* argues first for the spiritual equality and then the superiority of women, reversing biblical and natural-philosophical commonplaces. Eve is the culmination of creation; Adam bears greater responsibility for the Fall; women are not naturally barred from education, authority, or office; subordination arises from unjust custom.

Interpretations differ. Some scholars read a deliberately absurd humanist paradox designed for display and patronage; others see a serious reversal of misogynist tradition or primarily a vehicle for unconventional theology rather than a social program. The patronage setting does not by itself prove insincerity. Nor does the work prove modern feminism or universal opposition to witch prosecution.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Legal Questions

### Heresy accusations

- **Documented accusation:** Catilinet’s 1509 charge of “Judaizing heresy.” No conviction followed.
- **Documented institutional condemnation:** Louvain theologians condemned *De vanitate*; the Mechelen government dismissed Agrippa and sought suppression. This was not a modern criminal conviction.
- **Documented publication obstruction:** Cologne’s council halted printing after an inquisitor’s heresy charge; Hermann von Wied overrode it.
- **Disputed/generalized claim:** that the Inquisition pursued Agrippa throughout Europe or sentenced him to death. The evidence shows several theological conflicts, not a single continuous international prosecution or documented death sentence.

### Imprisonment and debt

Agrippa was briefly jailed through creditor action in the Netherlands and feared recurrence. Wier reports a later French arrest for criticism of Louise of Savoy, followed by release through friends. Claims of repeated dungeon confinement for sorcery inflate the record.

### Witchcraft

The Metz defense is documented and important, but the image of Agrippa as a principled modern abolitionist is contested. He challenged bad law and inquisitorial procedure in one case; he did not deny all witchcraft or all punishment for harmful magic. The purported *Adversus lamiarum inquisitores* lacks a surviving text and is probably misattributed or imaginary.

### Astrology

Agrippa practiced and theorized astrology but rejected some forms of deterministic judicial prediction. His refusal to forecast Francis I’s fate was both an intellectual position and a career-damaging court act. It is inaccurate either to call him simply an astrologer-for-hire or to say he repudiated astrology wholesale.

### Self-presentation and status

Claims of noble descent, imperial knighthood, and multiple doctorates rely partly on Agrippa’s own titles. None is impossible; none is fully documented. His use of official titles on printed title pages was a normal credentialing strategy and later fed the legend that Charles V’s victories depended on Agrippa’s magic.

### Fraud, corruption, and criminal activity

No credible evidence was found for fraud, corruption, money laundering, homicide, or organized criminal activity. Hostile stories of corpse animation, demonic military assistance, and diabolical familiars are legends. Debt and abrasive patronage tactics are documented; they are not evidence of financial crime.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The black dog familiar

**Story:** Agrippa kept a large black dog that was the Devil or a familiar spirit. At death he removed its collar, cursed it as the cause of his ruin, and ordered it away; the dog plunged into a river and vanished.

**Appearance and circulation:** The legend developed after Agrippa’s death. Paolo Giovio’s *Elogia* helped establish the demonized death narrative; Johannes Manlius’s 1563 commonplace collection transmitted a dog-as-devil claim associated with Melanchthon’s circle. Later demonologists, biographers, and Faust traditions elaborated it.

**Evidence:** Wier, who knew Agrippa’s household, said his teacher owned ordinary dogs, including a favored black dog, and rejected the demonic story. No contemporary legal or household document establishes a familiar spirit.

**Effect:** The story became Agrippa’s most durable emblem and blended with the black-poodle motif in the Faust tradition. A real household animal supplied a plausible seed; the demonic interpretation is polemical folklore.

### The magically animated corpse

**Story:** A curious lodger or student entered Agrippa’s forbidden study, was killed by a demon, and Agrippa ordered a spirit to animate the corpse and walk it into the street so death would appear to have occurred outside his house.

**Source/history:** Paolo Giovio is an early influential source; later compilations repeated and dramatized it.

**Evidence:** No contemporary death record, witness, prosecution, or letter corroborates the event. The tale uses a standard magician’s-chamber taboo and concealment motif.

**Effect:** It presented learned magic as homicide and deception and helped assimilate Agrippa to Faust.

### Magical victories for emperors

**Story:** Agrippa secured Maximilian’s or Charles V’s victories through magic.

**Circulation:** Giovio and later writers associated his claimed imperial service and court title with supernatural warfare. André Thevet found the claim sufficiently widespread to refute in 1584.

**Evidence:** Agrippa may have served militarily and diplomatically, but there is no documentary evidence that imperial command credited magic with victory.

**Effect:** The story magnified both his power and his alleged diabolism and overlapped with boasts attributed to the historical Faustus.

### Demon-conjured images of the dead

**Story:** At court or in his study Agrippa made dead persons appear, sometimes specifically classical or royal figures, as moving shadows.

**Circulation:** Sixteenth-century literary allusions, including John Lyly’s 1584 reference to “Agrippa his shadows,” show the motif’s currency.

**Evidence:** No eyewitness documentation survives. *De occulta philosophia* discusses apparition theories, which made the attribution believable to readers.

**Effect:** It converted a textual theorist of images and spirits into a theatrical necromancer and influenced English Renaissance literature.

### Poverty, inn death, and abandonment by the Devil

**Story:** Agrippa died destitute in a disreputable Lyon inn, deserted by friends and finally by his demon dog.

**Source:** Giovio’s hostile, anecdotal portrait was influential.

**Evidence:** Financial difficulty is well documented, but Wier and French antiquarian testimony place death in Grenoble and report friends intervening for his release shortly beforehand. Extreme poverty is plausible but the inn scene is unverified.

**Effect:** The narrative supplied a providential ending: illicit knowledge leads to abandonment and disgrace.

### Banished or condemned to death by Charles V

**Story:** The emperor banished Agrippa from Germany or condemned him as a heretic.

**Evidence:** He lost Habsburg employment and left Bonn, but no surviving banishment decree or death sentence has been identified. The story likely rationalized his movements and amplified real conflicts.

### Secret societies and occult intelligence networks

Later occult histories sometimes turn Agrippa’s Paris friends into a formal secret brotherhood and his diplomatic travel into an intelligence career. His letters do show a confidential transnational network exchanging esoteric knowledge, and imperial diplomacy necessarily handled political information. A named, continuous occult order or professional spy service is not established.

### Faust identification

Agrippa was not the historical Georg/Doctor Faustus. Posthumous stories about each—imperial victories, demonic companions, magical travel, and a bad death—cross-pollinated. Agrippa became a “historical Faust” in reputation, but conflation is reception history, not biography.

### Rabelais’s “Her Trippa,” infernal money, and literary caricature

In the *Tiers livre* (1546), **François Rabelais** caricatured Agrippa as the diviner “Her Trippa,” encyclopedically able to read signs yet unable to see alleged infidelity in his own household. Later writers, notably **Martin Del Rio**, circulated stories in which Agrippa or Faustus paid innkeepers with coins that reverted to horn or worthless matter. These are satirical and demonological narratives without contemporary documentary support. **Christopher Marlowe’s Doctor Faustus** invokes Agrippa as an ideal of magical fame; the literary allusion records reputation, not personal association.

### Critical defenses

The mythology also produced early source criticism. **Johann Wier**, who had lived in Agrippa’s household, denied the demonic-dog and pseudo-Fourth-Book claims. **Gabriel Naudé’s** *Apologie pour tous les grands personnages qui ont esté faussement soupçonnez de magie* (1625) treated Agrippa among learned figures falsely accused of magic. These defenses did not erase the folklore but initiated its critical dismantling.

## Works by Agrippa

Dates below distinguish composition from first print where possible. Agrippa’s posthumous *Opera* (usually assigned to Basel, ca. 1580, despite a false Lyon/Bering imprint) collected genuine, disputed, and correspondence materials; its imprint and contents vary.

### Major genuine works

1. **_*De occulta philosophia libri tres*_** (*Three Books of Occult Philosophy*). First draft completed 1509–10 and dedicated to Trithemius, 8 April 1510; revised throughout life. Book I printed Antwerp/Paris, 1531; first complete edition Cologne, 1533. Genuine. The foundational synthesis of natural, celestial, and intellectual magic.
2. **_*De incertitudine et vanitate scientiarum et artium atque excellentia Verbi Dei, declamatio invectiva*_** (*On the Uncertainty and Vanity of the Sciences and Arts and the Excellence of the Word of God*). Composed chiefly 1526; first printed Antwerp, 1530; Cologne edition 1531. Genuine.
3. **_*De nobilitate et praecellentia foeminei sexus declamatio*_** (*Declamation on the Nobility and Preeminence of the Female Sex*). Composed Dôle, 1509; first printed in the Antwerp collection, 1529; independent Latin edition 1532; early translations French 1530, English 1542, German 1540, Italian 1544, Polish 1575, Dutch 1611. Genuine.

### Other genuine or generally accepted works

- **_*Expostulatio super expositione sua in librum De verbo mirifico cum Ioanne Catilineto*_** (c. 1509–10): defense against Catilinet.
- **_*De triplici ratione cognoscendi Deum*_** (*On Three Ways of Knowing God*, 1516): dedicated in Montferrat.
- **_*Dialogus de homine*_** (*Dialogue on Man*, c. 1516).
- **_*De originali peccato declamatio*_** (*Declamation on Original Sin*, Italian period).
- **_*De sacramento matrimonii declamatio*_** (*On the Sacrament of Marriage*), dedicated to Marguerite; 1520s.
- **_*Dehortatio gentilis theologiae*_** (*Warning Against Gentile Theology*), later work, showing reservations about pagan theology.
- **_*De beatissimae Annae monogamia ac unico puerperio*_**, with defense, propositions, and letters: composed from Metz controversy beginning 1519; printed 1534.
- **_*Commentaria in artem brevem Raimundi Lullii*_** / commentary or explanation of Llull’s short art; versions/titles in later collections.
- **_*Regimen adversus pestilentiam*_**: plague regimen, generally accepted and consistent with his medical work.
- **Orations and lectures**, including material on Plato’s *Symposium*, the Hermetic *Pimander*, and theological subjects; some survive only through collected editions or later editing.
- **_*Historia coronationis Caroli V*_** / account of Charles V’s coronation (1530), written in his historiographical office.
- **Funeral oration for Margaret of Austria** (1530).
- **_*Querela super calumnia ob Declamationem de vanitate scientiarum*_** (c. 1531): complaint against accusers.
- **_*Apologia adversus calumnias*_** (c. 1531–32): point-by-point defense of *De vanitate*.
- **Correspondence / *Epistolae***: hundreds of letters, chiefly 1507–34, printed posthumously in the *Opera*. Genuine core archive, although editorial arrangement and dating require critical checking.

### Disputed, lost, or falsely attributed works

- **_*Dialogus de vanitate scientiarum et ruina Christianae religionis*_** (1534): pseudonymously presented as by a Cistercian defender; Paola Zambelli made a plausible case for Agrippa’s authorship. **Disputed**, not certain.
- **_*De arte chemica*_**: printed or edited under Agrippa’s name and included in modern bibliographies; its transmission and authorship require qualification. **Disputed**.
- **_*Adversus lamiarum inquisitores*_** (*Against the Inquisitors of Witches*): quoted or named by Sisto da Siena and long treated as lost Agrippa. Recent reassessment finds authorship and perhaps even the work’s independent existence doubtful. **Lost/contested attribution**.
- **_*De occulta philosophia liber quartus*_** (*Fourth Book of Occult Philosophy*), first printed at Marburg in 1559, twenty-four years after Agrippa’s death. Wier explicitly rejected it as unlike his teacher’s work. Modern consensus treats it as **pseudo-Agrippan**, though a minority has argued otherwise.
- **_*Heptameron, seu Elementa magica*_**, often printed with the Fourth Book and attributed to “Peter of Abano”: **not by Agrippa**.
- Various magical manuals, grimoires, talisman books, and “secret” compilations marketed under Agrippa’s name in later centuries are **pseudepigrapha or derivative compilations**.

## Death and Final Status

Agrippa was alive at Bonn on 22 February 1534. He subsequently returned to France, was reportedly arrested at Lyon and released, and died at Grenoble in 1535, with 1536 a minority cautious possibility. The commonly given day **18 February 1535** should be treated as traditional rather than firmly proven in the evidence consulted. Cause of death is undocumented. He left at least three sons and an unstable financial estate.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Agrippa’s reputation split almost immediately:

- **Learned magus:** the most systematic Renaissance compiler of occult philosophy.
- **Heretic/sorcerer:** a polemical figure linked to demons, necromancy, and Faust.
- **Skeptic and critic:** an ancestor in histories of skepticism because of *De vanitate*.
- **Biblical humanist/reformer:** a critic of scholasticism and clerical abuses who sought wisdom in Scripture and ancient theology.
- **Defender of women:** a central figure in the Renaissance *querelle des femmes*, though his intentions remain debated.
- **Legal critic of witch prosecution:** influential through the Metz case and through Wier, but overstated when turned into wholesale disbelief in witchcraft.

The *Occult Philosophy* shaped or supplied material for **Giordano Bruno, John Dee, Robert Fludd, Giambattista della Porta, Reginald Scot**, Rosicrucian and Cabalistic currents, seventeenth-century magical compilations, nineteenth-century occult revivalists such as **Éliphas Lévi**, the **Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn**, and twentieth- and twenty-first-century ceremonial magic. Its planetary squares, scales of number, correspondences, angelic names, alphabets, and talismanic figures became standard reference material.

Literary reception was equally strong. English Renaissance writers invoked “Agrippa’s shadows.” **Mary Shelley’s *Frankenstein*** makes Cornelius Agrippa one of Victor Frankenstein’s youthful authors, alongside Paracelsus and Albertus Magnus—an emblem of obsolete but intoxicating knowledge. Later fantasy, horror, games, and occult publishing repeatedly convert the historical scholar into an all-purpose wizard.

Historiography moved from demonological anecdote to nineteenth-century rehabilitation, then to Nauert’s crisis-of-Renaissance-thought model, Yates’s Hermetic Renaissance, and more recent textual studies emphasizing genre, rhetoric, source criticism, religious reform, gender, and the unity—rather than simple contradiction—of *De vanitate* and *De occulta philosophia*.

## Research Limits and Unresolved Questions

- Exact birthplace: Cologne is better supported than the traditional Nettesheim village attribution, but not absolutely settled.
- Parents, siblings, wives’ names, and total children remain incompletely documented.
- Law and medical doctorates are plausible claims without surviving degree records.
- The 1508 Spain mission and Italian military record remain obscure.
- Exact office titles and salary figures vary by archive and biography; no comprehensive financial account survives.
- Authorship of the 1534 pseudonymous *Dialogus* remains plausible but disputed.
- *Adversus lamiarum inquisitores* is not securely recoverable and probably not Agrippa’s.
- Exact death day, cause, circumstances, and burial place are not established by a surviving contemporary certificate or register located in this research.
