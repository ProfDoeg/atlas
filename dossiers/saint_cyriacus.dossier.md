# Saint Cyriacus of Rome: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Method

Saint Cyriacus of Rome is an early Roman martyr whose cult is substantially better documented than his biography. The evidence separates into three layers:

1. **Early documentary evidence:** the *Depositio martyrum* preserved in the Chronography of 354 records Cyriacus and five companions, their commemoration on 8 August, and their burial near the seventh milestone of the Via Ostiensis. This is the principal evidence for a historical martyr and an organized fourth-century cult.
2. **Later institutional evidence:** early-medieval papal records, calendars, sacramentaries, churches, relic translations, and pilgrimage traditions document the development of his cult.
3. **Hagiographical narrative:** a late-antique or early-medieval passion cycle supplies nearly every biographical detail—noble ancestry, wealth, adult conversion, diaconal ordination, work at the Baths of Diocletian, miraculous exorcisms, a journey to Persia, torture, and execution on the Via Salaria. These stories are not independently corroborated and contain chronological, geographical, and prosopographical problems.

The labels used below are:

- **Documented fact:** supported by early calendrical, archaeological, institutional, or other relatively direct evidence.
- **Reported fact:** found in a later source and plausible, but not independently established.
- **Hagiographical tradition / legend:** part of a saint’s passion, breviary lesson, or devotional retelling without adequate contemporary corroboration.
- **Disputed claim:** contradicted by other evidence or regarded as historically unreliable by specialist scholarship.
- **Unverified relic claim:** a claim of bodily identity or translation that cannot now be authenticated.
- **Inference:** a reconstruction from the surviving evidence rather than an explicit statement in it.

## Historical Baseline

### The *Depositio martyrum* of 354

The earliest usable evidence is the Roman *Depositio martyrum*, incorporated into the Chronography of 354 but drawing on an earlier Roman calendar. Its 8 August entry gives, in substantially varying spellings:

> Cyriacus, Largus, Crescentianus, Memmia, Juliana, and Smaragdus, on the Ostian Way at the seventh milestone.

**Documented fact:** by the middle of the fourth century, Roman Christians commemorated a martyr named Cyriacus as one member of a six-person group associated with a burial place on or near the Via Ostiensis.

This entry does **not** say that:

- Cyriacus was wealthy or aristocratic;
- he came from Tuscany;
- he was a deacon;
- he worked on the Baths of Diocletian;
- he knew Popes Marcellinus or Marcellus;
- he healed Diocletian’s daughter;
- he traveled to Persia;
- he was executed on 16 March;
- he was tortured with pitch, ropes, clubs, or dragging;
- he was originally buried on the Via Salaria.

Those details enter through later traditions.

### Was he certainly a martyr?

The source is a *depositio martyrum*, a list of Roman martyrs and their burial commemorations. Cyriacus’s inclusion, the early date of the calendar, and the subsequent shrine tradition make his existence as a Roman martyr probable and conventionally accepted.

The record nevertheless does not preserve the circumstances or exact date of his death. “8 August” may be the anniversary of burial, cult observance, or deposition rather than execution.

### The burial formula and later confusion

The early entry associates the group with the Via Ostiensis and a seventh milestone. Later martyrologies and passions introduce the Via Salaria as the place of execution or first burial, followed by a translation across Rome to the Via Ostiensis.

One topographical explanation proposes that a rare word in the early tradition, possibly *ballistaria*, was corrupted or reinterpreted as *Salaria*. This could have generated the two-stage burial narrative. The hypothesis is plausible but not conclusively demonstrated.

## Family and Ancestry

### Historically recoverable information

No contemporary or near-contemporary source identifies Cyriacus’s:

- parents;
- grandparents or ancestry;
- siblings;
- spouse or partner;
- children;
- social class;
- inherited property;
- household;
- legal status;
- citizenship;
- ethnic or regional origin.

There is no secure evidence that he was married, celibate, enslaved, freed, aristocratic, senatorial, or of equestrian status.

### Noble Tuscan genealogy

A later text printed in the *Acta Sanctorum* says that Cyriacus came from Tuscany, was born to prominent parents, and originally bore the name **Sextus Sexius**. It identifies his father as **Quintus Sexius** and his mother as **Quincia**.

**Evidentiary label: late hagiographical genealogy.**

This genealogy is absent from the fourth-century record and from the historically secure evidence. The Bollandist dossier itself distinguishes the ancient cult from the intricacies and contradictions of the later martyrologies. No inscription, civic record, or independent literary source confirms Sextus Sexius, Quintus Sexius, or Quincia.

### Wealth and renunciation

A widespread later tradition describes Cyriacus as a wealthy Roman or noble convert who distributed his property to the poor.

**Evidentiary label: hagiographical tradition.**

The motif resembles a standard ascetic and hagiographical pattern. No property record or early testimony supports it. The tradition supplied a narrative explanation for his charitable activity and transition into ecclesiastical service.

---

## Childhood and Early Life

Nothing historically reliable is known about Cyriacus’s childhood, place of upbringing, languages, social formation, or initial religion.

The following are sometimes presented as parts of his youth but are not securely documented:

- birth in Tuscany;
- membership in a prominent Roman or Italian family;
- the personal name Sextus Sexius;
- pagan upbringing followed by adult conversion;
- possession and voluntary disposal of substantial wealth.

No exact or approximate birth year can be calculated from the existing evidence. Placing his birth in the third century is simply an inference from the traditional setting of his death.

---

## Education and Formation

No source of adequate historical reliability identifies Cyriacus’s education, teachers, theological formation, literacy, or ordination training.

Because later art depicts him reading an exorcistic formula from a book, he became iconographically associated with liturgical literacy. This is an artistic and cultic development, not proof of his education.

The passion cycle associates him with Roman clerics Marcellinus or Marcellus, depending on the version. That association is chronologically problematic:

- Pope Marcellinus held office from 296 to 304.
- Pope Marcellus I is generally placed in 308–309.
- The legends variously place Cyriacus’s activity and death before or after Diocletian’s abdication in 305.

The shifting papal identity appears to reflect textual development rather than a stable biographical memory.

## Religious Formation and Office

### Diaconate

Cyriacus is regularly called a deacon in later Roman liturgy and hagiography.

**Evidentiary label: ancient but not contemporary ecclesiastical tradition.**

The *Depositio martyrum* names him but does not specify an office. Later martyrologies and the passion call him *diaconus*. His diaconate is therefore possible, and it became fundamental to his cult, but cannot be independently proven from the earliest evidence.

### Ordination stories

In the passion tradition, Cyriacus and Sisinnius aid imprisoned Christians and are ordained deacons by Pope Marcellus—or, in other retellings, by Marcellinus.

**Evidentiary label: hagiographical tradition with contradictory chronology.**

### Religious profile in the legend

The legendary Cyriacus is presented as:

- a distributor of alms;
- a minister to prisoners and forced laborers;
- a healer of blindness;
- an exorcist;
- a baptizer of imperial and royal converts;
- a missionary to Persia;
- a confessor who rejects sacrifice;
- a martyr who prays during torture.

This profile shaped his later invocations against possession and temptation at death.

## Chronological Life History

## Before approximately 300

**Documented facts:** none.

**Hagiographical tradition:** Cyriacus was born into a prominent family, possibly in Tuscany, under the name Sextus Sexius. He converted to Christianity as an adult, gave his property to the poor, and attached himself to the Roman church.

The texts provide no stable dates for these events.

## The Baths of Diocletian

Construction of the Baths of Diocletian began under Maximian and was completed or dedicated in the early fourth century. The hagiographical cycle places Cyriacus in this setting.

According to the story:

1. Christians and other condemned laborers were forced to excavate, carry earth, or otherwise work on the imperial baths.
2. A wealthy Christian benefactor named **Thrason** supplied food and clothing.
3. Cyriacus, Sisinnius, Largus, and Smaragdus acted as Thrason’s agents.
4. They ministered particularly to an elderly prisoner, **Saturninus**, and helped carry his assigned load.
5. Their activity exposed them as Christians.
6. Cyriacus and Sisinnius were arrested or themselves sentenced to forced labor.

**Evidentiary label: hagiographical narrative.**

The construction project is historical, and Christians were subject to persecution and penal labor under the Tetrarchy. No independent source, however, links Cyriacus or these companions to the baths.

The story probably helped explain the existence of a Roman church called San Ciriaco alle Terme or *in Thermis*. It is also possible that the church’s association with the baths helped generate the story.

## Ministry in Prison

The passion describes Cyriacus healing blind people and other prisoners through prayer. He and his companions allegedly left the prison unnoticed to receive baptism or ecclesiastical ministry, then returned.

**Evidentiary label: hagiographical miracle and prison topos.**

No names of independently verifiable patients survive.

## Exorcism of Artemia

The best-known legend says that **Artemia** or **Arthemia**, a daughter of Emperor Diocletian, was possessed by a demon. The demon proclaimed that only Cyriacus could expel it. Cyriacus was brought from prison, exorcised her, instructed her, and baptized her. Some versions add that Diocletian’s wife **Serena** was already secretly Christian or encouraged Artemia’s conversion.

Diocletian then allegedly:

- released Cyriacus, Largus, and Smaragdus;
- gave Cyriacus a house in Rome;
- allowed the group to live or minister there without interference.

**Evidentiary label: legend.**

Problems include:

- no securely attested daughter of Diocletian named Artemia;
- no contemporary imperial, ecclesiastical, or historical record of the episode;
- no reliable evidence that Diocletian’s wife Prisca was also called Serena;
- a strong resemblance to recurring hagiographical stories in which a persecutor depends on the healing power of a Christian prisoner.

The legend nevertheless became the defining scene in Cyriacus’s iconography.

## Supposed Journey to Persia

The exorcised demon allegedly foretold that it would compel Cyriacus to travel to Babylon. Soon afterward, the Persian king **Sapor** asked Diocletian to send the Roman exorcist to heal his daughter **Jobia**, **Jovia**, or **Jobia**.

In Persia, Cyriacus supposedly:

1. confronted the demon possessing Jobia;
2. expelled it;
3. baptized the princess;
4. baptized the king, queen, and 430 members of the court;
5. declined rewards;
6. returned voluntarily to Rome.

**Evidentiary label: elaborate hagiographical legend.**

The Persian ruler is usually identified devotionally as a Sasanian king named Shapur, but the story does not fit securely into known Roman–Persian chronology. No Persian, Syriac, Armenian, Greek, or Roman historical source corroborates a royal conversion of this scale. The claimed conversion of a Sasanian king and hundreds of courtiers would be difficult to reconcile with the political and religious history of the period.

The geographical terms “Persia” and “Babylon” also vary in retellings, suggesting literary geography rather than an itinerary.

## Return to Rome

The legend says that Cyriacus returned to the Roman house given to him by Diocletian, installed a baptismal font, and continued converting people.

**Evidentiary label: legend.**

Later Roman churches and relic traditions competed to identify buildings associated with Cyriacus. None provides archaeological proof of his residence.

## Final Arrest

Several incompatible political settings occur:

- Maximian arrests him while Diocletian is absent.
- Maximian arrests him after Diocletian’s abdication in 305.
- Galerius, angered by Artemia’s conversion, orders his humiliation.
- Maxentius appears in versions connecting the story to Pope Marcellus.

The Orthodox retelling says Galerius had Cyriacus stripped, chained, bloodied, and dragged behind a chariot. Latin traditions usually make Maximian the principal persecutor.

**Evidentiary label: contradictory hagiographical tradition.**

Diocletian, Maximian, Galerius, and Maxentius had distinct jurisdictions and reigns. Their unstable use in the passion prevents a reliable date from being extracted.

## Torture and Execution

The later Roman martyrology and breviary tradition gives a detailed sequence:

- prolonged imprisonment;
- molten pitch poured over him;
- stretching on the rack;
- limbs pulled with ropes;
- beating with clubs;
- final beheading;
- execution with Largus, Smaragdus, and twenty others;
- place near the Via Salaria or Gardens of Sallust;
- date 16 March.

Other versions say Cyriacus and companions died under torture rather than specifically by beheading.

**Evidentiary label: traditional martyrdom account, not independently documented.**

The specific tortures are absent from the fourth-century entry. They resemble established martyr-act conventions and should not be treated as forensic information.

---

## Death and Burial

### Date of death

Competing dates include:

- **303:** common devotional chronology;
- **304:** aligned with the Diocletianic persecution and bath construction;
- **305:** approximate date in some modern church references;
- **16 March 306:** based on the later double commemoration and a post-abdication narrative;
- **309:** in traditions coordinating the story with Pope Marcellus.

**Finding:** the exact year and day of death are unknown.

### First burial

The passion says the priest **John** buried the martyrs on the Via Salaria.

**Evidentiary label: legend or unverified tradition.**

### Translation to the Via Ostiensis

Later martyrologies say Pope Marcellus translated the bodies on 8 August to the estate of the matron **Lucina**, at the seventh milestone of the Via Ostiensis.

**Evidentiary label: later translation tradition.**

The earlier calendar already places the group on the Via Ostiensis on 8 August. This may preserve the original burial commemoration rather than a fourth-century translation from the Via Salaria.

### Best reconstruction

The narrowest responsible reconstruction is:

> A Roman Christian named Cyriacus, probably killed during an otherwise unidentified persecution, was commemorated by the Roman church no later than the mid-fourth century. He belonged to a cult-group with Largus, Crescentianus, Memmia, Juliana, and Smaragdus and was associated with a burial site near the seventh milestone of the Via Ostiensis. The manner, date, and immediate circumstances of his death are unknown.

## Companions and Networks

### Early cult-group

**Documented fact:** the *Depositio martyrum* links Cyriacus with:

- Largus;
- Crescentianus;
- Memmia;
- Juliana;
- Smaragdus.

The document proves joint commemoration, not necessarily that all six were arrested, tried, or executed together.

### Later companions

The expanded passion and martyrologies add:

- Sisinnius;
- Saturninus;
- Thrason;
- Apronian;
- Papias;
- Maurus;
- Sergius;
- Secundus;
- Albanus;
- Victorianus;
- Faustinus;
- Felix;
- Sylvanus;
- Cyriacides;
- Donata;
- additional anonymous martyrs, bringing the total to twenty-three or twenty-four.

**Evidentiary label: later hagiographical aggregation.**

The similarity between this group and other groups commemorated under variants of Cyriacus/Quiriacus has led scholars and hagiographers to suspect duplication, contamination, or conflation.

### Pope Marcellinus

**Legendary relationship:** possible ordainer or ecclesiastical superior.

### Pope Marcellus I

**Legendary relationship:** ordainer, supporter, or translator of the martyrs’ bodies.

The *Passio Marcelli* intertwined the biographies of Marcellus and Cyriacus. Modern historical treatments regard much of the *Passio Marcelli* as legendary.

### Thrason

**Legendary relationship:** wealthy Christian patron who financed relief for prisoners and used Cyriacus and his companions as intermediaries.

### John the priest

**Legendary relationship:** buried the martyrs after execution.

### Lucina

**Legendary relationship:** Roman matron whose estate on the Via Ostiensis received the bodies.

“Lucina” recurs in Roman martyr legends as the pious matron who retrieves or houses remains; whether one historical woman underlies any particular occurrence is unresolved.

### Diocletian, Artemia, and Serena

**Legendary relationships:** persecuting emperor who temporarily becomes Cyriacus’s patron; daughter exorcised and baptized; Christian imperial woman who assists the conversion.

### “Sapor” and Jobia

**Legendary relationships:** Persian king and daughter converted through Cyriacus’s exorcism.

## Employment, Institutions, Property, and Finances

No historically reliable evidence survives for Cyriacus’s employment, income, inherited wealth, investments, debts, business activity, or property.

### Noble wealth

The claim that he possessed a fortune and distributed it to the poor is hagiographical.

### Thrason’s charitable network

The passion describes a financed relief operation:

- Thrason supplies resources;
- Cyriacus and companions distribute food and clothing;
- prisoners and forced laborers receive assistance.

This may preserve a generalized memory of Christian charitable organization during persecution, but its named financial structure cannot be independently verified.

### House given by Diocletian

The emperor allegedly rewarded Cyriacus with a Roman house in which he established a baptistery.

**Evidentiary label: legend.**

Later churches or titular institutions may have influenced the formation of this story.

### Forced labor

Cyriacus is sometimes called patron of forced laborers and persons engaged in heavy work because the passion places him among condemned bath workers.

**Evidentiary label:** the patronage is documented as a later cultic development; his own forced labor is legendary.

---

## Political Activity

There is no evidence that Cyriacus:

- held Roman civil or military office;
- participated in a political faction;
- organized rebellion;
- advocated a political program;
- had an intelligence role;
- possessed court office;
- negotiated formal Roman–Persian relations.

The legends place him in direct contact with emperors and a Persian king, but his actions are religious—healing, exorcism, conversion, refusal of idolatry—not conventional political activity.

The passion does function symbolically as a reversal of imperial power: rulers who can imprison Christians cannot heal their own households and must appeal to a captive deacon. That is a literary and theological structure rather than evidence of an actual court relationship.

---

## Religious and Intellectual Development

No writings or securely transmitted teachings survive. The passion attributes conventional Christian professions of faith and prayers to Cyriacus, but they were composed by the hagiographer.

The legend presents a progression:

1. conversion from wealth or pagan society;
2. renunciation and charity;
3. formal diaconal ministry;
4. prison healing;
5. exorcistic authority;
6. missionary baptism;
7. rejection of imperial pressure;
8. martyrdom.

His later cult increasingly emphasized exorcism. By the late Middle Ages he was one of the Fourteen Holy Helpers and was particularly invoked against:

- possession;
- evil spirits;
- temptation at death;
- mental or spiritual affliction;
- blindness and eye disease;
- exhausting work.

Regional associations with winegrowing, frost, storms, and weather developed in German-speaking areas.

## Cult, Churches, and Relics

## Fourth century

The *Depositio martyrum* establishes an 8 August commemoration and Via Ostiensis burial association by 354.

## Sixth and seventh centuries

The cult entered Roman sacramentary and calendrical traditions. Gregory the Great’s sacramentary tradition commemorated Cyriacus on 8 August.

The *Liber Pontificalis* reports that **Pope Honorius I**—625–638—built a church to Cyriacus “from the foundations” at the seventh milestone of the Via Ostiensis and made an offering there.

**Evidentiary label: reported institutional fact from the papal biography.**

This confirms the importance of the site but does not prove every claim made by the passion.

## Eighth and ninth centuries

The *Liber Pontificalis* records gifts by later popes, including Leo III and Benedict III, to the church associated with Cyriacus.

A translation to **Santa Prassede** is attributed to Pope Paschal I, whose pontificate was 817–824.

**Evidentiary label: medieval relic-translation tradition.**

Translations undertaken during this period often removed relics from suburban cemeteries to churches within Rome. The historical practice is well attested, but identifying individual surviving bones is generally impossible.

## Santa Maria in Via Lata

Later Roman martyrology states that the remains were brought into the city and deposited in the deaconry of Santa Maria in Via Lata.

**Evidentiary label: medieval ecclesiastical and relic tradition.**

The church became associated with the former Cyriacan station and with relics said to belong to Cyriacus, Largus, and Smaragdus.

## San Ciriaco alle Terme

A Roman titular church called San Ciriaco alle Terme di Diocleziano existed by late antiquity or the early Middle Ages. A priest named Marcianus is associated with the title in 494.

The title’s existence is documented; whether it originated in a house owned by the martyr is not.

It was suppressed, restored, and reassigned in stages:

- suppressed by Sixtus IV in 1477;
- restored under Alexander VI in 1493;
- definitively suppressed or transferred by Sixtus V in 1587.

The church’s association with the Baths reinforced the story that Cyriacus served the workers who constructed them.

## Neuhausen near Worms

Bishop **Samuel of Worms**, who governed in the ninth century, reportedly obtained relics of Cyriacus from Rome and brought them to Neuhausen. A church formerly dedicated to Dionysius took Cyriacus as patron, and a collegiate foundation was established around 847.

Neuhausen became a pilgrimage center. Its shrine received royal and episcopal gifts.

In 1460, during conflict involving Frederick of the Palatinate and the archbishop of Mainz, soldiers attacked the foundation:

- the church and buildings burned;
- the lead-covered reliquary was broken open;
- relics were thrown about;
- lead was stolen;
- the remains were later said to have been recovered and taken to Worms Cathedral.

**Evidentiary label: documented or reported institutional history; identity of the relics remains unverified.**

The foundation was rebuilt but never fully recovered. In 1565 it was dissolved under Palatine rule and converted into a Reformed educational institution.

## Altorf in Alsace

A separate tradition says Pope Leo IX gave an arm of Cyriacus to Altorf Abbey in 1049. The abbey consequently bore Cyriacus’s name, and an arm relic continued to be venerated there.

**Evidentiary label: medieval relic tradition; anatomical identity unverified.**

## Ancona confusion

Relics in Ancona Cathedral are associated primarily with **Cyriacus/Judas Cyriacus of Jerusalem**, patron of Ancona, not securely with Cyriacus of Rome. Popular summaries sometimes conflate the two.

## Honors, Offices, and Liturgical Status

Cyriacus was never canonized through the later centralized papal process. His recognition is “pre-Congregation”: it rests on ancient cult and inclusion in liturgical books.

### Roman liturgical development

- Ancient and medieval calendars: 8 August.
- Later martyrologies: 16 March as martyrdom; 8 August as translation or principal feast.
- Tridentine calendar: feast of Cyriacus, Largus, and Smaragdus, traditionally of semidouble rank.
- 1955 reform: reduced to simple rank.
- 1962 calendar: reduced to a commemoration.
- 1969/1970 General Roman Calendar: removed from universal celebration.
- Modern *Martyrologium Romanum*: retains the martyrs on 8 August, naming Cyriacus, Largus, Crescentianus, Memmia, Juliana, and Smaragdus at the seventh milestone of the Via Ostiensis.

Removal from the universal calendar did not constitute a finding that Cyriacus was fictitious or a revocation of sainthood. It separated universal liturgical observance from continued martyrological recognition and local cult.

### Eastern tradition

Eastern Orthodox sources commemorate an expanded group on 7 June, including Sisinnius, Cyriacus, Smaragdus, Largus, and others. These accounts reproduce much of the Latin passion in adapted form and are not independent contemporary confirmation.

## Illnesses, Injuries, and Cause of Death

No contemporary medical or forensic evidence exists.

### Claimed injuries

Later sources describe:

- imprisonment and deprivation;
- dragging behind a chariot;
- beating;
- stretching on a rack;
- dislocation or extension of limbs with ropes;
- burning with molten pitch;
- clubbing;
- decapitation.

**Evidentiary label: hagiographical torture catalogue.**

### Cause of death

The traditional cause is execution by beheading. Some Eastern retellings describe death under torture.

**Finding:** martyrdom by state violence is probable from the cult category, but the execution method is not historically demonstrable.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Adversarial Findings

A dedicated adversarial search in English, Italian, German, and related terms was conducted for controversy, scandal, accusations, fraud, lawsuits, conspiracy, criticism, forgery, and false relics, including recent material.

No credible evidence was found that the historical Cyriacus was involved in:

- fraud;
- corruption;
- financial crime;
- sexual misconduct;
- political violence;
- litigation;
- criminal prosecution unrelated to Christian martyrdom;
- espionage;
- conspiracy;
- abuse of office.

Because the subject belongs to late antiquity and the recoverable biography is minimal, the meaningful controversies concern historical authenticity, textual construction, identity confusion, and relic claims.

### Historical value of the passion

Nineteenth-century hagiographer Sabine Baring-Gould called the acts “purely fabulous” and “undeserving of attention” as historical evidence. Modern Catholic summaries likewise describe them as historically worthless while accepting the ancient cult-entry as evidence of a real martyr.

**Status: documented scholarly criticism.**

The judgment applies to the narrative acts, not necessarily to Cyriacus’s existence.

### Chronological contradictions

The passion uses Popes Marcellinus and Marcellus, and Emperors Diocletian, Maximian, Galerius, and Maxentius, in combinations that do not form a stable chronology.

**Status: documented textual problem.**

### Artemia’s identity

There is no adequate independent evidence that Diocletian had a daughter named Artemia or that she became Christian through Cyriacus.

**Status: unverified hagiographical claim.**

### “Serena,” wife of Diocletian

The historical wife of Diocletian is generally known as Prisca. The Serena of the legend may be a conflation, a literary character, or another person.

**Status: unresolved identification; not established fact.**

### Persian mission

No independent record supports Cyriacus’s journey or the conversion of King Sapor, his daughter, and 430 courtiers.

**Status: legend contradicted by the absence of expected historical consequences and by unstable chronology.**

### Duplicate martyr groups

Variant martyrologies contain similar groups under Cyriacus, Quiriacus, or related spellings, sometimes at Rome and sometimes elsewhere. Names such as Largus, Crescentianus, Memmia/Nimmia, Juliana, and Smaragdus recur.

**Status: genuine textual and prosopographical problem.**

Possibilities include:

- duplicate commemorations of one group;
- copying errors;
- relocation through manuscript corruption;
- fusion of originally distinct groups;
- expansion of a six-person group to twenty-three or twenty-four.

### Via Salaria versus Via Ostiensis

The early record favors the Via Ostiensis. The Via Salaria story appears in later acts and may result from textual corruption or an invented first burial needed to explain a translation.

**Status: disputed topographical claim.**

### Relic multiplication

Rome, Worms/Neuhausen, Altorf, and other European institutions claimed relics. Medieval dispersal is possible, but no modern scientific chain of custody establishes that any particular relic belonged to the fourth-century martyr.

**Status: unverified relic claims, not proven fraud.**

The violent dispersal of the Neuhausen shrine in 1460 further weakened provenance.

### Confusion with other Cyriaci

Cyriacus of Rome has been confused with:

- Cyricus, the child martyr;
- Judas Cyriacus of Jerusalem;
- a legendary Pope Cyriacus associated with Ursula;
- Cyriacus of Ancona;
- Cyriacus the hermit;
- other martyrs in Rome.

Search results alleging papal conspiracies or connections to the Finding of the True Cross usually concern the supposed Pope Cyriacus or Judas Cyriacus, not the Roman deacon.

**Status: documented identity conflation.**

### Recent controversy search

Recent search results from 2024–2026 primarily concerned:

- continued online repetition of the passion;
- relic and iconographic discussions;
- confusion with Judas Cyriacus;
- debates over whether other saints named Cyriacus were historical.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

## Sextus Sexius of Tuscany

**Story:** Cyriacus was born Sextus Sexius in Tuscany to Quintus Sexius and Quincia.

**Appearance and circulation:** printed in the Bollandist dossier from a later genealogical or hagiographical source.

**Evidence for:** the late text itself.

**Evidence against:** absence from the earliest calendar and all contemporary evidence.

**Effect:** supplied Cyriacus with aristocratic Roman identity and a complete pre-conversion genealogy.

## The charitable nobleman

**Story:** a wealthy Roman converted, gave everything to the poor, and served persecuted Christians.

**Evidence:** later devotional biographies.

**Assessment:** standard renunciation motif; possible in general but unverified in this case.

**Effect:** made Cyriacus a model of almsgiving and voluntary poverty.

## Labor at the Baths

**Story:** Cyriacus secretly aided and then joined Christian forced laborers constructing the Baths of Diocletian.

**Evidence:** passion literature and later breviaries.

**Evidence against:** no contemporary record; the story may be etiological, explaining a church called San Ciriaco alle Terme.

**Effect:** patronage of forced laborers, heavy workers, and those under physical strain.

## Artemia’s exorcism

**Story:** the possessed imperial daughter named Cyriacus as the only person able to free her; he exorcised and baptized her.

**Evidence:** passion and dependent retellings.

**Evidence against:** no securely known Artemia in Diocletian’s family and no contemporary notice.

**Effect:** established Cyriacus as an exorcist and produced his principal artistic attribute: a bound or kneeling female figure and a subdued demon.

## Conversion of the Persian court

**Story:** Cyriacus traveled to Persia, healed Jobia, and baptized Sapor and 430 courtiers.

**Evidence:** hagiographical narrative.

**Evidence against:** no Persian or Roman corroboration; chronological and political implausibility.

**Effect:** transformed a local Roman martyr into an international missionary capable of subordinating both Roman and Persian rulers to Christian sacramental power.

## Chariot dragging and torture

**Story:** Galerius or Maximian had Cyriacus dragged behind a chariot, covered with pitch, racked, clubbed, and decapitated.

**Evidence:** expanded passion and liturgical readings.

**Assessment:** traditional martyr narrative; details unverifiable and partly inconsistent between versions.

**Effect:** supplied visual martyrdom scenes and emphasized endurance under state coercion.

## Pope Marcellus and the stable

Cyriacus’s passion became entangled with the *Passio Marcelli*, in which Maxentius allegedly turned a church associated with Marcellus into a stable and forced the pope into degrading labor.

**Assessment:** modern historical treatments regard this episode as legendary. Its incorporation shows how Roman martyr narratives were combined into larger topographical cycles.

## The 1541 vision at the Baths

A priest from Palermo, **Antonio del Duca**, reportedly experienced a vision at the Baths of Diocletian in 1541 involving seven martyrs in white, identified in later accounts as Cyriacus, Largus, Smaragdus, Saturninus, Sisinnius, Thrason, and Marcellus.

**Evidentiary label: reported visionary claim.**

The vision contributed to the religious reinterpretation of the Baths and the eventual construction of Santa Maria degli Angeli e dei Martiri. It is evidence for sixteenth-century devotion, not for fourth-century events.

## The Fourteen Holy Helpers

From the later Middle Ages, especially in German-speaking territories, Cyriacus belonged to the Fourteen Holy Helpers. Their joint intercession was sought against disease, danger, possession, and death.

Cyriacus’s specialty was commonly:

- demonic possession;
- evil spirits;
- temptation at death;
- sometimes eye disease.

**Evidentiary label: documented medieval cultic development.**

The group did not represent historical companions. Its members came from different centuries and regions and were assembled posthumously through devotional practice.

## Weather and wine

German regional traditions invoked Cyriacus against frost and destructive weather and treated him as a patron of winegrowers.

**Evidentiary label: regional folklore and devotional patronage.**

## The San Ciriaco hurricane

The destructive hurricane that crossed Puerto Rico on **8 August 1899** became known as the **San Ciriaco hurricane** because it struck on his feast day, following an older Caribbean practice of naming storms by the saint’s day.

Official and historical accounts give approximately 3,000–3,433 deaths in Puerto Rico, extensive crop destruction, and severe damage to the coffee economy.

The storm was not originally a miracle story or accusation against the saint. Its naming demonstrates the penetration of Cyriacus’s feast into Puerto Rican temporal and disaster memory.

## Iconography and Artistic Reception

Cyriacus is normally represented as a youthful or middle-aged deacon wearing the dalmatic and stole. Attributes include:

- book of exorcisms;
- raised hand of blessing or command;
- chains;
- chained or fleeing demon;
- Artemia kneeling, collapsed, or held by his stole;
- martyr’s palm;
- sword, axe, club, rack, or other generalized instrument of martyrdom.

### Matthias Grünewald’s *Saint Cyriacus*

The Städel Museum holds a panel by Mathis Gothart Nithart, called Matthias Grünewald, showing Cyriacus exorcising Artemia. He reads from a book and winds his stole around her neck. The panel turns the late legend into a detailed ritual scene and is among the most important surviving individual representations of the saint.

### Fourteen Holy Helpers imagery

Cyriacus appears in:

- late-medieval German altarpieces;
- manuscript litanies;
- sculptures by or associated with workshops such as Tilman Riemenschneider’s milieu;
- a 1529 predella catalogued by the Cranach Digital Archive;
- nineteenth-century neo-medieval relief programs.

His deacon’s vestments distinguish him from the bishops, knights, virgins, monk, physician, and child-bearing Christopher in the group.

### Genuine works

No genuine writings, letters, sermons, diaries, inscriptions, theological treatises, legal documents, or dictated memoirs by Cyriacus are known.

### Words placed in his mouth

Passion texts give him:

- exorcistic commands;
- prayers during torture;
- instructions before baptism;
- refusals of pagan sacrifice.

**Attribution status:** literary speeches composed by hagiographers, not authenticated transcripts.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Cyriacus’s reputation developed in several distinct phases.

### Local Roman martyr

By 354 he was one member of a six-person Roman cult-group commemorated at a suburban burial site.

### Deacon and miracle worker

Later passion literature made him a deacon, charitable organizer, prison healer, imperial exorcist, and Persian missionary.

### Roman topographical saint

Churches at the Via Ostiensis, near the Baths of Diocletian, and in the Via Lata tradition attached his memory to multiple areas of Rome. These associations encouraged increasingly elaborate geographical narratives.

### Relic saint of the Carolingian and post-Carolingian world

Translations or purported translations carried his cult to Worms-Neuhausen, Altorf, and other European churches. Relics supported pilgrimage, endowment, and institutional identity.

### Holy Helper

His incorporation into the Fourteen Holy Helpers gave him a durable place in central European popular religion. His specific association with exorcism arose directly from the Artemia story.

### Liturgical contraction

Twentieth-century Roman calendar reform reduced and then removed his feast from universal observance, while the Roman Martyrology retained the ancient six-person group. This produced a modern distinction between:

- historical-liturgical recognition of an ancient martyr cult;
- skepticism toward the detailed passion;
- reduced universal prominence;
- continued local and traditional devotion.

### Cultural afterlife in Puerto Rico

The naming of the 1899 San Ciriaco hurricane preserved the saint’s feast in secular historical vocabulary. For many Puerto Ricans, “San Ciriaco” became as much the name of a catastrophic storm and economic rupture as the name of a late-antique martyr.
