## Works by William James

### Books and author-shaped collections

- **1890:** *The Principles of Psychology*. A two-volume scientific and philosophical treatise, twelve years in preparation; it is James’s fullest systematic obra in psychology.
- **1892:** *Psychology: Briefer Course*. A substantially rewritten abridgment of *The Principles*, not merely a shortened edition: James estimated that about two-fifths was new or recast.
- **1897:** *The Will to Believe and Other Essays in Popular Philosophy*. An author-arranged collection of ten previously delivered or published essays, framed by a new preface as an expression of James’s radical empiricism and pluralism.
- **1899:** *Talks to Teachers on Psychology: and to Students on Some of Life’s Ideals*. A composite book made from public lectures to teachers and addresses at women’s colleges; it crosses psychological protocol, pedagogical counsel, and moral exhortation.
- **1899:** *Human Immortality: Two Supposed Objections to the Doctrine*. The published manifestation of James’s 1897 Ingersoll Lecture, enlarged from its first periodical printing.
- **1902:** *The Varieties of Religious Experience: A Study in Human Nature*. Twenty Gifford Lectures, plus postscript, reshaped into a book; James intended it as the psychological portion of a larger treatment of religion that he never completed.
- **1907:** *Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of Thinking*. Eight lectures delivered in Boston and New York and revised into a book; a public philosophical intervention closer to a manifesto than a neutral survey.
- **1909:** *A Pluralistic Universe*. Eight Hibbert Lectures with three appendices, combining new lectures with revised earlier essays into James’s sustained attack on monistic idealism.
- **1909:** *The Meaning of Truth: A Sequel to “Pragmatism”*. An author-made defensive collection of essays, replies, and controversies concerning the pragmatic theory of truth.
- **1911:** *Some Problems of Philosophy: A Beginning of an Introduction to Philosophy*. An unfinished project left in manuscript at James’s death and edited posthumously by Henry James Jr.; its incompleteness is part of the obra rather than an accidental defect.
- **1912:** *Essays in Radical Empiricism*. A posthumous collection edited by Ralph Barton Perry. James had substantially selected its title and contents, but Perry supplied the final editorial manifestation.
- **1912:** *On Some of Life’s Ideals*. A publisher’s posthumous extraction of “On a Certain Blindness in Human Beings” and “What Makes a Life Significant”; it is an edition-derived object rather than a new Jamesian work.
- **1915:** *Habit*. A posthumous excerpt from *The Principles of Psychology*, issued with addresses by other writers; it resists classification as an independent James work.
- **1917:** *Selected Papers on Philosophy*. A posthumous selection edited by Charles M. Bakewell; it is a reception artifact, not an author-shaped obra.
- **1920:** *The Letters of William James*. A posthumous two-volume documentary construction edited by Henry James Jr.; James made the individual letters, but not this book.
- **1920:** *Collected Essays and Reviews*. Ralph Barton Perry’s posthumous gathering of thirty-nine texts; indispensable as preservation, but not a collection James himself designed.

### Psychological and psychophysical investigations

- **1878:** *Remarks on Spencer’s Definition of Mind as Correspondence*. A theoretical psychology article making interest and preference central to mind.
- **1878:** *Brute and Human Intellect*. A comparative-psychology article combining experiment, evolutionary theory, and philosophical interpretation.
- **1879:** *Are We Automata?* A polemical scientific essay defending consciousness as causally efficacious and selective.
- **1879:** *The Spatial Quale*. An early statement of James’s nativist account of spatial perception.
- **1880:** *The Feeling of Effort*. An experimental and theoretical investigation of muscular feeling, volition, and the sense of effort.
- **1881:** *Notes on the Sense of Dizziness in Deaf-Mutes*. A preliminary experimental report.
- **1882:** *The Feeling of Effort*. A revised French manifestation of James’s research, published as *Le sentiment de l’effort*.
- **1882:** *The Sense of Dizziness in Deaf-Mutes*. A fuller report of the investigation announced in 1881.
- **1884:** *What Is an Emotion?* A theoretical article formulating what became known, with Carl Lange’s independent work, as the James–Lange theory.
- **1885:** *Experiments in Memory*. A review-essay on Hermann Ebbinghaus’s experimental research.
- **1886:** *Report of the Committee on Hypnotism*. A collaborative research report by James and George M. Carnochan; its joint authorship should not be collapsed into a sole-authored James work.
- **1886:** *Report of the Committee on Mediumistic Phenomena*. A committee report containing James’s first published account of Leonora Piper.
- **1886:** *The Perception of Time*. A psychological article later incorporated, almost verbatim, into *The Principles of Psychology*.
- **1887:** *The Perception of Space*. A two-part psychological study later absorbed into *The Principles*.
- **1887:** *The Consciousness of Lost Limbs*. A clinical and psychological study of phantom-limb experience.
- **1889:** *The Psychological Theory of Extension*. A short defense of James’s nativist theory of spatial perception.
- **1889:** *Note to the Second Report on Experimental Psychology*. A methodological comment on alleged thought-transference experiments.
- **1889:** *Notes on Automatic Writing*. A collection and analysis of cases, portions of which entered *The Principles*.
- **1889:** *The Psychology of Belief*. An article subsequently expanded into the chapter “The Perception of Reality” in *The Principles*.
- **1889:** *Report on the Congress of Physiological Psychology at Paris*. A report on the August 1889 international congress.
- **1890:** *A Record of Observations of Certain Phenomena of Trance*. A psychical-research report centered on Leonora Piper.
- **1890:** *Psychology at Harvard University*. An institutional account of psychological teaching and laboratory work.
- **1892:** *A Plea for Psychology as a “Natural Science.”* A disciplinary manifesto arguing for psychology’s empirical status while denying that it had achieved finished scientific unity.
- **1893:** *The Original Datum of Space-Consciousness*. A reply in the controversy over spatial perception.
- **1893:** *Mr. Bradley on Immediate Resemblance*. A philosophical-psychological criticism.
- **1893:** *Immediate Resemblance*. A continuation of James’s exchange with F. H. Bradley.
- **1894:** *Professor Wundt and Feelings of Innervation*. A priority dispute and correction concerning sensations of movement.
- **1894:** *The Physical Basis of Emotion*. A defense and refinement of James’s theory of emotion.
- **1894:** *The Hidden Self*. An essay on dissociation and subconscious mental life.
- **1895:** *The Knowing of Things Together*. An epistemological and psychological article on how one consciousness apprehends several objects.
- **1895:** *Degeneration and Genius*. A response to psychiatric theories pathologizing exceptional intellectual production.
- **1896:** *Psychical Research*. A short statement on the evidential and scientific status of psychical investigation.
- **1897:** *A Case of Demoniacal Possession*. James’s presentation of a case record; the underlying testimony is not wholly his creation.
- **1897:** *Professor James on Demoniac Control*. A reply concerning the same disputed case.
- **1898:** *Consciousness under Nitrous Oxide*. James communicated another person’s narrative; authorship of the reported experience and authorship of its publication are therefore divided.
- **1898:** *The Confidences of a “Psychical Researcher.”* An account of the methodological and personal difficulties of psychical research.
- **1898:** *Hodgson’s Observations of Trance Phenomena*. An extensive review-essay on Richard Hodgson’s Piper investigations.
- **1899:** *Lehmann and Hansen on Telepathy*. A letter continuing a methodological dispute about unconscious whispering.
- **1899:** *Telepathy Once More*. A further letter in the same controversy.
- **1899:** *Controversy with Professor Titchener on Messrs. Lehmann and Hansen’s Experiments in Unconscious Whispering*. A composite printed controversy incorporating several letters and editorial matter; it is not wholly a text made by James.
- **1901:** *Frederic Myers’s Services to Psychology*. A memorial assessment of Myers as an empirical investigator of abnormal mental phenomena.
- **1907:** *The Energies of Men*. A presidential address proposing that ordinary performance uses only part of human energetic capacity.
- **1907:** *Report on the Titus Case*. A psychical-research report assessing a claim of clairvoyant discovery.
- **1909:** *Report on Mrs. Piper’s Hodgson-Control*. A long report of sittings with Leonora Piper, offered as evidence but expressly not as an uncomplicated demonstration.
- **1909:** *Final Impressions of a Psychical Researcher*. A retrospective statement whose form lies between scientific report, intellectual testament, and personal confession.
- **1910:** *A Suggestion about Mysticism*. A brief psychological-philosophical hypothesis concerning mystical experience.

### Philosophical essays, lectures, and interventions

- **1878:** *Quelques considérations sur la méthode subjective*. A French-language communication outlining James’s practical account of belief.
- **1879:** *The Sentiment of Rationality*. The first, theoretical part of a projected larger work on the motives of philosophizing; that encompassing project was never completed as planned.
- **1881:** *Reflex Action and Theism*. A lecture arguing from the teleological character of mind; James identified it as another fragment of the unfinished “Sentiment of Rationality” project.
- **1882:** *On Some Hegelisms*. A polemical philosophical essay directed against characteristic Hegelian arguments.
- **1882:** *The Sentiment of Rationality*. The practical counterpart to the 1879 essay, later combined with it in revised form.
- **1884:** *The Dilemma of Determinism*. A lecture and ethical-philosophical manifesto for indeterminism and moral possibility.
- **1884:** *Absolutism and Empiricism*. An epistemological essay later incorporated into *Essays in Radical Empiricism*.
- **1885:** *The Function of Cognition*. A major epistemological article distinguishing acquaintance, knowledge-about, reference, truth, and error.
- **1891:** *The Moral Philosopher and the Moral Life*. An ethical address first delivered under the title “The Essentials of an Ethical Universe”; the title and occasion changed before publication.
- **1896:** *The Will to Believe*. A lecture that functions as a manifesto for permissible commitment under genuine, intellectually undecidable options.
- **1898:** *Philosophical Conceptions and Practical Results*. A Berkeley lecture introducing pragmatism through Charles S. Peirce’s principle; later publication under “The Pragmatic Method” was a revised manifestation.
- **1904:** *Does “Consciousness” Exist?* A foundational radical-empiricist essay denying consciousness as a separate substance or entity.
- **1904:** *A World of Pure Experience*. A two-part exposition of radical empiricism.
- **1904:** *Humanism and Truth*. A defense of F. C. S. Schiller and the humanist account of truth.
- **1904:** *The Pragmatic Method*. A revised and shortened manifestation of the 1898 Berkeley lecture.
- **1905:** *The Experience of Activity*. An American Psychological Association presidential address applying pragmatism and radical empiricism to experienced agency.
- **1905:** *The Thing and Its Relations*. An argument that relations are experienced and need not be treated as contradictions.
- **1905:** *The Essence of Humanism*. A concise formulation of pragmatist and radical-empiricist commitments.
- **1905:** *How Two Minds Can Know One Thing*. A specific application of radical empiricism to shared objects.
- **1905:** *Humanism and Truth Once More*. A reply to H. W. B. Joseph.
- **1905:** *Is Radical Empiricism Solipsistic?* A reply to the charge that radical empiricism traps each knower inside private experience.
- **1905:** *The Place of Affectional Facts in a World of Pure Experience*. An account of feelings and values as constituents of experience.
- **1905:** *Humanism and Truth*. A shortened republication assembled from “Humanism and Truth Once More”; it is a revised manifestation, not wholly a new argument.
- **1905:** *La notion de conscience*. A French-language exposition prepared for the International Congress of Psychology.
- **1906:** *G. Papini and the Pragmatist Movement in Italy*. A report and endorsement of Italian pragmatism.
- **1906:** *The Mad Absolute*. A short polemical reply concerning pluralism and world-consciousness.
- **1906:** *Mr. Pitkin’s Refutation of “Radical Empiricism.”* A reply clarifying James’s methodological restriction of reality to experience.
- **1907:** *The Present Dilemma in Philosophy*. A lecture contrasting “tender-minded” rationalism and “tough-minded” empiricism.
- **1907:** *What Pragmatism Means*. A lecture defining the pragmatic method.
- **1907:** *Pragmatism’s Conception of Truth*. A lecture and article on truth as verified or verifiable relation.
- **1907:** *The Absolute and the Strenuous Life*. An argument that pluralism better sustains moral exertion than absolutism.
- **1907:** *The Pragmatist Account of Truth and Its Misunderstanders*. A systematic response to objections.
- **1907:** *Humanism and Truth Once More*. A further manifestation in James’s continuing controversy over truth.
- **1908:** *The Pragmatist Account of Truth and Its Misunderstanders*. A revised philosophical article later collected in *The Meaning of Truth*.
- **1908:** *The Mutability of Truth*. A reply explaining how truths can change without truth becoming arbitrary.
- **1908:** *The Validity of Humanism*. A defense of the humanist formulation of pragmatism.
- **1908:** *A Word More about Truth*. A further controversy-piece.
- **1908:** *The Conclusions of a Pluralistic Philosophy*. A lecture anticipating the conclusion of *A Pluralistic Universe*.
- **1908:** *Hegel and His Method*. A Hibbert Lecture later incorporated into *A Pluralistic Universe*.
- **1909:** *Concerning Fechner*. A Hibbert Lecture on Gustav Fechner’s pluralistic metaphysics.
- **1909:** *The Confusion of Pragmatism*. A reply to critics of the movement’s terminology and claims.
- **1909:** *The Philosophy of Bergson*. A lecture-essay later substantially recast as “Bergson and His Critique of Intellectualism.”
- **1909:** *On a Very Prevalent Abuse of Abstraction*. A critique of “vicious intellectualism,” retitled in book form as “Abstractionism and ‘Relativismus.’”
- **1909:** *Bradley or Bergson?* A comparison of intellectualist and experiential philosophical methods.
- **1910:** *A Pluralistic Mystic*. An interpretation of Benjamin Paul Blood’s work and a late statement of James’s pluralism.
- **1911:** *Philosophy and Its Critics*. A posthumously printed address; publication followed James’s death, so its final textual state was not entirely controlled by him.
- **1911:** *Letter on Determinism*. A letter written in 1907 and printed posthumously as an appendix to Alfred C. Lane’s essay.

### Pedagogical protocols and addresses on conduct

- **1873:** *Vacations*. An unsigned periodical comment advocating leisure against American over-strenuousness.
- **1892–1899:** *Talks to Teachers on Psychology*. A lecture project developed from Cambridge lectures in 1892, serialized in 1899, and then revised into the book of that year.
- **1898:** *On a Certain Blindness in Human Beings*. A moral address about failures to perceive the significance of lives unlike one’s own.
- **1898:** *What Makes a Life Significant*. An address joining individual aspiration to social fellowship.
- **1899:** *The Gospel of Relaxation*. Practical counsel applying James’s account of emotion and habit to bodily and mental conduct.
- **1906:** *Comments on Teaching and Research*. An occasional academic statement rather than a finished philosophical essay.
- **1908:** *The Social Value of the College-Bred*. An address defining education through discrimination of human quality rather than credential alone.

### Projects, lectures, and bodies of work that resist single-work status

- **1879–1882:** *The Sentiment of Rationality*. A projected larger essay on the theoretical and practical motives of philosophizing. Its fragments became separate articles and later a recomposed essay, so the project—not any one printing—is the most coherent obra.
- **1878–1890:** *The Principles of Psychology project*. A long research-and-writing enterprise whose component articles were repeatedly tested in periodicals and then absorbed, revised, or partly superseded by the two-volume book.
- **1885–1910:** *Psychical-research investigations*. A body of sittings, committee work, correspondence, case reports, and methodological arguments. It resists treatment as one finished work, but also cannot be reduced to the individually published reports.
- **1897–1902:** *The Gifford Lectures on Natural Religion*. A lecture project originally intended to unite psychological description with a further philosophical treatment; only the psychological part became *The Varieties of Religious Experience*.
- **1904–1912:** *Essays in Radical Empiricism*. An author-conceived but unfinished collection project. James selected most of its conceptual shape, while Perry determined its posthumous final form.
- **1906–1907:** *Lowell Institute and Columbia lectures on pragmatism*. A spoken lecture cycle subsequently remade as *Pragmatism*; lectures and book are related manifestations, not simply identical texts.
- **1908–1909:** *Hibbert Lectures on the Present Situation in Philosophy*. A performed lecture cycle subsequently revised into *A Pluralistic Universe*.
- **1909–1910:** *Introduction to Philosophy*. James’s final book project, interrupted by illness and death and published as *Some Problems of Philosophy*. It must remain classified as unfinished.
- **Throughout his adult life:** *Correspondence*. Thousands of made texts addressed to particular recipients, many still known only through archival or edited selections. No published letters edition is the complete obra, and a responsible “everything” list cannot pretend that the surviving corpus has fixed boundaries.
- **Throughout his career:** *Lecture notes, course materials, notebooks, marginalia, and unpublished manuscripts*. These are textual artifacts rather than a stable list of titled works; the Harvard edition and archival catalogues preserve or identify many, but neither title nor work-status can safely be imposed on every fragment.
