# Johann Friedrich Overbeck: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Notes

Johann Friedrich Overbeck died in 1869. His life is documented principally through:

- his surviving correspondence and papers;
- Margaret Howitt’s two-volume 1886 biography, constructed substantially from original letters and manuscript materials;
- nineteenth-century biographies and criticism;
- modern museum catalogues, collection databases, and art-historical scholarship;
- institutional records concerning honors, collections, and archival holdings.

Nineteenth-century accounts frequently combine firsthand observation with confessional, nationalist, or aesthetic polemic. J. Beavington Atkinson knew Overbeck and visited his studio, but his 1882 biography is simultaneously admiring and strongly critical of Overbeck’s later Catholic art. Friedrich Pecht’s 1887 *Allgemeine Deutsche Biographie* article is openly hostile to Catholic conversion and “neo-Catholic” art. Their judgments are therefore cited as contemporary or near-contemporary criticism, not neutral findings.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by institutional records, surviving works, correspondence, or multiple strong sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally informed source but not independently verified here from the underlying document.
- **Disputed:** sources give incompatible accounts.
- **Contemporary allegation or criticism:** a claim or judgment made during or shortly after Overbeck’s lifetime.
- **Later interpretation:** an art-historical or biographical reading rather than a directly documented event.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulating story for which the evidentiary basis is weak, symbolic, or hagiographic.

No credible evidence was found of criminal conduct, fraud, litigation, corruption, imprisonment, intelligence activity, political violence, addiction, or sexual scandal involving Overbeck. Searches conducted in English, German, and Italian—including combinations with “controversy,” “scandal,” “allegation,” “lawsuit,” “fraud,” “accusation,” “conspiracy,” *Kritik*, *Skandal*, *Vorwurf*, *Betrug*, *Verschwörung*, *controversia*, and *accuse*—primarily returned aesthetic and confessional disputes, later attribution questions, and results about unrelated people named Overbeck.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Johann Friedrich Overbeck.

**Common working name:** Friedrich Overbeck.

**Recorded variants:** Johann Overbeck; F. Overbeck; J. F. Overbeck; Joh. Friedrich Overbeck; Giovanni Federico Overbeck; Russian form Иоганн Фридрих Овербек.

**Birth:** 3 July 1789, Lübeck, a Free Imperial City in the Holy Roman Empire.

An 1882 English biography gives 4 July and refers to a tablet on the family house. Modern authority records, the *Neue Deutsche Biographie*, the German National Library, and specialist museum catalogues consistently give **3 July 1789**. The one-day discrepancy may reflect an erroneous inscription, a baptismal-versus-birth-date confusion, or Atkinson’s transcription; the strongest available date is 3 July.

**Death:** 12 November 1869, Rome, Papal States, at approximately six in the evening.

**Burial:** Church of San Bernardo alle Terme, Rome. His body was taken there on 13 November; the funeral Mass and burial followed amid attendance by German artists and students. The sculptor Carl Hoffmann later made his epitaph.

**Nationality and cultural identification:** Lübeck-born German painter resident principally in Rome. Despite remaining in Italy, he maintained German correspondence and markets and continued to regard himself as German.

**Religion:** Raised Protestant/Lutheran in a learned, pietistic household; received into the Roman Catholic Church at Pentecost 1813.

**Professions:** Painter, draftsman, fresco painter, illustrator, designer of engravings and woodcuts, and occasional printmaker and writer.

**Movement:** Founder and principal ideological representative of the Brotherhood of Saint Luke, or Lukasbund, from which the Nazarene movement developed.

**Titles and epithets:**

- “Nazarene,” originally an externally applied and partly mocking name associated with the artists’ long hair, biblical appearance, and quasi-monastic habits.
- “Lukasbruder,” or Brother of Saint Luke.
- “The Hanseatic Raphael,” a later commemorative and exhibition epithet.
- “Apostle” or “high priest” of Christian art, in sympathetic nineteenth-century literature.
- “Oberhirte” or chief shepherd of the Nazarenes, in later cultural journalism.
- No pseudonym is documented.

He should not be confused with August Friedrich “Fritz” Overbeck (1869–1909), the Worpswede painter, or with the theologian Franz Overbeck.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal ancestry

**Documented or strongly reported genealogy:** The Overbeck family traced its remembered ancestry to Caspar Overbeck, described in nineteenth-century family tradition as a merchant and religious refugee. His descendants included several Protestant pastors:

- Christoph Overbeck, pastor;
- Caspar Nikolaus Overbeck, pastor, died 1752;
- Johann Adolph Overbeck, pastor;
- Levin Conrad Overbeck, pastor;
- Johann Georg Overbeck, pastor, died 1819.

Overbeck’s paternal grandfather was **Georg Christian Overbeck** (1713–1786), a Lübeck lawyer or doctor of laws. His paternal grandmother was **Eleonora Maria Jauch** (1732–1797). The Jauch connection appears in genealogical literature; some later summaries have mistakenly identified Eleonora as Friedrich’s mother.

A prominent great-uncle, **Johann Daniel Overbeck** (1715–1802), was a doctor of theology, rector of Lübeck’s Gymnasium/Katharineum, and a prolific writer on religion, classical learning, and education. Nineteenth-century sources credited him with more than thirty publications.

### Parents

Overbeck’s father was **Christian Adolph Overbeck** (21 August 1755–9 March 1821), jurist, poet, composer, Lübeck senator and burgomaster. He studied law at Göttingen, moved in the literary environment associated with the Göttinger Hain, and maintained a lifelong friendship with Johann Heinrich Voß. He served Lübeck in political and diplomatic capacities, including missions to Paris and Saint Petersburg. His poetry included children’s songs and religious, pastoral, classical, and sentimental verse. “Komm, lieber Mai, und mache,” later set by Mozart, was among the best-known texts associated with his *Frizchens Lieder*.

Friedrich’s mother was **Elisabeth Kretzschmer, née Lang**, a widow when she married Christian Adolph in 1781. She died in 1820. Nineteenth-century testimony describes her as active in supervising the children’s lessons. Her property or fortune reportedly made a substantial contribution to household finances.

The family was socially prominent but not exceptionally rich. Christian Adolph’s municipal and cathedral stipends were reportedly modest, and family biographers describe the household as comfortable but financially constrained.

### Siblings and half-sibling

Christian Adolph and Elisabeth reportedly had six children together—four sons and two daughters—with one son dying in childhood. Identified siblings include:

- **Christian Gerhard Overbeck** (1784–1846), judge in Lübeck;
- **Johannes Overbeck**, merchant, died 1830;
- **Charlotte Overbeck**, who married the physician and orthopedist Matthias Ludwig Leithoff;
- **Elisabeth “Betty” Overbeck**, who married the educator Johann Heinrich Meier;
- an unnamed brother who died in childhood.

Elisabeth also had a daughter from her first marriage; the surviving summaries consulted do not consistently name her.

### Wife

Overbeck married **Anna Schiffenhuber**, generally called **Nina**, in Rome.

**Date dispute:** The *Neue Deutsche Biographie* gives **1818**; Atkinson’s chronology gives **1819**, while noting that Nina was publicly described as Overbeck’s future wife at a Bavarian crown-prince fête in 1818. The safest reconstruction is marriage in late 1818 or 1819, with modern German biographical authority favoring 1818.

Nina was Viennese. The *Neue Deutsche Biographie* describes her as the illegitimate daughter of a nobleman and the foster daughter of **Joseph Hartl von Luchsenstein** (1760–1822), industrial entrepreneur, founder of the Pottendorf spinning factory, theatrical director, and Viennese court councillor. A nineteenth-century English dictionary claimed that she had “no right to a surname” but received a dowry from her noble father; that formulation reflects the period’s legal and social prejudices and has not been independently confirmed here.

Nina was praised by Madame Bunsen as beautiful, engaging, influential, and the most admired woman at the 1818 artists’ festival. The marriage was described as fundamentally happy. Her recurring poor health caused anxiety. Atkinson additionally reports that some of her demands upon Overbeck irritated Cornelius and other friends; this is a **reported interpersonal judgment**, not evidence of misconduct.

Nina died in 1853.

### Children

The *Neue Deutsche Biographie* records:

- **Alfons Maria Overbeck** (1819–1840), the surviving son, who studied architecture;
- two daughters who died young.

Atkinson’s genealogy instead gives Alfons’s birth as 1822 and says there was one daughter who died in childhood. A letter of 23 August 1829, however, calls that day Alfons’s tenth birthday, strongly supporting **23 August 1819**. Thus the modern 1819 date is preferable, and the 1822 date is erroneous.

Alfons was physically frail but regarded as highly promising. He was trained for architecture, which his father called mystical, musical, and an embodiment of religious enthusiasm. He died in autumn 1840, aged twenty-one if the 1819 birth date is accepted—not eighteen, as Atkinson states. Overbeck attended his sickbed and drew intermittently to control or redirect his grief. The loss profoundly affected the *Pietà* or *Lamentation* made for Lübeck.

### Adoptive daughter and estate

In 1855 Overbeck adopted **Caroline von Rünagel** (1817–1872), wife of the Roman sculptor **Carl Hoffmann** (1816–1872). Hoffmann was also described as Overbeck’s confirmation godson and later created his epitaph.

Atkinson alleges that adoption was arranged to give the widowed artist a family household and secure the management or succession of his estate. He further claims that Caroline and Hoffmann inherited the studio contents, correspondence, money, and other property, while Overbeck’s blood relatives received nothing and afterward “found little favour.”

This is a **reported and potentially partisan inheritance claim** from an author close enough to know the Roman circle but not accompanied in the consulted source by a will, probate record, or family correspondence. It should not be elevated to a proven family scandal without those documents.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1789–1805

Overbeck was born into the family house on Königstraße in central Lübeck. The nineteenth-century building was later modernized, but a portrait medallion commemorating him remained incorporated into the façade.

His childhood environment combined:

- Lutheran worship and family pietism;
- classical education;
- poetry and music;
- civic service;
- Lübeck’s medieval architecture and surviving religious art.

The nearby Marienkirche and cathedral preserved medieval and early Renaissance objects, including works associated with Hans Memling. Later biographers regarded the young Overbeck’s exposure to this environment as formative. Direct proof of specific childhood responses is limited, but his mature preference for early German, Netherlandish, Umbrian, and Tuscan religious art makes the association plausible.

Christian Adolph initially hesitated over an artistic career because he feared mediocrity. He ultimately supported it, laying down three requirements: classical learning, moral seriousness, and technical ability. His surviving admonitions joined Homer and the Bible as complementary sources and described the artist’s mind as a “spotless mirror.” These ideas prefigure Friedrich’s mature conviction that an artist’s work depended upon moral and religious character.

Overbeck was educated partly at home and in a privately organized class shared by several families. His mother helped prepare lessons. On Michaelmas 1803 he entered the highest class of Lübeck’s Katharineum.

His earliest drawing instruction is inconsistently described:

- an artillery noncommissioned officer named **Mau** is sometimes called his first drawing teacher;
- Professor **Federau** reportedly taught him through the Gymnasium drawing school;
- **Joseph Nikolaus Peroux** (1771–1849), a classicizing portrait and history painter, gave the first sustained professional instruction, beginning around 1805.

A signed ink-wash exercise dated 21 April 1805 survives in the nineteenth-century record and was probably a copy made for a teacher.

## Education and Formation, 1805–1810

### Lübeck and Hamburg

In 1805 Overbeck studied under Peroux. In 1806 his father arranged contacts in Hamburg with:

- **Johann Heinrich Wilhelm Tischbein**, painter and former director of the Naples Academy;
- **Philipp Otto Runge**, the Romantic painter;
- cathedral canon and travel writer **Friedrich Johann Lorenz Meyer**.

Meyer recommended him to **Heinrich Friedrich Füger**, director of the Vienna Academy of Fine Arts.

### Vienna Academy

Overbeck left Lübeck for Vienna in 1806 and entered the Academy. Füger’s teaching emphasized classical draftsmanship and compositional formulas influenced by Anton Raphael Mengs, Guido Reni, and the broader academic tradition. Overbeck learned figure drawing, including study from nude and draped models; later claims that the Nazarenes simply rejected anatomical study are contradicted by surviving academy drawings and Friedrich Wilhelm Schadow’s testimony.

His important formative influences in Vienna included:

- Raphael;
- Perugino and the Umbrian school;
- Fra Angelico;
- early German and Netherlandish painting;
- Albrecht Dürer;
- Eberhard Wächter;
- the writings of Wilhelm Heinrich Wackenroder;
- Ludwig Tieck;
- Friedrich Schlegel;
- the Bible as narrative and doctrinal foundation.

Overbeck came to regard the Academy as technically accomplished but spiritually empty. In letters he complained of vulgarity, lack of noble thought, and isolation. He contrasted “nature as master” with what he considered slavish academic study. Modern scholarship cautions that his later self-description was partly programmatic: he did acquire substantial academic skill, and his rejection of Vienna should not be mistaken for absence of academic training.

### Franz Pforr and the Brotherhood of Saint Luke

Overbeck formed his most consequential youthful friendship with **Franz Pforr** (1788–1812). They shared dissatisfaction with the Academy and an interest in early German and Italian painting. Their friendship became an artistic program: Pforr’s *Sulamith and Maria* and Overbeck’s evolving *Italia und Germania* treated friendship, complementary national traditions, and a hoped-for reconciliation of northern and southern Christian art.

On 10 July 1809—traditionally the anniversary of Pforr’s and Overbeck’s first meeting—Overbeck, Pforr, Ludwig Vogel, Joseph Wintergerst, Johann Konrad Hottinger, and Joseph Sutter founded the **Lukasbund**, or Brotherhood of Saint Luke. Members used an emblem combining Saint Luke, artistic tools, and the initials of their association. It was an early organized secession from a modern academy.

### Departure or expulsion

Sources describe the group’s separation from the Academy in different ways. Atkinson and several later summaries say Overbeck and his companions were expelled in 1810 after conflict with academic authorities. Other accounts describe voluntary withdrawal following irreconcilable differences. The likely reconstruction is that institutional exclusion and voluntary secession overlapped: the dissident group lost its place in the Academy’s reorganization and chose Rome rather than reconciliation.

In 1809 Overbeck began *Christ’s Entry into Jerusalem*. It deliberately combined early Italian, German, and Netherlandish sources and incorporated contemporary portraits. He carried the unfinished canvas to Rome and did not complete it until 1824.

## Chronological Life History

## 1810: Journey to Italy and settlement in Rome

In May 1810 Overbeck traveled toward Rome with Pforr, Vogel, and Hottinger. A documented visit to Urbino on 15 June 1810 was associated with his intense engagement with Raphael. Sources differ when loosely summarizing his migration—some say 1809—but contemporary chronology and the *Neue Deutsche Biographie* support **May–June 1810**.

The group settled in the abandoned Franciscan monastery of **San Isidoro**, near the Villa Malta on the Pincian Hill. Assistance in obtaining the lodging is sometimes credited to the director of the French Academy in Rome.

Their life was deliberately communal and quasi-monastic:

- shared work and criticism;
- austere rooms;
- early rising and disciplined labor;
- study of Rome’s churches, frescoes, mosaics, and collections;
- rejection of much fashionable social life;
- religious reading and observance;
- long hair and archaizing dress.

The artists did not take monastic vows. Descriptions of them as literal monks are therefore incorrect, though their cultivated monastic analogy was real.

### The name “Nazarene”

The German artists were called *Nazarener*, *Nazarites*, or variants of *I Nazareni*. The term was initially mocking, referring to their Christ-like hair, religious pretensions, or both. They more commonly called themselves Lukasbrüder. The once-hostile term became the standard art-historical name for the movement.

## 1811–1813: Expansion of the Roman circle and conversion

Wintergerst and Johannes Veit arrived in 1811. **Peter Cornelius** came from Düsseldorf later that year and formed an intimate, lifelong friendship with Overbeck. Cornelius’s forceful temperament and experience complemented Overbeck’s inward, lyrical disposition.

The Roman network included or later attracted Wilhelm von Schadow, Philipp Veit, Julius Schnorr von Carolsfeld, Joseph von Führich, Eduard von Steinle, Johann David Passavant, and other German-speaking artists.

Overbeck supported less secure companions when possible, though his own finances were limited. Lübeck’s political and economic troubles eventually reduced or interrupted his father’s support, making commissions more urgent.

Franz Pforr died of tuberculosis in Albano Laziale on 16 June 1812, aged twenty-four. His death was a severe personal and artistic loss. Overbeck’s portrait of Pforr and the paired *Italia und Germania* project became memorial objects central to Nazarene mythology.

### Catholic conversion

At Pentecost 1813 Overbeck was received into the Roman Catholic Church. His spiritual director and confessor was Professor—later Cardinal—**Andrea Ostini**. The poet and Catholic convert **Zacharias Werner** served as godfather or sponsor.

**Documented motives:** Overbeck’s correspondence and artistic program show a long search for doctrinal authority, continuity with historic Christian art, sacramental religion, and a church he believed capable of uniting art and faith.

**Contemporary Protestant interpretation:** Barthold Georg Niebuhr and Christian Karl Josias von Bunsen saw the conversions among German artists as an organized Roman proselytizing campaign. Niebuhr referred to anti-Protestant pamphleteering and sought Lutheran books to counter it. He described Overbeck as amiable and imaginative but “illiberal,” dependent, and susceptible to Catholic authority.

**Later hostile interpretation:** Pecht called the movement a conversion mania encouraged by Roman clergy and a scandal. He nevertheless expressly conceded Overbeck’s sincerity.

There is no evidence that Overbeck converted for money, office, coercion, or espionage. Claims of manipulation belong to confessional controversy; influence by Werner, Ostini, Roman art, and the convert circle is documented, but Overbeck’s conviction was longstanding.

## 1813–1819: Early commissions and public breakthrough

Overbeck’s early Roman works included:

- *The Adoration of the Magi* (1811–1813);
- *Christ in the House of Mary and Martha* (1812/13–1816);
- *The Raising of Jairus’s Daughter* (about 1815);
- portraits, Madonnas, biblical drawings, and devotional compositions.

### Casa Bartholdy, 1816–1817/18

The Prussian consul **Jakob Ludwig Salomon Bartholdy** commissioned Cornelius, Overbeck, Philipp Veit, and Wilhelm Schadow to decorate rooms in the Casa Bartholdy, part of the Palazzo Zuccari complex near Trinità dei Monti, with the story of Joseph.

Overbeck painted:

- *Joseph Sold by His Brothers*;
- *The Seven Lean Years*.

The cycle re-established fresco as a viable modern collaborative medium and brought the Nazarenes international attention. The detached frescoes were transferred to Berlin in the late nineteenth century and are now associated with the Alte Nationalgalerie.

The famine scene was reportedly informed by Overbeck’s observation of hunger in the Roman countryside. This is biographically plausible but should be treated as a reported source connection, not the sole origin of the composition.

### Casino Massimo, 1817–1820s

Prince Francesco Massimo commissioned a second collaborative fresco cycle for the Casino Massimo near San Giovanni in Laterano. Rooms were assigned to subjects from Dante, Tasso, and Ariosto. Overbeck designed and worked on scenes from Torquato Tasso’s *Gerusalemme liberata*, including the calling of Godfrey of Bouillon.

Overbeck did not complete every planned fresco personally. Joseph von Führich and others carried portions of the program forward. The cycle extended into the later 1820s.

### 1819 Palazzo Caffarelli exhibition

An exhibition of German artists at Palazzo Caffarelli in 1819 displayed works by Overbeck, Schadow, Veit, Cornelius, and others. Friedrich Schlegel praised the breadth and seriousness of the emerging school. Overbeck showed a Madonna, *Flight into Egypt*, and designs for the Massimo frescoes.

## 1818/19–1831: Marriage, family, Assisi, and consolidation

Overbeck married Nina Schiffenhuber and established a family household in Rome. His son Alfons was born on 23 August 1819. Two daughters, according to the *Neue Deutsche Biographie*, died young.

His principal residences are incompletely documented, but later he lived in the Palazzo Cenci before moving after Nina’s death.

### Major works of the 1820s

- *The Easter Morning* (about 1818);
- *The Annunciation* (1820);
- portrait of **Vittoria Caldoni** (1821), the celebrated Italian model;
- *The Raising of Lazarus* (1822);
- *The Finding of Moses* (1822–1824);
- completion of *Christ’s Entry into Jerusalem* (1824);
- *Mary and Elizabeth with the Christ Child and Saint John* (1825);
- *Italia und Germania*, completed about 1828 after a long development;
- portraits of Roman friends and associates;
- designs for devotional prints.

*Christ’s Entry into Jerusalem* went to Lübeck’s Marienkirche. It was destroyed in the British bombing and fire of Palm Sunday, 28–29 March 1942.

### Refused institutional appointments

Overbeck repeatedly declined opportunities to return to German institutional life:

- A Städel Institute offer in 1818 reportedly did not reach him.
- He declined another Städel offer in 1823.
- He declined professorial calls or invitations connected with Munich and Düsseldorf, including a Munich offer from King Ludwig I in 1826.
- He later declined leadership of the Städel Institute, with the exact year variously given as 1829 or 1830, and recommended Philipp Veit.

These refusals preserved his independence and Roman vocation but prevented him from directly administering a German academy or museum.

### Assisi and *The Vision of Saint Francis*

Overbeck received a commission for a large fresco, *The Vision of Saint Francis*, at Santa Maria degli Angeli near Assisi, associated with the Porziuncola. Work occupied the later 1820s and was finished in 1830 or 1831.

He lived within or near the religious establishment while working. Steinle and Führich visited him. He considered entering a monastery; according to repeated biographical tradition, the pope advised that his proper vocation was art and that vows would reduce his usefulness. This is a **reported story with a plausible ecclesiastical context**, but the consulted sources do not reproduce the papal document.

## 1831: First return to German lands

Overbeck left Rome in early summer 1831, traveling through Florence and the Tyrol to Munich with Cornelius. Munich artists reportedly greeted them outside the city, unhitched their carriage horses, and drew the carriage themselves through cheering crowds. The episode is supported by near-contemporary biographical tradition, although the rhetoric of a “triumphal entry” contributed to his public legend.

His itinerary included:

- Munich, as Cornelius’s guest;
- Stift Neuburg near Heidelberg;
- Heidelberg;
- Frankfurt am Main;
- Cologne;
- Düsseldorf;
- Strasbourg and Switzerland on the return route.

In Frankfurt he met **Clemens Brentano**, renewed relations with Philipp Veit and Johann David Passavant, and discussed work for the Städel. In Cologne he was consulted in connection with cathedral decoration. In Düsseldorf he visited Wilhelm Schadow and the academy circle.

He corresponded closely with **Emilie Linder**, a Basel artist, collector, philanthropist, and later Catholic convert. Their relationship was an intense religious and artistic friendship conducted substantially through letters. No credible evidence identifies it as a sexual partnership.

Overbeck returned to Rome by December 1831, writing that Germany’s conflicting doctrines had disturbed him and that Roman seclusion better suited his vocation.

## 1833–1840: Raphael’s tomb and the great painted manifesto

In September 1833 Overbeck witnessed the opening of Raphael’s tomb in the Pantheon. He wrote an emotional account to Philipp Veit, calling Raphael the incomparable master. This contradicts the loose characterization of Overbeck as simply “anti-Raphael”: he opposed post-Raphaelite academism while maintaining deep reverence for Raphael himself.

Other major works included:

- *Christ’s Agony in the Garden*, for Hamburg, dated approximately 1831–1835;
- *The Marriage of the Virgin* or *Lo Sposalizio*, commissioned by Count Athanasius Raczyński and completed in 1836;
- *The Death of Saint Joseph*, including a version for the bishop of Algiers;
- numerous drawings and cartoons.

### *The Triumph of Religion in the Arts*, 1829–1840

Commissioned for the Städel Institute in 1829, *Der Triumph der Religion in den Künsten* occupied Overbeck for approximately a decade and was delivered in 1840. Alternative contemporary titles included *The Magnificat of Art* and *The Christian Parnassus*; hostile critics called it a “triumph of Mariolatry.”

The painting assembles more than one hundred sacred figures, artists, and historical personages around the Virgin and Child. Overbeck supplied a written explanation. The central fountain signified the divine and natural sources of art. Raphael, dressed in white, embodied a synthesis of artistic gifts. Michelangelo was placed apart, and Venetian and post-Renaissance painters received comparatively unfavorable treatment.

The Städel records the work as acquired in 1840. A nineteenth-century account says Overbeck received £1,300 for the finished painting and valued one associated study at £100. These figures are historically reported, not converted here into modern currency.

Reception was divided. Admirers regarded it as the consummation of Christian art. Liberal Frankfurt critics and later art historians attacked its hierarchy, exclusions, anti-naturalism, and confessional program. The Städel itself notes that it met fierce criticism in the atmosphere of a liberal bourgeois city.

## 1840–1853: Bereavement and devotional cycles

Alfons died in autumn 1840. Overbeck’s correspondence describes overwhelming grief and the use of drawing as emotional support.

A Lübeck association had begun collecting funds in 1837 to obtain a major work from its celebrated native son. The resulting *Pietà* or *Lamentation over the Dead Christ* was developed after Alfons’s death and completed in 1845–1846. Overbeck explicitly connected it with grief for both his son and a recently deceased brother. He sent it to Lübeck in March 1846 for installation in the Marienkirche.

### *The Incredulity of Saint Thomas*

A Yorkshire patron, Mr. Rhodes, commissioned an altarpiece for the Anglican church of Saint Thomas in Leeds. Overbeck completed *The Incredulity of Saint Thomas* in 1851. It did not enter the intended church. Ernst Förster reported that people were dissatisfied, but the exact objection remains unclear. A. J. Beresford Hope acquired it for £300. It later entered the Schäfer collection in Schweinfurt.

The transaction complicates claims that Overbeck categorically refused all Protestant commissions. He showed confessional reluctance in letters and sometimes asked that commissions be redirected, but he did execute at least this Anglican work.

### *The Gospels*

Between approximately 1843 and 1852 Overbeck developed forty Gospel cartoons. They were commissioned through or for a Prague art dealer and later owned by Baron Lotzbeck. Published as *L’Évangile illustré: Quarante compositions de Frédéric Overbeck*, they were engraved by several German artists. Their linear clarity made them highly suitable for reproduction and gave them an influence beyond the original drawings.

Nina died in 1853. Overbeck left the Palazzo Cenci and moved to a quieter garden or vineyard house on the Esquiline near Santa Maria Maggiore. He later lived in a convent-owned house on the Via di Porta Pia near San Bernardo alle Terme.

## 1854–1858: Cologne, second German journey, Pius IX, and papal politics

Overbeck completed the *Assumption of the Virgin* for Cologne Cathedral in 1854 or 1855. The project had evolved from an abandoned proposal for a Gothic high-altarpiece into a painting for a Marian chapel.

On 20 July 1855 he left Rome, traveled through Florence and Switzerland, and visited Frankfurt, Düsseldorf, Cologne, Mainz, Heidelberg/Stift Neuburg, Speyer, and Munich. Cologne held a festival in his honor. Friends observed pronounced physical frailty. Emilie Linder wrote that bodily life had become a burden to him. He returned to Rome after about four months, crossing the Brenner and sending a symbolic final greeting northward through the rivers flowing toward Germany.

### Pius IX

Overbeck developed a close relationship with Pope **Pius IX**:

- the pope gave sittings for a portrait;
- expressed interest in Overbeck’s *Via Crucis*;
- sent apostolic blessings;
- commissioned work for the Quirinal;
- visited Overbeck’s studio on 7 February 1857.

The *Via Crucis*, fourteen watercolor Stations of the Cross, was completed in 1857, with a period of work at Perugia in 1856. Related versions and studies survive in Vatican and other collections.

### *Christ Escaping from the Jews*

Pius IX commissioned the large tempera *Christ Escaping from the Jews* for the Quirinal, completed in 1858. Christ is shown escaping a hostile crowd at the edge of a precipice, supported or protected by angels.

**Documented political meaning:** The composition was understood as an allegory of Pius IX’s flight from Rome in 1848, his restoration, and the triumph of spiritual over temporal opposition. It consequently functioned as a visual intervention in the conflicts preceding Italian unification.

**Controversy:** Critics regarded the subject as unpaintable or absurd. It was also characterized as a direct attack upon the emerging Italian temporal government. After the Quirinal became a royal palace, the painting was covered or displaced; it later entered Vatican custody. Overbeck produced or authorized a large oil version associated with Antwerp.

This was not party politics in the modern electoral sense. Overbeck held no political office, but his art supported papal authority and opposed the secularizing, nationalist direction of the Roman and Italian revolutions.

## 1859–1869: Final decade

Overbeck continued working despite frailty and eye disease.

### *The Seven Sacraments*

Completed in cartoon form around 1861, *Die sieben Sakramente* consisted of seven principal designs surrounded by Old Testament types, New Testament fulfillments, borders, predellas, and roughly forty subsidiary subjects. August Gaber translated the cycle into woodcuts; an edition appeared in 1865, with later editions.

Overbeck’s Eucharistic design replaced the conventional Last Supper table with an altar-like arrangement and Christ elevating a host. Critics charged that he had replaced the biblical event with later Catholic liturgy. Even some Catholic observers regarded the cycle as doctrinally excessive. The objection is iconographic and theological, not an allegation of deception.

### Other late projects

Late works and designs included:

- *Baptism* (about 1862–1864);
- an oil version of *Christ Escaping from the Jews* for Antwerp;
- cartoons of the Twelve Apostles and Four Evangelists for the Torlonia chapel at Castel Gandolfo;
- designs for Saint Catherine’s Church in Hamburg;
- cathedral designs associated with Mexico;
- drawings for Bishop Josip Juraj Strossmayer’s cathedral at Đakovo;
- a small *Last Judgment*;
- an unfinished allegorical series on human life.

He maintained a regular studio routine, beginning early, pausing at midday, and continuing until darkness. Sundays brought international visitors. His reliance on memory and internal visualization increased as his health declined.

### Final illness and death

During summer 1869 he worked at **Rocca di Papa** in the Alban Hills, a favored retreat. A June letter to Eduard von Steinle described the landscape and his desire to use his remaining time productively.

After returning to Rome in autumn, he developed a severe chest cold approximately fourteen days later. Treatment initially appeared successful, but he suffered a sudden collapse of strength. His mind was reportedly lucid for most of the final illness. A day or two before death he requested charcoal and added marks to a drawing; attendants later recalled hand movements like drawing even when consciousness was impaired.

He died peacefully at about six in the evening on Friday, 12 November 1869. The immediate documented cause can be stated only as complications following a severe chest cold and systemic decline; no modern diagnosis is justified.

His funeral included a German bishop, Cistercian monks, artists, German students, and psalm settings associated with Franz Liszt.

## Residences, Travel, and Geographical Movements

- **1789–1806:** Königstraße, Lübeck; education at home and at the Katharineum.
- **1806:** Hamburg contacts; relocation to Vienna.
- **1806–1810:** Vienna, Academy of Fine Arts.
- **May–June 1810:** journey through Italy, including documented presence in Urbino on 15 June.
- **1810 onward:** Rome; first communal residence at San Isidoro.
- **1812:** movement within the Roman/Alban artistic circle, including Albano in connection with Pforr’s illness and death.
- **1816–1818:** Casa Bartholdy work at Trinità dei Monti.
- **From 1817:** Casino Massimo work near the Lateran.
- **1820s–1830:** extended work at Santa Maria degli Angeli near Assisi.
- **1831:** Florence, Tyrol, Munich, Stift Neuburg/Heidelberg, Frankfurt, Cologne, Düsseldorf, Strasbourg, Switzerland, and return to Rome.
- **1856:** Perugia while working on *Via Crucis*.
- **1855:** Florence, Switzerland, Frankfurt, Düsseldorf, Cologne, Mainz, Heidelberg, Speyer, Munich, Brenner route, Rome.

- **Summer retreats:** Rocca di Papa.
- **1869:** final return from Rocca di Papa to Rome.

Apart from the two German journeys, Assisi, Perugia, and nearby Roman/Alban travel, Overbeck chose a settled Roman life and declined institutional migration.

## Artistic, Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Early synthesis

Overbeck’s childhood formation united Protestant pietism, classical literature, and artistic moralism. His father recommended both Homer and the Bible. This was initially an inclusive synthesis rather than strict confessional Catholicism.

### Vienna revolt

At Vienna he increasingly rejected:

- formulaic classicism;
- virtuosity without moral content;
- academic hierarchy;
- mythology as the presumed highest artistic language.

He did not reject classical skill itself. His surviving work shows academic draftsmanship, anatomical study, and familiarity with classical composition. His objection was to the Academy’s goals and rhetoric.

### Christian-German program

The Lukasbund sought a “new-old” art based upon:

- sincere religious belief;
- moral reform of the artist;
- collaborative brotherhood;
- early Renaissance and medieval models;
- direct study of nature disciplined by spiritual meaning;
- revival of monumental fresco.

The “German” component was never exclusively nationalist. *Italia und Germania* imagined complementary German and Italian traditions.

### Catholic consolidation

After 1813 Overbeck increasingly identified the historic continuity of Christian art with Roman Catholicism. He regarded art as subordinate to revelation and church teaching. The artist’s personal purity was, in his view, integral to valid sacred representation.

### Later dogmatism

By the 1830s his letters sometimes ranked religious meaning above artistic execution. He said that direct recourse to a model might disrupt an internally formed sacred conception. This produced a recurring contradiction: an artist trained through close drawing from life increasingly subordinated observed nature to inherited type and doctrinal symbolism.

His mature hierarchy favored:

- Fra Angelico;
- Perugino;
- Raphael, particularly the earlier and Vatican Raphael;
- Dürer;
- Jan and Hubert van Eyck;
- early Tuscan and Umbrian schools.

He judged Michelangelo ambivalently and treated Correggio and Venetian painting severely. Critics saw these rankings as sectarian and historically distorted.

### Closest artistic relationships

**Franz Pforr:** closest youthful friend, co-founder of the Lukasbund, companion from Vienna to Rome, and partner in the *Sulamith and Maria*/*Italia und Germania* program. Pforr’s death in 1812 became foundational to Overbeck’s personal and artistic memory.

## Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Overbeck was not a businessman, inventor, or patent holder. His economic life centered on commissions, drawings, engravings, paintings, frescoes, subscriptions, and institutional patronage.

### Institutional affiliations

- Vienna Academy of Fine Arts, student, 1806–1810;
- Brotherhood of Saint Luke/Lukasbund, co-founder, 1809;
- Roman Nazarene community;
- Accademia di San Luca, honorary member and professor from 1831; council member from 1837;
- numerous European and American honorary academies;
- reported board role in the Archconfraternity of the Campo Santo Teutonico, date uncertain.

### Workshop and production structure

Overbeck maintained a Roman studio rather than a large commercial workshop on the Rubens model. Assistants and pupils participated in cartoons, transfers, engravings, and related commissions. Printmakers—including Samuel Amsler, August Gaber, and engravers of the Gospel cycle—made his designs available to a mass devotional market.

The reproductive network was central to his influence. His designs appeared in:

- engravings;
- etchings;
- woodcuts;
- chromolithographic or color reproductions;
- books of hours;
- editions of the Gospels;
- editions of Thomas à Kempis’s *Imitation of Christ*;
- devotional prints.

### Income and wealth

The best available general conclusion is that Overbeck was never wealthy. He had many commissions but worked slowly, sometimes for years or decades, and charged sums later observers considered low.

Reported examples:

- approximately £1,300 for *The Triumph of Religion in the Arts*;
- £300 for *The Incredulity of Saint Thomas*;
- £100 quoted for an associated study or cartoon.

He sometimes lacked ready cash despite a full order book. After early insecurity he achieved a stable but comparatively modest livelihood. No documented commercial investments, company holdings, patents, speculative enterprises, or large landed properties were found.

## Health, Psychological Strain, and Personal Habits

**Documented physical condition:** Overbeck was repeatedly described as physically delicate. Nina and Alfons were also chronically frail. In old age he suffered an eye disorder and declining manual control.

**Grief crises:** The deaths of Pforr, his daughters, Alfons, a brother, Nina, and Cornelius affected him deeply. The clearest documented acute crisis followed Alfons’s death. He used drawing as consolation and incorporated grief into the *Pietà*.

**Asceticism:** Overbeck cultivated simplicity, withdrawal, and disciplined work. Critics inferred that fasting or mortification weakened him, but no clinical evidence connects his frailty to deliberate self-starvation. The “starved” bodies in his art became a satirical motif in criticism.

**Mental-health descriptions:** Contemporaries called him melancholy, sensitive, nervous, withdrawn, or incapable of ordinary society. These are character judgments, not diagnoses. No documentary basis was found for psychosis, mania, addiction, or institutionalization.

**Final illness:** severe chest cold followed by sudden loss of strength, November 1869.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Contested Questions

### 1. Confessional proselytism

**Documented fact:** Overbeck converted in 1813 and became a prominent Roman Catholic artistic authority. Several associates also converted.

**Contemporary allegation:** Niebuhr and others believed organized Catholic pamphleteering and clerical influence were targeting German artists. Overbeck was described as possessing a “proselyting spirit.”

**Evidence assessment:** Clerical guidance, convert networks, and confessional advocacy are documented. No evidence establishes coercion, payment, fraud, or a secret political operation. Overbeck’s own development toward Catholicism preceded the formal reception.

### 2. “Fanaticism” and “conversion mania”

Pecht’s 1887 biography called Overbeck increasingly fanatical and the conversions a scandal. These are **hostile confessional and nationalist judgments**, although they accurately register a real nineteenth-century Kulturkampf over Catholic influence. Pecht explicitly denied that Overbeck was hypocritical.

### 3. Withdrawal from non-Catholics

Bunsen reported that Overbeck, after a period of intimacy, withdrew from companions of other confessions. Other evidence shows durable friendship and work across confessional lines, including Cornelius, Bunsen, Protestant patrons, and an Anglican commission. The extent of systematic exclusion is therefore **disputed and probably varied over time**.

### 4. Exclusionary program of *The Triumph of Religion*

Critics argued that the painting’s hierarchy privileged Catholics and excluded Protestant contemporaries, particularly Julius Schnorr von Carolsfeld, while including Overbeck, Cornelius, and Veit. The selection is documented; the motive is interpretive. Atkinson accused Overbeck of religious intolerance, but no surviving statement consulted here explicitly says Schnorr was omitted solely because he was Protestant.

### 5. Rejection of nature and technical weakness

Major criticisms included:

- weak or chalky color;
- wooden bodies and drapery;
- insufficient material differentiation;
- dependence on quotations from old masters;
- sentimental or monotonous expression;
- doctrinal illustration replacing pictorial invention;
- lack of direct observation in later work.

These are aesthetic judgments, not factual scandals. Evidence from Overbeck’s letters supports increasing distrust of models, while his early drawings demonstrate strong anatomical and observational training.

### 6. Papal political propaganda

*Christ Escaping from the Jews* was a visual allegory defending Pius IX and spiritual authority against the revolutionary or national state. Its removal or covering at the Quirinal after political change shows its perceived political charge.

It is properly described as **documented papal propaganda or political-religious allegory**, not covert activity.

### 7. Family estate and disinheritance

Atkinson claimed that the Hoffmann household inherited Overbeck’s property and that blood relatives received nothing.

**Status:** reported allegation or family account requiring confirmation from will and probate records. No evidence found of litigation, fraud, undue influence, or a court challenge.

### 8. Academy expulsion

Some sources say Overbeck was expelled; others say he withdrew. The institutional restructuring of 1810 appears to have removed or excluded the dissident students while they consciously rejected the Academy. “Expelled” is defensible but should not imply disciplinary misconduct beyond artistic rebellion.

### 9. Misattributions and market questions

Overbeck’s highly reproducible linear style generated many copies, engravings, workshop variants, and later works “after Overbeck.” Modern authentication questions occur in the art market. No evidence found connects Overbeck himself with forgery or fraudulent attribution.

### Adversarial-search conclusion

No substantiated criminal, sexual, financial, or legal scandal emerged. The major negative material concerns:

- Catholic conversion and proselytism;
- alleged artistic sectarianism;
- political support for papal temporal authority;
- anti-naturalism and technical decline;
- possible family disinheritance;
- later attribution problems.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### The literal “monk-artist”

**Story:** Overbeck and the Nazarenes abandoned ordinary life and became monks at San Isidoro.

**Origin and circulation:** Contemporary visitors, Romantic writers, later biographies, and images of praying or archaically dressed artists.

**Evidence:** They lived communally in an abandoned monastery and adopted ascetic routines, but they did not constitute a religious order or take collective vows.

**Effect:** The image made Overbeck a prototype of the self-sacrificing Christian artist and also exposed him to ridicule as theatrical or affected.

### “Saint Overbeck”

**Story:** Overbeck was personally saintly, almost incapable of perceiving evil.

**Circulation:** Friends, Catholic admirers, pupils, and sympathetic biographies. Pforr and Alfons were similarly memorialized in sanctified language.

**Evidence:** Numerous sources attest gentleness, devotion, generosity, chastity of manner, and disciplined work. Claims of sanctity are hagiographic evaluations, not verifiable biographical facts.

**Effect:** His personality became inseparable from judgments of his art. Admirers treated moral character as proof of artistic truth; critics saw cultivated piety as mannerism.

### The pope preventing him from becoming a monk

**Story:** Overbeck intended to enter a monastery, but the pope told him that art was his true vocation.

**Evidence:** Repeated in nineteenth-century biographies; no underlying papal letter was located in the consulted sources.

**Status:** plausible reported tradition, not fully documented.

**Effect:** It cast his painting as a divinely or papally sanctioned substitute for monastic vows.

### “The Hanseatic Raphael”

**Story or symbolic title:** Overbeck was Lübeck’s or northern Germany’s Raphael.

**Origin:** Nineteenth-century admiration and later exhibition culture, prominently the 1989 Lübeck retrospective.

**Evidence:** It expresses his Raphael cult, draftsmanship, and leadership, not an objective ranking.

**Effect:** It supported civic commemoration while inviting criticism that his fame exceeded his painterly achievement.

### Founder of an entirely new Christian art

**Claim:** Overbeck single-handedly revived Christian painting.

**Evidence:** He was a crucial founder and organizer, but the movement depended on Pforr, Cornelius, Schadow, Veit, Schnorr, Führich, patrons, engravers, and earlier Romantic writers. Fresco revival was collective.

**Status:** hagiographic simplification.

### Destroyer of German national art

**Claim:** By submitting art to Rome, Overbeck diverted a promising German movement into ultramontane imitation.

**Circulation:** Protestant, liberal, nationalist, and anti-clerical criticism, especially Pecht.

**Evidence:** Overbeck did privilege Roman Catholic authority and remained in Rome, but he retained German identity, served German patrons, and influenced German institutions. The claim is polemical interpretation.

### Heroic Academy martyr

**Story:** A spiritually pure young genius was expelled solely because a decadent Academy feared truth.

**Evidence:** Real conflict and institutional exclusion occurred. Yet Overbeck received extensive academic training, and the Academy’s actions arose in a more complex reorganization.

**Effect:** The story furnished later secessionist movements with an origin myth.

### The Nazarenes as hypocritical ascetics

Pecht compared their demeanor to people who “drink wine secretly and preach water publicly,” implying affected humility or hypocrisy. He offered no evidence of hidden vice and separately affirmed Overbeck’s sincerity. The accusation is **hostile rhetoric**, not documented conduct.

## Death and Burial

Overbeck’s final illness began shortly after his autumn 1869 return from Rocca di Papa. A severe chest cold appeared to respond to treatment, followed by sudden systemic collapse. He continued attempting to draw near death.

He died on 12 November 1869 in Rome, aged eighty. His funeral at San Bernardo alle Terme drew members of the German artistic colony. The service included music or psalm settings by Franz Liszt. Carl Hoffmann created the epitaph, completed around 1871.

No autopsy, medical certificate, or more precise pathological cause was found in the consulted sources.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Overbeck’s posthumous standing has moved through several phases.

### Late nineteenth century

Catholic admirers treated him as the exemplary Christian artist. His engravings and devotional cycles circulated widely. Church painters in Germany, Austria, France, Italy, Britain, and Central Europe adopted his types and compositional methods.

Critics simultaneously attacked:

- weak color;
- derivative composition;
- sentimentality;
- bodily thinness;
- priestly dogmatism;
- subordination of painting to theology.

### Early and mid-twentieth century decline

Modernism and formalist criticism generally disfavored Nazarene art. Overbeck’s historical importance remained acknowledged, but his paintings were often treated as academic, literary, or devotional artifacts rather than autonomous masterpieces.

His Lübeck *Entry into Jerusalem* was destroyed in 1942, a major loss. Other frescoes were detached, relocated, obscured, or difficult to access.

### Rehabilitation since the 1960s

Keith Andrews and later scholars restored the Nazarenes to histories of Romanticism, avant-garde brotherhoods, fresco revival, artistic identity, and institutional secession. Major exhibitions in Frankfurt, Rome, Munich, and Lübeck reassessed Overbeck’s drawings, literary sources, networks, and politics.

Recent scholarship tends to interpret him not simply as a failed imitator of the Renaissance but as:

- an organizer of one of the earliest modern avant-garde brotherhoods;
- a creator of a deliberately antimodern artistic identity;
- a pioneer in the modern revival of fresco;
- an important mediator between German Romanticism and Catholic revival;
- an influence upon European religious art and, indirectly, British Pre-Raphaelitism;
- an example of how modern art could be built through selective historical reconstruction.

The critical questions remain the same ones visible during his lifetime: whether the subordination of art to doctrine gave his work unity and force, or narrowed its human and pictorial range.

### Memorials

His name survives in:

- the Overbeck-Gesellschaft in Lübeck;
- the Overbeck Prize for Visual Art;
- streets in Lübeck, Cologne, Dresden, Vienna, Berlin, and elsewhere;
- his grave and epitaph at San Bernardo;
- prominent holdings in Lübeck, Frankfurt, Munich, Berlin, Rome, and Cologne.

## Atlas Connections

The following entries record only relationships supported by the consulted sources. Biblical, legendary, or earlier artists represented or imitated by Overbeck are not counted as personal connections.

### Franz Pforr — **Documented fact**

Overbeck met Pforr at the Vienna Academy and formed a close friendship by 1808. They co-founded the Lukasbund in Vienna on 10 July 1809, traveled to Rome together in 1810, and lived in the San Isidoro community. Their paired artistic projects—Pforr’s *Sulamith and Maria* and Overbeck’s *Italia und Germania*—articulated a shared program of friendship and German-Italian artistic reconciliation. Pforr’s death at Albano on 16 June 1812 profoundly affected Overbeck.

### Peter von Cornelius — **Documented fact**

Cornelius joined the Roman Nazarene circle in 1811. He and Overbeck became intimate lifelong friends, drew reciprocal portraits, collaborated on the Casa Bartholdy and Casino Massimo frescoes, and traveled together to Munich in 1831. Their artistic and confessional emphases diverged without ending the friendship. Cornelius died in 1867.

### Johann Heinrich Wilhelm Tischbein — **Documented fact**

Christian Adolph Overbeck arranged contact between his son and Tischbein in Hamburg in 1806. Tischbein gave the young artist guidance or encouragement before the move to Vienna. The encounter belongs to Overbeck’s formal transition from Lübeck training to academy study.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe — **Reported fact; indirect intellectual conflict**

No personal meeting or correspondence between Goethe and Overbeck was established in the consulted material. Goethe knew of the Nazarenes and reacted critically to aspects of their archaizing and religious program. Pecht later grouped Goethe with Niebuhr among those angered by the movement’s Roman-Catholic direction. This is an intellectual and reputational intersection, not a documented personal relationship.

### Dante Gabriel Rossetti — **Documented movement-level influence; no personal meeting established**

Rossetti’s British Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood was founded in 1848 partly against academic convention and within a European environment already shaped by the Nazarenes. Histories of the Pre-Raphaelites identify the Nazarene brotherhood as an important precedent. No meeting, correspondence, or direct Overbeck-to-Rossetti instruction was found. The connection is therefore indirect and institutional rather than personal.

### Sir John Everett Millais — **Documented movement-level influence; no personal meeting established**

Millais co-founded the British Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood, whose brotherhood structure and medievalizing revolt had a recognized Nazarene precedent. No personal contact with Overbeck was established.

### William Holman Hunt — **Documented movement-level influence; no personal meeting established**

Hunt was another founder of the British Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood. The historical connection is through the Nazarene precedent and broader Christian-art revival, not documented correspondence or a meeting.

### Sir Edward Coley Burne-Jones — **Reported indirect influence**

Burne-Jones belonged to the later British medievalist and Pre-Raphaelite tradition. Overbeck’s influence reached Britain through Nazarene reproductions, Catholic/Gothic revival networks, and Pugin. No direct relationship or meeting was found.

### John Ruskin — **Reported indirect intellectual connection**

Ruskin defended the British Pre-Raphaelites and promoted moral seriousness and close study of nature. These concerns overlap with early Nazarene principles, but no meeting or correspondence with Overbeck was established. The connection is contextual rather than personal.

### William Blake — **No documented direct connection found**

Both artists joined religious vision and image-making, and later scholarship may compare their spiritual art, but no evidence of meeting, correspondence, or acknowledged influence by Overbeck was found.

### Jesus of Nazareth, Mary Magdalene, Paul the Apostle, Francis of Assisi, Dante Alighieri, Michelangelo, Raphael-related roster figures, and other historical or sacred subjects — **Not biographical connections**

Overbeck represented, interpreted, or drew upon many religious and historical figures, but artistic depiction or retrospective influence is not a life crossing under the atlas criteria.

### Overall atlas finding

The strong roster intersections are Franz Pforr, Peter von Cornelius, and Johann Heinrich Wilhelm Tischbein. Goethe belongs as a documented contemporary critic at the level of reception. The British Pre-Raphaelite figures have a substantiated movement-level lineage but no demonstrated personal contact. No other roster connections were established strongly enough to record without inference.
