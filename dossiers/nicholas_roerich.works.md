## Works by Nicholas Konstantinovich Roerich

### Paintings, drawings, and graphic obra

- **1885–1947:** *Painted and graphic oeuvre*. A body of several thousand temperas, oils, studies, and drawings rather than a single readily enumerable work; no universally complete catalogue raisonné exists, and titles, dates, series assignments, and attributions vary among collections. The Nicholas Roerich Museum’s searchable catalogues document 210 paintings and separate groups of drawings, while historical exhibition catalogues and other museums preserve additional works; this entry therefore names the corpus rather than pretending that one institutional catalogue is complete. [Museum painting catalogue](https://www.roerich.org/museum-paintings-catalogue.php); [drawing catalogue](https://www.roerich.org/museum-drawings.php); [historical catalogues](https://www.roerich.org/roerich-museum-archive/catalogues.php).
- **1897:** *Messenger: Tribe Has Risen against Tribe*. Roerich’s graduation painting and an early historical canvas, acquired for the Imperial Russian Museum; it helped establish the archaeologically informed vision of ancient Rus that recurs throughout his painting.
- **1897–1916:** *Ancient Rus and prehistoric-Slavic paintings*. A sustained, porous body of historical pictures—among them *The Elders Converge*, *Idols*, *Building the Boats*, *Guests from Overseas*, *The Ominous Ones*, and *Slavs on the Dnieper*—rather than a consistently fixed series.
- **1903–1904:** *Architectural Studies*. A cycle produced during journeys through old Russian towns, recording churches, monasteries, fortresses, and other threatened monuments; it is simultaneously painting, architectural record, and an early cultural-preservation project.
- **1905:** *Sketches for Northern Life*. Two architectural-frieze designs survive in the Nicholas Roerich Museum; they belong to Roerich’s broader production of murals, mosaics, and decorative schemes and should not be mistaken for autonomous easel paintings alone. [Museum study](https://www.roerich.org/roerich-paintings-about/northern-life/northern-life-700232-700233.php).
- **1907–1910:** *Battle in Heaven*. A monumental decorative painting commissioned for the dining room of the Bazhanov house in Saint Petersburg; it is architectural decoration as much as a picture.
- **1912:** *The Last Angel*. An apocalyptic painting anticipating war and social catastrophe; versions and related compositions complicate treatment of the title as one wholly isolated object.
- **1914:** *The Doomed City*. An allegorical painting made on the eve of the First World War.
- **1914:** *The Cry of the Serpent*. An apocalyptic landscape-painting belonging to the same wartime visionary constellation as *The Last Angel* and *The Doomed City*.
- **1914:** *The Conflagration*. A prophetic or allegorical painting rather than a documentary war scene.
- **1914:** *Human Works*. A painting contrasting human construction with the scale of nature.
- **1917–1918:** *Karelia and Finland drawings and paintings*. A landscape corpus made during Roerich’s enforced residence in Finland; the drawings form a documented group, while the boundaries of the corresponding painted series are not fixed. [Museum drawing catalogue](https://www.roerich.org/museum-drawings.php).
- **1917–1918:** *Heroica*. A cycle of visionary northern landscapes and legendary subjects; later publications also use *Heroica* as the title of a textual collection, so the painted and literary objects must not be conflated.
- **1919:** *Dreams of Wisdom*. A cycle of paintings or panels shown in Scandinavia; its extent and surviving membership are not consistently reported.
- **1921:** *Arizona drawings and paintings*. A journey-made group of Southwestern American landscapes, documented as a distinct drawing corpus but not a closed, author-certified series.
- **1922:** *Monhegan drawings and paintings*. A group made on Monhegan Island, Maine; like the Arizona material, it is best understood as a place-bound corpus.
- **1922:** *And We Are Trying*. A religious-symbolic painting in which monastic labor becomes an image of collective spiritual work.
- **1923:** *Sancta*. A series of paintings presenting Russian saints and monastic life; translated titles and the precise membership of the cycle differ between catalogues.
- **1923:** *New Mexico*. A group of landscapes made during Roerich’s American travels; “series” is useful descriptively but does not identify a single bounded artifact.
- **1924–1925:** *Himalayan and Ladakh drawings*. Field drawings made during the Asian expedition, functioning simultaneously as travel records, compositional studies, and autonomous works. [Museum drawing catalogue](https://www.roerich.org/museum-drawings.php).
- **1924–1947:** *Master of the Mountains*. The museum’s retrospective designation for Roerich’s extensive Himalayan landscape production, not necessarily a single series title fixed by the artist. [Museum selected works](https://www.roerich.org/roerich-paintings-selected.php).
- **1925:** *Maitreya*. A Central Asian series concerning the expected Buddha and the advent of a new era; its individual pictures combine expedition observation, prophecy, and sacred geography.
- **1925:** *His Country*. A cycle associated with the Himalayan realm of the spiritual teacher or Mahatma; it resists separation into straightforward landscape and religious icon.
- **1925:** *Banners of the East*. A series devoted to spiritual teachers and culture-bearing figures from several traditions; catalogues differ in the number and membership of its panels.
- **1925–1928:** *Central Asian expedition paintings*. Hundreds of landscapes, monuments, encampments, sacred sites, and visionary scenes made during or from the trans-Himalayan expedition. This is a journey-corpus rather than one securely bounded series.
- **1926:** *Remember*. A Himalayan painting coupling landscape with an admonitory human figure; it belongs to Roerich’s recurrent fusion of image and ethical message.
- **1927:** *Drops of Life*. A Himalayan landscape whose title makes the natural phenomenon emblematic rather than merely topographical.
- **1928–1947:** *Kullu and Himalayan paintings*. The dominant late corpus, made from Roerich’s Naggar base and on subsequent journeys; repeated mountains, passes, monasteries, fires, riders, saints, and prophetic signs often exist in related versions.
- **1929–1932:** *Shambhala*. A group of paintings, essays, and expeditionary ideas rather than a purely pictorial series: a small Gesamtkunstwerk of sacred geography, political hope, travel narrative, and image.
- **1931:** *Madonna Laboris*. A religious painting based on the apocryphal image of the Virgin aiding suffering souls; also catalogued within Roerich’s broader group of female sacred figures.
- **1931:** *Mother of the World*. One of Roerich’s several treatments of the veiled cosmic feminine; title and motif recur, so “the work” is not always one unique object.
- **1931:** *The Great Mother of the Banner of Peace*. A painting joining the maternal sacred figure to Roerich’s cultural-protection emblem.
- **1932:** *Saint Sergius*. A devotional-historical painting of Sergius of Radonezh; Roerich returned to the saint in more than one composition.
- **1932:** *Fiat Rex*. A symbolic painting associated with spiritual sovereignty; its Latin title functions almost as a proclamation.
- **1933:** *Lao Tse*. A painting of the sage traveling through a mountain landscape, part portrait, part sacred-geographical vision.
- **1933:** *Milarepa, the One Who Harkened*. A painted image of the Tibetan poet-yogi listening amid the mountains.
- **1934–1935:** *Manchurian expedition paintings*. A journey-bound corpus of landscapes, plants, peoples, monasteries, and sacred sites; it overlaps with Roerich’s botanical, ethnographic, political, and spiritual projects and is not a closed formal series.
- **1936:** *Gesar*. A painting invoking the Central Asian epic hero; Roerich made multiple Gesar-related compositions.
- **1936:** *Song of Shambhala*. A symbolic Himalayan painting, distinct from the textual discussions of Shambhala bearing similar language.
- **1936:** *From Beyond*. A visionary landscape in which apparition and mountain cannot be separated into ordinary figure painting and scenery.
- **1937:** *Arjuna*. A painting of the *Mahabharata* hero, joining Indian epic to Himalayan landscape.
- **1938:** *Kanchenjunga*. One of numerous treatments of the mountain; the recurring title names a motif and a succession of works rather than one definitive canvas.
- **1938:** *Command of Rigden Jyepo*. A prophetic painting centered on the ruler of Shambhala.
- **1939:** *Sophia—Wisdom of God*. A religious-symbolic painting drawing on the Russian Sophiological image of divine wisdom.
- **1939:** *Most Sacred Treasure of the Mountain*. A Himalayan sacred-landscape painting.
- **1940:** *Tibet*. A late mountain and architectural subject; Roerich used “Tibet” and related place names for more than one work.
- **1942:** *Alexander Nevsky*. A wartime historical painting that transforms a medieval prince into an emblem of resistance.
- **1942:** *Partisans*. A wartime painting linking contemporary Soviet resistance with Roerich’s heroic-historical imagery.
- **1943:** *Glory of the Himalayas*. A late landscape in which observed mountains become a devotional manifestation.
- **1943:** *Victory*. A wartime allegory, not a record of a particular battle.
- **1944:** *Remembering the Past*. A late historical-symbolic painting.
- **1945:** *Himalayas*. One of many works bearing this generic title; without collection and catalogue number, the title alone does not identify a unique object.
- **1947:** *The Master’s Command*. A late visionary painting, sometimes treated as one of Roerich’s final works.
- **1947:** *Guga Chohan*. A late painting associated with the North Indian folk hero and saint.
- **1947:** *Treasure of the Mountain*. A late Himalayan work; related titles and motifs make catalogue identity important.

### Monumental and architectural decoration

- **1906–1907:** *Church of the Intercession of the Virgin, Parkhomovka*. Roerich designed mosaics and religious imagery for the church built by architect Vladimir Pokrovsky; the realized building is collaborative architecture, not a work attributable to Roerich alone.
- **1907–1910:** *Bazhanov House decorative scheme*. Roerich supplied monumental panels and decorative designs for the Saint Petersburg interior, including *Battle in Heaven*; the surviving ensemble is a partial Gesamtkunstwerk involving architect Pavel Alyoshin and numerous craftsmen.
- **1908–1914:** *Church of the Holy Spirit, Talashkino*. Roerich designed the façade mosaic and the interior mural commonly called *Queen of Heaven at the River of Life*. The building was Maria Tenisheva’s project and involved other makers; Roerich’s portion survives incompletely and should not be equated with the whole church.
- **1913–1915:** *Gunzechoinei Buddhist Temple windows and decoration*. Roerich designed stained glass and decorative elements for the Saint Petersburg datsan; execution belonged to a larger collaborative architectural project.
- **1897–1916:** *Murals, mosaics, friezes, stained glass, and decorative designs for Russian buildings*. A dispersed and only partly preserved corpus. Museum documentation explicitly warns that Roerich produced numerous such works, but no complete surviving catalogue makes an item-by-item claim responsible. [Museum study](https://www.roerich.org/roerich-paintings-about/northern-life/northern-life-700232-700233.php).

### Theatre as Gesamtkunstwerk

- **1907:** *The Three Magi*. Roerich’s first documented stage-design project, for a medieval religious drama; designs rather than a complete performance-object are the durable obra.
- **1907–1908:** *Princess Maleine*. Sets and costumes for Maurice Maeterlinck’s drama at the Theatre of Ancient Drama; a collaborative theatrical manifestation.
- **1908–1912:** *The Snow Maiden*. Several distinct scenic and costume projects for Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera and Alexander Ostrovsky’s play, made for productions in Saint Petersburg and later elsewhere. At least four design campaigns are reported, so the title must not be collapsed into one work. [Russian Museum exhibition](https://rusmuseumvrm.ru/online_resources/art_gallery/rerih/index.php?lang=en&mp5=2&mpage=2&p=0&ps=20&show=asc&t=829); [design history](https://macdougallauction.com/catalogue/view?id=5075).
- **1909:** *Prince Igor / Polovtsian Dances*. Sets and costumes for Alexander Borodin’s opera and its danced episode in Diaghilev’s Paris season; a major collaborative Gesamtkunstwerk involving composer, performers, choreographers, workshops, and impresario. [National Gallery of Australia](https://nga.gov.au/exhibitions/diaghilevs-designers/).
- **1909:** *Ivan the Terrible / The Maid of Pskov*. Scenic designs for Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera; sources sometimes identify the production by the opera and sometimes by the ruler who dominates it.
- **1910:** *Khovanshchina*. Designs for Modest Mussorgsky’s opera; later London plans were not fully realized, so surviving designs may represent both performance and unrealized project.
- **1911–1912:** *Peer Gynt*. Sets and costumes for Henrik Ibsen’s drama at the Moscow Art Theatre; a theatrical environment rather than an illustrated text. [Russian Museum exhibition](https://rusmuseumvrm.ru/online_resources/art_gallery/rerih/index.php?lang=en&mp5=2&mpage=2&p=0&ps=20&show=asc&t=829).
- **1912:** *Tristan and Isolde*. Scenic-design project for Richard Wagner’s opera; its production history is less secure than that of *Prince Igor* or *The Rite of Spring*.
- **1913:** *The Rite of Spring*. Roerich devised the scenario with Igor Stravinsky and designed sets and costumes for Vaslav Nijinsky’s choreography. It is paradigmatically collaborative and resists attribution as Roerich’s work alone, while his visual and ethnographic conception was fundamental. [Cambridge research survey](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/dance-research-journal/article/abs/nicholas-roerich-and-his-theatrical-designs-a-research-survey/F0788F90210A33DFA631E97D6E5564DB); [National Gallery of Australia](https://nga.gov.au/exhibitions/diaghilevs-designers/).
- **1913–1914:** *Sister Beatrice*. Sets and costumes for Alexei Davidov’s opera after Maeterlinck, staged at the Petrograd Musical Drama Theatre in 1915. [Russian Museum exhibition](https://rusmuseumvrm.ru/online_resources/art_gallery/rerih/index.php?lang=en&mp5=2&mpage=2&p=0&ps=20&show=asc&t=829).
- **1919–1920:** *The Tale of Tsar Saltan*. Designs for a projected London production of Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera; the production was prevented by Thomas Beecham’s financial collapse, making the surviving designs an unrealized project rather than remnants of a performed whole. [Sotheby’s research essay](https://www.sothebys.com/en/articles/nikolai-roerichs-set-design-for-the-celebrated-russian-opera-sadko).
- **1919–1920:** *Sadko*. Four or more designs for a projected London staging of Rimsky-Korsakov’s opera; Beecham’s bankruptcy prevented realization and the designs were dispersed. Titles assigned to individual sheets vary, including *Sadko’s Palace*, *Sadko’s Chamber*, and *Chamber in Sadko’s Palace*. [Sotheby’s research essay](https://www.sothebys.com/en/articles/nikolai-roerichs-set-design-for-the-celebrated-russian-opera-sadko).
- **1919–1920:** *Khovanshchina, London project*. A second, unrealized design campaign for Mussorgsky’s opera, distinct from Roerich’s earlier production work.
- **1920:** *The Rite of Spring, Massine revival project*. New or revised designs prepared for a projected Léonide Massine staging; it should not be silently merged with the 1913 Nijinsky production.
- **1921:** *The Snow Maiden, Chicago production*. Sets and costumes for the Chicago Opera Company, a new realization of a subject Roerich had designed before.
- **1921–1922:** *Stage décor corpus in the United States*. Designs for operatic and theatrical revivals and proposed productions; museum catalogues preserve individual sheets whose performed, revised, or unrealized status is not always certain. [Museum stage-design drawings](https://www.roerich.org/museum-drawings.php).
- **1944:** *Prince Igor, late designs*. Roerich returned to Borodin’s opera in a late design project; its relationship to an actual production is less clearly documented than the 1909 designs.
- **1907–1947:** *Theatrical designs*. The full body of scenic and costume drawings cannot responsibly be reduced to the famous productions above: museum and scholarly sources describe work extending across four decades, including realized, revised, and abandoned schemes. [Museum selected works](https://www.roerich.org/roerich-paintings-selected.php); [research survey](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/dance-research-journal/article/abs/nicholas-roerich-and-his-theatrical-designs-a-research-survey/F0788F90210A33DFA631E97D6E5564DB).

### Archaeological and journey projects

- **1894–1900:** *Excavations in the Saint Petersburg, Novgorod, Pskov, Tver, and related regions*. Archaeological field projects yielding excavated objects, drawings, records, and scholarly reports. The “made thing” is partly a body of knowledge and partly a displaced material assemblage, so it resists treatment as a conventional authored work.
- **1899:** *Some Antiquities of the Shelon Pyatina and Bezhetsk Region*. An illustrated archaeological study.
- **1900:** *The Archaeological Institute’s Excursion of 1899 in Connection with the Question of Finnish Burials in Saint Petersburg Province*. A field report recording a collective expedition; Roerich authored the publication but not the journey’s entire scholarly production.
- **1903:** *Some Antiquities of the Dereva and Bezhetsk Regions*. An archaeological monograph or report.
- **1903–1904:** *Journey through ancient Russian towns*. A cultural-survey project undertaken with Helena Roerich, generating the *Architectural Studies* paintings, photographs, notes, collecting, and preservation advocacy. It is closer to a multimedia atlas than to one artwork.
- **1904:** *In Olden Times*. An illustrated archaeological-historical publication.
- **1905:** *The Stone Age on Lake Piros*. An archaeological study based on field research.
- **1923–1928:** *Central Asian Expedition*. An expeditionary project through India, Sikkim, Ladakh, Chinese Turkestan, the Soviet Union, Mongolia, and Tibet, producing paintings, drawings, collections, observations, writings, diplomacy, and contested political activity. Its route, purposes, and political interpretation remain disputed; it is not solely a scientific expedition or solely an artwork.
- **1928:** *Urusvati Himalayan Research Institute*. A research institution founded by the Roerich family at Naggar to coordinate botanical, ethnographic, linguistic, medical, archaeological, and other work. It is a collective institutional project, not Nicholas Roerich’s sole creation.
- **1934–1935:** *Manchurian Expedition*. A collecting and research journey formally connected with the United States Department of Agriculture, especially the search for drought-resistant grasses, but also entangled with Roerich’s cultural and political ambitions. Its scientific administration and Roerich’s conduct became subjects of controversy.

### Poems, tales, and literary cycles

- **Before 1914:** *Early stories, poems, legends, parables, art criticism, and archaeological articles*. A dispersed periodical corpus later gathered selectively in books; no consulted bibliography establishes an uncontested complete list of every early contribution.
- **1914:** *Collected Works, Book I*. Roerich’s first substantial authorial collection, assembling essays, stories, legends, and other prose; “Book I” promised a continuation but did not become a completed lifetime collected edition.
- **1916:** *Tales and Parables*. A literary collection; some contents overlap with writings collected elsewhere.
- **1919:** *Violators of Art*. An English-language pamphlet or essay-publication advocating the defense of art.
- **1921:** *Flowers of Morya*. A collection of blank-verse poems, later also known in English as *Flame in Chalice*. The alternate title reflects translation and republication, not necessarily a wholly separate work.
- **1922:** *Adamant*. A collection of essays, addresses, legends, and reflections rather than a continuous treatise.
- **1924:** *Ways of Blessing*. A prose collection combining cultural essays, spiritual reflections, reminiscence, and exhortation.
- **1927:** *Altai–Himalaya: Thoughts on Horseback and in the Tent*. A diary-like account of the Central Asian expedition; it is travel writing, spiritual geography, and selective record rather than a neutral expedition log.
- **1929:** *Heart of Asia*. A synthetic travel-philosophical book interpreting Asian history, landscape, prophecy, and contemporary politics.
- **1930:** *Flame in Chalice*. The English publication of poems associated with *Flowers of Morya*; it is an English manifestation of the poetic corpus rather than securely an independent new collection.
- **1930:** *Shambhala*. A collection of essays, legends, travel observations, and spiritual-political reflection; it forms part of the wider Shambhala project across image, journey, and institution.
- **1931:** *Realm of Light*. A collection of essays and “sayings of eternity.”
- **1931:** *The Power of Light*. A Russian-language essay collection, published as *Derzhava Sveta*; it overlaps conceptually, but not simply bibliographically, with *Realm of Light*.
- **1931:** *To Women*. A published address associated with the opening of a women’s organization in Riga; it is an occasional manifesto-like text rather than a book-length argument.
- **1932–1933:** *Fiery Stronghold*. A collection of essays and addresses; Russian bibliographies give the Paris edition as 1932 or 1933, so the date should remain qualified.
- **1934:** *Banner of Peace*. A collection or pamphlet centered on Roerich’s cultural-preservation campaign; it should be distinguished from the physical banner, emblem, movement, and international treaty.
- **1934:** *Holy Watch*. A collection of essays presenting cultural guardianship as an ethical duty.
- **1936:** *Gateway into the Future*. A collection of cultural, educational, and spiritual essays.
- **1936:** *The Invincible*. A collection published in Russian as *Nerushimoe*; the title names moral or cultural indestructibility rather than a narrative subject.
- **1937:** *Roerich Essays: One Hundred Essays*. A two-volume English collection; many constituent essays had separate periodical lives, so the collection and the individual texts are different bibliographic objects.
- **1946:** *Beautiful Unity*. A collection of essays advocating synthesis among cultures and creative disciplines.
- **1946:** *Himavat: Diary Leaves*. A collection of short reflective essays Roerich termed “diary leaves”; these are shaped literary texts, not simply private diary transcription.
- **1947:** *Himalayas—Abode of Light*. A late collection of Himalayan essays.
- **1934–1947; published posthumously 1995–1996:** *Diary Leaves*. A large body of dated or occasion-based essays, later issued in three Russian volumes for 1934–1935, 1936–1941, and 1942–1947. The posthumous editorial corpus is not identical to a diary manuscript or to the later English selection bearing the same title.
- **Various dates; collected posthumously:** *Heroica*. A later English collection of Roerich’s heroic, historical, and cultural essays; it should not be confused with the 1917–1918 painting cycle.
- **Various dates; collected posthumously:** *Vigil: Letters from Asia*. An editorial collection of letters and public communications, not a single lifetime-authored book project.
- **Various dates; collected posthumously:** *Diary Leaves*. An English editorial selection from Roerich’s short essays; its 2021 publication date belongs to the edition, not to the original texts.
- **1898–1947:** *Articles, addresses, appeals, letters, and “diary leaves”*. The Nicholas Roerich Museum archive identifies hundreds of individual publications, often with variant English titles, translations, and reprints. Because the archive itself distinguishes articles from reprints and remains an archival bibliography rather than a critical complete-works edition, asserting a closed one-bullet-per-text canon would manufacture completeness. [Museum writings archive](https://www.roerich.org/roerich-museum-archive/nicholas-roerich-writings.php).

### Manifestos, protocols, symbols, and movements

- **1920:** *Cor Ardens*. An international artists’ fraternity conceived by Roerich in New York. It is an institution-as-project and collective manifestation, not a sole-authored object.
- **1921:** *Master Institute of United Arts*. An interdisciplinary New York school founded under Roerich’s direction, deliberately refusing the separation of painting, music, theatre, architecture, dance, and education; institutionally, it approaches a Gesamtkunstwerk.
- **1921:** *Corona Mundi*. An international art center or association created to exhibit and connect artists across national boundaries; its realization depended on collaborators and patrons.
- **1923:** *Roerich Museum, New York*. A museum organized around Roerich’s art and cultural program. Roerich supplied the originating vision and works, but the institution was collectively founded, financed, administered, and later reorganized.
- **1920s:** *Agni Yoga / Living Ethics*. A philosophical-textual corpus associated with Nicholas and Helena Roerich and presented as teachings received from spiritual Masters. Helena Roerich was the principal recorder and editor; Nicholas’s precise authorship is inseparable from collaborative and claimed revelatory origins, so the books should not be silently listed as works by him.
- **1928–1929:** *Draft international pact for the protection of cultural property*. A protocol originated by Roerich and legally drafted with Georges Chklaver and other jurists. Its operative purpose was to establish neutrality for cultural, scientific, educational, and artistic institutions; the formal language is collaborative rather than Roerich’s unaided composition. [1947 documentary history](https://nrm.s3.amazonaws.com/website/archive/05_Roerich_Pact_and_Banner_of_Peace/1947%20-%20The%20Roerich%20Pact%20and%20Banner%20of%20Peace.pdf).
- **1929–1935:** *Roerich Pact movement*. A transnational campaign of committees, conferences, appeals, exhibitions, and diplomacy. It is a performative cultural project whose object was the production of international law, not merely a text.
- **1929–1935:** *Banner of Peace*. A white banner bearing three red discs within a red circle, proposed as the protective emblem of the pact. Roerich selected and interpreted an ancient, widely distributed sign rather than claiming to invent its basic geometry; the modern emblem is simultaneously graphic design, legal signal, sacred symbol, and manifesto. [Nicholas Roerich Museum](https://www.roerich.org/roerich-pact.php).
- **1931:** *World League of Culture*. An international cultural-cooperation project promoted by Roerich and allied organizations; it remained more a federative intention and network than a stable world institution.
- **1935:** *Treaty on the Protection of Artistic and Scientific Institutions and Historic Monuments (Roerich Pact)*. A binding international protocol signed by twenty-one American states. Roerich originated and campaigned for it, but states, diplomats, and jurists made the final treaty; it therefore resists classification as his sole-authored work. [Nicholas Roerich Museum](https://www.roerich.org/roerich-pact.php).
- **1942:** *American-Russian Cultural Association*. A wartime organization fostering cultural relations between the United States and the Soviet Union. Roerich participated in its creation and served as honorary president, but it was a collective institutional manifestation rather than his individual obra.
