# Charlemagne: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Charlemagne’s reign is unusually well documented for the eighth and early ninth centuries, but the evidence is uneven. Royal annals, capitularies, charters, letters, coins, liturgical books, and papal records illuminate government and war; childhood, private motives, and intimate relationships are much less secure.

This dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact:** directly attested by contemporary documentary, material, or independent narrative evidence.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a near-contemporary source but not independently demonstrable.
- **Disputed reconstruction:** a modern conclusion drawn from incomplete or contradictory evidence.
- **Allegation or hostile report:** an accusation preserved in partisan evidence without a decisive finding.
- **Rumor or myth:** a later narrative unsupported by contemporary evidence, whether literary, devotional, political, or folkloric.

The principal narrative sources have identifiable perspectives:

- The *Annales regni Francorum* (“Royal Frankish Annals”) are court-associated, year-by-year records whose presentation favors the Carolingian monarchy.
- Einhard’s *Vita Karoli Magni*, written after Charlemagne’s death, probably in the 820s or early 830s, is an admiring literary biography modeled partly on Suetonius. Einhard knew the court and the adult emperor but was not a witness to Charlemagne’s childhood.
- The *Liber pontificalis* represents papal institutional memory.
- Notker of St Gall’s *Gesta Karoli Magni*, written in the later ninth century, preserves anecdotes, moral tales, and already-developed legend rather than straightforward eyewitness history.
- Numerous capitularies survive only through later manuscript copies; none survives as a securely identified original instrument. The Cologne *Capitularia* project and Monumenta Germaniae Historica provide the principal modern research infrastructure for this corpus. ([MGH Digital](https://mgh.bbaw.de/de/mgh-digital); [Capitularia resources](https://capitularia.uni-koeln.de/en/resources/))

## Basic Identifying Information

**Principal name:** Charles.

**Contemporary Latin forms:** *Carolus*, *Karolus*; in formal documents, after 800, variants of *Karolus serenissimus augustus, a Deo coronatus, magnus pacificus imperator Romanum gubernans imperium*.

**Later and vernacular names:**

- Charlemagne, from French *Charles le Grand*
- Charles the Great
- Latin *Carolus Magnus* or *Karolus Magnus*
- French *Charlemagne*
- German *Karl der Große*
- Italian *Carlo Magno*
- Spanish *Carlomagno*

“Charlemagne” was not a modern-style surname. The expression “Charles the Great” became the conventional means of distinguishing him from other Carolingian rulers named Charles.

**Dynasty:** Carolingian, a name applied retrospectively from Charles/Carolus; his family had earlier been called Pippinids or Arnulfings.

**Titles and offices:**

- King of the Franks, 768–814
- Sole king of the Franks after 4 December 771
- King of the Lombards, 774–814
- Patrician of the Romans, a title inherited or renewed within the Frankish-papal alliance
- Emperor, crowned at Rome on 25 December 800
- From 812, recognized as emperor by the Byzantine government, although not accepted as the sole “emperor of the Romans”

The familiar title “Holy Roman Emperor” is anachronistic when used as Charlemagne’s exact contemporary office. He was an emperor ruling a Christian Roman empire in the West; the settled expression “Holy Roman Empire” belongs to later centuries.

**Birth:** Uncertain. The principal modern alternatives are:

- 2 April 742, calculated from Einhard’s statement that he died in his seventy-second year;
- 2 April 747;
- 2 April 748, often favored because some annalistic evidence places his birth in the year following the birth of his sister Gisela.

The exact year cannot be established. Even the date 2 April depends on later calendrical testimony rather than a surviving birth record.

**Birthplace:** Unknown. Herstal, Aachen, Ingelheim, Quierzy, and other royal centers have been proposed, but no contemporary source identifies a birthplace.

**Death:** 28 January 814, approximately 9 a.m., Aachen.

**Reported cause:** Fever accompanied by severe pain in the side, identified by Einhard as pleurisy. “Pneumonia” is a possible modern interpretation, not a documented clinical diagnosis.

**Burial:** Palatine chapel at Aachen, now Aachen Cathedral.

**Physical description:** Einhard describes him as large, strong, broad-bodied, with a prominent head, large lively eyes, a somewhat long nose, gray or white hair, a short thick neck, and a projecting belly. Einhard says his height was seven times the length of his own foot, an internally proportioned description rather than a modern measurement. A twentieth-/twenty-first-century examination of the attributed skeletal remains estimated an unusually tall, slender man, broadly compatible with the tradition, but the identification and completeness of the assemblage require caution. ([Einhard, full translation](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/einhard1.asp); [study of the attributed remains](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1570677X18301825))

## Family and Ancestry

### Dynastic background

Charlemagne belonged to the Austrasian aristocratic family formed through the alliance and intermarriage of the Pippinid and Arnulfing houses. Its power base lay especially in the Meuse, Moselle, and lower Rhine regions. The family accumulated landed, ecclesiastical, military, and mayoral offices before replacing the Merovingian dynasty.

### Grandparents and earlier ancestry

**Paternal grandfather:** Charles Martel, effective ruler of the Frankish kingdoms as mayor of the palace, though never king.

**Paternal grandmother:** Rotrude, whose precise parentage remains disputed.

The lineage traditionally ran backward through Pepin of Herstal, Ansegisel, Begga, Arnulf of Metz, and Pepin of Landen. Some individual links are well supported; increasingly elaborate pedigrees connecting Charlemagne to late Roman senators, Trojan heroes, biblical figures, or later royal houses are genealogical constructions rather than demonstrable ancestry.

### Parents

**Father:** Pepin III, “Pepin the Short” (*Pippinus rex*), born circa 714, king of the Franks from 751, died 24 September 768.

Pepin and his brother Carloman inherited Charles Martel’s political position in 741. Pepin later displaced the last Merovingian king, Childeric III, with papal approval and was consecrated king. Pope Stephen II anointed Pepin, his wife, and their sons in 754, giving the new dynasty sacral legitimacy.

**Mother:** Bertrada or Bertha, commonly called Bertrada of Laon and later “Bertha Broadfoot.” She was the daughter of Count Charibert of Laon and Gisela. Her marriage to Pepin was initially regarded by some churchmen as canonically problematic because of kinship, but it remained intact.

### Siblings

- **Carloman**, born 751, joint king with Charlemagne in 768, died suddenly on 4 December 771.
- **Gisela**, born 757, became abbess of Chelles and participated in Carolingian literary and religious culture.
- Several other supposed siblings appear in genealogies, but their existence or identity is less secure.

### Partners, wives, and concubines

Early medieval terminology does not map cleanly onto modern categories of “wife” and “mistress.” Narrative sources distinguish some unions, but canonical status is debated.

1. **Himiltrude**, relationship by the late 760s or circa 770. Pope Stephen III referred to an existing Frankish union when opposing a projected Lombard marriage, but Paul the Deacon later described Pepin the Hunchback as born before legal marriage. Whether Himiltrude was a lawful wife, a noncanonical spouse, or a concubine remains disputed.

2. **The unnamed daughter of Desiderius**, king of the Lombards, married circa 770 and repudiated in 771. She is conventionally called “Desiderata,” but that may result from misreading *desideratam* (“the desired one”) as a personal name. The name “Gerperga” also appears through confusion with Carloman’s wife. Even the exact characterization of the union has been reexamined because the closest sources are inconsistent. ([study of the disputed Lombard marriage](https://iris.univr.it/handle/11562/1010180))

3. **Hildegard**, from an Alemannic noble family, married in 771; died 30 April 783 after childbirth. She was Charlemagne’s most consequential queen and the mother of most of his surviving dynastic children.

4. **Fastrada**, married in 783; died in 794. Einhard says her cruelty contributed to two conspiracies against Charlemagne. This is a reported interpretation, possibly reflecting later court hostility; direct evidence establishing her responsibility is lacking.

5. **Liutgard**, Alemannic, married circa 794; died 4 June 800. Einhard praised her character and reported that she was childless.

Later partners or concubines named in the sources include **Madelgard**, **Gerswind**, **Regina**, and **Adallind**. The chronology and status of these unions are not always recoverable.

### Children

Charlemagne had at least eighteen children, possibly more.

**By Himiltrude:**

- **Pepin the Hunchback**, born circa 769/770; implicated in a conspiracy in 792; tonsured and confined at Prüm Abbey; died 811.
- **Amaudru**, reported in some reconstructions but less securely attested.

**By Hildegard:**

- **Charles the Younger**, circa 772/773–811; assigned territories and military commands; crowned or anointed king by Leo III in Rome in 800, but predeceased his father.
- **Adalhaid**, born 773/774 during the Italian campaign; died in infancy.
- **Rotrude**, circa 775–810; betrothed to the Byzantine emperor Constantine VI, but the marriage never occurred; later associated with Count Rorgon and mother of Louis, later abbot of Saint-Denis.
- **Carloman**, 777–810; renamed Pepin at his baptism by Pope Hadrian I in 781 and made king of Italy.
- **Louis**, 778–840, born at Chasseneuil; made king of Aquitaine in 781; succeeded Charlemagne as emperor, later called Louis the Pious.
- **Lothair**, Louis’s twin, died in infancy.
- **Bertrada/Bertha**, circa 779/780–after 823; partner of Angilbert, court poet and lay abbot of Saint-Riquier; mother of Hartnid and the historian Nithard.
- **Gisela**, circa 781–possibly 808.
- **Hildegard**, 782–783.

**By Fastrada:**

- **Theodrada**, later abbess of Argenteuil.
- **Hiltrude**, whose later life is uncertain.

**By Madelgard:**

- **Rotildis**, later abbess of Faremoutiers.

**By Gerswind:**

- **Adaltrude**.

**By Regina:**

- **Drogo**, 801–855, later bishop of Metz and an important figure under Louis the Pious.
- **Hugh**, circa 802–844, later abbot of several monasteries and archchancellor.

**By Adallind:**

- **Theodoric**, born 807.

Charlemagne kept his daughters at court and did not arrange completed dynastic marriages for them, though Rotrude’s Byzantine betrothal was negotiated. Einhard criticized the resulting informal relationships but said Charlemagne reacted to scandals as though no suspicion existed. Modern explanations—that he feared powerful sons-in-law or wished to retain familial control—are plausible reconstructions, not explicitly documented policy.

## Childhood and Early Life

No contemporary narrative gives a reliable childhood biography. Einhard explicitly avoided the subject, saying that no one then living could provide trustworthy information.

Charlemagne grew up within an itinerant royal and aristocratic household rather than at a single permanent capital. Likely formative environments included royal estates and palaces in Austrasia, Neustria, and the Rhineland. He probably observed:

- his father’s struggle to consolidate control after Charles Martel;
- Pepin’s deposition of Childeric III in 751;
- the new dynasty’s alliance with the papacy;
- the papal anointing at Saint-Denis in 754;
- campaigning in Aquitaine and Italy;
- the integration of religious consecration with Frankish kingship.

**Documented fact:** At the 754 anointing, Pope Stephen II consecrated Pepin and his sons, making the boys participants in the dynasty’s new sacral status.

**Reported fact:** Charlemagne may have joined ceremonial missions while young, including receiving Pope Stephen, but detailed reconstructions depend on sparse annalistic notices.

## Education and Formation

Charlemagne was not educated like a later university-trained ruler. His formation combined aristocratic household instruction, warfare, hunting, religious observance, oral counsel, and later court scholarship.

Einhard reports that he:

- spoke his native language fluently;
- learned Latin well enough to speak it almost as readily;
- understood Greek better than he could speak it;
- studied grammar with Peter of Pisa;
- studied rhetoric, dialectic, and especially astronomy with Alcuin of York;
- listened to readings during meals, particularly histories and Augustine’s *City of God*;
- attempted to learn writing late in life, keeping tablets under his pillow, but made limited progress because he began too late.

The statement that he “could not write” is therefore oversimplified. He may not have mastered fluent scribal handwriting, but he functioned in a multilingual documentary government, understood written culture, corrected texts through learned advisers, and may have authenticated documents with a monogram. His ability to read silently or independently cannot be determined with certainty.

### Teachers and intellectual associates

- **Peter of Pisa:** grammar.
- **Alcuin of York:** rhetoric, dialectic, biblical interpretation, moral and political counsel.
- **Paul the Deacon:** Lombard historian, grammarian, poet, and court intellectual.
- **Theodulf of Orléans:** bishop, poet, biblical scholar, and administrator.
- **Einhard:** courtier, administrator, architecturally knowledgeable adviser, and later biographer.
- **Pauline of Aquileia:** theologian and ecclesiastical reformer.
- **Hildebold of Cologne:** royal chaplain and archbishop.
- **Angilbert:** court poet, diplomat, abbot, and partner of Charlemagne’s daughter Bertha.

Court members used learned nicknames drawn from biblical and classical culture. Charlemagne was called **David**, presenting him as a warrior, psalmic, and sacral king. Alcuin was “Flaccus”; Angilbert “Homer.”

## Early Career and Accession, 754–771

### Anointing and dynastic apprenticeship

In 754 Charlemagne and Carloman were anointed with Pepin by Pope Stephen II. Their father’s campaigns, especially the prolonged conquest of Aquitaine, provided a practical apprenticeship in seasonal warfare and aristocratic mobilization.

### Partition of 768

Pepin died on 24 September 768. Frankish practice divided the kingdom between the sons:

- Charlemagne received a crescent-shaped set of territories generally including Neustria, western Aquitaine, and northern Austrasian lands.
- Carloman received a more compact central realm including Burgundy, Alemannia, and parts of southern Austrasia.

Both were crowned separately on 9 October 768, Charlemagne at Noyon and Carloman at Soissons.

The arrangement produced immediate strategic friction because each brother’s lands constrained the other.

### Aquitanian revolt, 769

Charlemagne campaigned against the Aquitanian rebel Hunald. Carloman declined to assist him. Charlemagne suppressed the revolt and induced Duke Lupus of Gascony to surrender Hunald.

### Lombard marriage and repudiation

Bertrada promoted an alliance with King Desiderius of the Lombards. Charlemagne married Desiderius’s daughter despite papal opposition to Frankish-Lombard intermarriage. He repudiated her in 771 and married Hildegard. The motives are uncertain: dynastic realignment, personal incompatibility, infertility, and altered political calculations have all been proposed.

### Death of Carloman

Carloman died unexpectedly on 4 December 771. His widow, Gerberga, fled with their sons to Desiderius’s court. Carloman’s leading men recognized Charlemagne rather than the young nephews. Charlemagne thereby became sole king.

No evidence establishes that Charlemagne caused his brother’s death. Later suspicion belongs to retrospective political speculation.

## Chronological Life History

## Consolidation and the Lombard conquest, 772–776

### First Saxon campaign, 772

Charlemagne invaded Saxony, captured the Eresburg, and destroyed the Irminsul, a major Saxon cult object or sanctuary. The action combined frontier war, punitive expedition, seizure of treasure, and religious aggression.

### War against Desiderius, 773–774

Pope Hadrian I sought Frankish aid against Lombard pressure. Charlemagne crossed the Alps in 773, using routes through Mont Cenis and possibly the Great St Bernard. He besieged Pavia while another force contained Verona.

At Easter 774 he visited Rome, renewed Frankish promises to the papacy, and received exceptional ceremonial honors. Pavia surrendered in June. Desiderius was deposed and sent into monastic confinement; his son Adelchis escaped to Byzantine territory.

Charlemagne assumed the unprecedented combined title **king of the Franks and Lombards**. Rather than annexing Lombardy as an undifferentiated province, he preserved much of its law, aristocracy, and administrative structure under his kingship.

The ultimate fate of Carloman’s widow and children after their capture is not recorded. Claims that Charlemagne killed them are unsupported; claims that they were merely confined are plausible but also unproven.

### Revolts and consolidation

Duke Hrodgaud of Friuli rebelled in 776 and was killed. Charlemagne redistributed offices and installed Frankish or loyal Lombard personnel while retaining cooperative local elites.

## Saxon Wars, 772–804

The “Saxon Wars” were not a continuous modern-style war but a succession of invasions, rebellions, submissions, assemblies, baptisms, hostage-taking, deportations, fort construction, and renewed revolts over more than thirty years.

### Principal phases

- **772:** destruction of Irminsul.
- **774–776:** Saxon attacks followed by Frankish countercampaigns and fortress construction.
- **777:** assembly at Paderborn; many Saxon leaders submitted, but Widukind remained absent in Denmark.
- **778:** renewed Saxon uprising during Charlemagne’s Spanish campaign.
- **779–780:** campaigns to the Weser and Elbe; mass submissions and baptisms.
- **782:** Frankish defeat at the Süntel, followed by the executions at Verden.
- **783:** major battles at Detmold and on the Hase.
- **785:** Widukind submitted and was baptized at Attigny, with Charlemagne as sponsor.
- **792–799:** renewed revolts, military operations, forced transfers, and resettlement.
- **804:** final large deportation reported by Frankish sources; Saxon territory incorporated more securely.

### Verden, 782

The Royal Frankish Annals report that, after a Saxon force destroyed a Frankish army at the Süntel, Charlemagne demanded the surrender of participants and caused **4,500** Saxons to be killed at Verden in one day.

**Documented core:** A mass execution at Verden is reported by the court-associated annals themselves and is not merely a later hostile invention.

**Unresolved details:**

- The figure 4,500 cannot be independently verified.
- Whether the victims were prisoners of war, surrendered insurgents accused of treason, or a broader group is uncertain.
- A once-popular argument that the Latin verb was corrupted and really meant “deported” has not displaced the manuscript reading “beheaded” or “put to death.”
- Later nationalist, confessional, Nazi, and anti-Christian narratives greatly expanded the event’s symbolism, but that political use does not erase the underlying annalistic report.

### Compulsory conversion and Saxon law

The *Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae*, conventionally dated around 785, prescribed death for a range of offenses: attacking churches, killing clergy, certain pagan rites, refusing baptism, conspiring with pagans, and disloyalty. It required church tithes and treated Christianization as part of political obedience. ([Latin manuscript/project record](https://capitularia.uni-koeln.de/en/capit/pre814/bk-nr-026/); [English translation](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/source/carol-saxony.asp))

The later *Capitulare Saxonicum* of 797 used negotiated fines and greater accommodation. This suggests a movement from exceptional terror legislation toward integration, though coercion and aristocratic domination remained.

### Deportations

Einhard and the annals report the transfer of Saxon populations into other parts of the empire and the settlement of Franks in their place. The often-repeated number “10,000” may refer to households or people and cannot be converted confidently into a modern population statistic.

## Spain and Roncesvalles, 777–778

At Paderborn in 777 Charlemagne received Muslim dissidents from al-Andalus, including Sulayman al-Arabi, who offered cooperation against the Umayyad emir Abd al-Rahman I.

In 778 Charlemagne led forces across the Pyrenees:

- one army moved through the eastern passes;
- Charlemagne moved through the west toward Pamplona;
- Zaragoza did not open its gates as expected;
- the expedition withdrew without its principal objective;
- Pamplona’s fortifications were destroyed on the return.

On 15 August 778, Basque forces ambushed the Frankish rearguard at Roncesvalles. Einhard names among the dead Eggihard, Anselm, and **Hruodland/Roland**, prefect of the Breton March.

This was a strategic failure: Zaragoza was not taken, the promised coalition did not function as expected, and the rear guard was annihilated. Later Christian-Muslim epic converted the Basque ambush into a vast battle against “Saracens.”

Frankish power subsequently expanded south through the Spanish March. Girona entered the Frankish sphere in 785; Barcelona was captured by Louis of Aquitaine’s forces in 801. Charlemagne himself did not create modern Catalonia, but Carolingian frontier structures contributed to its medieval political formation.

## Italy, Bavaria, Benevento, and Byzantium, 776–797

### Italy

Charlemagne repeatedly crossed the Alps, confirmed his son Pepin as king in Italy, and used counts, bishops, monasteries, and royal assemblies to govern the Lombard kingdom.

### Bavaria and Tassilo III

Duke Tassilo III of Bavaria, Charlemagne’s cousin by marriage and an old vassal of Pepin, maintained a semi-independent principality. Under escalating pressure:

- Tassilo renewed submission in 787;
- in 788 a Frankish assembly accused him of disloyalty, including the old offense of deserting Pepin’s army in 763;
- he was condemned to death, then tonsured and confined in a monastery;
- his family was also placed in religious houses;
- Bavaria was absorbed into Charlemagne’s political system.

The proceeding had juridical forms but occurred under overwhelming royal control. It is best described as a politically managed trial rather than an independent adjudication.

### Benevento

Duke Arichis II of Benevento submitted in 787 and gave hostages, but Frankish control in southern Italy remained incomplete. His successor Grimoald maintained considerable autonomy.

### Byzantium

Relations alternated among diplomacy, marriage negotiations, rivalry, and war.

Rotrude was betrothed to Constantine VI, son of Empress Irene, but the engagement was broken. After 800, Byzantine governments initially treated Charlemagne’s imperial title as an illegitimate western claim. Warfare in the Adriatic and Venice ended in accommodation. In 812 Emperor Michael I recognized Charlemagne as **basileus** or emperor, while withholding the exclusive Roman formulation claimed at Constantinople.

---

## Avar Wars and Eastern Frontiers, 788–803

The Avar Khaganate in the middle Danube region had accumulated substantial movable wealth through tribute and warfare.

- Avar attacks or interventions occurred in 788.
- Charlemagne led a major campaign in 791 along the Danube.
- Internal Avar conflict weakened the khaganate.
- In 795–796, Duke Eric of Friuli and King Pepin of Italy captured the Avar central stronghold conventionally called the “Ring.”
- Enormous treasure was transported west and distributed among the royal household, churches, nobles, and foreign allies.

Einhard regarded the Avar war as exceptionally lucrative and said the Franks, previously poor by comparison, became rich. This is rhetorical but reflects an unusually large seizure and redistribution of elite treasure.

The Avar polity fragmented under military defeat, internal war, Bulgar pressure, and political reorganization. Claims that Charlemagne alone “exterminated” the Avars compress a more complex process.

Eastern marches were organized to manage Slavic, Avar, and other frontier populations. Frankish relations with Obodrites, Wilzi, Sorbs, Bohemians, and Danes combined tribute, alliance, war, and support for client rulers.

## Religious and Intellectual Program, 780s–814

### Reform legislation

The **Admonitio generalis** of 23 March 789 presented kingship as responsibility for the religious and moral correction of the Christian people. It addressed clerical discipline, preaching, liturgy, schools, texts, monastic conduct, and lay behavior.

Its school provision instructed monasteries and bishoprics to teach psalms, notation, chant, computus, and grammar, and to ensure accurate copying of sacred books. It did not create a modern universal public-school system, nor did Charlemagne “invent school.” It expanded and regularized ecclesiastical education for religious and governmental purposes. ([Italian translation of the school provision](https://www.rm.unina.it/didattica/fonti/frova/sez1/par2_stampa.htm); [oldest surviving manuscript description](https://diglib.hab.de/?catalog=Westphal&db=mss&id=496a-helmst&list=ms))

Other major texts include:

- *Epistola de litteris colendis*, on learning and correct language;
- *Capitulare missorum generale* of 802;
- *De villis*, regulating royal estates, though its precise date and geographical application remain debated;
- *Divisio regnorum* of 806, planning succession;
- the Aachen council materials of 809 concerning the Holy Spirit’s procession.

### Court scholarship and the “Carolingian Renaissance”

The court recruited scholars from England, Ireland, Italy, Spain, and Frankish territories. Activities included:

- correction and copying of biblical and liturgical texts;
- improvement of Latin grammar and orthography;
- production of theological, historical, poetic, legal, and educational works;
- standardization and diffusion of Carolingian minuscule;
- development of libraries and scriptoria;
- instruction of clerics and members of the royal household;
- compilation and circulation of law and capitularies.

Charlemagne did not personally invent Carolingian minuscule, author all court reforms, or single-handedly preserve classical literature. The results came from networks of scribes, bishops, abbots, teachers, aristocrats, and royal agents operating with political sponsorship.

### Theology and images

Charlemagne’s court produced the *Libri Carolini* or *Opus Caroli regis contra synodum*, criticizing the court’s understanding of the Byzantine Second Council of Nicaea’s teaching on images. The work was composed principally by Theodulf or another court theologian, not personally written by Charlemagne, despite being issued in the king’s voice.

Charlemagne opposed Spanish Adoptionism, supported councils addressing the controversy, and intervened in the dispute over the **Filioque**. He accepted the theology that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father “and the Son,” while Pope Leo III reportedly resisted changing the Greek-derived creed’s text. This episode illustrates both cooperation and limits within Frankish-papal relations.

### Conversion and coercion

Christianization under Charlemagne operated through preaching, patronage, baptism, church construction, elite alliance, law, tribute, hostage-taking, deportation, and capital punishment. Saxony provides the clearest documented case of forced conversion being joined to conquest.

## Government, Institutions, and Political Economy

## Kingship and itinerant rule

Charlemagne had no capital in the modern bureaucratic sense. He traveled among palaces, monasteries, assemblies, hunting grounds, and campaign bases, including:

- Aachen
- Herstal
- Quierzy
- Attigny
- Worms
- Frankfurt
- Paderborn
- Ingelheim
- Thionville
- Nijmegen
- Compiègne
- Regensburg
- numerous Italian centers, especially Pavia, Ravenna, and Rome

From the 790s Aachen became his favored residence, partly because of its thermal springs, strategic location, family heartland, and palace complex.

## Assemblies and aristocracy

Major decisions were announced and negotiated at assemblies attended by bishops, abbots, counts, royal relatives, military leaders, and other magnates. Royal power depended upon aristocratic cooperation. Capitularies should not be mistaken for statutes automatically enforced by a centralized state; their application varied locally.

## Counts and missi dominici

Counts exercised judicial, military, and fiscal responsibilities. Royal envoys, the **missi dominici**, traveled in circuits to hear complaints, supervise officials, publish instructions, administer oaths, and report abuses. The 802 program regularized their ideological role, often pairing an ecclesiastic with a secular aristocrat.

The system provided inspection and communication but did not eliminate local domination or corruption. Repeated admonitions against bribery, unjust tolls, abuse of the poor, false judgment, and appropriation of royal rights indicate persistent problems.

## Law

Different peoples continued to live under distinct legal traditions—Salic, Ripuarian, Lombard, Bavarian, Saxon, Roman, and others—supplemented by royal capitularies. Charlemagne ordered legal texts corrected or recorded, but did not impose one uniform imperial code.

## Oaths

After the imperial coronation, especially in 802, free men were required to swear loyalty directly to Charlemagne. The oath joined political fidelity with Christian moral obligations.

## Coinage

A late eighth-century monetary reform based the system on the silver pound, divided notionally into 20 shillings and 240 denarii. Only the silver denarius was routinely struck. Royal control of mints and standardized weight strengthened fiscal and ideological authority, though barter, payment in kind, local exchange, and noncoin wealth remained central.

## Royal estates and property

The ruler’s resources came from:

- extensive dynastic estates;
- rents and renders in produce, animals, textiles, tools, and labor;
- judicial fines;
- tolls and market rights;
- tribute;
- ecclesiastical and aristocratic service;
- confiscations;
- booty, especially Avar treasure.

The *Capitulare de villis* gives detailed instructions concerning estate managers, livestock, tools, workshops, food production, gardens, accounting, and delivery to the court. It shows intensive managerial aspirations but should not be read as a complete balance sheet.

No reliable modern net-worth figure can be calculated. Charlemagne’s “wealth” consisted primarily of rights over land, people, offices, tribute, stored treasure, and service rather than liquid private capital.

## Church property and patronage

The dynasty granted immunity, land, treasure, and legal privileges to churches and monasteries, while also drawing clergy into royal government and demanding military or material service from ecclesiastical institutions. “Church” and “state” were not autonomous modern sectors.

## Aachen complex

The Aachen palace included an audience hall, residences, administrative spaces, and the palatine chapel, constructed under Odo of Metz with Italian and late antique models. Materials and columns were brought from Rome and Ravenna with papal permission. The chapel provided a spatial statement of Christian Roman kingship.

## Diplomacy, Travels, and Foreign Relations

### Papacy

Charlemagne’s relations with Hadrian I and Leo III mixed protection, negotiation, ceremonial kinship, territorial bargaining, and competing claims over Rome and Italy.

Hadrian and Charlemagne exchanged letters and gifts over decades. After Hadrian’s death in 795, Charlemagne sent an elaborate epitaph to Rome.

### Pope Leo III and the events of 799–800

In April 799 Roman enemies attacked Leo III, attempting to blind him and cut out his tongue according to papal accounts. He escaped and traveled to Charlemagne at Paderborn. Accusations of perjury and sexual misconduct circulated against Leo, but surviving evidence is partisan and no independent tribunal established the allegations.

Charlemagne entered Rome in November 800. On 23 December Leo swore an oath clearing himself; the process avoided formally placing the pope under lay judgment.

### Imperial coronation, 25 December 800

At Christmas Mass in St Peter’s, Leo placed a crown on Charlemagne’s head and the congregation acclaimed him emperor. Contemporary and near-contemporary sources differ in emphasis:

- papal narrative stresses Leo’s act;
- Frankish annals frame imperial elevation as collective recognition;
- the Lorsch annals connect it to Roman authority and the perceived vacancy created by Irene’s rule in Constantinople;
- Einhard says Charlemagne would not have entered the church had he known Leo intended to crown him.

Einhard’s claim of surprise is **reported fact**, not established interior knowledge. Prior negotiation is highly probable, but whether Charlemagne objected to coronation itself, papal precedence, ritual sequence, or diplomatic consequences remains disputed. The coronation sources continue to generate competing interpretations. ([study of the four major narratives](https://pims.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/MS82-149.pdf); [Oxford study of coronation context](https://academic.oup.com/book/46073/chapter-abstract/404570498))

### Abbasid caliphate

Charlemagne exchanged embassies and gifts with the Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid. The most famous gift was the elephant **Abul-Abbas**, brought west by the Jewish envoy Isaac and delivered in 802. The elephant accompanied the court and died in 810, reportedly near the Rhine during a Danish campaign.

Later claims of a formal Frankish-Abbasid alliance against both Byzantium and the Umayyads are plausible geopolitical interpretations but exceed the explicit surviving evidence. Claims that Harun legally transferred sovereignty over Jerusalem to Charlemagne are later exaggerations. Frankish sources do indicate privileges and interests concerning Christian holy places.

### British Isles

Charlemagne corresponded with or received communications from rulers such as Offa of Mercia. A dispute temporarily disrupted trade and travelers between their realms; negotiation restored relations. Alcuin’s migration from York to the Frankish court was one of the reign’s most important intellectual transfers.

### Denmark

Relations with King Godfred were hostile. Godfred attacked Frankish-aligned Obodrites and threatened the northern frontier. Charlemagne organized defenses and campaigns. Godfred was killed by one of his own men in 810 before a major confrontation occurred.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Bertrada

Charlemagne’s mother participated actively in dynastic diplomacy, including the Lombard alliance. Einhard reports that Charlemagne treated her respectfully but that the Lombard marriage temporarily caused tension.

### Carloman

Brother, co-ruler, and political rival. Their failure to coordinate in Aquitaine and competing alliances threatened civil conflict. Carloman’s sudden death prevented an open succession war.

### Desiderius

Initially father-in-law and ally, then enemy. Charlemagne repudiated his daughter, invaded his kingdom, deposed him, and assumed the Lombard crown.

### Hadrian I

Long-term papal partner and correspondent. Their interests overlapped but did not always coincide over Italian territories, Benevento, and the practical fulfillment of Frankish donations.

### Leo III

Dependent upon Charlemagne’s protection after the 799 attack, yet the pope’s coronation of Charlemagne became a foundation for later papal claims about imperial legitimacy.

### Widukind

Leader or principal symbol of Saxon resistance. After years of conflict he submitted and was baptized at Attigny in 785, reportedly with Charlemagne as sponsor. His later career is obscure.

### Tassilo III

Cousin, vassal, rival territorial prince, and eventually deposed duke. His fall demonstrates Charlemagne’s capacity to use assemblies, oaths, historical accusations, and monastic confinement to eliminate a competing dynasty.

### Alcuin

Teacher, adviser, correspondent, biblical scholar, and sometimes critic. Alcuin urged moderation in conversion, warned against burdensome tithes, and framed kingship as Christian moral service. He did not simply dictate policy, and Charlemagne did not always follow his counsel.

### Einhard

Courtier and later biographer. Charlemagne entrusted him with responsibilities, including carrying the 806 succession plan to Rome. His biography shaped nearly all later images of the emperor’s body, habits, education, family grief, and death.

### Angilbert and Bertha

Angilbert was a diplomat, court poet, and lay abbot, as well as Charlemagne’s daughter Bertha’s partner. Their sons included Nithard. The relationship shows that Charlemagne tolerated unions for his daughters that he did not regularize through dynastic marriage.

### Harun al-Rashid

Distant diplomatic counterpart rather than a personally documented friend. Embassies, gifts, and shared interests connected their courts; no meeting occurred.

## Appearance, Habits, Health, and Private Life

Einhard’s portrait is detailed but shaped by classical biography.

### Clothing

Charlemagne usually wore Frankish dress:

- linen shirt and drawers;
- tunic;
- cross-gartered hose or leg bands;
- fur vest in winter;
- blue cloak;
- sword.

He disliked foreign costume, though he wore Roman clothing in Rome at papal request. On major festivals he used jeweled dress, gold buckles, and a diadem.

### Food and drink

Einhard says he was moderate in wine and disliked drunkenness. He enjoyed roasted meat and resisted physicians who urged him to substitute boiled food. Four courses were customary at meals, apart from the roast brought by hunters.

### Exercise and bathing

He rode, hunted, and swam. Aachen’s thermal baths were a major attraction. Einhard says he sometimes bathed with sons, nobles, friends, and guards, occasionally numbering more than one hundred participants.

### Religion

He attended worship regularly, supported church music and reading, gave alms, and cultivated Roman liturgical connections. His sexual relationships and use of violent conversion did not prevent contemporaries at his court from presenting him as a sincere Christian ruler.

### Health

Einhard reports generally strong health until late life, followed by:

- recurrent fevers;
- gout or pain in the feet;
- lameness in later years;
- a fall from a horse;
- final fever and pleuritic pain.

There is no sound basis for retrospective psychiatric diagnosis, addiction, or a specific chronic disease beyond the reported symptoms.

### Family losses

Charlemagne outlived several close relatives:

- Hildegard, 783;
- Fastrada, 794;
- Liutgard, 800;
- Pope Hadrian I, 795;
- his sister Gisela, 810;
- daughter Rotrude, 810;
- son Pepin of Italy, 810;
- son Charles the Younger, 811;
- Pepin the Hunchback, 811.

Einhard reports that Charlemagne wept openly and was deeply affected, contradicting a purely impassive warrior image.

---

## Succession and Final Years, 806–814

### Divisio regnorum, 806

Charlemagne planned to divide the kingdoms among his legitimate sons:

- Charles;
- Pepin;
- Louis.

The arrangement regulated territories, mutual defense, churches, daughters, and family peace. Einhard carried it to Pope Leo III for confirmation.

### Deaths of heirs

Pepin of Italy died in 810 and Charles in 811, leaving Louis as the sole surviving legitimate adult son. Pepin’s son Bernard retained the Italian kingship.

### 813 ceremony at Aachen

In September 813 Charlemagne summoned Louis from Aquitaine. At Aachen he installed him as co-emperor and required him, according to Einhard, to take the imperial crown from the altar and place it on his own head. This presentation deemphasized papal agency. Bernard was confirmed as king in Italy.

### Testament and property division

Einhard preserves a detailed account of a property division or testament made several years before Charlemagne’s death. Treasure was divided principally among the metropolitan churches of the empire, with portions for children, grandchildren, palace servants, the poor, and the chapel.

Named possessions included gold, silver, precious objects, books, furnishings, and ceremonial vessels. Some objects were to be sold, with proceeds distributed as alms. This is the closest source to a personal estate plan, though it is not a modern probate inventory.

---

## Death and Burial

In January 814 Charlemagne developed a high fever at Aachen. He fasted, as was his customary response to fever, and consumed little beyond fluids. Pain in his side followed. He remained bedridden for seven days.

Einhard reports that he received communion and died on 28 January at approximately the third hour, usually interpreted as about 9 a.m.

His body was washed, prepared, and buried at Aachen on the same day or very shortly afterward. The burial inscription gave his title and death date. He was interred in the palatine chapel, but the exact original position and form of the grave remain disputed.

### Sitting burial story

Later narratives claim Charlemagne was found seated upright on a throne, crowned and holding a Gospel book or scepter when Otto III opened the tomb in 1000.

- **Status:** Rumor or myth built around a historical grave opening.
- **Evidence:** Thietmar of Merseburg, writing relatively near Otto III’s time, says the remains were found *in solio regio*, which can mean “in the royal throne” or royal seat, but later accounts became increasingly elaborate.
- **Historical effect:** The image presented Charlemagne as an eternally enthroned ruler and repository of imperial wisdom.
- **Caution:** Archaeology and the earliest report do not verify the fully developed seated-corpse tableau. ([study of Otto III and Aachen memory](https://www.regionalgeschichte.net/bibliothek/aufsaetze/goerich-erinnerung-aachen-karl-der-grosse-otto-iii.html))

## Controversies, Violence, Allegations, and Unresolved Questions

## Dynastic usurpation

Charlemagne inherited a kingship created when Pepin displaced Childeric III. Papal approval and Frankish acclamation legitimized the new dynasty within its own political-religious framework, but the transfer was also a successful seizure of royal office by the family already exercising effective power.

## Carloman’s heirs

**Documented fact:** Gerberga and her sons fled to the Lombard court; Charlemagne captured Desiderius’s kingdom.

**Unresolved question:** Their later fate. No reliable contemporary source records execution, natural death, or precise confinement.

## Repudiated partners

Charlemagne ended unions when political needs changed. The canonical nature of the Himiltrude relationship and Lombard marriage is disputed. Contemporary and later church writers did not apply a consistent modern distinction between marriage and concubinage.

## Pepin the Hunchback’s conspiracy, 792

A conspiracy involving Pepin the Hunchback and Frankish nobles was uncovered. Pepin was spared execution, tonsured, and sent to Prüm; several associates were executed or otherwise punished.

Einhard blamed resentment of Fastrada’s cruelty. This causal attribution is reported but unproven. Pepin’s exclusion from succession and ambiguous legitimacy provide an evident dynastic context.

## Hardrad conspiracy, 785/786

A group of eastern Frankish nobles conspired against Charlemagne. Punishments reportedly included death, blinding, confiscation, and exile. The sources’ sparse explanations make it difficult to reconstruct participants’ aims.

## Verden and Saxon repression

The mass killing of 782, capital legislation, forced baptisms, hostage-taking, destruction of sanctuaries, and deportations are recorded in Frankish sources. The legal or political categories assigned to victims do not make the violence legendary. Exact numbers and implementation remain uncertain.

## Tassilo’s trial

Tassilo’s condemnation used remembered desertion, broken oaths, and accusations of alliance with Avars. The death sentence was commuted to monastic confinement. His wife and children lost political freedom. The proceeding removed a rival dynasty and transferred Bavaria to Charlemagne.

## Pope Leo III’s accusers

Leo’s Roman opponents accused him of serious offenses before their 799 attack. The pope purged himself by oath in 800. The surviving process did not test the accusations by modern evidentiary standards. The charges therefore remain allegations, neither established facts nor decisively disproved.

## Wealth from conquest

Avar treasure was seized and redistributed. Lombard royal property, confiscated estates, tribute, and church-linked resources also underwrote government. No evidence supports modern accusations of hidden banking, money laundering, or private corporate structures; those concepts do not fit the surviving institutions.

## Sexual and family conduct

Einhard acknowledged multiple partners and children and criticized the household situation surrounding Charlemagne’s daughters. No evidence supports lurid later allegations of incest sometimes attached to medieval rulers. The documented issue is plural and sequential unions, informal partnerships, and the ruler’s refusal to marry daughters while tolerating their relationships.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

## “Father of Europe”

Charlemagne was called *pater Europae*—“father of Europe”—in Carolingian-era poetic praise. The phrase did not mean father of the modern European Union or a unified European nation-state.

Later use:

- medieval imperial ideology presented him as founder or restorer;
- French and German national traditions competed to claim him;
- post-1945 European institutions recast him as a symbol of continental integration;
- the annual Charlemagne Prize at Aachen uses his name for European political unity.

The historical empire covered much of western and central Europe but excluded Britain, most of Iberia, Scandinavia, much of eastern Europe, and the Byzantine world. It was a dynastic Christian empire, not a democratic federation.

## Inventor of schools

**Story:** Charlemagne founded schools or invented compulsory education.

**Origin and circulation:** Later schoolbooks and popular histories simplified the *Admonitio generalis* and Carolingian educational reform.

**Evidence:** Schools, monastic learning, and classical educational traditions long predated him. His government did encourage schools at monasteries and episcopal centers and demanded more accurate clerical learning.

**Effect:** The story made him a civilizing schoolmaster in French and European public memory.

## Flowering or white beard

French tradition calls him *l’empereur à la barbe fleurie* or depicts a magnificent white beard.

Einhard describes a round face and white hair but does not give the enormous flowing beard of later art. Medieval epic and iconography made the beard a sign of age, authority, and sacral kingship. Charlemagne was only in his mid-thirties, under many birth chronologies, at Roncesvalles, despite epic depictions as an ancient patriarch.

## Roland and Roncesvalles

**Historical core:** A Basque ambush destroyed the rear guard in 778; Roland was among the dead.

**Epic transformation:** The *Chanson de Roland*, composed in surviving form around the late eleventh century, turns the attackers into a vast Muslim army, Roland into Charlemagne’s heroic nephew, Ganelon into a traitor, and the campaign into sacred Christian warfare.

**Evidence against literal historicity:** Near-contemporary accounts identify Basques, not the immense Saracen host of the poem. The supernatural events, horn blast, angelic intervention, judicial duel, and scale of vengeance belong to epic.

**Effect:** The legend became foundational to the *Matter of France*, crusading culture, French literature, European chivalric romance, and Italian epics such as *Orlando innamorato* and *Orlando furioso*.

## The Twelve Peers or Paladins

Later literature surrounded Charlemagne with twelve peers—Roland, Oliver, Archbishop Turpin, and changing others—modeled partly on apostolic and heroic structures.

Some names derive from historical persons; the fixed fellowship and adventures are literary constructions. The word “paladin” ultimately became a generic term for a heroic champion.

## Journey to Jerusalem

Medieval texts claimed that Charlemagne personally journeyed to Jerusalem or Constantinople, obtained relics, and received custody of holy places.

Contemporary evidence documents embassies, gifts, and Frankish religious interests in Jerusalem, but no journey by Charlemagne to the Holy Land. The *Pèlerinage de Charlemagne* is literary fantasy.

## Secret marriage with a Byzantine empress

Sources report diplomatic discussion of marriage between Charlemagne and Empress Irene near the end of her reign, but Irene was deposed in 802. Whether a serious marriage project existed and how advanced it became remain disputed. Later narratives turn this into a near-unification of East and West; no marriage occurred.

## Canonization and sainthood

In 1165, at Frederick Barbarossa’s initiative, Antipope Paschal III canonized Charlemagne at Aachen.

- **Documented fact:** A cult and liturgical office followed, especially at Aachen and in parts of the empire.
- **Canonical qualification:** Because Paschal III was an antipope, the canonization did not acquire ordinary universal standing in the Roman Catholic Church.
- **Later status:** Local veneration as “Blessed Charlemagne” persisted; 28 January remains his local feast in some places.
- **Political effect:** The cult supported imperial sacrality and Frederick’s rivalry with the papacy.

## Proto-crusader

Medieval chansons and crusading writers projected eleventh- and twelfth-century holy-war concepts backward onto Charlemagne. His historical wars undeniably included religious coercion and claims of Christian expansion, but the mature crusading institutions and indulgence system postdated him.

## National ownership: French or German

Charlemagne lived before modern France and Germany. West Frankish and East Frankish successor traditions both claimed him. French monarchs appropriated Carolingian legitimacy; German emperors treated Aachen and Charlemagne’s relics as imperial foundations.

Calling him exclusively “French” or “German” imposes later national categories. His native speech was a continental West Germanic Frankish dialect; his written government operated mainly in Latin; his empire included multiple linguistic and legal populations.

## Charlemagne as founder of the Holy Roman Empire

The coronation of 800 restored an imperial office in western Europe and became fundamental to later imperial tradition. Nevertheless:

- the exact institutional term “Holy Roman Empire” is later;
- the Carolingian empire fractured after his descendants;
- Otto I’s coronation in 962 is another conventional starting point;
- the later empire was not a simple uninterrupted administrative continuation of Charlemagne’s realm.

## Genetic ancestor of Europeans

Genealogical modeling suggests that a prolific early medieval ruler with surviving descendants could be an ancestor of very many living Europeans. Charlemagne is frequently singled out because elite pedigrees are unusually traceable.

This does not mean:

- every claimed line is documented;
- his DNA has been identified in modern descendants;
- descent from him is rare or confers a unique biological status.

At the time depth involved, many contemporaries with surviving lines would be ancestors of enormous modern populations.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### From Frankish king to Christian imperial ruler

Charlemagne’s development can be reconstructed through changes in titulature, law, ritual, and advisers:

1. **Dynastic Frankish kingship:** inherited from Pepin, legitimated through anointing and aristocratic recognition.
2. **Multiregional kingship after 774:** rule over Franks and Lombards without erasing their separate laws.
3. **Corrective Christian monarchy:** ruler responsible for discipline, doctrine, worship, education, and salvation.
4. **Imperial kingship after 800:** expanded oath-taking, Roman language, court ceremonial, diplomatic competition with Byzantium, and intensified claims to supervise the Christian people.
5. **Succession-conscious final phase:** partition in 806, then concentration on Louis after the deaths of Charles and Pepin.

Charlemagne’s ideology was not written by him alone. It was negotiated and articulated by church councils, royal assemblies, bishops, abbots, scribes, poets, papal correspondents, and foreign courts.

## Successes and Failures

### Major successes

- Unified the Frankish kingdoms after Carloman’s death.
- Conquered the Lombard kingdom and retained its crown.
- Defeated or subordinated competing regional dynasties, notably Bavaria.
- Extended Frankish influence into Saxony, northern Spain, Italy, and the middle Danube.
- Built an unusually durable alliance between Carolingian rulership and the Latin Church.
- Regularized coinage, royal inspection, oath-taking, ecclesiastical discipline, and documentary governance.
- Sponsored a major expansion of book production, education, textual correction, and scholarship.
- Secured an imperial title and eventual Byzantine recognition.
- Arranged a peaceful immediate succession to Louis.

### Major failures or limited outcomes

- The 778 Spanish expedition failed in its primary objectives and ended with the Roncesvalles disaster.
- Saxony required decades of repeated campaigns; submissions repeatedly collapsed.
- Benevento and the northern Spanish frontier remained only partially controlled.
- Government depended heavily on magnates and local officeholders whom repeated capitularies accused of abuse.
- The three-son succession plan failed through premature deaths.
- The empire did not become a permanently indivisible state; civil war among Louis the Pious’s heirs and the 843 Treaty of Verdun partitioned it.
- Educational and moral legislation expressed aspirations whose implementation varied greatly.
- Relations with Byzantium never produced a unified Roman imperial order.

## Works by or Issued in the Name of Charlemagne

Charlemagne was not an author in the modern sense. Most surviving “works” are collective governmental or court productions issued under his authority, dictated in his name, framed as his speech, or attributed to his program.

### Legislative and administrative texts

Important items include:

- **Capitulary of Herstal** (779): ecclesiastical and governmental measures.
- **Admonitio generalis** (789): general religious, educational, and disciplinary program.
- **Epistola de litteris colendis** (circa 780s): letter urging cultivation of learning and correct expression; probably drafted by a court scholar.
- **Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae** (circa 785): exceptional coercive law for conquered Saxony.
- **Capitulare Saxonicum** (797): revised Saxon settlement emphasizing fines and negotiated integration.
- **Capitulare missorum generale** (802): imperial loyalty, justice, moral duties, and instructions to envoys.
- **Capitulare de villis vel curtis imperii** (date disputed, commonly late eighth or early ninth century): management of royal estates.
- **Divisio regnorum** (806): succession plan.
- **Capitulary concerning military service** (various texts, especially 802–811).
- **Aachen council and theological directives** (especially 809).

These survive in dispersed manuscript collections, usually not as autograph originals. Their authorship may include bishops, abbots, counts, notaries, and assembly participants. ([Corpus and bibliography](https://capitularia.uni-koeln.de/en/resources/); [MGH Digital](https://mgh.bbaw.de/de/mgh-digital))

### Charters and diplomas

Hundreds of royal acts are attributed to Charlemagne’s chancery:

- grants and confirmations of land;
- immunities and privileges for churches and monasteries;
- judicial decisions;
- fiscal concessions;
- appointments and protections;
- diplomas concerning frontier territories.

They were issued in his name but drafted and written by chancery personnel. Authenticity must be assessed instrument by instrument because later institutions forged or interpolated Carolingian privileges.

### Letters

Surviving or reconstructed correspondence includes:

- letters to Popes Hadrian I and Leo III;
- communications with bishops, abbots, rulers, and royal agents;
- letters associated with Alcuin, Angilbert, and other court figures;
- instructions to envoys;
- diplomatic exchanges with foreign courts.

Most are secretarial or formulaic productions, and some survive only because recipients or later compilers copied them.

### Testament/property division

Einhard transmits the substance of Charlemagne’s division of treasure and movable property. This is a dictated legal-administrative disposition, not an autograph memoir.

### *Libri Carolini* / *Opus Caroli regis contra synodum*

A court theological response to the Second Council of Nicaea, composed circa 790–793.

- **Published under royal authority:** yes.
- **Personal authorship by Charlemagne:** unlikely.
- **Probable principal author:** often identified as Theodulf of Orléans, though scholarly debate remains.
- **Purpose:** to state the Frankish court’s position on religious images, partly on the basis of a problematic Latin translation of the Greek council.

### *Disputatio de rhetorica et de virtutibus*

A dialogue between Alcuin and Charlemagne concerning rhetoric and virtues.

- **Genuine participation:** may reflect court teaching and dialogue.
- **Literary authorship:** Alcuin.
- **Status:** not a sole-authored book by Charlemagne.

### Lost or indirectly attested projects

Einhard says Charlemagne:

- ordered a grammar of his native language begun;
- gave vernacular names to months and winds;
- collected “very ancient barbarous songs” about former kings and wars.

No securely identified manuscript of the song collection or vernacular grammar survives.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate dynastic succession

Louis the Pious succeeded without a contested accession in 814. He retained imperial structures but reconfigured the court, disciplined some of his father’s household arrangements, and sent Charlemagne’s unmarried daughters away from the palace.

Charlemagne’s empire remained dynastically viable but was not institutionally indivisible. Louis’s sons fought civil wars; the Treaty of Verdun in 843 divided the empire among Lothair I, Louis the German, and Charles the Bald.

### Imperial model

Ottonian, Salian, and Hohenstaufen emperors appropriated Charlemagne’s memory. Otto III opened the Aachen grave in 1000. Frederick Barbarossa sponsored the 1165 canonization. Frederick II had the remains placed in the Karlsschrein in 1215.

Aachen became a major coronation site for German kings. Later emperors treated Charlemagne’s throne, chapel, sword, relics, and memory as instruments of legitimacy.

### French monarchy

Capetian and later French traditions also claimed him as a predecessor. Epic literature made “Charlemagne” a French Christian emperor surrounded by French peers, though his political and linguistic world preceded France as a nation-state.

### Literature and chivalric culture

The historical ruler became the center of the Matter of France. Roland, Oliver, Ganelon, Turpin, Renaud de Montauban, Fierabras, and many other figures accumulated around him. This fictional Charlemagne could be wise, holy, ancient, irascible, deceived, or comic depending on genre.

### Church memory

His local cult celebrated:

- defense of the Church;
- construction and endowment of churches;
- Christianization;
- imperial justice;
- recovery or donation of relics.

The same cult largely omitted or transformed forced conversion, dynastic coercion, and sexual irregularities.

### Nationalist appropriations

- French narratives treated him as an ancestral French monarch.
- German narratives treated him as founder of a German imperial mission.
- Nineteenth-century scholarship debated whether “Karl” belonged primarily to French or German history.
- Nazi-era cultural politics struggled with him: some ideologues condemned the “Saxon butcher,” while the regime later appropriated his imperial symbolism.
- Postwar European discourse emphasized reconciliation and continental unity.

### Modern Europe

The **International Charlemagne Prize of Aachen**, founded after the Second World War, honors contributions to European unification. Its symbolic Charlemagne is a constructed postwar political ancestor, not a direct institutional founder of the European Union.

### Scholarship

Modern research has moved away from both heroic founder narratives and simple portraits of a centralized state. Current emphases include:

- negotiated aristocratic power;
- regional diversity;
- communication and literacy;
- family politics and women’s roles;
- coercive Christianization;
- manuscript transmission;
- ritual and memory;
- environmental, archaeological, and numismatic evidence;
- uncertainty surrounding apparently familiar events.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by sources are included. Mere appearance in the same broad historical tradition has not been treated as a personal link.

### Charles Martel — documented fact

Charlemagne was Charles Martel’s paternal grandson. Martel’s Austrasian power base, military following, ecclesiastical relationships, and control of the mayoralty formed the political inheritance that Pepin III converted into kingship and Charlemagne expanded into empire. Charles Martel died in 741, before Charlemagne’s uncertain birth year, so no meeting is documented.

### Paul the Deacon — documented fact

Paul the Deacon, Lombard historian, poet, grammarian, and former associate of the deposed Lombard dynasty, spent time at Charlemagne’s court in the 780s. He sought help for his brother Arichis, who had been implicated in resistance to Frankish rule. Paul composed poetry, grammatical work, and a homiliary associated with the court’s reform program. He later returned to Monte Cassino. Their connection was that of ruler, petitioner, patron, and court intellectual.

### Aurelius Augustinus — documented intellectual influence

Augustine of Hippo died centuries before Charlemagne. Einhard reports that Charlemagne particularly enjoyed hearing Augustine’s *De civitate Dei* (*City of God*) read aloud. This is a documented literary and theological influence reported by a court insider, not a personal relationship.

### Clovis — documented dynastic and political precedent

Clovis was not a biological ancestor securely demonstrable through a continuous pedigree; Charlemagne’s family displaced the Merovingian dynasty descended from Clovis. Nevertheless, Clovis provided an explicit model of Frankish Christian kingship. Carolingian political memory appropriated Merovingian precedents while replacing Clovis’s dynasty.

### Constantine the Great — documented ideological model

Charlemagne’s imperial and Christian rulership was repeatedly interpreted through the precedent of Constantine: Christian emperor, protector of the Church, patron of Rome, and convener or supervisor of religious order. This was a posthumous ideological connection, not personal contact. Aachen’s imperial program and later representations intensified the comparison.

### Otto III — documented posthumous cultic and imperial connection

Otto III visited Aachen and ordered Charlemagne’s grave opened in 1000. Near-contemporary and later sources disagree about what was found, but the opening itself and Otto’s veneration of Charlemagne are documented. Otto used Carolingian memory in his program of Roman imperial renewal.

### William the Conqueror — reported symbolic influence, no personal connection

No personal contact was possible. Norman and Anglo-Norman culture inherited Carolingian epic, imperial, and royal traditions, but the consulted sources do not document William explicitly acknowledging Charlemagne as a personal model. Accordingly, no stronger atlas connection is asserted.

### Arthur of Lotharingia, Guinevere, Lancelot, Merlin, Morgan le Fay, the Lady of the Lake, Parzival, and other Arthurian figures — absence is a finding

Carolingian and Arthurian legendary cycles interacted in later European manuscript and court culture, but the consulted evidence supplies no historical connection between Charlemagne and these figures. Literary coexistence is not treated as a biographical crossing.

### Abd al-Rahman ibn Abd Allah al-Ghafiqi — no personal connection

He died in 732, before Charlemagne’s birth. He fought Charlemagne’s grandfather Charles Martel. That intergenerational relationship does not establish a meeting or direct influence on Charlemagne, so it is excluded as a substantive personal connection.

### Adam of Bremen and Rimbert of Hamburg-Bremen — no direct lifetime connection established

Both belong to later northern ecclesiastical historiography. Their works concern political and missionary worlds shaped by Carolingian expansion, but the consulted sources do not document a personal relationship, correspondence, or explicitly acknowledged influence involving Charlemagne.

### Charlemagne — subject identity

Charlemagne himself appears in the atlas roster. This is the dossier’s subject, not a separate connection.
