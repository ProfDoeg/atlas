# Herbert Osborn Yardley: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Herbert Osborn Yardley (1889–1958)  
**Scope:** Life, career, writings, institutions, disputes, myths, and legacy  
**Prepared:** 30 August 2026  
**Method:** Chronological reconstruction from government histories and declassified records, contemporary or near-contemporary publications, archival descriptions, specialist scholarship, and later biographical work. Claims are qualified where the underlying evidence is incomplete or contradictory.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and ancestry

- **Father:** Robert Kirkbride Yardley, a railroad station agent and telegrapher for the Indianapolis & Vincennes Railroad. He moved from Freedom, Indiana, to Worthington in 1886. He was associated with the Quaker tradition and local fraternal and civic life. His work gave Herbert early access to railroad communications and Morse telegraphy.
- **Mother:** Mary Emma Osborn Yardley, described in later family accounts as reserved and domestic. She died suddenly of a heart attack on 9 February 1903, when Herbert was thirteen. Her death was a major break in his adolescence.
- **Home:** The family lived at 127 West Union Street, Worthington.
- **Siblings:** Herbert was the second of four children. An older boy died after falling from a tree. A younger brother, Richard or “Dick,” appears in the family record. The consulted sources do not establish a complete, consistent list of all sibling names and dates.
- **Ancestral story:** The family traced itself to Thomas Yardley, said to have emigrated from England to Pennsylvania in 1703. A more extravagant family tradition linked the Yardleys to a signer of Magna Carta. That claim is genealogical legend: Magna Carta bears seals rather than a modern roster of signatures, and the asserted line is not established in the records used here. The legend mattered as family status mythology, not as demonstrated ancestry.

### First marriage and child

Yardley married **Hazel Milam** on 20 May 1914 at the Washington, D.C., home of the Reverend James H. Taylor. Both were twenty-five. Hazel, born 4 March 1889, came from a locally prominent Worthington family. Her father, Abraham Lincoln Milam, operated furniture and undertaking businesses, took part in civic affairs, and was identified in local history as Worthington’s first librarian. During the First World War Hazel worked as a government typist, reportedly earning $1,000 a year.

The couple lived at several Washington addresses, including 1009 Seventh Street NE and 542 Shepherd Street NE, and later in New York and Indiana. Their known child was:

- **Jack Yardley**, born about 1925. He was four in 1929 and nine in 1934 in contemporary family descriptions. He later married Barbara. No second child is securely identified in the consulted sources.

The marriage deteriorated during Yardley’s periods of unemployment and foreign service. Yardley and Hazel were effectively separated by 1937. Neighbors later recalled that Hazel and Jack experienced financial difficulty while Yardley was away; those recollections are evidence of estrangement and hardship, not adjudicated findings of legal abandonment.

### Edna Ramsaier Hackenberg

**Edna Ramsaier**, also found as Edna Ramsaier Hackenberg or Hackenburg, had worked with Yardley in the Cipher Bureau. By the late 1930s she was his intimate partner and later his second wife. She also worked in the U.S. Army’s Signal Intelligence Service. Chinese officials blocked her proposed journey to join him in Chungking, apparently because they did not want another foreigner inside the operation.

Yardley obtained a Nevada divorce from Hazel on 28 August 1944 and married Edna the same day in Reno. Kahn reports that the Nevada decree rested on Yardley’s sworn claim of local domicile although he did not intend to remain in Nevada; on that evidence the proceeding involved a false domicile representation. The decree was nevertheless issued, and no prosecution or later invalidation is documented in the consulted record.

Edna remained with him through his later government work, contracting ventures, poker writing, strokes, and death.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1889–1907

Yardley grew up in a small Indiana railroad town. Contemporary school and local accounts reconstructed by Kahn show a socially prominent, physically active student: he played football as quarterback and captain, caught and pitched in baseball, debated, sang in a quartet, acted in school theatricals, and served as class president. He read widely and for a time hoped to become a criminal lawyer.

His mother’s death in 1903 was followed by poorer conduct and more elaborate pranks. He joined Worthington’s Presbyterian church in August 1905, although no evidence establishes sustained adult religious observance or a later theological commitment.

In May 1906 he rode freight trains west to Denver, worked briefly as a bartender, and returned. He repeated part of the trip the following year with a friend, John Owen. These youthful trips were not exile or military travel; they were improvised working journeys and early expressions of the risk-taking persona he later cultivated.

A school prank in 1907 became decisive. Yardley and companions interfered with or moved a school flag, and he refused the demanded apology. Worthington High School suspended him permanently. He completed his secondary education at Eaton Rapids, Michigan, in June 1907. Accounts that simply say he graduated in Worthington omit the expulsion and transfer.

## Education and Formation

### Formal education

Yardley did not earn a university degree. The frequently repeated statement that he studied English for a year at the University of Chicago compresses a more limited record. While already employed in Washington, he took **University of Chicago Home Study correspondence courses** in English. His grades were generally in the B range. One instructor was **Carl Grabo**, later his friend and coauthor. Yardley did not complete the full sequence.

### Telegraphy

His father taught him Morse and railroad operating practice. Yardley worked at Indiana stations and in Indianapolis. Telegraphy trained him to handle abbreviated traffic accurately, recognize patterns, and think of communications as systems rather than isolated texts. It also introduced him to the porous boundary between official privacy rules and what operators could physically see or copy.

### Self-education in cryptanalysis

At the State Department Yardley discovered that high-level American diplomatic messages used vulnerable codes. He tested himself against intercepted or filed traffic and developed methods from pattern analysis, frequency, context, clerical habits, and accumulated code groups. In 1916–17 he prepared a roughly one-hundred-page memorandum usually called **“Solution of American Diplomatic Codes.”** It demonstrated that he could solve his own government’s systems and helped force stronger procedures.

Yardley later said he solved a five-hundred-word cable between President Woodrow Wilson and Colonel Edward M. House in less than two hours. The episode is consistent with the weakness of the system and appears in his own account and later government history, but the precise elapsed time is self-reported.

### Mentors and formative professional contacts

- **Ralph H. Van Deman**, the Army officer often called the father of U.S. military intelligence, accepted the case for a specialized code-and-cipher section and gave Yardley his wartime opening.
- **John Matthews Manly**, a University of Chicago English scholar and medievalist, supplied linguistic depth and became one of MI-8’s strongest analysts.
- **Charles J. Mendelsohn**, a classicist and cryptanalyst, became a principal collaborator in both government and commercial code work.
- **Frederick Livesey**, **Ruth Wilson**, **Victor Weiskopf**, **John Meeth**, and **Edna Ramsaier** were among the working cryptanalytic network around him.
- British and French specialists, including personnel associated with the British War Office and French cryptanalysis, showed him larger, more mature wartime organizations. British naval intelligence chief **Reginald “Blinker” Hall** reportedly denied him access to Room 40’s inner work, reflecting early doubts about Yardley’s discretion.
- **William F. Friedman** and **Elizebeth Smith Friedman**, trained at George Fabyan’s Riverbank Laboratories, represented a parallel and eventually rival current in American cryptology: more mathematically and technically systematic, and more cautious about secrecy.

## Early Career, 1907–1917

After high school Yardley worked as a railroad telegrapher at several Indiana points. In 1909, during a disturbance involving Independence Day firecrackers, a local marshal’s pistol discharged and a bullet struck Yardley’s thigh. The injury was not life-threatening.

In 1912 he sat the federal civil-service examination for a State Department clerkship and achieved the highest score in his group. He took the oath as a code-room clerk on 23 December 1912 at **$900 per year**. The work involved encoding and decoding U.S. diplomatic messages, handling codebooks, and maintaining the routines of the department’s communications office.

Yardley did more than his assignment required. He studied weaknesses in American systems and, by his own later account, induced or paid commercial telegraph operators to give him copies of foreign coded telegrams. The precise extent of this prewar acquisition is hard to reconstruct, but later Cipher Bureau operations unquestionably depended on covert access to commercial cable traffic.

After the United States entered the First World War, he argued for an office devoted to cryptanalysis. He was commissioned a first lieutenant in the Signal Officers’ Reserve Corps on 29 June 1917, entered active duty on 5 July, and became chief of **MI-8**, the eighth section of the Military Intelligence Division. Some capsule biographies call him a second lieutenant; Hannah and Kahn give the commission as first lieutenant.

## Chronological Life History

### 1917–1919: MI-8 and the First World War

MI-8 began with Yardley and two civilian assistants and expanded into five subsections. At its November 1918 maximum, the organization had approximately 151 personnel. Its work included:

- solving enemy, neutral, and suspected-agent codes and ciphers;
- examining secret inks and chemically concealed writing;
- developing U.S. military codes and ciphers;
- processing material obtained through cable censorship, radio interception, mail examination, military units, and the State Department;
- supporting counterespionage and diplomatic reporting.

Much traffic concerned Mexico, where German agents and sympathizers operated and where U.S. security officials feared hostile use of the border. MI-8 assisted in the case of German agent **Lothar Witzke**, arrested near the Mexican border with a coded message. Yardley later described the episode as a triumph of his unit. The cryptanalytic work was real, but later retellings tend to minimize the contributions of investigators, prosecutors, other intelligence personnel, and the evidentiary chain.

Yardley traveled to Britain in 1918. He initially stayed at the Ritz, then moved to cheaper lodging, and studied War Office practice. British officials shared some methods while excluding him from the most sensitive naval cryptanalysis. He later worked in Paris during the peace negotiations, at times living at 18 rue Gustave-Zédé. He met French analysts and observed the political uses of decoded communications. His later recollections also describe a romance or affair with a dancer named Jacqueline; independent documentation is sparse, so the episode remains principally autobiographical.

MI-8 demonstrated that the United States could maintain a professional cryptanalytic capability. It also revealed the structural fragility that followed Yardley throughout his career: the unit depended on wartime emergency powers, access supplied by censorship and private carriers, and a small number of personally recruited specialists.

Yardley left active duty on 1 October 1919 and remained in the reserve until 1931.

### 1919–1921: Creation of the Cipher Bureau

The Army initially intended to demobilize most wartime communications intelligence. In May 1919 the State and War Departments agreed to fund a peacetime **Cipher Bureau** jointly. Yardley was selected to direct it. New York City was chosen because major cable companies and international traffic were concentrated there and because an ostensibly private office provided cover.

The organization is now best known as the **American Black Chamber**, although “Cipher Bureau” was the administrative name. It operated outside normal civil-service arrangements. Funds and salaries were handled secretly; employees lacked the security and benefits of ordinary departmental appointments.

The office occupied Manhattan premises, with Yardley and Hazel for a time living in the same building. Its commercial cover was the **Code Compiling Company**, incorporated in New York on 3 May 1920 with $500 capital. Yardley and Charles Mendelsohn each held forty-nine shares; a lawyer held two. The company produced legitimate commercial codes, including the **Universal Trade Code** of 1921, and took private clients. Its genuine business activity helped conceal the government work.

Cable companies supplied copies of foreign diplomatic messages through informal and secret arrangements. This was operationally central and legally questionable. Federal communications law protected message privacy, and the carriers were not openly authorized to deliver foreign telegrams wholesale to cryptanalysts. State Department intermediaries insulated the bureau from direct written requests.

### 1921–1922: The Washington Naval Conference

The Black Chamber’s most celebrated operation concerned Japanese diplomatic traffic during the Washington Conference on naval limitation. The [Office of the Historian’s conference summary](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1921-1936/naval-conference) records the final capital-ship ratio as 5:5:3 for the United States, Britain, and Japan, with France and Italy at 1.75 each. Japanese negotiators had pressed for a larger share; American negotiators wanted a lower Japanese ratio.

The bureau read Japanese instructions revealing bargaining positions and the authority available to the delegation. These translations went to Secretary of State **Charles Evans Hughes** and other American officials. They provided confirmation that Japan could accept less than its public demand and helped American officials judge how firmly to negotiate.

Three propositions must be kept separate:

1. **Documented fact:** the Black Chamber solved relevant Japanese systems and delivered translations to American officials.
2. **Reasonable historical conclusion:** the intelligence strengthened Hughes’s confidence and reduced uncertainty.
3. **Popular legend:** one decisive intercept exposed Japan’s absolute fallback and single-handedly produced the 5:5:3 settlement.

Kahn’s reconstruction shows that some messages were timely while others were translated only after key Japanese decisions. One often-cited telegram was completed after Japan had already accepted the essential ratio on 12 December 1921. Diplomatic bargaining, press signals, budget pressures, interservice calculations, and the interests of Britain and Japan also shaped the agreement. The decrypts were valuable intelligence, but the “single magic telegram” account is not supported.

Yardley received the **Army Distinguished Service Medal** under War Department General Orders No. 56 of 1922. The citation credited exceptionally meritorious service as chief of the communication section of Military Intelligence during the war. A later NSA study contains a stray reference to another medal in 1926; the underlying Army citation supports one Distinguished Service Medal, awarded in 1922.

After the conference Yardley developed mild tuberculosis and recuperated in Arizona. He returned to the bureau.

### 1922–1928: Expansion, prosperity, and institutional decline

Yardley later claimed that the Black Chamber attacked the systems of thirty-five or forty countries and solved nearly two dozen. The [NSA’s institutional history](https://www.nsa.gov/History/Cryptologic-History/Historical-Events/Article-View/article/2740622/the-black-chamber/) accepts broad international activity but cautions that lost and incomplete records prevent verification of every numerical claim. Documented targets included Japan, Mexico, Nicaragua, Peru, China, and several European and Latin American states.

The bureau had no large intercept service of its own. It depended on traffic supplied by other agencies or private carriers. Its methods were predominantly manual: card indexes, frequency counts, recovered code groups, stereotyped diplomatic phrasing, operator errors, and comparative files. Yardley was inventive in organizational exploitation, though William Friedman’s later Signal Corps program moved more decisively toward mechanization, formal training, and continuity.

The peacetime staff contracted from about fourteen to six. Appropriations fell, at one point from roughly $50,000 to $35,000. By the late 1920s the bureau had lost some traffic access, cryptographic systems were becoming harder, and Army officials questioned the value of an office outside military command.

Yardley nevertheless prospered for part of the decade:

- His government salary rose to about **$7,500 a year**, unusually high for the period.
- He earned additional income from commercial-code consultation, reportedly reaching several thousand dollars in good years.
- He invested in Queens and Long Island real estate, worked with Cody Realty and other partners, carried substantial mortgages, and participated in building or property sales in Astoria, Woodside, and Jackson Heights.
- He golfed intensively, won club competitions, and cultivated a sociable identity organized around golf, alcohol, poker, and storytelling.

This prosperity was leveraged rather than secure. He saved little, and the late-1920s property reversal left him exposed.

### 1928–1929: Reorganization and closure

The simplified story says that Secretary of State **Henry L. Stimson** discovered the bureau in 1929, declared that gentlemen do not read one another’s mail, and immediately abolished it. The documentary sequence is more complex:

- On 18 March 1929, before Stimson had taken office, the Army began planning to transfer cryptanalytic functions into the Signal Corps.
- The Secretary of War approved the plan on 4 April.
- A change to Army Regulation 105-5 followed on 10 May.
- State Department support was then withdrawn, effective 1 November 1929.
- The New York office became practically inactive during the summer and closed formally at the end of October.

Stimson’s distaste for peacetime reading of friendly diplomatic traffic was real, and the State Department’s withdrawal made the existing bureau impossible. But Army reorganization, budgets, limited current output, insecure access to commercial cables, and the desire for a uniformed in-house capability were independent causes.

Yardley supplied later counter-narratives. One story said President Herbert Hoover himself ordered the closure and communicated the decision through a prominent woman correspondent. Another said Thomas E. Dewey later heard Stimson acknowledge Hoover’s responsibility and apologize to Yardley. These versions come through Yardley and friendly recollections; firm corroboration has not emerged.

The Army offered Yardley a subordinate position at about half his former compensation, approximately $3,750. He rejected it. Friedman, who had built the Signal Corps cryptanalytic program and received Black Chamber files, earned about $5,600. Yardley applied for a Navy teaching position in 1930 and was not selected.

The result was both institutional and personal. American cryptanalysis did not cease; it moved into the Signal Corps under Friedman. Yardley lost his office, status, income, and a professional identity built around personal command.

### 1929–1931: Financial collapse and The American Black Chamber

Yardley returned to Worthington. Real-estate investments and mortgages deteriorated in the crash. He spent family resources, sought consulting and teaching work, and briefly associated with Northwestern University’s Scientific Crime Detection Laboratory, offering instruction on decoding.

He wrote **The American Black Chamber**, drawing on memory and government material he had retained. Bobbs-Merrill published it on 1 June 1931, after the Saturday Evening Post ran three major excerpts. The State Department publicly disavowed the disclosures.

The book revealed:

- the existence and organization of the Cipher Bureau;
- examples of foreign systems and plaintext;
- the covert delivery of diplomatic cable traffic;
- American penetration of Japanese communications;
- the use of cryptanalysis in diplomacy;
- names and operational episodes that foreign governments could correlate with their own records.

Yardley’s motives were mixed and unusually well documented:

- immediate family financial need;
- anger at dismissal and official ingratitude;
- a belief that old systems had become obsolete;
- a desire to make the public recognize the strategic disadvantage of American cryptographic weakness;
- literary ambition and an appetite for celebrity.

The government examined prosecution but found no statute that squarely criminalized publication of old diplomatic communications by a former official. Yardley’s reserve resignation became effective 1 April 1931. Official correspondence treated his public disclosures as incompatible with reserve status. Yardley later said he resigned to avoid court-martial proceedings; the record supports official pressure but not a completed court-martial case.

Japan reacted angrily. Newspapers serialized and translated the book; Foreign Minister **Kijūrō Shidehara** was attacked politically, and the disclosures fed anti-American criticism. The book damaged diplomatic trust and exposed sources and methods.

The scale of the technical damage is disputed. Friedman’s contemporary damage assessment treated it as grave and suggested foreign governments would replace compromised systems. Later research by Kahn found no immediate worldwide blackout: traffic volumes did not abruptly collapse, and Japan did not instantly replace every system. The defensible conclusion is that the publication caused diplomatic and security damage, while later official and popular accounts overstated the speed and universality of the cryptographic response.

### 1931–1933: Lectures, puzzles, and an attempted second exposé

Yardley lectured across the United States and published cryptograms and articles. **Yardleygrams** converted his reputation into a puzzle brand. He worked with literary agent **George T. Bye** and with his former correspondence instructor Carl Grabo.

He next assembled **Japanese Diplomatic Secrets, 1921–1922**, using carbon copies of decrypts retained from government service. **Marie Stuart Klooz** ghostwrote much of the prose from materials and direction supplied by Yardley. Bobbs-Merrill declined it. When Macmillan considered the manuscript, a U.S. marshal seized it on 20 February 1933 after Army Chief of Staff Douglas MacArthur pressed the matter through State and Justice. Publisher and agent personnel appeared before a federal grand jury; the surviving record is unclear on whether Yardley himself testified.

Yardley explored removing his name and publishing under Klooz’s. Dewey later claimed that he persuaded Yardley over drinks to abandon publication. Whatever the private intervention, the manuscript did not appear then.

Congress closed the statutory gap. Legislation introduced on 27 March passed rapidly and became law on 10 June 1933. The provision, now [18 U.S.C. § 952](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title18-section952), criminalizes unauthorized publication or use of diplomatic codes and correspondence obtained through cryptanalysis. The [1933 session law](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/statute/48/122) provided penalties of up to ten years’ imprisonment or a $10,000 fine. It was widely understood as the “Yardley law,” although he was not prosecuted under it because the triggering conduct preceded enactment.

The Army demanded return of official material in September 1932. Yardley denied retaining anything harmful, but later recoveries and the seized manuscript demonstrate that he had retained sensitive documents.

### 1933–1937: Fiction, radio, Hollywood, secret ink, and whiskey

Yardley cut his right palm while compounding or handling a secret-ink product. Infection spread; a finger—identified in Kahn’s account as the second finger—blackened and was amputated on 21 May 1933. Medical bills worsened his financial position. His secret-paper or secret-ink venture, including sales of several thousand sheets and a later transfer to Virgil Vandeventer, did not become a stable business.

With Carl Grabo he published:

- **The Blonde Countess** (1934), a cryptanalytic spy novel and roman à clef. Its Nathaniel Greenleaf resembles Yardley.
- **Red Sun of Nippon** (1934), another novel using Japanese-American conflict and espionage themes.

Army reviewers examined The Blonde Countess and did not identify a prosecutable new disclosure.

In 1935 NBC broadcast a fifteen-minute serial, generally titled **The Black Chamber** or **Stories of the Black Chamber**, three times a week for roughly a year. Grabo collaborated on scripts. Forhan’s toothpaste sponsored the program and used secret-ink or decoder promotions. The program transformed the once-secret bureau into popular entertainment.

MGM bought screen interests and employed Yardley as a technical adviser on **Rendezvous** (1935), directed by William K. Howard and starring William Powell and Rosalind Russell. The film derived loosely from The Blonde Countess but was substantially rewritten as comedy and romance. A proposed direct film of The American Black Chamber was not made. Some film databases attribute uncredited screenplay work on **The Great Impersonation** (1935) to Yardley; the consulted archival scholarship does not firmly establish the credit, so it should be treated as an attribution rather than confirmed authorship.

Using Hollywood income, Yardley acquired a controlling or substantial interest in **M. J. Nolder Distilleries, Inc.**, at Colton, California. The plant reportedly reached production near 1,100 gallons a day in July 1936. Falling whiskey prices, insufficient capital, and conflict with local partners ended his control. A later shareholder record involved 2,812.5 shares held by Yardley through a proxy against a much smaller Nolder block, but paper ownership did not yield recovery. He returned east without recouping the investment.

By September 1937 he and Hazel were separated. Hazel and Jack lived in or returned to Worthington; Yardley resumed life in the New York area and then prepared for China.

### 1938–1940: China and the “Chinese Black Chamber”

Nationalist China faced sustained Japanese attack and sought foreign cryptanalytic help. Major **Hsiao Hsin-ju**, also rendered **Pu Hsiao**, recruited Yardley for the intelligence organization led by **Dai Li**. The contract reportedly offered **$10,000 a year**, initially for six months with an option to continue.

Yardley traveled under the name **Herbert Osborn**. He left Europe in August or September 1938 and traveled through Le Havre, Gibraltar, Marseille, Malta, Port Said, Suez, Aden, Bombay, Colombo, Penang, Singapore, and Hong Kong, reaching the Hong Kong area on 12 October before continuing by difficult overland and river routes to Chungking. The alias was intended to conceal his identity from Japanese intelligence.

His office operated in a deteriorated château or mansion outside the bombed capital. He selected and trained approximately fifteen students and worked with a comparable group of radio operators. The curriculum included frequency analysis, solution techniques, traffic procedure, codebook reconstruction, and disciplined recording. Yardley imposed long hours and tried to create a self-sustaining Chinese unit.

He later claimed solution of nineteen Japanese systems and partial success against a twentieth. Independent assessments are mixed:

- American military attachés monitored him and wanted useful results.
- Some reports credited him with meaningful training and solutions.
- Others said Chinese sponsors were dissatisfied with the output or with his management.
- Chinese cryptanalytic work was institutionally fragmented, making individual attribution difficult.

The strongest conclusion is that Yardley established a training-and-analysis program and obtained genuine low- and middle-level results, but his exact numerical claims cannot be independently verified. The modern Cambridge chapter on [grassroots Sino-American intelligence cooperation](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/uneasy-allies/herbert-yardley-and-the-grassroots-origins-of-sinoamerican-wartime-intelligence-cooperation/D09EC4C36E3E88CB564630762137F5F1) places his mission within a broader, improvised Chinese-American network rather than treating it as a one-man service.

Yardley met journalists and expatriates including **Theodore H. White** and **Emily Hahn**. White remembered a funny, energetic, hard-drinking companion; Hahn described a loud American whose supposed secrecy was widely penetrated. Their memoirs help establish social reputation but also helped create the colorful “cryptographic adventurer” image.

His health deteriorated. He lost approximately forty pounds, suffered the effects of bombing, heat, poor sanitation, disease risk, and chronic strain, and later described recurring nightmares. No contemporary diagnosis of post-traumatic stress disorder exists; retrospective use of that diagnosis would go beyond the evidence.

Sexual conduct in China is a disputed and sensitive part of the record. Yardley’s letters include explicit talk, sexual boasting, and intense interest in women. Later accounts based on expatriate recollections alleged orgies, coercive arrangements, or a “comfort college” involving women procured for foreign advisers. The surviving evidence does **not** establish that Yardley owned sexual slaves or directed a coercive system. His own letters sometimes describe encounters and at other times say fear of venereal disease kept him from intercourse. Accordingly:

- sexual preoccupation and extramarital intimacy are documented;
- particular consensual encounters are partly self-reported;
- coercion and “sex slave” claims remain allegations derived from recollection and rumor;
- no investigation, charge, or court finding is documented.

Edna wanted to join him, but Hsiao refused permission. Yardley extended his stay beyond the first contract despite poor health and the high cost of travel. He returned to the United States in 1940.

### 1940–1941: Signal Intelligence Service work

The U.S. Army’s Signal Intelligence Service, despite Friedman’s distrust, paid Yardley about **$4,000** for a series of technical studies. He produced six “brochures” or reports, including an approximately 218-page treatment of Japanese field systems, operating procedures, frequencies, and language features. He also developed or demonstrated a Japanese-keyboard typewriter.

This episode qualifies the claim that Washington wholly blacklisted him after 1931. Senior cryptologists did distrust him, and he was denied permanent sensitive employment, but the Army purchased work it thought potentially useful.

### June–December 1941: Canada’s Examination Unit

Canada created the **Examination Unit** on 9 June 1941 within the National Research Council. Yardley became its first director on 10 June. The office began in two rooms at the NRC Aeronautical Laboratories on Montreal Road, Ottawa. The initial staff was about nine, drawing personnel from the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, universities, and communications services. Edna joined on six months’ leave from the U.S. Signal Intelligence Service.

The Unit worked on diplomatic and agent communications, including lower-level Japanese systems and German Abwehr traffic involving South America. A useful success against German agent networks showed that the office was not merely ceremonial. The official [Communications Security Establishment Canada timeline](https://www.cse-cst.gc.ca/en/culture-and-community/history/timeline) dates the unit’s creation and later development; a Canadian government [CSIS anniversary history](https://www.canada.ca/en/security-intelligence-service/corporate/publications/celebration-of-csis-40th-anniversary.html) identifies the nine-person beginning and a $10,000 allocation.

Yardley’s appointment created an alliance problem. British cryptographic authorities would not share their highest-grade material with a man who had publicly revealed former work. U.S. officials, including Friedman, also opposed entrusting him with secrets. **Lester B. Pearson**, then a Canadian diplomat and an advisory-committee chairman, initially defended Yardley and traveled to Washington to investigate. He concluded that Britain and the United States would withhold cooperation while Yardley remained.

Yardley’s contract ended on 9 December 1941, two days after Pearl Harbor. The timing sometimes produces the false claim that he was dismissed because he failed to predict the attack. The operative record instead shows that allied security objections had matured before the attack. Canadian officials continued salary or special-duty payments for roughly two and a half months—about $1,325 to Yardley and $500 to Edna—and paid moving expenses. British cryptanalyst **Oliver Strachey** succeeded him in January 1942.

Yardley felt betrayed. Pearson and other officials expressed personal regard while accepting the institutional necessity. A detailed Canadian reconstruction, [Canada’s Bletchley Park](https://home.imagesandyhill.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/10.-Bernier-Pepall-coverHSO.pdf), quotes his bitter later reference to a “Gestapo separation of old friends.”

### 1942–1947: Surveillance, restaurant, and the Office of Price Administration

Yardley sought work from the FBI and other agencies. FBI agents interviewed him in January 1942. He wanted formal removal from what he regarded as a blacklist. Thomas Dewey and Lester Pearson made or supported approaches on his behalf without restoring him to cryptographic command.

The FBI investigated and surveilled a Washington restaurant with which he was associated, the **Rideau**. An agent speculated that it might be an intelligence front, but observation found no subversive activity. The case was closed in September 1942. Yardley’s restaurant operated around the clock, paid about $350 monthly rent, and employed roughly thirteen people. Operating costs made it unsuccessful, and he sold or withdrew.

On 9 November 1942 he joined the **Office of Price Administration** as a legal or enforcement investigator at approximately $2,600 a year. His work concerned price, rent, and rationing violations. Performance reports were favorable. He stayed until the OPA wound down in 1947.

Edna broke a leg on 20 April 1944. On 28 August that year Yardley completed the Nevada divorce and remarriage described above.

With Grabo he published **Crows Are Black Everywhere** in 1945. The novel drew on Chungking experience. A reported $1,000 advance was divided between the collaborators and did not solve Yardley’s finances.

### 1946–1952: Sales, housing, and contracting

Yardley invested about **$1,400** in the **Osborn Sales Company** in 1946. It marketed electrical appliances and related goods and had failed by 1948.

In January 1949 he became a salesman for a federal housing agency—identified in the record through the Public Housing Administration/Housing and Home Finance system—and retired in March 1952. He also built houses in the Silver Spring and Kensington, Maryland, area, functioning as a small general contractor. The record shows practical construction and sales work, not a large incorporated development empire.

His lifetime finances followed a recurring pattern: high earnings or advances, speculative entry into property or products, weak capitalization, and losses. There is no evidence of hidden wealth, money laundering, or a durable family fortune.

### 1952–1958: Poker author and final illness

Yardley continued to play poker, often in National Press Club circles although he was not a club member. His patient, conservative style earned the nickname **“Old Adhesive.”** His last major book, **The Education of a Poker Player: Including Where and How One Learns**, appeared from Simon & Schuster in November 1957. It combines strategic instruction with fictionalized or reconstructed games from Indiana, Washington, China, and other settings. It became his most enduring non-cryptographic work.

He suffered a mild stroke in October 1957, with partial paralysis. A major stroke in the summer of 1958 left him gravely ill. He died in Washington, D.C., on 7 August 1958, aged sixty-nine, about a week after the major event. He was buried with military honors at Arlington National Cemetery.

The British edition of The Education of a Poker Player appeared posthumously from Jonathan Cape in 1959 with an introduction by **Ian Fleming**. Fleming’s association linked Yardley’s real intelligence career to the developing James Bond cultural world, but Fleming did not collaborate on the original American text.

### Technology and patents

No issued U.S. patent has been identified in the consulted record. Yardley developed procedures, codebooks, training methods, a commercial code, secret-writing products, and a Japanese-character typewriter or keyboard arrangement. These were practical projects rather than a demonstrated patent portfolio.

### Financial profile

Exact lifetime net worth cannot be reconstructed. Known peaks and troughs include:

- State Department entry salary: $900 annually.
- Cipher Bureau peak salary: about $7,500, plus private code work.
- China contract: reportedly $10,000 annually.
- Canadian separation or special-duty payments: approximately $1,325 to Yardley and $500 to Edna over two and a half months.
- OPA entry salary: about $2,600.
- Osborn Sales investment: about $1,400.
- Repeated losses in leveraged real estate, secret products, distilling, restaurant operation, and appliance sales.

Book advances, serialization, radio, and movie money gave intermittent relief. The American Black Chamber sold substantially, but exact lifetime totals vary by edition and national market. No reliable evidence supports claims of great wealth from intelligence work or book royalties.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Politics

Yardley held no elected office and left no systematic political doctrine. He formed a durable friendship with Republican prosecutor and presidential candidate **Thomas E. Dewey**, who intervened privately on his behalf. That relationship and some later affiliations suggest Republican social connections, but the record does not establish substantial party organization work.

His professional outlook was nationalist and pragmatic: states read foreign communications; the United States should not leave itself uniquely vulnerable; and cryptanalysis should influence diplomacy and war. He did not object in principle to espionage. His conflict with government concerned who controlled secrets, whether former service entitled him to disclose them, and whether officials had treated him fairly.

### Religion

His father’s background was Quaker. Herbert joined a Presbyterian church as a teenager. Adult writings rarely frame conduct in religious terms. No evidence used here establishes conversion, clerical ties, atheism, or sustained denominational practice.

### Intellectual style

Yardley was a gifted pattern recognizer, administrator, recruiter, and narrator. He favored practical attack:

- exploit human and procedural error;
- obtain the largest possible traffic sample;
- use repetitions, stereotyped openings, dates, addresses, and diplomatic context;
- reconstruct codebooks incrementally;
- combine technical work with knowledge of personalities and policy.

He was less mathematically systematic than Friedman and less interested in building a quiet bureaucracy insulated from publicity. His literary development turned secret work into first-person adventure, then fiction, broadcast entertainment, and gambling instruction. The same skill—reading incomplete information about an opponent—connected codebreaking and poker in his self-presentation.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Legal Questions

### Unauthorized acquisition of cable traffic

**Status: documented practice; legal authority doubtful; no prosecution.**

The Cipher Bureau received foreign diplomatic telegrams from commercial cable companies through secret arrangements. Contemporary communications law protected message secrecy. No public warrant system or general statutory authority has been identified. The practice was government-sponsored intelligence collection but rested on carrier cooperation that the companies did not publicize.

### Retention and publication of official secrets

**Status: documented retention and publication; investigated; no indictment or conviction.**

Yardley retained decrypts and internal material, used them in The American Black Chamber, and supplied them for Japanese Diplomatic Secrets. The Army demanded return of records. A grand jury considered the later manuscript, and a marshal seized it. Existing law did not support a successful prosecution for the already completed disclosure. Congress then enacted the statute now codified as 18 U.S.C. § 952.

### Accuracy and self-glorification

**Status: demonstrated exaggeration in some episodes; not equivalent to fabrication of the entire career.**

Government reviewers and Friedman accused Yardley of distortion and self-promotion. Later archival checking confirms real operations and major achievements, while correcting:

- claims that one decrypt alone decided the Washington treaty;
- the implied disappearance of all U.S. cryptanalysis after 1929;
- inflated exclusivity in the Witzke case;
- numerical claims about countries and systems that cannot all be verified;
- dramatic episodes known only from Yardley’s narration.

### Alleged sale of secrets to Japan

**Status: serious documented allegation; investigated; no charge, court finding, or conclusive proof of a direct sale.**

Ladislas Farago’s **The Broken Seal** (1967) alleged that Yardley sold Japanese diplomatic material or technical information to Japanese representatives for **$7,000**. A Japanese Foreign Ministry memorandum dated 10 June 1931 stated that the ministry had previously paid Yardley $7,000 and considered responses to his book. The NSA reopened the matter in 1968; the resulting file is part of the [Yardley Collection](https://www.nsa.gov/Helpful-Links/NSA-FOIA/Declassification-Transparency-Initiatives/Historical-Releases/Yardley-Collection/).

The evidentiary problem is attribution. Surviving records do not fully identify the transaction, intermediary, date, or consideration, and some details in Farago’s account are inconsistent or unsupported. David Kahn rejected the accusation. John F. Dooley’s study, [“Was Herbert O. Yardley a Traitor?”](https://www.academia.edu/28496371/Was_Herbert_O_Yardley_a_Traitor), found evidence warranting inquiry but no conclusive “smoking gun”; a related [Knox College account](https://www.knox.edu/news/professor-and-student-examine-history-of-secret-codes) explains the missing-document problem.

The correct classification is:

- a Japanese official memo recording a prior $7,000 payment exists;
- Farago circulated a specific betrayal narrative in 1967;
- the NSA investigated in 1968;
- no U.S. indictment, prosecution, or conviction followed;
- the record does not prove beyond dispute that Yardley personally sold the claimed material;
- the issue remains unresolved.

### Nevada divorce domicile

**Status: documented decree; biographer’s evidence of false sworn domicile; no prosecution.**

Kahn concludes that Yardley falsely swore Nevada domicile to obtain the 1944 divorce. The evidence supports describing the statement as false, but there was no adjudicated perjury case.

### Sexual allegations in China

**Status: some conduct documented in private letters; coercive stories unverified; no investigation or charge known.**

Sexual boasting, infidelity, and pursuit of women appear in Yardley’s correspondence. Claims of organized coercion, sexual slavery, or a “comfort college” derive from later recollection and rumor and are not established by court, police, or contemporaneous investigative records.

### Alcohol and gambling

**Status: heavy social drinking and regular gambling documented; medical addiction not documented.**

Friends, colleagues, and Yardley himself describe extensive drinking and poker. There is no clinical diagnosis of alcoholism or gambling disorder in the consulted sources, and employment reviews do not establish that drinking disabled him at work. Financial losses came primarily from property and businesses, not from a documented gambling collapse.

### FBI inquiry and surveillance

**Status: documented investigation; no adverse finding or charge.**

The FBI interviewed Yardley and surveilled the Rideau restaurant amid speculation it might conceal intelligence activity. Agents found no subversive operation, and the case closed in September 1942.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “Gentlemen do not read each other’s mail”

**The story:** Stimson saw decrypted friendly diplomatic messages, uttered the sentence, and personally killed the Black Chamber in 1929.

**Appearance and circulation:** The secure printed formulation appears in Stimson and McGeorge Bundy’s **On Active Service in Peace and War** (1948), which says that Stimson, dealing as a gentleman with foreign gentlemen, later said, “Gentlemen do not read each other’s mail.” Later intelligence histories shortened or altered it to “Gentlemen do not read other gentlemen’s mail.” Yardley, journalists, NSA histories, and Cold War writers made it a set piece.

**Evidence for and against:** Stimson’s ethical objection is well supported, and State funding ended under him. No contemporaneous 1929 document used here records the exact utterance. Army reorganization preceded his arrival. Louis Kruh’s study, [“Stimson, the Black Chamber, and the ‘Gentlemen’s Mail’ Quote”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0161-118891862819), treats the phrase as a fair expression of his view but not securely documented as the words that closed the office at a single meeting.

**Reputational effect:** The anecdote turned a bureaucratic reorganization into a parable of American innocence and made Stimson either a principled gentleman or a naïve enemy of intelligence, depending on the teller. It also made Yardley the victim of one sentence rather than of multiple institutional changes.

### 2. The one decrypt that won the Washington Conference

**The story:** Yardley read Japan’s absolute minimum naval ratio, handed it to Hughes, and thereby saved the United States billions or forced Japan to accept 5:5:3.

**Appearance and circulation:** Yardley’s book and publicity established the narrative; biographies, newspaper features, museum treatments, and popular cryptologic histories repeated it.

**Evidence:** The bureau did read Japanese bargaining instructions, and American negotiators received them. Some intelligence was timely and useful. But at least one famous telegram was solved after the decisive Japanese acceptance, and the treaty emerged from multilateral diplomacy and domestic constraints.

**Reputational effect:** It made Yardley’s career legible through one heroic event and supported both hagiography and claims that his later book betrayed a uniquely decisive secret.

### 3. Stimson ended American codebreaking

**The story:** The 1929 closure left the United States cryptographically blind until Pearl Harbor.

**Evidence:** The New York bureau closed, but Army cryptanalysis moved to the Signal Corps under Friedman. Navy cryptanalysis also continued. Capacity and interagency coordination remained imperfect, but the profession did not disappear.

**Effect:** This myth magnified the moral of the “gentlemen” quotation and obscured the institutional lineage leading to the Signal Intelligence Service.

### 4. Yardley sold the Black Chamber to Japan

**The story:** A resentful Yardley privately sold Japanese officials their own decrypts and American methods, then published them.

**Appearance:** Farago’s The Broken Seal gave the charge wide circulation in 1967. The 1968 NSA inquiry sustained official interest.

**Evidence:** A Japanese memo mentions $7,000 paid to Yardley; the precise transaction remains unproved, and parts of Farago’s reconstruction fail documentary checking. There was no prosecution. See the classified-status analysis above.

**Effect:** The allegation shifts Yardley from indiscreet memoirist to traitor. Its unresolved nature has polarized biographies and commemorations.

### 5. The attempted poisoning of President Wilson

**The story:** Allied or foreign conspirators planned to poison Woodrow Wilson during the Paris Peace Conference, and American cryptanalysis exposed the plot.

**Appearance:** Yardley used the episode in The American Black Chamber and publicity.

**Evidence:** War and State officials and Wilson’s physician denied or failed to corroborate the plot. No underlying prosecutorial or medical record in the consulted sources establishes an attempt. It remains a sensational claim from Yardley’s narrative.

**Effect:** It gave the memoir the stakes of a thriller and encouraged later doubt about episodes that were otherwise documentable.

### 6. The beautiful spy and the apartment trap

**The story:** A beautiful woman lured Yardley socially while agents searched his room or apartment, demonstrating that foreign powers feared him.

**Appearance:** Yardley’s memoir and later retellings, including the [Smithsonian’s reconstruction](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-spy-who-exposed-the-secrets-of-the-black-chamber-one-of-americas-first-code-breaking-organizations-180985947/).

**Evidence:** The story is plausible as counterintelligence but rests chiefly on Yardley’s later interpretation. No foreign operational file has been produced.

**Effect:** It fused codebreaking with sexual danger and helped define the modern popular image of the glamorous cryptanalyst.

### 7. “The American Admiral Hall”

**The story:** Yardley was the American equivalent of Britain’s legendary naval intelligence chief, Admiral Reginald Hall.

**Circulation:** Yardley encouraged the comparison in publicity and self-presentation.

**Evidence:** He created an important U.S. office, but it was smaller, more fragile, and more dependent on other agencies than Hall’s wartime system. Hall himself reportedly limited Yardley’s access.

**Effect:** The comparison supported Yardley’s campaign for status after dismissal.

### 8. The sole “father of American cryptology”

**The story:** Yardley alone founded American cryptology.

**Evidence:** He founded the first durable official U.S. peacetime diplomatic cryptanalytic bureau and was a crucial institutional entrepreneur. Earlier military and diplomatic codebreaking existed; Riverbank Laboratories, George Fabyan, William and Elizebeth Friedman, Manly, Mendelsohn, Navy cryptanalysts, and numerous unnamed clerks also built the field.

**Effect:** The phrase aided posthumous restoration but fostered a false contest in which either Yardley or Friedman must be the sole father. Their contributions were different and overlapping.

### 9. A dismissed failure in China and Canada

**The story:** Because both missions ended badly, Yardley produced nothing.

**Evidence:** Chinese satisfaction and the exact number of solutions are disputed, but he trained a functioning group and produced technical material later purchased by the U.S. Army. Canada’s unit solved useful agent traffic. His Canadian removal resulted mainly from allied trust and liaison, not proof of technical incompetence.

**Effect:** This hostile simplification mirrored hagiographic claims of spectacular high-level successes.

### 10. Yardley foresaw or could have prevented Pearl Harbor

**The story:** Had Canada or the United States retained Yardley, he would have decoded the attack plan.

**Evidence:** No source demonstrates that Yardley had access to, or could have solved, the Japanese naval operational system carrying the relevant planning. His Canadian work concerned other systems, and his dismissal process preceded 7 December 1941.

**Effect:** The counterfactual was useful in attacking prewar intelligence leadership but has no documentary foundation.

- **“Whistleblowers, Leakers, and Traitors: A History of American Intelligence Disclosures”**: lecture material placing Yardley in disclosure history, with an [Embry-Riddle archival page](https://commons.erau.edu/publication/100/) and [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1fV-OP-javc).

No definitive feature-length documentary devoted solely to Yardley was identified in the consulted sources. Numerous television and podcast episodes on cryptology, Pearl Harbor, or intelligence leaks mention him; such incidental appearances should not be mistaken for primary evidence.

### Major archival collections

1. **National Security Agency, Yardley Collection.** More than two hundred declassified records dated 1918–1968, with a gap for 1924–1927. Includes MI-8/Cipher Bureau material, manuscripts, and the 1968 investigation into the Japanese-payment allegation. The [collection portal](https://www.nsa.gov/Helpful-Links/NSA-FOIA/Declassification-Transparency-Initiatives/Historical-Releases/Yardley-Collection/) links individual PDFs.
2. **National Archives and Records Administration.** Relevant record groups include RG 38 (Naval intelligence/communications), RG 43 (international conferences), RG 59 (State Department), RG 165 and RG 319 (Army intelligence), and RG 457 (NSA and predecessor cryptologic records). Yardley’s surviving cryptologic manuscripts and Cipher Bureau traffic are especially associated with RG 457.
3. **National Cryptologic Museum Library.** Holds approximately sixteen boxes of Cipher Bureau intercepts and translations. Older descriptions sometimes called them Yardley’s personal collection; later cataloguing identifies them as bureau records.
4. **Henry L. Stimson Papers, Yale University Library.** Diaries, correspondence, and official papers, described in the [Yale archival catalogue](https://archives.yale.edu/repositories/12/resources/4819), are essential for the State Department decision and Stimson’s later recollection.
5. **William F. Friedman Collection, George C. Marshall Foundation Library.** Includes professional papers, assessments, correspondence, and Friedman’s annotated material on Yardley.
6. **John Matthews Manly Papers and University of Chicago records.** Relevant to MI-8, correspondence instruction, and the scholarly cryptanalytic network.
7. **George T. Bye papers, Columbia University.** Literary-agent files concerning The American Black Chamber, the suppressed Japanese manuscript, articles, and negotiations.
8. **Library and Archives Canada and Canadian security records.** Department of National Defence/NRC files on the Examination Unit, its personnel, and allied liaison. The official CSE timeline is a public guide.
9. **MGM production and publicity files.** Relevant to Rendezvous and Yardley’s technical-adviser work.
10. **Army award and honor records.** The [Military Intelligence Hall of Fame member portal](https://www.ikn.army.mil/apps/MIHOF/Home/Members) records the 1988 induction; the [Military Times valor record](https://valor.militarytimes.com/recipient/recipient-18395/) reproduces the Distinguished Service Medal citation.

### Major modern long-form treatments

- The Smithsonian article [“The Spy Who Exposed the Secrets of the Black Chamber”](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-spy-who-exposed-the-secrets-of-the-black-chamber-one-of-americas-first-code-breaking-organizations-180985947/) synthesizes the career and the seductive-spy story for a general audience.
- The Atlantic’s [“82 Years Before Edward Snowden, There Was Herbert O. Yardley”](https://www.theatlantic.com/politics/archive/2013/12/82-years-before-edward-snowden-there-was-herbert-o-yardley/282019/) illustrates the modern recasting of Yardley as a predecessor of intelligence leakers. The comparison is interpretive: Yardley published a commercial memoir containing old diplomatic intelligence after dismissal; Snowden disclosed large digital collections while employed as a contractor. The legal and technological settings differ.

## Death and Later-Life Status

Yardley died on 7 August 1958 in Washington, D.C. The immediate documented cause was the aftermath of a major stroke; a milder stroke had occurred in October 1957. He received full military honors at Arlington despite long official distrust.

Documented health events across his life include:

- gunshot wound to the thigh in 1909, accidental and not fatal;
- mild tuberculosis after the Washington Conference, treated by recuperation in Arizona;
- infected hand injury and finger amputation in May 1933;
- severe weight loss and recurring nightmares after wartime China;
- mild stroke in October 1957;
- major stroke and death in August 1958.

No reliable diagnosis of chronic psychiatric illness, alcoholism, drug addiction, or gambling disorder was found. Recollections of heavy drinking and compulsive risk-taking should not be converted into diagnoses.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Institutional legacy

Yardley created and directed the first sustained official American organization devoted to peacetime diplomatic cryptanalysis. MI-8 and the Cipher Bureau established practices that outlived their particular offices:

- permanent specialist staffing;
- systematic traffic accumulation and indexing;
- integration of cryptanalysis with diplomatic decision-making;
- commercial and administrative covers;
- training and methodological writing;
- recognition that communications intelligence required an institution, not an occasional gifted amateur.

The direct organizational line after 1929 ran primarily through Friedman’s Signal Corps service rather than through Yardley personally. The long-term American system therefore combined Yardley’s institutional precedent with the Friedmans’ technical discipline and later Army, Navy, and allied organizations.

### Disclosure legacy

The American Black Chamber was simultaneously:

- an important historical source;
- a commercially successful memoir;
- an unauthorized exposure of government operations;
- a catalyst for 18 U.S.C. § 952;
- a prototype for later disputes over whistleblowing, leaking, memoir review, and official secrecy.

Yardley did not present himself as an antiwar dissident or civil-liberties whistleblower. He defended intelligence collection and objected to being discarded. Later comparison with Edward Snowden and other leakers reflects a shared structure—insider disclosure and state retaliation—more than shared motive or ideology.

### Technical reputation

Older official accounts alternated between erasing him and treating his book as catastrophic. Later scholarship produced a more differentiated estimate:

- exceptional intuitive cryptanalyst and organizer;
- effective recruiter and trainer;
- major contributor to diplomatic intelligence;
- less technically systematic than Friedman;
- prone to exaggeration, insecure documentation, and self-dramatization;
- capable of real work after 1931 despite official mistrust.

### Honors

- **Army Distinguished Service Medal**, 1922.
- **U.S. Army Military Intelligence Hall of Fame**, inducted 1988.
- **NSA Cryptologic Hall of Honor**, inaugural class, 1999.

The [NSA profile](https://www.nsa.gov/History/Cryptologic-History/Historical-Figures/Historical-Figures-View/Article/1623030/herbert-o-yardley/) frames him as both pioneer and cautionary figure.

### Cultural legacy

Yardley helped create the modern public figure of the codebreaker: solitary pattern genius, government insider, card player, traveler, drinker, and teller of forbidden stories. His novels and radio scripts blurred documentary and fiction before intelligence memoir became an established genre. The Education of a Poker Player remains in print and is cited in poker history for combining position, probability, patience, and psychological observation.

The durability of the phrase “gentlemen do not read each other’s mail” has often exceeded knowledge of the man. It packages several real conflicts—law versus espionage, diplomatic etiquette versus state practice, personal loyalty versus official secrecy—into a sentence whose exact use in 1929 cannot be proven.

## Atlas Connections

### William F. Friedman

- **[T3]** **Documented fact.** Yardley and Friedman maintained direct professional correspondence beginning in 1919, when Yardley attempted to recruit William—and, through him, Elizebeth Friedman—for the proposed peacetime Cipher Bureau. Their surviving exchanges also concern cryptographic systems and machinery in the late 1920s. Friedman later annotated and critically evaluated Yardley’s writings, opposed restoring him to sensitive Allied work, and continued investigating the circumstances surrounding the Black Chamber’s closure. The NSA catalogues both [Yardley–Friedman correspondence](https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/about/cryptologic-heritage/museum/national-cryptologic-museum-library-catalog.pdf) and [Friedman’s annotated copy of *The American Black Chamber*](https://www.nsa.gov/Helpful-Links/NSA-FOIA/Declassification-Transparency-Initiatives/Historical-Releases/Friedman-Documents/Reports-Research/).

### Elizebeth Smith Friedman

- **[T3]** **Documented fact.** Elizebeth Friedman was included in Yardley’s 1919 effort to recruit the Friedmans for his planned postwar cryptanalytic organization: surviving correspondence records an offer of $3,000 annually for William and $1,520 for Elizebeth. The negotiations were conducted principally through William, so the evidence supports written professional engagement within the same recruitment exchange, but not a close personal relationship between Yardley and Elizebeth. Her later oral-history recollections also discussed Yardley’s organization and personnel. Sources: [NSA oral history with Elizebeth Friedman](https://media.defense.gov/2021/Jul/15/2002763459/-1/-1/0/NSA-OH-1976-17-EFRIEDMAN.PDF) and the surviving 1919 correspondence described in the Friedman archival record.

### Ian Fleming

- **[T3]** **Documented fact.** In 1957 Fleming wrote the introduction or preface for the British edition of Yardley’s *The Education of a Poker Player*, published by Jonathan Cape in 1959 after Yardley’s death. This was a one-sided literary engagement with Yardley’s work; no reliable evidence located establishes that the two men met or corresponded personally. Christopher Moran’s study of Fleming’s intelligence connections confirms the 1957 commission and identifies Yardley and *The American Black Chamber* in Fleming’s prefatory treatment. [Source](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/110589/1/WRAP-secret-life-IIan-Fleming-spies-lies-social%20ties-Moran-2018.pdf).
