# Ángel Gregorio Villoldo: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs Villoldo’s life from surviving civil, ecclesiastical, census, military-enrolment, publishing, phonographic, press, and later historical records. His private life and childhood remain poorly documented. Later tango histories often repeat anecdotes without identifying their original evidence; such material is labeled accordingly.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary record, surviving publication, recording, score, or archival document.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a specialist or institutional source but not independently verified here from the underlying document.
- **Allegation or theory:** an argued attribution or reconstruction for which the evidence remains incomplete.
- **Rumor or myth:** oral tradition, retrospective anecdote, or symbolic story without adequate corroboration.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Canonical name:** Ángel Gregorio Villoldo Arroyo.
- **Name in the original baptismal record:** Ángel Gregorio **Ghigiola Roggio**. Spellings such as *Ghigiolla* also appear in discussion of the record.
- **Later correction:** Villoldo petitioned ecclesiastical authorities to replace the parental surnames in the baptismal entry, changing **Ghigiola to Villoldo** and **Roggio to Arroyo**. The reason remains unresolved.
- **Common professional name:** Ángel Villoldo; frequently printed as Ángel G. Villoldo.
- **Known pseudonyms and attributed pen or recording names:** **Fray Pimiento**, **A. Gregorio**, **Gregorio Giménez**, **Ángel Arroyo**, **Fausto Mendoza**, **T. Barrios**, **Mario Reguero**, and probably **Lope de la Verga**. Not every attribution has been equally well documented.
- **Honorific or posthumous titles:** “El padre del tango,” “El papá del tango,” and “padre del tango canción.” These are retrospective distinctions, not offices held during his lifetime.
- **Occupations:** composer, lyricist, singer, guitarist, harmonica player, popular entertainer, comic reciter, actor or variety performer, writer of dialogues and short theatrical scenes, occasional journalist or periodical contributor, and—according to early records and recollections—printer or typesetter and *cuarteador*.
- **Born:** 16 February 1861, Buenos Aires.
- **Baptized:** 25 February 1861, according to the recovered baptismal record.
- **Probable precise birthplace:** Calle Mayo no. 37, corresponding to present-day Calle 25 de Mayo, central Buenos Aires.
- **Traditional but superseded birthplace:** Barracas. He was closely associated with Barracas, but the surviving baptismal evidence indicates that he was not born there.
- **Died:** 14 October 1919, Buenos Aires, aged 58.
- **Documented or best-supported cause of death:** prostate cancer.
- **Burial:** Cementerio de la Chacarita, Buenos Aires. Since 28 July 1967 his remains and those of his mother have been recorded in the Petenello family vault, not—as frequently claimed—in the SADAIC pantheon.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and the surname problem

Villoldo’s baptismal certificate, discovered in December 2014, records his parents as **Juan Ghigiola** and **Victoria Roggio**. Villoldo subsequently asked the Church to correct these names to **Juan Villoldo** and **Victoria Arroyo**.

The available evidence permits several competing reconstructions:

1. **Documented elements**

   - The child was entered as Ángel Gregorio Ghigiola Roggio.
   - His mother was known in later records as **Victoria Arroyo de Villoldo**.
   - Her death certificate records her death at **Irala 22, La Boca, on 17 May 1898**, reportedly aged 82.
   - Ángel signed that death record and described himself as 37, consistent with an 1861 birth.
   - His sister Irene’s 1888 matrimonial inquiry called her the daughter of the deceased Juan Villoldo and the living Victoria Arroyo.
   - Irene reportedly described Juan as “oriental,” meaning Uruguayan.

2. **Unresolved questions**

   - No baptismal or death record securely identifying Juan Villoldo has been found in Argentina or Uruguay.
   - No marriage record for Juan and Victoria has been located.
   - It is unknown whether *Ghigiola/Roggio* were clerical errors, the parents’ original surnames, or evidence of a more complicated parentage.
   - The surname correction may have regularized the documentary identity under which Ángel had become publicly known.

3. **Theories proposed by Tito Rivadeneira, Osvaldo Cattaneo, and Marcelo Castelo**

   - Juan Ghigiola may have been the biological father and may have abandoned the family.
   - A later partner named Juan Villoldo may have supplied the surname.
   - “Villoldo” may initially have been an artistic or assumed name that Ángel later incorporated into his ecclesiastical identity.
   - The original entry may simply have contained two serious clerical errors.

None of these explanations has been conclusively proven.

### Mother

**Victoria Arroyo**, or Victoria Arroyo de Villoldo, was born in Santa Fe according to the 1895 census. Her age is inconsistent across documents:

- The 1895 census listed her as 75, implying birth around 1820.
- Her 1898 death certificate reportedly gave an age of 82, implying birth around 1816.

The census said that she was widowed, had been married for thirty years, and had borne seven children. These declarations create chronological difficulties and may contain enumeration errors.

No secure occupation has been identified for her. The family appears to have lived in considerable poverty.

### Siblings

- **Irene Villoldo:** documented sister, born approximately 1857; she declared herself aged thirty when preparing to marry in 1888. She remained illiterate. A baptismal certificate has not been located.
- **Petrona Villoldo:** accepted by the family researchers as a documented sister, although substantially less detail survives.
- **Possible siblings or half-siblings:** Juana, Juan, and Victoria have been proposed from census and family evidence, but their relationship to Ángel is uncertain.
- The mother’s 1895 declaration of seven children cannot presently be reconciled with the securely identified children.

### Petenello family

**Reported family tradition:** Spiro or Espiridión Petenello and Luisa María Botto, Italian immigrants who arrived around 1871, found Ángel and Irene in a state of neglect or “picked up the little orphans from the street” and assisted them.

This tradition was transmitted by Petenello descendants interviewed in 2010. No contemporary guardianship record has been found. “Orphans” may have meant that the children had lost their father or were effectively unsupported, since their mother was alive.

Irene later reportedly performed domestic work for both the Petenello and Captain Alfredo Lamas families. The continuing bond is reflected in the eventual transfer of Ángel’s and Victoria’s remains from the Lamas family vault to the Petenello vault.

### Spouse or partner

The 1895 census described Villoldo as married. In the same household was **Victoria Molina**, aged 36, an unmarried Buenos Aires-born laundress or ironer. Researchers have proposed that she was the “Victorina” whom Oscar del Priore identified as Villoldo’s life companion.

- **Documented:** Villoldo declared himself married in the census; Victoria Molina was present and listed as single.
- **Reported interpretation:** the two were a cohabiting couple and Villoldo called himself married to avoid social embarrassment.
- **Unresolved:** no marriage certificate has been found, and the exact nature and duration of their relationship are unknown.

### Children

No securely documented children have been identified. Claims that he left direct descendants require evidence not found in the consulted record.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1861–1880s

Villoldo was born while Buenos Aires was expanding rapidly through immigration, railway and tramway construction, print culture, commercial entertainment, and the consolidation of a mixed urban popular music.

The recovered baptismal document places his birth in central Buenos Aires. His later identity, repertoire, employment, and family residences nevertheless became closely associated with La Boca and Barracas.

The Petenello oral history suggests a childhood of abandonment or severe material insecurity. The following can presently be said:

- **Documented:** the family was in Buenos Aires; his mother survived until 1898; Ángel learned to read and write.
- **Reported fact:** the family was extremely poor.
- **Reported family tradition:** the Petenellos sheltered Ángel and Irene.
- **Unknown:** where he attended school, how long he remained in formal education, and who taught him music.

No conservatory training has been documented. His career and surviving performances suggest largely practical formation through street, café, carnival, theatre, circus, payador, and popular-print culture.

---

## Education and Formation

No identified school, conservatory, formal music teacher, or literary mentor can be securely assigned to Villoldo.

His formation appears to have included:

- guitar-based criollo song and payador practice;
- Spanish *tanguillo*, *cuplé*, zarzuela, and comic-song traditions;
- Afro-Rioplatense and habanera-derived rhythmic idioms circulating in Buenos Aires;
- carnival comparsas and their topical verses;
- popular theatre, café-concert, circus, and variety performance;
- newspapers, cheap song sheets, and illustrated magazines;
- the everyday speech of conventillos, laborers, immigrants, street characters, dancers, and petty hustlers.

Later accounts call him a transformer rather than the sole inventor of tango: he recast Spanish and Atlantic forms into a specifically porteño comic and dance-song language.

He played or was credited with playing guitar, harmonica, piano, violin, and concertina, and was known as a capable whistler. His characteristic performance apparatus fixed a harmonica above the guitar so that he could play both; when singing, he removed or moved away from the harmonica.

## Early Employment and Career

Villoldo accepted varied work before or alongside his musical career.

### Typesetter and printing work

The **1895 national census** lists him as a **tipógrafo**, providing firm contemporary evidence for employment in printing. Printing work would also have placed him close to the world of song sheets, periodicals, publishers, and inexpensive urban literature.

### Cuarteador

Later biographical accounts describe him as a *cuarteador*: a mounted worker who waited near muddy roads or steep inclines and used his horse and rope to help heavy carts or trams move. This occupation is plausible in the nineteenth-century geography of Buenos Aires but has not been substantiated here by a contemporary employment record.

### Circus, clowning, and miscellaneous work

He is repeatedly described as having been a circus clown or performer. The broad claim is consistent with his comic recitations and variety-stage career, but details of particular circuses, contracts, or dates remain sparse.

### Popular writer and carnival author

Villoldo wrote verses for carnival comparsas and short topical compositions. He later contributed poems, prose sketches, dialogues, and scenes to periodicals including:

- *Caras y Caretas*
- *P.B.T.*
- *Fray Mocho*

Some pieces circulated under pseudonyms. The precise periodical bibliography remains incompletely indexed.

### 1889 publication

Biographical sources say that he published a collection of his own criollo songs or verses for guitar in **1889**. The title is often rendered generically as *Cantos criollos*. No consulted source supplied a fully verified title-page transcription, publisher, and pagination, so those details should remain provisional.

## Chronological Life History

### 1890s: printer, companion, performer

On 10 March 1895 the census recorded Villoldo in Buenos Aires as an Argentine-born typesetter, aged 35, and married. His stated age was one year too high if the 1861 birth date is used. Victoria Molina, 36, single, and employed in laundry or ironing work, was enumerated in the household.

By the later 1890s Villoldo was active in popular entertainment. The record points to performances in modest cafés, carnival environments, theatres, and other venues where recitation, song, comedy, instrumental music, novelty acts, and dance shared a program.

His mother died in La Boca in 1898. Villoldo signed the certificate.

### 1900–1902: early phonography and popular entertainment

Buenos Aires became a recording site for cylinders and discs at the beginning of the century. Villoldo participated early in this market as singer, reciter, guitarist, songwriter, and sometimes pseudonymous performer.

Later discographical research identifies him or “Gregorio Giménez” among soloists recorded in the first wave of criollo phonography. Some recordings consisted of comic dialogues or deliberately coarse verses made for private or commercial consumption.

The 18 October 1902 issue of *Caras y Caretas* advertised the cylinder or record **“La carcajada del negro Juan”**, reportedly selling one hundred copies in one day. The documentation shows an active market for racialized comic material. Whether every title later assigned to Villoldo was written, merely performed, or announced by him requires item-level discographical checking.

### 1903: breakthrough year

Villoldo’s best-known early works emerged in the period around 1903:

- *El porteñito*
- *El esquinazo*
- *El choclo*
- *Yunta brava*
- numerous other tangos and comic numbers

At the café-concert **Variedades**, Rivadavia in the 1200 block, he reportedly appeared on mixed bills that included Pepita Avellaneda and the future actor Florencio Parravicini.

#### Premiere of *El choclo*

The best-established public premiere occurred on **3 November 1903** at the elegant restaurant **El Americano**, Cangallo 966, Buenos Aires. The orchestra was led by **José Luis Roncallo**, who is reported to have transcribed or arranged the music.

Because tango was still considered unsuitable for a refined restaurant, the program presented it as a **“danza criolla.”** The preserved venue program supports the date and designation.

Villoldo’s initial text concerned the corn cob as an especially satisfying component of a stew. He later supplied a different lyric, *Cariño puro*. Other lyrics appeared after his death.

### 1904: urban commentary and the tango scene

A March 1904 *Caras y Caretas* article signed “Goyo Cuello,” entitled “Baile de moda,” described tango as the libertine master of carnival dance programs. It illustrates the social environment in which Villoldo’s work circulated, although authorship by Villoldo has not been established.

Villoldo’s songs presented compadritos, dancers, urban workers, policemen, immigrants, flirtation, sexuality, alcohol, rent increases, technology, electoral and commercial trickery, and the small humiliations of city life. Their narrators are frequently boastful or unreliable comic characters.

### 1905: publication of *El choclo* and creation of *La morocha*

*El choclo* reached print around 1905. Villoldo reportedly said it was the first tango he had printed and that he sold its performance rights. Later histories state that authors commonly received as little as **25 pesos per piece**, leaving Villoldo without the later international revenue his music generated.

#### *La morocha*

The music was written by **Enrique Saborido**; Villoldo supplied the words. Saborido’s retrospective account says the lyric was produced rapidly at the Bar Ronchetti, at Reconquista and Lavalle, after a social challenge to produce a tango suitable for singing.

The heroine was reportedly modeled on the Uruguayan dancer **Lola Candales**.

Competing premiere accounts name:

- **Lola Candales**, according to Saborido’s recollection; or
- **Flora Rodríguez de Gobbi**, according to another tradition.

The song’s comparatively decorous feminine voice helped it cross from popular entertainment into broader domestic and international circulation. Claims that 280,000 copies of the sheet music were printed or sold are widely repeated but should be treated as reported commercial figures rather than audited sales data.

### 1906: international circulation of *La morocha*

Flora and Alfredo Gobbi recorded *La morocha*, generally dated to 1906. Copies of the score reportedly traveled aboard the Argentine training frigate **ARA Presidente Sarmiento**.

The often-repeated claim that this single voyage made it the first Argentine tango heard in Europe is difficult to prove. Tangos and tango-like pieces were already circulating through maritime, theatrical, publishing, and commercial networks. The frigate story remains historically important as a national origin narrative.

Villoldo’s *Carbonada criolla*, published in Buenos Aires by Luis F. Rivarola around 1906, was dedicated to *Caras y Caretas* director Carlos Correa Luna.

### 1907 and the disputed Paris journey

The standard biography states that the Buenos Aires department store **Gath & Chaves** contracted Villoldo, **Alfredo Eusebio Gobbi**, and **Flora Rodríguez de Gobbi** to travel to Paris and make records for the store’s label. Villoldo is also said to have danced tango, recorded extensively, and registered with the French authors’ society.

There is evidence that:

- Villoldo gave the French society his birth date as 16 February 1861.
- Gath & Chaves marketed records featuring the Gobbis, Villoldo’s works, and other Argentine performers.
- European orchestras recorded Villoldo compositions.
- Villoldo’s music circulated in France and elsewhere before the First World War.

However, recent phonographic research raises a serious qualification:

- Surviving labels call Gath & Chaves a **sociedad anónima**, a corporate status reportedly not formalized until 1908.
- The voice announcing many of the recordings traditionally attributed to Villoldo appears to be Alfredo Gobbi.
- Some discographical series fit **1909** better than 1907.
- The earliest Bibliothèque nationale de France evidence located by a recent researcher places Gobbi publications in 1910 and a European *El choclo* publication in 1909.

**Assessment:** a Villoldo–Gobbi recording relationship and European commercial circulation are documented. Villoldo’s personal 1907 presence in Paris is a strong traditional report supported indirectly by the French authors’ registration, but the date, extent of his recording participation, and identification of particular Paris sessions require further archival verification.

On **26 December 1907**, Villoldo recorded *El negro alegre* in Buenos Aires for Victor, singing and accompanying himself on guitar; the surviving discographical entry gives matrix F-27 and issue 62133.

### 1908–1910: recordings, adaptations, and topical songs

Villoldo recorded for companies including Victor, Columbia, and Odeon or their local commercial partners. Collaborators included:

- pianist **Manuel Campoamor**;
- singer and actress **Lola Membrives**;
- reciter or singer **José María Silva**;
- **Eugenio López**;
- Alfredo and Flora Gobbi;
- unnamed house orchestras and studio musicians.

His output crossed genre boundaries: tango, milonga, estilo, zamba, vals, mazurka, polka, marcha, jota, maxixe, fado, furlana, fox-trot, comic scene, dialogue, monologue, and topical recitation.

Representative recordings include *El negro alegre*, *La suba de alquileres*, *Remate de muebles importados*, *Los dos paisanos*, *Remate de rezagos*, *La farra de Rosa “La Piojito”*, and *El criollo más criollo*.

#### *La bicicleta*: attribution controversy

Villoldo recorded *La bicicleta* with Manuel Campoamor in 1909 and later registered or circulated it under his name.

Research into Cádiz carnival repertory identifies an earlier song of the same title written by **Antonio Rodríguez Martínez, “El Tío de la Tiza,”** for the 1897 comparsa *Los Abanicos*. Its text appeared in Cádiz printing and in *Blanco y Negro* in 1898. Villoldo’s text makes localized substitutions—Avenida de Mayo for Cádiz’s Alameda and pesos for pesetas—but retains substantial material.

- **Documented:** the Cádiz text predates Villoldo’s recording.
- **Strong scholarly attribution:** Antonio Rodríguez Martínez was the original author.
- **Open interpretive issue:** whether Villoldo understood his version as an adaptation within a permissive oral and theatrical borrowing culture, or deliberately represented another author’s work as his own.
- **Not documented:** a lawsuit, criminal charge, or contemporary adjudication against Villoldo.
- Calling the episode “plagiarism” is a modern evaluative conclusion; the textual dependency itself is the firmer finding.

### 1910–1911: copyright registration and military enrolment

The first Argentine intellectual-property law enabled more formal registration. Surviving research gives the following example:

- *El porteñito*—music, tango criollo; registration no. 399, **20 February 1911**.

Villoldo registered numerous compositions, texts, dialogues, and arrangements. Attribution problems nevertheless persist because early discs could credit only the composer, omit lyricists, conflate performer and author, or reproduce older oral material.

In 1911 he obtained a military enrolment booklet under Law 8129:

- Region I, Military District no. 3;
- individual registration no. 75;
- birth declared as 16 February 1861 in the Federal Capital;
- signature and right-thumb impression preserved.

This independently confirms the birth date and city.

### 1912–1915: international afterlife during his lifetime

By 1912 *El choclo* was being issued in Germany as an “Argentinischer Original Tango,” under a French publishing license. European bands and dance orchestras adopted it.

Villoldo continued publishing topical and theatrical material. He wrote lyrics for music by other composers, including:

- *La morocha* — Enrique Saborido;
- *El trece* — Alberico Spatola;
- *El entrerriano* — Rosendo Mendizábal;
- *Apolo* — Alfredo Bevilacqua;
- pieces associated with A. F. Bellomo and other composers.

*El trece* dates from 1913; a related *El catorce* followed around 1914. *Trigo limpio* is dated to approximately 1915.

### 1916–1918: teaching publication and final compositions

In 1916 Villoldo published **Cantos populares argentinos**, a patriotic collection associated with the centenary of Argentina’s declaration of independence.

In 1917 Casa América issued his **Método América**, a method for learning guitar through numerical or symbolic notation. It represents his clearest documented pedagogical project.

The Gardel–Razzano duo selected Villoldo’s **Cantar eterno** for the beginning of its 1917 recording cycle for Max Glücksmann’s Nacional/Odeon enterprise. It was recorded in April 1917, with José Ricardo associated with the guitar accompaniment. Gardel later revisited the composition.

The often-repeated assertion that *Cantar eterno* was Villoldo’s final or posthumous composition is false. In 1918 he published:

- *Mentira*, with words by Luis Roldán and music credited to Villoldo; and
- the fox-trot *Atlántida*.

Other registrations from the period include *Beso criollo* and *Cariño gaucho*.

### 1919: illness and death

Villoldo died in Buenos Aires on **14 October 1919**.

A later medical-certificate-based investigation by Tito Rivadeneira identifies **prostate cancer** as the cause. Documentation of his final treatment, hospital, and exact place of death has not been located in the sources consulted.

He died poor despite the success and international circulation of his works. The immature copyright and royalty system, outright sales of compositions, uncertain foreign registrations, and the commercial power of publishers and record companies prevented fame from becoming durable wealth.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Publishers

Villoldo’s music circulated through publishers including:

- Luis F. Rivarola;
- Breyer Hermanos;
- Casa América;
- European firms such as Salabert and associated German licensees;
- additional local sheet-music houses whose item-level catalogues require reconstruction.

A 1903 Breyer Hermanos catalogue contained 82 tango titles by nineteen composers; Villoldo was responsible for seventeen, making him the most heavily represented composer in that batch.

### Record companies and retailers

His performances or compositions appeared through:

- Victor Talking Machine Company;
- Columbia;
- Odeon;
- Gath & Chaves;
- local agents and retailers such as Casa Tagini;
- European military, salon, and studio orchestras.

Authorship, performance, matrix, and recording-place metadata are inconsistent across early records.

### Musical technology

Villoldo participated in the transition from:

- printed song sheets and live café performance;
- wax cylinders;
- single-sided acoustic discs;
- larger 78-rpm commercial disc systems.

He also wrote topical lyrics about telephones, microphones, phonographs, cinematographs, bicycles, electricity, and invented mock machines. Technology in these pieces is both urban spectacle and comic evidence that modern life is becoming absurd.

### Copyright and finance

Villoldo was not known to own a major business, investment portfolio, patent, or substantial property.

The major financial pattern was unfavorable:

- he sold some works outright;
- formal royalty collection was rudimentary;
- foreign performances were difficult to monitor;
- later publishers and performers derived value from internationally successful works;
- he remained economically insecure.

Francisco Canaro later recalled composers selling tangos for approximately 25 pesos each. This is contextual testimony, not an itemized account of every Villoldo transaction.

## Artistic, Intellectual, and Political Development

### Artistic position

Villoldo’s work belongs to the **Guardia Vieja**, before the fully developed tango-song associated with Pascual Contursi and Carlos Gardel.

His characteristic early tango was:

- rhythmically close to tango-milonga and habanera;
- generally in 2/4;
- suited to dancing;
- comic, boastful, satirical, and topical;
- indebted to *cuplé*, Spanish *tanguillo*, carnival, and criollo song;
- structured around recognizable urban social types.

He did not invent tango single-handedly. His importance lies in consolidating music, lyrics, performance persona, print, and phonography into a highly portable popular form.

### Social commentary

Works such as *Matufias o el arte de vivir* catalogue fraud, bribery, influence, speculative schemes, adulterated products, and other tactics for surviving modern urban society. *La suba de alquileres* addresses housing costs. Other pieces stage immigrant speech, relations between men and women, working life, police, and social aspiration.

Modern writers have called him a precursor of the protest tango. That is a retrospective category: his satire was topical and socially observant, but no stable revolutionary program follows from it.

### Political material

Villoldo wrote patriotic and historical pieces and a composition honoring **Aparicio Saravia**, the Uruguayan National Party caudillo. This demonstrates engagement with contemporary regional political symbolism, but it does not by itself prove formal party membership.

No documented political office, imprisonment, exile, intelligence relationship, participation in political violence, or membership in an anarchist, socialist, conservative, or nationalist organization was located.

### Religion and philosophy

The baptismal record establishes Catholic baptism. Religious imagery appears in works such as *Cantar eterno*, whose speaker sings to a “Santo Cristo.” No evidence establishes sustained church activity, formal theological commitment, conversion, anti-clerical organization, or a systematic philosophical position.

### Race, class, and gender in the repertoire

Some surviving titles and performance conventions use racial caricature and ethnic imitation, including *El negro alegre* and *La carcajada del negro Juan*. Immigrant dialects and women were also frequent objects of parody. These materials document the racial, sexual, and class hierarchies of Buenos Aires popular entertainment.

They should not automatically be read as literal statements of Villoldo’s private beliefs; they were commercial stage personae. Nor should their content be omitted: racialized comedy and sexual innuendo were part of his repertoire and market.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### José Luis Roncallo

Orchestra leader, transcriber or arranger, and central collaborator in the first documented public success of *El choclo*. His orchestra premiered it at El Americano in 1903.

### Enrique Saborido

Composer of *La morocha*. Villoldo wrote its lyric. Their collaboration produced one of the earliest internationally successful tango songs.

### Alfredo Eusebio Gobbi and Flora Rodríguez de Gobbi

Performers, recording collaborators, and central agents in the international circulation of Villoldo’s music. They recorded *La morocha* and *Cariño puro*. The traditional account places all three in Paris in 1907, although session chronology is disputed.

### Lola Candales

Uruguayan dancer traditionally identified as the model and first singer of *La morocha*. The first-performance attribution competes with one naming Flora Gobbi.

### Manuel Campoamor

Pianist who accompanied Villoldo on recordings, including the 1909 version of *La bicicleta*.

### Lola Membrives

Actress and singer who recorded with Villoldo and later became a major interpreter of Hispanic theatre and song.

### Periodical and publishing network

Villoldo’s professional circle included editors, theatre operators, printers, sheet-music publishers, recording technicians, café proprietors, payadores, dancers, actors, and comic reciters. Carlos Correa Luna, director of *Caras y Caretas*, received the dedication of *Carbonada criolla*.

### Carlos Gardel and José Razzano

They recorded *Cantar eterno* in 1917. The recording documents artistic transmission, but no evidence consulted establishes a close personal friendship or mentor–student relationship.

### Petenello and Lamas families

The Petenellos were remembered as childhood protectors; the Lamas family employed Irene and first housed Ángel’s and Victoria’s remains. These relationships appear to have extended beyond ordinary professional acquaintance.

## Controversies, Allegations, Authorship Disputes, and Financial Questions

### 1. Identity and parentage

- **Documented:** baptism as Ángel Gregorio Ghigiola Roggio; later surname correction.
- **Unresolved:** biological parentage, legitimacy, and the identity of Juan Ghigiola/Villoldo.
- **Unsupported overstatement:** treating either the corrected or original surnames as a complete solution to his ancestry.

### 2. Birth in Barracas

- **Traditional claim:** he was born in Barracas.
- **Evidence against:** the baptismal certificate gives Calle Mayo 37 in central Buenos Aires.
- **Assessment:** Barracas is a documented later association, not the best-supported birthplace.

### 3. The Paris journey

- **Traditional reported fact:** Gath & Chaves sent Villoldo and the Gobbis to Paris in 1907.
- **Supporting evidence:** later biographies, French authors’ registration, and European dissemination.
- **Complication:** label chronology and voice identification suggest that recordings assigned to this trip may date from 1909 and feature Alfredo Gobbi as announcer.
- **Assessment:** European circulation is certain; Villoldo’s exact itinerary and recording role remain incompletely reconstructed.

### 4. *La bicicleta*

- **Documented:** Antonio Rodríguez Martínez’s Cádiz composition predates Villoldo’s adaptation.
- **Strongly supported finding:** Villoldo’s version derives from the Cádiz work.
- **Disputed characterization:** “plagiarism” versus adaptation under contemporary popular-theatre conventions.
- **No evidence found:** lawsuit, indictment, conviction, or contemporary judicial finding.

### 5. Authorship of *El choclo*

The standard documentary attribution is to Villoldo. A later counterclaim attributes the melody, or an earlier version of it, to Afro-Argentine violinist **Casimiro Alcorta**, who died in poverty.

- No manuscript or contemporary registration consulted here conclusively transfers authorship to Alcorta.
- The claim is historically significant because Black musicians’ contributions were frequently erased from written tango history.
- At present it should be labeled an **attribution theory**, not an established finding.

A different *El choclo* was registered in Spain in 1909 under Manuel Sarrablo y Clavero. Specialist cataloguing suggests that it may be a separate composition in Villoldo’s stylistic line rather than proof that Sarrablo wrote Villoldo’s melody.

### 6. Sale and foreign exploitation of *El choclo*

Villoldo reportedly sold execution rights in 1905 for a small sum. Poor documentation of royalties allowed later international uses to generate limited benefit for him or his estate.

In 1952 Lester Allen and Robert Hill adapted the melody as **“Kiss of Fire.”** Early foreign editions and labels did not always credit Villoldo adequately. Subsequent publications acknowledged the underlying composition.

Accounts sometimes describe a SADAIC lawsuit or “intense proceedings,” but the consulted sources do not provide a case number, pleadings, judgment, or settlement. The credit dispute is documented; the exact legal mechanism remains to be established.

### 7. Personal criminality, fraud, and scandal

A dedicated adversarial search in Spanish and English—including material published in 2024–2026—found no credible evidence that Villoldo was arrested, prosecuted, convicted, accused of financial fraud, implicated in corruption or money laundering, attached to an intelligence service, or involved in organized political violence.

His songs describe fraud, sex work, petty crime, police, and urban scams. These fictional or satirical narrators are not evidence of his own criminal conduct.

### 8. Poverty

His impoverished death is consistently reported and fits the known copyright economy. Exact assets, debts, rent, royalty income, publisher payments, and probate records were not located.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “Father of tango”

- **Story:** Villoldo invented tango or was its singular father.
- **Circulation:** twentieth-century tango histories, cultural institutions, and commemorations; one source says an Argentine authors’ organization formally gave him the title in 1970.
- **Evidence:** he was exceptionally prolific and important in joining words, music, stage performance, print, and records.
- **Against literal interpretation:** tango developed collectively from many musicians, dancers, communities, and Atlantic musical forms.
- **Effect:** the title guaranteed Villoldo canonical status while obscuring other founders.

### *El choclo* disguised as “danza criolla”

- **Story:** Roncallo had to conceal the tango’s identity to perform it in an elegant restaurant.
- **Evidence:** the 1903 program used the designation “danza criolla”; the precise motives are preserved through later testimony.
- **Assessment:** the labeling is documented; the dialogue and dramatic details are partly retrospective.

### Meaning of “El choclo”

Competing stories include:

1. Villoldo meant the corn cob, “the tastiest ingredient of the stew.”
2. “El Choclo” was a red- or blond-haired compadrito or pimp near Junín and Lavalle; Irene Villoldo reportedly supported this explanation.
3. *Choclo* was a phallic reference arising from a brothel setting.

The first is consistent with Villoldo’s original food-based lyric. The second is family testimony recorded later. The third belongs to sexual folklore and is not established by contemporary documentation.

### The Argentine anthem at the German front

- **Story:** during the First World War, musicians honoring Argentine journalist Tito Livio Foppa did not know Argentina’s anthem and played *El choclo*, believing it to be the national tune.
- **Source of circulation:** Foppa and later tango biographies.
- **Evidence:** no contemporaneous military program or press report was located.
- **Significance:** the story dramatizes the melody’s international recognizability.
- **Assessment:** reported anecdote, not independently verified fact.

### The frigate *Sarmiento* carried tango to Europe and Japan

- **Story:** sailors carried *La morocha* or *El choclo* abroad in 1906, beginning tango’s international conquest.
- **Evidence:** scores may indeed have traveled aboard the ship.
- **Against exclusivity:** commercial travelers, immigrants, publishers, theatre companies, records, and other vessels also transmitted the music.
- **Assessment:** plausible episode inflated into a single-origin legend.

### The tramway death

- **Story:** Villoldo died after colliding with a tram.
- **Evidence:** Rivadeneira reports that Villoldo did have a tram accident, but earlier than his death.
- **Against:** a medical certificate reportedly identifies prostate cancer.
- **Assessment:** false causal legend probably created by merging two separate events.

### Death “of grief” beside an amnesiac sex worker

A modern discussion of tango and sexuality repeats a story that Villoldo loved a sex worker who lost her memory, remembered everything except him, and that he died of grief without writing a tango for her.

No names, contemporary documents, medical evidence, or traceable early account accompany the story. It should be treated as **romantic folklore**, not biography.

### Burial in the SADAIC pantheon

- **Story:** Villoldo rests in SADAIC’s Chacarita pantheon.
- **Documentary correction:** cemetery records inspected by researchers place him and his mother in the Petenello family vault from 1967.
- **Effect:** the mistaken version reinforced his symbolic institutional adoption as “father of tango.”

## Films, Recordings, Performances, and Archival Material

### Villoldo as historical recording artist

His surviving acoustic records are the most direct audiovisual evidence of his performance style. They preserve a relatively high, light voice, direct guitar accompaniment, speech-song transitions, comic timing, and early tango rhythm.

Useful repositories and collections include:

- Biblioteca Nacional Mariano Moreno, Music and Audiovisual Media Department;
- historical-disc transfer collections;
- Todo Tango’s score, lyric, and recording catalogue;
- Library of Congress National Jukebox for early recordings of his compositions;
- specialist discographies of Victor, Columbia, Odeon, Gath & Chaves, and the Gramophone Company;
- Wikimedia Commons public-domain audio.

### Later screen use of his music

- *Gran Casino* (Mexico, 1947), directed by Luis Buñuel: Libertad Lamarque performs *El choclo* with Enrique Santos Discépolo’s lyric; credits also associate Carlos Marambio Catán with the song.
- *La historia del tango* (Argentina, 1949): Tita Merello performs *El choclo*.
- *Kiss of Fire* (United States, 1955), directed by Joseph M. Newman: its title capitalized on the popularity of the English adaptation, although the film is not a Villoldo biography.
- Villoldo’s music has appeared in later films and tango documentaries, including *Si sos brujo: una historia de tango*.
- *Ciudad: 1900* (1968), staged by Teatro Libre Florencio Sánchez, incorporated texts by Villoldo and other popular writers.

No full-length, widely distributed documentary devoted exclusively to Villoldo was located in the consulted sources.

## Death, Burial, and Posthumous Reputation

Villoldo died poor on 14 October 1919, reportedly of prostate cancer. His remains initially rested in the Lamas family vault at Chacarita.

On 28 July 1967:

- Ángel’s coffin and his mother Victoria’s were transferred;
- both were entered in the Petenello family vault;
- the cemetery’s family burial booklet records the transfer.

His reputation underwent several stages:

1. **1919–1940s:** survival through scores, early recordings, dance orchestras, Gardel’s repertoire, and continuing performance of *El choclo*.
2. **1947:** Discépolo’s new lyric and Lamarque’s performance in *Gran Casino* transformed *El choclo* into a retrospective anthem about tango itself.
3. **1952–1950s:** *Kiss of Fire* made the melody an international popular standard. Georgia Gibbs topped the American chart; versions were recorded by Tony Martin, Billy Eckstine, Louis Armstrong, Guy Lombardo, Nat King Cole, and others.
4. **Mid- to late twentieth century:** tango historians institutionalized Villoldo as “father of tango,” often repeating an uncertain Barracas birthplace and Paris itinerary.
5. **1967 onward:** the rediscovery and transfer of his remains strengthened memorial attention.
6. **1970:** an Argentine authors’ organization reportedly declared him “Padre del Tango.”
7. **Twenty-first century:** family-document research corrected his birthplace and reopened questions of parentage; phonographic research questioned the standard Paris-session chronology; Cádiz research reassigned *La bicicleta* to Antonio Rodríguez Martínez; musicology placed Villoldo within Atlantic, racialized, theatrical, and technological networks rather than treating him as an isolated inventor.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity or participation in “tango” is excluded.

### Carlos Gardel — documented artistic transmission

In April 1917, the Gardel–Razzano duo recorded Villoldo’s *Cantar eterno* at the opening of its new cycle for Max Glücksmann’s record enterprise in Buenos Aires. Gardel later recorded the work again. This demonstrates direct repertorial use; a personal friendship or meeting has not been established.

### Max Glücksmann — documented commercial connection through repertoire

Gardel and Razzano recorded *Cantar eterno* for Glücksmann’s Nacional/Odeon operation. Villoldo’s composition therefore entered Glücksmann’s catalogue and distribution system. The evidence does not establish a separate personal contract between Villoldo and Glücksmann.

### Enrique Santos Discépolo — documented posthumous collaboration

In 1947 Discépolo wrote the now-canonical lyric for Villoldo’s *El choclo*. Villoldo had been dead for twenty-eight years, so this was not a personal collaboration. The new lyric was introduced by Libertad Lamarque in *Gran Casino* and profoundly reshaped the work’s meaning and reputation.

### Ada Falcón — documented posthumous/interpretive repertorial connection

Ada Falcón recorded or performed *La morocha*, whose words were by Villoldo and music by Enrique Saborido. No personal meeting with Villoldo is documented; she was a child during his last years.

### Astor Piazzolla — no documented personal connection found

Piazzolla was born in 1921, after Villoldo’s death. Both belong to tango history, but no specific documented compositional borrowing, correspondence, family relation, or explicit acknowledgment connecting them was located.

### Osvaldo Pugliese — no documented personal connection found

Pugliese was born in 1905 and was fourteen when Villoldo died. Shared inclusion in tango’s historical canon and burial in Chacarita does not establish a personal relationship.

### Enrique Santos Discépolo, Carlos Gardel, Max Glücksmann, and Ada Falcón are the only roster members for whom a specific supported link emerged

No supported direct connection was found with Jorge Luis Borges, Alfonsina Storni, Victoria Ocampo, Xul Solar, Carlos María della Paolera, the Buenos Aires architects on the roster, or the remaining listed figures. Their presence in the same city, historical period, or cultural mythology is insufficient evidence.

## Compact Chronology

- **16 February 1861:** born at Calle Mayo 37, Buenos Aires, according to the later-recovered baptismal record.
- **25 February 1861:** baptized as Ángel Gregorio Ghigiola Roggio.
- **Circa 1871–1875:** according to Petenello family tradition, Ángel and Irene receive assistance or shelter from Spiro Petenello and Luisa Botto.
- **1888:** sister Irene’s matrimonial inquiry names the parents as Juan Villoldo and Victoria Arroyo.
- **1889:** reported publication of a collection of criollo songs or verses.
- **10 March 1895:** census lists Villoldo as a Buenos Aires-born typesetter, aged 35 and married; Victoria Molina appears in the household.
- **17 May 1898:** his mother Victoria dies at Irala 22, La Boca; Ángel signs the record.
- **Circa 1900–1902:** active in early phonographic recording, comic recitation, cafés, and popular theatre.
- **18 October 1902:** *Caras y Caretas* advertises “La carcajada del negro Juan” in the expanding criollo-record market.
- **1903:** *El porteñito*, *El esquinazo*, *Yunta brava*, and other works circulate.
- **3 November 1903:** Roncallo’s orchestra premieres *El choclo* as a “danza criolla” at El Americano.
- **1904:** Villoldo’s urban and dance repertory expands; *La caprichosa* is dated to this period.
- **1905:** *El choclo* published; Villoldo writes the lyric of Saborido’s *La morocha*; writes *Homenaje a Saravia*.
- **1906:** *La morocha* recorded by the Gobbis; *Carbonada criolla* published approximately this year.
- **1907:** traditional date of the Villoldo–Gobbi Paris expedition; exact discography disputed. On 26 December Villoldo records *El negro alegre* in Buenos Aires for Victor.
- **1908–1910:** extensive recording and variety work; records *La bicicleta* with Manuel Campoamor in 1909.
- **1910–1911:** formal intellectual-property registrations begin.
- **20 February 1911:** *El porteñito* music registered under no. 399.
- **1911:** military enrolment booklet confirms birth on 16 February 1861 in the Federal Capital.
- **1912:** *El choclo* issued in Germany under a French license.
- **1913–1915:** *El trece*, *El catorce*, *Trigo limpio*, and other later works.
- **1916:** publication of *Cantos populares argentinos*.
- **April 1917:** Gardel–Razzano record *Cantar eterno*.
- **1917:** Casa América publishes *Método América*.
- **1918:** publication or registration of *Mentira*, *Atlántida*, *Beso criollo*, and other late works.
- **14 October 1919:** dies in Buenos Aires, best-supported cause prostate cancer.
- **28 July 1967:** his and his mother’s remains transferred to the Petenello vault at Chacarita.
- **1970:** reportedly designated “Padre del Tango” by an Argentine authors’ organization.
- **2011:** Rivadeneira and Cattaneo publish their documentary investigation of his origins.
- **2014:** Rivadeneira publishes the major varieté study; Villoldo’s baptismal record is discovered.
- **2016 onward:** the baptismal findings and surname correction enter scholarly circulation.
- **2023–2026:** new studies reinforce Antonio Rodríguez Martínez’s authorship of *La bicicleta* and re-examine the chronology of early transatlantic tango recording.
