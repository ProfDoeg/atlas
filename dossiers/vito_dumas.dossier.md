# Vito Dumas: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier concerns the Argentine sailor, athlete, writer, and visual artist **Vito Dumas (1900–1965)**. It distinguishes among:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporaneous records, institutional publications, or multiple independent sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated in later biographies or family testimony but not independently verified here.
- **Claim or interpretation:** an author’s explanation of events.
- **Rumor or myth:** historically circulated but unsupported as a factual proposition.
- **Unresolved:** sources conflict or the surviving evidence is insufficient.

The principal source limitations are substantial. Dumas guarded his private life; no comprehensive critical edition of his papers has been identified; some personal writings and possessions were reportedly destroyed; and later commemorative literature frequently repeats inconsistent dates and measurements. His own books remain indispensable but are autobiographical narratives rather than neutral logs.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Vito Dumas.
- **Alternative forms:** no securely documented additional given names.
- **Nationality:** Argentine.
- **Occupations:** ocean sailor, swimming instructor, competitive and endurance swimmer, writer, farmer/agricultural worker, painter, draftsman, sculptor, and briefly an actor according to later biographical accounts.
- **Naval status:** appointed **teniente de navío de la reserva**—lieutenant in the Argentine naval reserve—by presidential decree dated 27 August 1949. This was an honorary/reserve appointment recognizing his maritime record, not evidence of a conventional career as an active-duty naval officer.
- **Titles and epithets:** *El Navegante Solitario*; *el ermitaño de los mares*; retrospectively *el innombrable* within the Río de la Plata yachting superstition.
- **Born:** **26 September 1900**, Palermo, Buenos Aires, Argentina.
- **Died:** **28 March 1965**, at his home in Vicente López, Buenos Aires Province.
- **Cause of death:** a later technical-biographical study gives **coronary thrombosis**. Some derivative sources instead say stroke; the coronary-thrombosis account is more specific but the original death certificate was not located.
- **Burial:** initially reported as buried in Olivos. In September 2000 his remains were ceremonially transferred by water and then reinterred in the Naval Pantheon at Chacarita Cemetery, Buenos Aires.
- **Physical description:** his wife described him as approximately 1.75 metres tall, athletic, with dark-brown hair, light skin, broad forehead, and green eyes.
- **Known boats:** *Atlántida*, *Lehg*, *Lehg II*, *Sirio*, and *Sirio II*.
- **Aggregate achievement:** four major solo ocean voyages between 1931 and 1955, totaling approximately 50,835 nautical miles and 699 elapsed days according to the 2000 official commemoration.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and sibling

- **Father:** Victorio Dumas, described in later sources as Italian or of Italian origin.
- **Mother:** Vicenta Grillo, Argentine, reportedly from Dolores in Buenos Aires Province.
- **Sibling:** a brother named **Remo Dumas**.

The family was initially described as comfortably situated. Its finances subsequently deteriorated, probably during Dumas’s childhood or adolescence. Dumas later recalled having to clean floors, run errands, and polish shop fittings while still young. No reliable evidence was found establishing the family’s precise business, landed property, or cause of financial loss.

Dumas reportedly spent part of his childhood on rural properties around **Trenque Lauquen and Salliqueló**, west of Buenos Aires, before returning to Palermo for primary school. His later repeated retreats to farms in Buenos Aires Province may therefore have continued an early rural connection.

### Marriage and descendants

- **Wife:** **Adela Navarro**, reportedly eight years younger than Dumas.
- **Son:** **Vito Diego Dumas**, an architect.
- **Grandchildren reported in derivative biographical sources:** María Isabel, Diego, Virginia, and Cecilia Dumas. Only Diego Dumas, who appeared in the 2019 documentary, was independently corroborated during this research.

The timing of Dumas’s marriage, periods of cohabitation and separation, and the effect of his long absences on the marriage are inadequately documented. There is no reliable evidence in the consulted material of additional spouses, acknowledged partners, or other children.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1900–1916

Dumas was born in Palermo when it was still a rapidly expanding Buenos Aires district. Later biographical accounts place parts of his childhood in rural Buenos Aires Province and then at a Palermo primary school.

His family’s loss of means interrupted the conventional education available to him. One account says that at fourteen he left formal study to work; another says that he continued secondary studies while doing menial jobs. These versions can be reconciled only tentatively: he may have continued intermittently without graduating. Later accounts consistently state that he lacked a completed secondary-school credential.

The later social interpretation that Dumas entered elite yachting as an outsider rests partly on this educational and economic background. The underlying facts—financial decline, youthful work, incomplete secondary education—are reasonably well attested; the precise degree to which individual yacht-club members opposed him on class grounds is less directly documented.

## Education and Formation

### Sport

At approximately sixteen, Dumas joined the **Asociación Cristiana de Jóvenes**, where he trained in gymnastics, swimming, athletics, wrestling, and boxing. Swimming became his principal discipline.

He subsequently taught swimming at several institutions:

- Club de Gimnasia y Esgrima de Buenos Aires;
- Asociación Cristiana de Jóvenes;
- Colegio Militar de la Nación;
- Club Sportivo Barracas;
- reportedly, free instruction at the Parque Avellaneda pool.

In 1923 he remained in the water for more than twenty-two hours during an attempted swim between Colonia del Sacramento and Buenos Aires. Institutional and retrospective accounts describe this as a world endurance record. Some sources date the achievement to 1925, but the Centro Naval’s history and the detailed later study favor **1923**.

He made repeated unsuccessful attempts to swim across the Río de la Plata. Counts vary between five and eight attempts. The precise number cannot presently be established. He also suffered a facial injury or scar while practicing diving.

### Fine arts

Dumas studied drawing and sculpture at the **Academia Nacional de Bellas Artes**, apparently during adolescence or early adulthood. The extent of his enrollment, instructors, and whether he completed a program are unknown. Painting and sculpture remained important throughout his life. He later transported fifty of his paintings toward New York in hopes of exhibiting and selling them.

His carved figure **Lisandro** accompanied him on voyages and became both a personal object and part of his self-created maritime world.

### Seamanship

At about seventeen he began sailing on the Río de la Plata in the 4.5-metre chalupa *Chubut*, owned by Club Náutico Belgrano. He also sailed through the Tigre Sailing Club.

At twenty-five he acquired the 6.9-metre *Atlántida*, using it for cruises to Uruguay before selling it around late 1928. One newspaper feature says that after secondary school he took an entrance or qualifying examination at a nautical school but abandoned the prospect of commercial seafaring. This episode has not been corroborated by school records.

Dumas’s formation was primarily practical. No named master has been securely established. Naval architect **Manuel M. Campos** later became his most important technical collaborator.

### Aviation

Some later biographies say that he took a sporting-pilot course. The consulted sources do not provide a license number, school, dates, or flight record; this remains a **reported but unverified fact**.

### Intellectual and religious formation

Dumas’s writings reveal an individualist ethic centered on will, endurance, simplicity, intuition, patriotism, and mastery of fear. Before leaving Arcachon, he reportedly spent approximately an hour praying in the church of Saint-Ferdinand. During the circumnavigation he invoked God and Saint Teresa. His writing and behavior support identifying him as personally Catholic, although no sustained ecclesiastical affiliation or theological program is documented.

His pre-departure statement in 1942—“Voy a realizar una empresa romántica en esta época materialista para ejemplo de la juventud”—summarized the public meaning he assigned to the voyage: an exemplary act of personal resolve in wartime.

## Early Career: Swimming, Teaching, and Initial Sailing

### Endurance swimming

Dumas’s early reputation derived from swimming rather than sailing. His repeated Río de la Plata failures were real failures, publicly followed at the time. Later legend converted them into evidence either of admirable persistence or inherent bad luck.

In 1931 he traveled to France intending to swim the English Channel. He abandoned the project because he could not afford the required escort or support vessel. The episode is significant because the failure led directly to his first ocean voyage.

### Employment and rural activity

Apart from menial adolescent work and swimming instruction, Dumas repeatedly worked in agriculture. After his first Atlantic crossing he established himself on a farm in **Capitán Sarmiento**, Buenos Aires Province. He later sold *Lehg II* to buy a tractor. The exact nature of his title to the farms, income, partners, crops, and debts remains undocumented.

He was not a wealthy yachtsman financing voyages from inherited capital. His expeditions depended on personal savings, the resale or reacquisition of boats, friends’ assistance, club repairs, and later public recognition.

## Chronological Life History

## 1925–1931: *Atlántida*, failed swims, and France

Around 1925 Dumas bought *Atlántida*, his first known personally owned sailboat. He cruised the Río de la Plata and Uruguayan coast until selling it in 1928.

In 1931 he crossed to Europe to attempt the English Channel swim. When the plan proved financially impossible, he traveled to **Arcachon**, southwest France. There he acquired an abandoned International Eight Metre racing yacht, *Titave II*, which had been out of the water for several years.

Sources differ on whether the boat was built in 1912 or 1918. French local research attributes it to the Bonnin yard, to a Joseph Guédon design, and favors **1912**. Dumas renamed it **Lehg**, converted or re-rigged it as a yawl, closed deck openings, partly enclosed its cockpit, and prepared it economically for an Atlantic passage.

The meaning of *Lehg* became part of the Dumas mythology. A widely repeated expansion is **Lucha, Entereza, Hombría, Grandeza**. Other accounts imply a private or deliberately concealed meaning. No contemporary naming document establishing the acronym was located.

## 1931–1932: First solo Atlantic crossing

### Departure dispute

- A contemporaneous almanac gives **12 December 1931**.
- Dumas-derived and French accounts commonly give **13 December 1931**.
- The likely explanation is movement through the Arcachon approaches on the 12th and final departure to sea on the 13th, but this remains an inference.

Dumas sailed from Arcachon with little offshore experience and elementary equipment: magnetic compass, stove, bucket, and basic supplies. He could not stand upright inside the yacht.

### Route

The most reconstructable route is:

- Arcachon;
- Vigo;
- La Graciosa/Las Palmas, Canary Islands;
- the Atlantic crossing;
- grounding near Mostardas/Rio Grande do Sul;
- Rio Grande;
- Montevideo;
- Buenos Aires.

Contemporaneous chronology records his arrival at Vigo around 22–24 December and departure on 26 December. He reached the Canaries in January 1932. After roughly forty-six days of the ocean passage, *Lehg* grounded near **Mostardas**, Brazil, while Dumas slept.

Accounts vary over who effected the rescue. Some say the Brazilian Navy; another says that assistance obtained through his brother enabled the boat to be refloated after twenty-two days. The Rio Grande Yacht Club records that it sheltered and assisted him and preserves a mast crosspiece that he later gave in gratitude.

He left Montevideo on 11 April and entered Buenos Aires on **13 April 1932**, received by a large crowd and municipal authorities. A contemporary account gives 131 elapsed days and seventy-eight sailing days; a later reconstruction gives 121 days and 7,325 nautical miles. Differences probably reflect starting points, port time, and nautical-mile calculations.

The crossing made him internationally known. The yacht, unsuitable and deteriorated by oceanic standards, survived. *Lehg* is now preserved at the Museo de Transportes in Luján.

## 1932–1941: Farming, *Lehg II*, financial reversal

After declaring that he might leave navigation, Dumas returned to rural work at Capitán Sarmiento.

In 1934 he commissioned **Lehg II**:

- designer: Manuel M. Campos;
- builder: José Parodi/the Parodi yard at Tigre;
- double-ended or “Norwegian stern” hull;
- ketch rig;
- length on deck approximately 9.55 m;
- beam 3.30 m;
- draft reported between 1.60 and 1.70 m;
- iron ballast about 3,500 kg;
- sail area approximately 42.15 m².

The design combined Scandinavian double-ended influences with local and contemporary small-cruiser practice. It was robust, simple, and intended to remain manageable by one person.

Dumas sailed it to Uruguay and Brazil. During one voyage toward Rio de Janeiro, a violent storm reportedly rolled the vessel through approximately 360 degrees. The hull recovered. Such experiences helped Dumas develop his practice of running before storms under sail rather than lying entirely passive.

Economic difficulties forced him to sell *Lehg II* around 1937, reportedly to buy a tractor. Sources identify the later owner as **Rafael Gamba**. By 1941 Dumas negotiated to reacquire it. Campos had meanwhile considered a larger project, *La Argentina*, but insufficient money ended that plan.

Friends, Campos, and the Yacht Club Argentino helped refit *Lehg II*. Its stores included roughly 400 litres of water, 100 litres of kerosene, salted meat, hard biscuit, potatoes, salted butter, pulses, rice, flour, yerba mate, bottled milk, chocolate, tobacco, spirits, and a small medical kit.

Dumas chose not to carry radio equipment. The surviving accounts attribute this partly to weight, expense, simplicity, and wartime security concerns. The popular formulation “without radio and without engine” is substantially correct, but it should not be transformed into “without instruments”: he used a compass, chronometer, sextant, charts, and conventional celestial navigation.

## 1942–1943: Circumnavigation through the Roaring Forties

### Authorization and departure

On **27 June 1942**, Dumas departed Buenos Aires for Montevideo. Because *Lehg II* and/or Dumas did not satisfy Argentine requirements for unrestricted deep-sea clearance, he did not depart directly into the Atlantic from Argentina. He reached Montevideo on 28 June and sailed into the South Atlantic on **1 July**.

This administrative obstacle later became mythologized as officials forbidding the venture because they thought it suicidal. Official caution and technical noncompliance are documented; a political or conspiratorial prohibition is not.

### Buenos Aires/Montevideo–Cape Town

- Distance: approximately 4,200–4,560 nautical miles.
- Sailing time: 55 days.
- Arrival: **24 August 1942**.

Dumas endured severe weather, leakage, prolonged exhaustion, and a serious infection of his right arm. With no mechanical bilge pump, he bailed manually. Later retellings describe him cutting or draining the wound himself; the underlying infection is well established, but the exact improvised surgery is principally autobiographical.

### Cape Town–Wellington

- Departure: **14 September 1942**.
- Distance: approximately 7,400–7,500 nautical miles.
- Sailing time: 104 or 105 days.
- Arrival: **27 December 1942**.

This was the longest and most severe leg. Dumas suffered incipient scurvy after fresh food was exhausted, continual cold, storms, sleep deprivation, and psychological strain.

Wartime authorities in Wellington initially suspected that the unexplained small vessel might involve enemy intelligence. The suspicion was resolved. No evidence supports the later extrapolation that Dumas actually worked for German, Argentine, Allied, or any other intelligence service.

### Wellington–Valparaíso

- Departure: sources give **3 January** or, less plausibly, **30 January 1943**.
- Distance: about 5,400 nautical miles.
- Sailing time: 72 days.
- Arrival: **12 April 1943**.

The 3 January date is consistent with the seventy-two-day sailing figure only if there were unrecorded calendar discrepancies; conversely, 30 January to 12 April is approximately seventy-two days. The latter date may therefore be correct despite some detailed narratives giving 3 January. This remains unresolved without consulting the original log.

Persistent leakage required frequent bailing. Dumas received extensive hospitality and recognition in Valparaíso.

### Valparaíso–Cape Horn–Mar del Plata

- Departure: **30 May 1943**.
- Cape Horn passed: **24 June 1943**.
- Arrival in Mar del Plata: **7 July 1943**.
- Distance: about 3,000 nautical miles.
- Sailing time: approximately 38 days.

Dumas rounded Cape Horn from west to east. A wave threw him against the vessel and fractured his nasal septum. The final approach brought cold and snow.

He is often called the first person to round Cape Horn alone. More precisely, later naval histories credit **Al Hansen** with a solo east-to-west passage in 1934 and Dumas with the first solo west-to-east rounding. Some global priority claims depend on definitions of the Horn route and the documentation accepted.

### Final Argentine and Uruguayan stages

Dumas left Mar del Plata on 15 July, fell asleep, and *Lehg II* grounded near Faro Querandí. It was recovered; he returned to Mar del Plata, departed again, called at Montevideo, and arrived at Buenos Aires on **8 August 1943**.

Some editions and derivative sources give **7 September 1943**. The contemporaneous reception and detailed itinerary support **8 August**. The September date is an editorial error.

### Totals and record language

Figures vary:

- detailed leg table: approximately **20,650 nautical miles**, 276 sailing days, 402 elapsed days;
- official and popular accounts: 20,420 miles, 274 sailing days, 437 elapsed days;
- rounded press accounts: “more than 20,000” or “22,000” miles.

The variation results from differing start/end ports, repeated Mar del Plata legs, port time, rounding, and later transcription. “Approximately 20,500 nautical miles” is the safest summary.

His reception was massive. A later reconstruction estimates about 50,000 people at Dársena Norte. Contemporary newspapers devoted extensive coverage to the return.

The voyage was historically important because it constituted the first documented single-handed circumnavigation using the high southern-latitude route later associated with the Roaring Forties and involved Cape Horn without engine, radio, external routing, or modern emergency communications.

## 1943–1945: Fame, publication, and cultural recognition

Dumas received a gold medal from the Centro Naval in 1943. He was made an honorary life member of numerous nautical clubs and an honorary citizen of Montevideo according to his own account.

On 5 August 1943, while his return was imminent, Carlos Di Sarli’s orchestra with singer Roberto Rufino recorded the tango **“Navegante,”** music by Jaime Yanin and lyrics by José Horacio Staffolani, inspired by Dumas.

His first two voyage narratives were collected as *Mis viajes* in 1945. He also lectured, painted, and planned further sailing.

## 1945–1947: The “cruise of the unexpected”

On **1 September 1945**, Dumas again departed in *Lehg II*, intending to reach New York.

The reconstructed route was:

- Buenos Aires;
- Montevideo;
- Punta del Este;
- Rio de Janeiro;
- Havana;
- approach to New York/Coney Island;
- involuntary drift eastward across the North Atlantic;
- sighting or passing the Azores, Madeira, and Canary regions;
- encounter with the merchant vessel *Serantes* south of the Canaries;
- Ceará, Brazil;
- Montevideo;
- Buenos Aires.

Near Coney Island, calm and adverse current carried him away from the coast. Rather than reaching New York, he was driven back into the Atlantic. Newspapers reported him missing or presumed dead.

The *Serantes* supplied him with food but he continued aboard *Lehg II*. When he finally reached Ceará, he had reportedly lost twenty kilograms after **106 days alone** during the ordeal. The full journey amounted to more than 17,000 nautical miles and 234 elapsed days.

Why he did not secure New York landfall remains contested. Contemporary institutional language calls his conduct “inexplicable”; Dumas presented it as a product of current, calm, weather, and circumstance. No evidence located supports fraud, a fabricated position, or a clandestine purpose.

The voyage became *El crucero de lo imprevisto*. Some bibliographies incorrectly call it *El cruce de lo imprevisto*.

In 1947 the Ministry of the Navy acquired *Lehg II*. It served as a training vessel for the Escuela Naval Militar and later nautical instruction. After grounding and deterioration, it was repaired and eventually placed in the Museo Naval de la Nación at Tigre.

## 1947–1954: State recognition and nautical education

President **Juan Domingo Perón** appointed Dumas lieutenant in the naval reserve by decree dated **27 August 1949**.

Dumas was also placed in charge of a state-supported popular nautical school, usually called the **Escuela de Náutica Deportiva** or Escuela Popular de Náutica. Its purpose was to extend nautical training beyond wealthy yacht-club circles.

A training schooner was named **Presidente Perón**. A later participant, Emilio Villafañe, recalled sailing under Dumas and a wreck or grounding south of Brazil. Precise dates, casualty records, and an official inquiry were not located, so the incident is best labeled a **reported fact requiring archival confirmation**.

The appointment has been interpreted in three ways:

1. **Documented fact:** the state recognized a globally successful civilian sailor and employed his skill.
2. **Reported political interpretation:** Perón used the civilian appointment to provoke an officer corps that resented an outsider wearing naval uniform.
3. **Partisan interpretation:** Dumas became a committed Peronist beneficiary.

The third interpretation is not established. Dumas accepted Peron-era recognition and institutional employment, and his vessel or school displayed Perón’s name. No party-membership record, campaign office, ideological tract, or evidence of participation in repression was found.

## 1955–1956: *Sirio* and the last great crossing

Dumas commissioned a smaller boat designed by Campos and built in San Isidro. Sources disagree on the builder: the detailed study names **Eduardo Porto**, while documentary reporting identifies **Luis Cueto** as the constructor interviewed. The discrepancy may reflect multiple participants.

*Sirio*—named after Dumas’s favorite dog—was:

- approximately 7 m long;
- 2.2 m beam;
- 0.9 m draft;
- double-ended;
- Marconi sloop rig;
- approximately 38 m² sail area.

It was reportedly built in only seventy-two days.

Dumas departed Buenos Aires on **23 April 1955**, not 16 September as some summaries incorrectly state. His intention was a nonstop Buenos Aires–New York voyage. He carried fifty paintings for a prospective American exhibition and sale.

After more than ninety days without news, he approached Bermuda severely dehydrated, with swollen feet and gums and symptoms described as scurvy. On or about **7 August**, fisherman Milton Pitman aboard *Saltwo* reportedly towed him toward the channel. He was admitted to King Edward Memorial Hospital in Hamilton. A retrospective report gives his systolic pressure as “28,” an extraordinary figure whose units or accuracy cannot be confirmed.

After approximately twelve days of treatment, he resumed sailing despite medical advice. A contemporaneous September newspaper reported that he departed Bermuda on 10 September and was again temporarily overdue.

He reached New York on **23 September 1955**, during severe weather associated in Dumas literature with Hurricane Ione. The voyage covered approximately 7,100 nautical miles in 117 sailing days, excluding the Bermuda interruption.

The Revolución Libertadora overthrew Perón while Dumas was at sea. He remained in New York for nearly a year, ill, short of money, and unable to establish a durable project. A later account says he contracted liver disease. He sold *Sirio* to Spanish naval technician **Rafael Cruces** and returned to Argentina aboard a cargo or passenger vessel.

## 1956–1965: Final years

Dumas returned to writing and painting. He reportedly experienced financial strain and reduced public attention.

In 1961 Campos and the Parodi brothers built **Sirio II**:

- approximately 9.9 m on deck;
- 2.9 m beam;
- 1.5 m draft;
- five tonnes;
- approximately 42 m² sail area.

Dumas entered the 1962 Buenos Aires–Rio de Janeiro race. During pre-race ceremonies he received the **Blue Water Medal** of the Cruising Club of America. He unexpectedly withdrew near Cabo Polonio without a public explanation. This was a significant failed or abandoned project; illness, equipment, and personal choice have all been suggested, but no firm cause is documented.

He made a final Buenos Aires–Mar del Plata voyage in 1964.

On **28 March 1965**, Dumas died at home in Vicente López. The reported cause was coronary thrombosis. He was sixty-four.

## Boats, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### *Atlántida*

A 6.9-metre yacht used for Río de la Plata and Uruguayan-coast cruises. Purchased around age twenty-five and sold in 1928.

### *Lehg*

Formerly *Titave II*, an International Eight Metre racing yacht. Technical figures vary:

- overall length: about 11–12.8 m;
- waterline length: reported between 6.12 and 7.94 m;
- beam: approximately 2.15–2.60 m;
- draft: approximately 1.6 m;
- original builder: Bonnin;
- probable construction: 1912, although 1918 is often repeated;
- rig after conversion: yawl.

Preserved at the Luján transport museum.

### *Lehg II*

Campos-designed, Parodi-built 9.55-metre ketch. Its double-ended hull, heavy ballast, simple systems, manageable rig, and recovery from knockdowns were central to Dumas’s survival.

Dumas’s storm technique—running before heavy seas under controlled sail, often quartering the waves—was later described by French sailor **Bernard Moitessier**, who reportedly applied a Dumas-derived method after consulting Jean Merrien’s work.

The vessel was sold and reacquired before the circumnavigation, then transferred to the Ministry of the Navy after the 1945–47 voyage. It is preserved by the Museo Naval de la Nación.

### *Sirio*

Seven-metre double-ended sloop used in 1955. Dumas sold it in New York.

### *Sirio II*

Built in 1961. Used in the abandoned 1962 race and Dumas’s final coastal passage. Later preserved afloat at Club Náutico Mar del Plata.

### Financial structure

No company, trust, formal expedition corporation, patent, or major investment vehicle was found. Dumas’s finances were personal and precarious:

- swimming instruction and other employment;
- agricultural activity;
- boat purchases and sales;
- friends’ and clubs’ in-kind assistance;
- book income;
- painting sales or attempted sales;
- later state position;
- sale of *Sirio* in New York.

The anecdote that he began the circumnavigation with only ten Argentine pesos and received ten pounds from a friend is widely repeated but rests on retrospective narrative rather than financial records.

### Inventions and patents

No patent by Dumas was found. His principal technical contribution was practical seamanship—particularly heavy-weather handling of a small double-ended sailing vessel—not a patented device.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Politics

Dumas left no identified political manifesto and is not known to have held elected office or formal party leadership.

His documented political relationships consist chiefly of:

- acceptance of Perón’s honorary/reserve naval appointment;
- leadership of a Peron-era nautical school;
- association with the training vessel *Presidente Perón*;
- distress over the 1955 coup, according to later biographers and documentary testimony.

The claim that he was “stuck to Peronism without having been a Peronist” is a biographical interpretation, not a demonstrable membership finding. Equally, the claim that every later professional setback was official anti-Peronist retaliation is stronger than the available evidence.

### Religion

His recorded prayers, devotion to Saint Teresa, references to Providence, and Catholic ritual before departure support a personal Catholic faith. He treated the ocean as both physical trial and spiritual solitude. No evidence was found of clerical office, conversion, esoteric society, or formal religious movement.

### Philosophy of navigation

Recurring principles in his work include:

- individual responsibility;
- simplicity of equipment;
- distrust of unnecessary mechanisms;
- intuitive reading of wind and sea;
- acceptance of pain and isolation;
- voyages as examples for youth;
- self-command rather than conquest of nature.

A maxim attributed to him states that the fewer and simpler the things aboard, the fewer things can break.

### Painting and sculpture

Dumas produced maritime paintings, drawings, and sculpture. Surviving works have appeared in museum collections and auctions. The fifty paintings carried aboard *Sirio* show that art was also intended as a source of income. No complete catalogue raisonné was found.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Adela Navarro

Wife and important posthumous witness to Dumas’s temperament. She described him as active, intelligent, intuitive, introverted, and inclined to begin projects enthusiastically but abandon some abruptly.

### Remo Dumas

Brother. A later account credits him with helping arrange aid after the 1932 grounding.

### Manuel M. Campos

Naval architect, friend, and principal technical collaborator. Designed *Lehg II*, *Sirio*, and *Sirio II*, and considered the unrealized *La Argentina*. Later testimony also credits Campos with helping arrange *Lehg II*’s restoration.

### José Parodi and the Parodi family

Builders associated with *Lehg II* and *Sirio II* at Tigre.

### Rafael Gamba

Owner of *Lehg II* after Dumas was forced to sell it. Agreed to its return or resale before the circumnavigation.

### Eduardo Porto / Luis Cueto

Both are named in later accounts concerning *Sirio*’s construction. Their exact respective roles remain unresolved.

### Agustín Vila

Mar del Plata friend who sailed locally with Dumas and helped sell his paintings in his later years. Vila later maintained a café-museum dedicated to him.

### Al Hansen

Norwegian solo sailor and personal friend according to Centro Naval. Hansen’s earlier Cape Horn voyage provided a relevant precedent. He disappeared off Chile not long afterward.

### Bernard Moitessier

No personal meeting was established. The relationship is one of documented professional influence: Moitessier regarded Dumas as a master and cited or employed a heavy-weather technique associated with him.

### Jean Merrien

French maritime writer whose *Aux limites du possible* publicized Dumas’s achievement and seamanship. Merrien described the voyage in superlative terms.

### Yacht Club Argentino

A complex institutional relationship. The club supported refitting and welcomed Dumas in 1932 and 1943, contradicting simplistic claims of uniform institutional persecution. At the same time, multiple retrospective witnesses place class hostility and the “mufa” legend among sectors of elite yachting, including club circles.

### Argentine Navy and Centro Naval

Also a mixed relationship. The Navy purchased *Lehg II*, employed it in training, and later recognized and commemorated Dumas. Some officers reportedly resented his 1949 reserve commission and political association. The institutional record therefore shows both recognition and reported hostility at different levels and times.

## Awards, Honors, and Appointments

- 1932: mass public and municipal reception in Buenos Aires.
- 1932: official reception in Uruguay, including President Gabriel Terra according to Centro Naval.
- 1943: gold medal from the Centro Naval.
- 1943: honorary citizenship or civic medal from Montevideo, according to Dumas’s account.
- 1943 onward: honorary life membership in numerous Argentine nautical clubs.
- 27 August 1949: appointed teniente de navío in the Argentine naval reserve.
- Perón era: director of the Escuela de Náutica Deportiva.
- **Slocum Award:** institutional literature gives **1956**, for the most notable solo transoceanic passage, while some biographies attach a Slocum Society distinction to the 1932 voyage. It is possible that he received recognition at different times, but the exact award history requires consultation of original society records.
- **Blue Water Medal, Cruising Club of America:** awarded without a conventional annual date or ceremonially presented in 1962, recognizing the circumnavigation and other voyages. Sources sometimes conflate it with the Slocum Award.
- 2000: centenary commemorations by the Armada, Prefectura, Centro Naval, Yacht Club Argentino, and other organizations; reinterment in the Naval Pantheon.
- Continuing commemoration: Campeonato Argentino de Solitario Vito Dumas; Regata Río de la Plata–Rio Grande trophy; streets, clubs, and nautical memorials bearing his name.

## Health, Injuries, and Psychological Strain

Documented or consistently reported episodes include:

- facial scar from springboard diving;
- serious right-arm infection during the 1942 South Atlantic leg;
- scurvy symptoms during the Indian Ocean passage;
- fractured nasal septum near Cape Horn;
- exhaustion, hallucination-like experiences, and loss of consciousness during extreme passages, described in his writings;
- approximately twenty-kilogram weight loss during the 1945–47 ordeal;
- severe dehydration and scurvy symptoms before Bermuda in 1955;
- hospitalization in Hamilton;
- later liver illness in New York;
- declining health by 1961–64;
- death reportedly from coronary thrombosis.

There is no sourced diagnosis of chronic mental illness, addiction, or substance-use disorder. He carried tobacco, rum, and champagne as provisions, but that is not evidence of addiction.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Criminal and civil record

The dedicated adversarial search found **no documented lawsuit, criminal investigation, indictment, conviction, fraud finding, corruption case, money-laundering allegation, political-violence involvement, abuse allegation, or intelligence-service connection involving Dumas**.

Searches combining his name with Spanish and English terms for controversy, scandal, allegation, lawsuit, fraud, conspiracy, and criticism—including material indexed in 2024–26—produced no substantive criminal accusation. The negative material is reputational, political, and superstitious.

### Wartime espionage suspicion

- **Documented or strongly reported event:** Wellington authorities initially treated the unexplained arrival of a foreign single-handed sailor with suspicion during World War II.
- **Disposition:** the concern was resolved.
- **Evidence of espionage:** none found.
- **Classification:** temporary security suspicion, not an accusation established by investigation.

### Compliance with navigation rules

- **Documented fact:** Dumas and/or *Lehg II* did not meet Argentine requirements for deep-sea clearance in 1942.
- **Consequence:** he proceeded through Montevideo before beginning the oceanic leg.
- **No criminal finding:** no prosecution was identified.

### Groundings and abandoned ventures

Dumas grounded *Lehg* in Brazil, grounded *Lehg II* near Faro Querandí after falling asleep, failed repeatedly at Río de la Plata swims, abandoned his Channel swim, missed New York in the 1945 voyage, and abandoned the 1962 Buenos Aires–Rio race. These failures complicate the sanitized heroic narrative but do not establish incompetence or fraud.

### Peronism and state patronage

- **Fact:** Perón’s government commissioned Dumas in the naval reserve and supported his nautical school.
- **Reported fact:** some naval officers and yachtsmen resented these appointments.
- **Interpretation:** the appointments were both recognition and political theater aimed at the officer corps.
- **Unresolved:** how much of Dumas’s post-1955 neglect resulted from official retaliation, ordinary changes in public attention, ill health, personality, financial limitations, or the marginal status of sailing.

The later claim that the military “erased” Dumas should not be treated literally without documentation of orders, censorship directives, dismissals, pension actions, or archival suppression. What is established is that his profile declined, the nautical superstition persisted, and later witnesses interpreted both through anti-Peronism.

### Class conflict in yachting

Later writers depict Dumas as a working or lower-middle-class intruder into aristocratic yachting. His economic hardships and incomplete education are factual. Yet Yacht Club Argentino also repaired his boat, received him, and participated in his later rehabilitation. “The club opposed Dumas” is therefore too broad; hostility appears to have belonged to particular social circles or members rather than an unchanging institutional policy.

### Personality criticism

Some witnesses described him as private, difficult, impulsive, inconsistent, proud, or unsociable. His wife said he sometimes abandoned projects after an enthusiastic beginning. A later admiring author explicitly found him personally unsympathetic. These are character assessments, not evidence of misconduct.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## “Vito Dumas was mufa”

### The story

Within Argentine and Río de la Plata sailing, speaking Dumas’s name aboard a boat supposedly brought bad luck, grounding, dismasting, or shipwreck. He became “the unnameable.”

### Appearance and circulation

Retrospective accounts trace an early form to Arcachon, where acquaintances joked that, after failed swims and an abandoned Channel attempt, his decrepit yacht would never clear the bay. In Argentina it became entrenched among yacht-club and naval circles, reportedly lasting into the 1990s. Anecdotes claim instructors taught novices not to say his name aboard.

### Evidence

No causal evidence exists, and the claim is inherently unfalsifiable as superstition. The real groundings of *Lehg* and *Lehg II* supplied memorable incidents that could be retrofitted to the myth, but Dumas survived voyages in which the statistical opportunities for mishap were enormous.

### Social and political explanations

Roberto Alonso and Ricardo Cufré argued that class resentment and post-1955 anti-Peronism amplified the superstition. This explanation is plausible and supported by oral testimony but is not proven by a single originating document.

### Effect

The legend discouraged commemoration, supplied a hostile nickname, and became inseparable from Dumas’s posthumous story. The 1995 biography, the 2000 institutional reinterment, later regattas bearing his name, and Rodolfo Petriz’s film explicitly worked to reverse it.

## Meaning of “Lehg”

The familiar expansion is **Lucha, Entereza, Hombría, Grandeza**. It is repeated in museum and sailing literature. Other accounts present the name as an unresolved private code. Without a contemporary statement by Dumas, the expansion should be treated as a **traditional explanation**, not incontrovertibly established fact.

## “First solo sailor around the world”

Dumas was not the first person to circumnavigate solo; Joshua Slocum had done so decades earlier. Dumas’s distinctive priority was the first documented single-handed circumnavigation through the high southern-latitude “impossible route,” rounding the major southern capes.

## “First solo rounding of Cape Horn”

This requires qualification. Al Hansen is credited with a 1934 east-to-west solo rounding; Dumas’s was west-to-east in 1943. Dumas’s crossing was part of a completed high-latitude circumnavigation, which gives it a different historical status.

## “No instruments”

He had no radio, engine, GPS, satellite weather, or modern self-steering. He did use traditional navigational instruments. “Without instruments” is hagiographic exaggeration.

## The ten-peso voyage

The story says he began the circumnavigation with ten pesos, telling a skeptical friend there was nowhere at sea to spend money; the friend added ten pounds. It captures his poverty and confidence but remains an anecdote without contemporary financial corroboration.

## The heroic ascetic

Dumas cultivated and was assigned the image of a solitary, prayerful, almost superhuman ascetic. His physical privation was real, but his voyages also depended on architects, builders, clubs, relatives, sailors, doctors, port officials, donors, and rescuers. “Alone” accurately describes the ocean passages, not the infrastructure that made them possible.

## Political martyr narrative

The documentary and revisionist biographies portray Dumas as deliberately erased for proximity to Perón. Post-1955 political hostility is supported by testimony; a centrally organized erasure campaign has not been documented. The narrative became important because it tied Dumas to Argentina’s larger conflict over Peronism and historical memory.

## Destroyed writings and possessions

The documentary tradition reports that papers and personal objects may have been burned. Who destroyed them, when, and why remain unclear. This is an **unresolved report**, not proof of political document destruction.

## Works by Vito Dumas

Bibliographic records are inconsistent, and later publishers sometimes split or combine the narratives.

### Books with genuine authorship

1. **_Solo, rumbo a la Cruz del Sur: del libro de navegación de Lehg en su crucero a través del Atlántico_**  
   Buenos Aires: published by or under the imprint of Vito Dumas, **1933**, approximately 165 pages. First-person account of the 1931–32 crossing. Genuine authorship.

2. **_Los cuarenta bramadores: la vuelta al mundo por la “ruta imposible”_**  
   Buenos Aires, **1944**. First-person circumnavigation narrative. Reissued many times, including Kraft and Continente editions. Genuine authorship. One 1985 Kraft edition reportedly introduced the erroneous 7 September 1943 return date.

3. **_Mis viages: Solo rumbo a la Cruz del Sur y Los cuarenta bramadores_**  
   Buenos Aires: Editorial Atlántida, **1945**, 243 pages. The catalog preserves the archaic or typographically unusual spelling *viages*. This is an authorized collected edition of the first two narratives, not a wholly separate voyage.

4. **_El crucero de lo imprevisto_**  
   Account of the 1945–47 voyage. Genuine authorship. Frequently mis-cited as *El cruce de lo imprevisto*. Original publication year was not securely established from the consulted catalogs.

5. **_El viaje del Sirio_**  
   Account of the 1955 Buenos Aires–Bermuda–New York voyage. Genuine authorship. Original publication details remain uncertain.

### Translations and later collections

- **_Alone Through the Roaring Forties_**, English translation/edition, later issued by McGraw-Hill in 2001.
- French editions including **_Seul, cap sur la Croix du Sud_** and translations of the Roaring Forties narrative.
- German translations under titles corresponding to *Auf unmöglichem Kurs*.
- Italian editions combining *Verso la croce del sud* and *I quaranta ruggenti*.
- Later Spanish Continente editions of *Solo, rumbo a la Cruz del Sur* and *Los cuarenta bramadores*.

These are translations or republications, not new works.

### Articles, speeches, letters, and audio

Dumas published or supplied voyage reports to newspapers and clubs, wrote letters from ports, and delivered speeches at receptions. No complete periodical bibliography or collected correspondence was found.

Known surviving media include:

- a recorded reading or narration from *Los cuarenta bramadores*;
- archival newsreel and radio material used in *El navegante solitario*;
- letters read or reproduced in the documentary;
- voyage logs underlying his books, whose present archival completeness is uncertain.

### Visual art

Dumas produced maritime paintings, watercolors, drawings, and sculptures, including *Lisandro*. There is no identified complete catalogue. Auction and museum references establish genuine artistic production, but individual attribution should be checked work by work.

### No identified patents or ghostwritten works

No patent or technical paper was located. No firm evidence establishes that his major books were ghostwritten. Editorial shaping is probable, as with most popular voyage narratives, but genuine first-person authorship is accepted.

Substantial modern treatments include articles in *La Nación*, *Página/12*, Cinestel, Socompa, Fundación La Balandra, *La Capital* of Mar del Plata, and regional maritime-history sites in France, Spain, Uruguay, Brazil, and Argentina.

## Death, Burial, and Posthumous Reputation

Dumas died in Vicente López on 28 March 1965. A six-block funeral procession is reported. Accounts differ on whether his initial burial was at Chacarita or Olivos; the most specific 2000 report says that his remains had been deposited at **Olivos Cemetery**.

For decades his international sailing reputation coexisted with reduced Argentine public visibility. The “mufa” superstition persisted among sailors, while his books went in and out of print.

The rehabilitation proceeded through several stages:

- 1968 retrospective coverage in *Todo es Historia*;
- 1995 publication of Alonso and Cufré’s biography;
- centenary articles and exhibitions in 2000;
- institutional “reparation” by the Armada, Prefectura, Centro Naval, Yacht Club Argentino, and other organizations;
- ceremonial transfer of his remains on 30 September 2000 from Olivos by naval procession to Dársena Norte and then to the Naval Pantheon at Chacarita;
- restoration and public exhibition of his boats;
- regattas and trophies carrying his name;
- Petriz’s 2019–20 documentary;
- continuing Latin American and European maritime commemoration.

His legacy rests on four distinguishable elements:

1. the first high-southern-latitude solo circumnavigation;
2. practical demonstration that a small, simple, well-found yacht could survive the Southern Ocean;
3. influence on later sailors’ heavy-weather practice and imagination;
4. an Argentine memory conflict involving class, Peronism, the Navy, popular heroism, and superstition.

## Atlas Connections

A source-by-source audit was made against the supplied atlas roster. Only one direct connection was established securely.

### Juan Domingo Perón — documented fact

- **Nature:** political patronage and state appointment.
- **Date:** presidential decree of 27 August 1949.
- **Connection:** Perón’s government appointed Dumas lieutenant in the Argentine naval reserve and supported his direction of a public nautical-training institution. A school vessel bore the name *Presidente Perón*.
- **Interpretive dispute:** later witnesses claimed Perón also intended the appointment to challenge or irritate the professional naval hierarchy. That motive is a **reported theory**, not established by the decree itself.
- **Aftermath:** Dumas’s association with the Peron-era state became part of later explanations for his post-1955 marginalization.

### Other roster names

No sourced personal meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, mentorship, institutional tie, family relationship, or explicitly acknowledged influence was found between Dumas and the other rostered individuals.

In particular:

- References to **Diego Maradona** in later essays are comparisons of fame, not a relationship.
- Dumas’s Catholic invocations do not establish a connection with any rostered saint or biblical person.
- **San Vito** shares his given name but no documentary connection.
- Joshua Slocum, Al Hansen, Manuel Campos, Bernard Moitessier, Jean Merrien, Francis Chichester, and Robin Knox-Johnston are relevant to Dumas’s maritime history but are not on the supplied atlas roster.

Absence of further supported connections is therefore the finding.
