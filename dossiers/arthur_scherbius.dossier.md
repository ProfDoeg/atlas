# Arthur Scherbius: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Jacob Ludolf Arthur Scherbius (also recorded as Arthur Ludolf Jacob Scherbius)  
**Scope:** Reconstructable life history, inventions, businesses, networks, death, myths, writings, and legacy  
**Prepared:** 30 August 2026  

## Research Note

Arthur Scherbius left no known published memoir, diary, or large edited correspondence, and no full scholarly biography has yet appeared in print. The surviving record is unusually uneven: university and patent records are strong; the corporate and government record is substantial but scattered; and private life is reconstructed largely from later family papers and correspondence cited by historians. This dossier therefore distinguishes among contemporary documents, archival reconstruction, later recollection, and unsupported repetition.

The most important modern reconstruction is Claus Taaks's archive-based study of the years 1917–1925, supported by German, British, Dutch, and diplomatic records. Hartmut Petzold's entry in the *Neue Deutsche Biographie* remains the fullest concise life. Friedrich L. Bauer preserved technical and biographical testimony from people connected with Enigma. Patent records and specialist machine histories supply the model-by-model development. Where these sources disagree, the disagreement is retained below.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and ancestry

- **Father:** Gustav Theodor Alexander Scherbius (1836–1907), described in the Hessian register as a *Finanzmakler* (financial broker) and in the *Neue Deutsche Biographie* more generally as a merchant.
- **Mother:** Hedwig Anna Scherbius, née Nacken (1854–1945 in the Hessian register). A 1954 death year is repeated in some secondary material; the discrepancy has not been resolved here by a civil death certificate.
- **Siblings:** No sibling can be identified confidently from the sources consulted.
- **Wider ancestry:** The *Neue Deutsche Biographie* genealogy indicates a connection with the family of the Rhineland liberal statesman Ludolf Camphausen, but its compressed genealogical notation is not sufficient to define the exact descent without the underlying family tables. It should not be expanded into a precise relationship on present evidence.

The family appears to have belonged to Frankfurt's commercially established bourgeoisie. Arthur's long secondary education, engineering studies in several cities, and year of factory training would have required substantial family support, although no account of the household's wealth or property has been located.

### Marriage, household, and child

Scherbius met **Elisabeth Adelaide Taaks** (20 May 1896, Bremen–28 August 1945, Berlin) in Partenkirchen in early summer 1917. They became engaged in June at Frankfurt and married on **10 November 1917** in Bremen. The witnesses were her father, Georg Taaks, and her uncle Otto Christian Taaks. She was about nineteen years younger than Arthur. Her recurrent tuberculosis led to stays in sanatoria and shaped the couple's domestic life.

The couple had **no biological children**. On **15 January 1929**, they received a boy from a children's home and began an adoption. Arthur died before the legal process was complete. Elisabeth adopted the child in 1930 as **Rainer Scherbius**; after her later remarriage he was also known as Rainer Pungs. It is therefore accurate to call him the child whom Arthur and Elisabeth intended to adopt, but inaccurate to say that Arthur completed a legal adoption.

In 1924 the couple moved to a newly built house at Königstraße 40, Berlin-Wannsee, on land associated with Scherbius & Ritter's enterprise. Their domestic circle included engineers and figures from artistic, literary, and political life. In 1925, amid business difficulties, the spouses entered a separation-of-property arrangement. Elisabeth received the house and two accounts while Arthur retained his business interests. The available record establishes the transaction but not an adjudication that it was fraudulent or intended to defeat creditors. The subsequent collapse of the companies nevertheless consumed the household's security, and Elisabeth eventually had to leave the Wannsee house.

Elisabeth's later communist associations, antifascist resistance, arrests, and death shortly after the Second World War belong to her post-Arthur biography. No evidence found makes Arthur a participant in that activity or permits her later politics to be projected back onto him.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1878–1898

Arthur Scherbius was born in Frankfurt am Main on 30 October 1878. From **1885 to 1898** he attended primary school and an *Oberrealschule* in Frankfurt. This type of secondary school emphasized modern languages, mathematics, and natural science rather than the classical curriculum of a humanistic Gymnasium. He passed the *Abitur* in 1898.

He then spent approximately one year working in a machine factory. The factory has not been securely identified. The placement gave him practical workshop experience before university and fits the German engineering ideal of combining shop-floor training with mathematical education. No reliable source identifies a childhood invention, decisive schoolteacher, religious formation, or adolescent political commitment.

## Education and Formation, 1899–1904

The educational chronology contains a real source conflict:

- The *Neue Deutsche Biographie* states that Scherbius began electrical engineering at the Technical University of Munich in 1899, continued in Zurich, and completed the diploma at the Technical University of Hanover in 1903.
- University-record-based later accounts place him principally at the **Technical University of Hanover from 1899 to 1903**, with two semesters at the **Technical University of Munich in 1901–1902**. They do not confirm study at Zurich.

The second version is more specific and appears to rest on matriculation material, but the unexplained Zurich statement in the national biography should remain on record. What is secure is that he received the **Dipl.-Ing. in electrical engineering at Hanover in 1903**.

After graduation he became a project engineer with **Felten & Guilleaume-Lahmeyerwerke**, an electrical-engineering concern. His doctoral work concerned the control of hydraulic turbines. On **14 July 1904**, the Technical University of Hanover awarded him the Dr.-Ing. for:

> *Vorschläge zum Bau eines indirekt wirkenden Wasser-Turbinen-Reglers*  
> (“Proposals for the Construction of an Indirect-Acting Water-Turbine Governor”)

No dissertation supervisor or formal mentor has been securely established in the consulted sources. The subject already shows the concern that ran through his early career: stable, economical regulation of large power systems.

## Early Career, 1903–1914

### Felten & Guilleaume-Lahmeyer and Siemens-Schuckert

After his Hanover diploma, Scherbius worked as a project engineer for Felten & Guilleaume-Lahmeyerwerke. He then had a brief association with **Siemens-Schuckertwerke**. Exact appointment and departure dates are not preserved in the accessible summaries. By **1905** he was operating as an independent inventor and consultant.

### Controllable polyphase motors and the “Scherbius cascade”

Scherbius developed circuits for efficiently regulating the speed of induction motors by recovering or controlling slip power instead of dissipating it. He sold or licensed patents to major electrical firms, including **Brown, Boveri & Cie. (BBC)**. From approximately **1906 to 1912**, he worked at BBC in Baden, Switzerland, helping turn the concepts into practical systems.

The resulting arrangements became known internationally as the **Scherbius system** or **Scherbius cascade**. They were valuable where large motors had to run efficiently at adjustable speeds: rolling mills, mine and metallurgical equipment, pumps, compressors, and fans. The technology established his engineering reputation before cryptography and produced continuing patent income. During the inflationary early 1920s, payments from General Electric and other foreign licensees in stable foreign currency helped finance his new Berlin enterprises.

His technical circle at BBC and in heavy electrical engineering included Karl Schnetzler, Heinrich Meyer-Delius, Fritz Marguerre, H. A. W. Klinkhamer, and Leo Pungs. The surviving literature identifies professional collaboration and correspondence, not necessarily intimate friendship.

### Independent consulting and other electrical research

From about **1912**, Scherbius again worked independently. He investigated mercury-vapor rectifiers and acted as a consultant to Siemens. He also pursued high-voltage direct-current transmission and an ambitious direct-current machine that remained unfinished. These projects show that cryptography was one branch of a much wider electrical-engineering program.

In **1914**, as wartime shortages made metal conservation important, he collaborated with an established ceramics manufacturer on ceramic radiators. Later capsule biographies sometimes describe these simply as “ceramic heating parts.” The contemporary economic purpose was to replace scarce metal, not to create a cipher-machine component.

## Chronological Life History

### 1914–1916: Mobilization and signals work

The war-service chronology has been clarified by archival reconstruction. Scherbius was drafted in **October 1915** into the German Army's telegraph troops. He probably worked in a technical or instructional capacity connected with the directorate at Great Headquarters. Wilhelm Fenner later remembered the “talented engineer Dr. Scherbius of Supreme Army Command,” but the exact office and day-to-day duties are not documented. The *Neue Deutsche Biographie* describes an initial assignment teaching wireless telegraphy. These accounts are compatible but not complete enough to define his posting precisely.

No evidence found shows that he was an intelligence officer, field commander, or combatant. His service lay in the technical communications establishment.

### 1917: WuMBA, marriage, and the military cipher-machine commission

On **1 February 1917**, Scherbius was seconded to the **Waffen- und Munitionsbeschaffungsamt** (Weapons and Munitions Procurement Office, WuMBA), probably as a deputy department head concerned with electrical machines and equipment.

In spring 1917, the Prussian War Ministry commissioned work on a cipher machine. The commissioning order and full technical specification have not been found, but the order is independently referred to in multiple later government documents. Scherbius's objective was an automatic polyalphabetic cipher in which the electrical substitution changed after each keystroke.

He also met Elisabeth Taaks in Partenkirchen that summer, became engaged, and married her in Bremen on 10 November.

### 1918: The rotor patents, military prototypes, and first technical failure

On **23 February 1918**, Scherbius filed the German patent application that became **DE416219**, the foundational patent for his rotating-wired-wheel cipher apparatus. A keyboard sent current through one or more internally cross-wired rotors to a lamp panel. Rotation after a keystroke altered the substitution alphabet. The filing illustrated a 25-letter, 5×5 keyboard and display and contemplated a machine with as many as ten exchangeable rotors and irregular movement.

A two-rotor demonstrator was probably constructed in Ernst Ritter's workshop. In a letter of **15 April 1918**, Scherbius reported that a demonstration would take place at Great Headquarters in Spa and then at the Imperial Navy Office in Berlin. Officials regarded the principle as cryptographically strong but wanted more rotors. Two seven-rotor machines were tested later in 1918. They failed because the large number of electrical contacts was mechanically unreliable. Wartime shortages also prevented completion of the hoped-for writing or printing version. This was the project's first major failure: the powerful abstract design could not yet be made robust enough for field use.

On **2 June 1918**, he filed a supplementary patent, later **DE416833**, covering hydraulic or pneumatic movement of cipher elements. No German machine is known to have embodied this mechanism, although similar fluidic ideas appeared in later British and Czechoslovak machines.

The German patent system did not publish DE416219 until 1925. The long delay helps explain later confusion about priority. Edward Hebern in the United States, Arvid Damm in Sweden, and Hugo Koch in the Netherlands independently pursued rotor cipher systems in roughly the same era. “Inventor of the rotor machine” is therefore a contested shorthand. Scherbius indisputably filed his own German design in February 1918 and created the lineage that became Enigma; he did not derive that filing from Koch's later patent.

### 1919: Armistice work, redesign, and an unsuccessful writing machine

After the armistice, Scherbius worked as a consultant for the **Reichsverwertungsamt**, in an armistice section, and for the **Army Peace Treaty Commission**. These bodies needed secure communications, giving him continued contact with state cryptographic requirements. This constitutes a government technical relationship; it is not evidence of a covert intelligence career.

He reduced the impractical seven-rotor apparatus to three or four electromechanical rotors. That version did not meet military expectations. He then concentrated on a printing machine and added a second, line-by-line transposition stage intended to transform the order of whole lines after the rotor substitution. The Foreign Office showed interest but wanted a tested writing machine before committing. The Reichspost simultaneously examined Scherbius's work and Arvid Damm's competing Swedish system.

Scherbius also published a substantial monograph on magnetic induction in closed coils. Its subject—frequency conversion, transformers, and brushless machines—shows that his intellectual identity in 1919 remained that of a general electrical engineer rather than a specialist cryptologist.

### 1920: Scherbius & Ritter and negotiations with the Reich

On **1 April 1920**, Scherbius and **Ernst Richard Ritter** founded the general partnership **Scherbius & Ritter oHG**, initially at Schiffbauerdamm 30 in Berlin. It was distinct from Ritter's earlier firm, Ritter & Co. The new enterprise developed cipher machines and marketed practical electrical goods, notably Birka thermostatic controllers and electrically regulated heating cushions.

On **5 April**, Scherbius offered the state extensive rights in his cipher work in exchange for purchase commitments. The Navy demanded secrecy for the line-transposition feature but lacked money. Civil and military ministries accepted that development costs were real yet would not reimburse approximately two years of work. On **17 September**, Scherbius offered a machine deliverable in nine months using a Siemens Wheatstone tape punch. A demonstration followed on **2 December**, and an agreement with the Reichspost was reportedly concluded on **19 December**. The project then disappears from the surviving administrative trail; there is no evidence of the contemplated procurement being completed.

On **26 September 1920**, he filed what became **DE425147**, covering irregular movement of several cipher discs, including pneumatic drive concepts. The repeated attempt to produce irregular stepping matters because the commercial and military Enigmas later became vulnerable partly through more regular stepping behavior.

### 1921–1922: Transfer to Securitas, commercial finance, and patent disclosure

On **21 November 1921**, Scherbius transferred ten cipher patents or applications—one granted and nine pending—to **Gewerkschaft Securitas**, a Berlin precision-engineering concern then associated with “Audioma” wireless equipment. In exchange, Scherbius and Scherbius & Ritter obtained a minority interest and manufacturing cooperation. “Gewerkschaft” here was a legal business form, not a labor union.

Securitas sat within a speculative postwar financial network. Archival records describe promoters seeking very large returns, foreign financial vehicles, and efforts to attract politicians, officers, and advisers through board seats, loans, and gifts. Scherbius belonged to the inventors' side of the venture. The records reviewed do **not** establish that he personally bribed officials, embezzled funds, or participated in money laundering. The distinction is important: he dealt with a network later associated with financial scandal, but that association is not itself a criminal finding against him.

Patent publication created another setback. **DE385682**, applied for on 10 May 1922, disclosed the line-by-line scrambling mechanism. Once public, the feature could no longer serve as a protected military secret. Taaks characterizes this as the project's second major failure.

Securitas and later the cipher-machine company also used Dutch corporate and patent vehicles. Some historians infer that foreign registration helped conceal military development from Allied controls imposed after the war. The corporate cover is documented; the specific Versailles-evasion motive is described in the literature as a probability or suspicion, not proved by an explicit surviving order.

### 1923: Chiffriermaschinen AG and the public debut of “Enigma”

On **9 July 1923**, **Chiffriermaschinen Aktiengesellschaft** (ChiMaAG) was founded in Berlin. The structure matters:

- Securitas contributed patents, drawings, experimental machines, tools, and other rights valued nominally at 300,000 marks.
- Four investors represented through Dutch structures subscribed nominally 200,000 marks, of which only a quarter initially had to be paid.
- The founding board named Franz Henke, Carl Duhm, and Bruno Weigandt; Henke apparently did not take office.
- The supervisory network included Centre Party, Christian trade-union, and industrial figures, as well as businessmen whose inflation-era practices later attracted investigation.

It is common to call Scherbius “founder of ChiMaAG.” More precisely, the company was created around the Securitas-held patent package derived from him and collaborators; he was its crucial inventor and later served on its management board, but the documented legal founders and financiers were a broader group. Contracts had already diluted his control over both patents and company.

Foreign Office cryptanalyst **Rudolf Schauffler** tested the commercial machine in June. He concluded that the *Handelsmaschine* was theoretically attackable, perhaps by a specially built counterpart, but that the effort could be so high as to permit practical use. This was neither a declaration of mathematical unbreakability nor an immediate break.

On **29 November 1923**, Scherbius published “'Enigma' Chiffriermaschine” in the *Elektrotechnische Zeitschrift*. This is the best-known contemporary public exposition. He explained the general commercial need for secure communications and promoted the writing machine developed by Scherbius & Ritter. The article did not disclose the later military machine's full wiring or operational procedures. “Enigma” was by then the trade name, but no surviving document proves who coined it.

### 1924: Commercial production, international exhibition, and reliability problems

ChiMaAG contracted for a thousand writing machines using components associated with Cardinal typewriters. **Paul Bernstein** and other engineers worked on the design. The resulting large printing Enigma was heavy, expensive, mechanically unreliable, and reportedly failed German electrical-safety requirements. It weighed roughly 50 kilograms and was far removed from the portable lamp machines later familiar from museums.

The company displayed an Enigma at the **Universal Postal Union Congress in Stockholm** in 1924. Foreign military and intelligence services borrowed or tested examples. The exhibition spread awareness but did not create a viable mass civilian market. Banks, commercial houses, and ministries found the price high, while users had to coordinate keys securely and train operators.

Shareholder conflict intensified. Investors alleged diversion or withholding of funds; lawsuits and challenges to meetings followed; reporting and accounting were delayed. The surviving record establishes corporate disputes and allegations of financial irregularity, but not a criminal conviction of Scherbius.

Scherbius and Elisabeth moved into their Wannsee house this year. Their home and business premises became physically and financially intertwined.

### 1925: Höfle affair, parliamentary scrutiny, and ruin of the first company

On **10 February 1925**, Reich Post Minister **Anton Höfle** was arrested in a wider investigation involving embezzlement and passive bribery. ChiMaAG was not the principal allegation against him, but the company's network included relevant financial connections, including a bank loan associated with businessman Hermkes. Hermkes resigned on 21 February. ChiMaAG's 1924 annual report obscured lawsuits, losses, and unresolved transactions.

On **15 July 1925**, the Reichstag's Barmat investigatory committee examined ChiMaAG's dealings with Höfle and the Reichspost. National newspapers reported the hearing. The reputational and financial damage ruined the first corporate structure. The company carried substantial debt; only a small series of machines had been produced and sold; and the Reichspost machine had failed as a procurement project. Later restructurers continued to pay old liabilities into the early 1930s.

The same year, Scherbius's foundational 1918 patent was finally published as **DE416219**. Publication seven years after filing made its chronological priority visible, but came as the company that owned the technology was fighting for survival.

The spouses' 1925 separation-of-property agreement occurred in this context. It protected specific household assets, but the accessible evidence does not show a court finding that the agreement was unlawful.

### 1926–1927: Portable Enigma, naval adoption, and Reichswehr orders

Engineering changes turned the failed writing machine into a smaller lamp-display machine. The product sequence is approximate because model names, production dates, and surviving serial numbers do not always align:

| Model/line | Approximate debut | Significance |
|---|---:|---|
| *Handelsmaschine* | 1923 | Four cipher rotors with separate toothed drive wheels; large printing machine. |
| Enigma A/B | 1924 | Early commercial writing variants. |
| Enigma C | 1925 | More compact lamp machine. |
| Enigma D | 1926 | Portable commercial architecture exported to several countries. |
| *Funkschlüssel C* | Ordered 1925, delivered 1926 | Naval derivative; an early German military adoption. |
| Enigma K / A27 | 1927 | Commercial/government derivative. |
| Enigma I | Ordered 1927; service from 1930 | Reichswehr/Wehrmacht three-rotor plugboard machine. |
| Enigma H / Enigma II | 1929 | Eight-rotor printing machine, developed for high-level government use. |

The Reichsmarine ordered fifty *Funkschlüssel C* machines in 1925, with delivery beginning in 1926. A Reichswehr order for thirteen printing Enigmas in April 1926 was cancelled in June. On **17 May 1927**, the Army placed a much more consequential order for **400 Enigma I** machines. This is the clearest commercial turning point in Scherbius's lifetime, although full service introduction and wartime expansion came later.

The military Enigma was not the work of one inventor. Scherbius supplied the initiating rotor concept and entrepreneurial drive. **Paul Bernstein** worked on irregular drive and printing arrangements; **Willi Korn** developed the reflector (*Umkehrwalze*) and adjustable ring settings; **Elsbeth Rinke** was an important company engineer; **Ernst Ritter** was the business and engineering partner; **Rudolf Heimsoeth** later led military sales and succession. The Reichswehr's specifications added the front plugboard (*Steckerbrett*) in 1927–1928, sharply increasing the number of substitutions.

### 1928–May 1929: Board role, incomplete recovery, final work

Corporate records place Scherbius on ChiMaAG's management board in **1927–1928**, rather than continuously from its 1923 incorporation. The firm stabilized enough to continue, but accumulated losses meant no dividend and did not make Scherbius rich. He remained exposed through Scherbius & Ritter, patents, and the Wannsee property.

He continued work on electrical regulators and switches as well as cipher machines. A vacuum-switch invention received a United States patent only after his death. In 1929 the company completed or offered the large eight-rotor Enigma H/II. Its complexity echoed the strong multi-rotor ambitions of 1918, but it was expensive and did not become the standard field machine.

On 15 January 1929, Arthur and Elisabeth received the boy they intended to adopt. Less than four months later, Arthur was fatally injured.

### Technologies and projects

#### Motor control

The Scherbius cascade used a secondary machine or converter to recover and feed back slip energy from a wound-rotor induction motor. It permitted speed control at powers where resistive control wasted too much energy. “Scherbius drive” remains an engineering term and is a direct legacy independent of Enigma.

#### High-voltage direct current and rectification

Scherbius examined mercury-vapor rectifiers, direct-current transmission, and a DC machine. The *Neue Deutsche Biographie* describes the last as unfinished. These investigations anticipated fields that later became central to efficient power transmission but did not produce a comparably famous product in his lifetime.

#### Birka control and the Scherip heating cushion

Scherbius & Ritter acquired or used Swedish **Birka** thermostat rights. The business manufactured low-cost temperature regulators and a controlled electric heating cushion marketed as **Scherip**. The regulator made the cushion safer by limiting temperature. These goods supplied a more immediate civilian market than cryptography and helped sustain the company. After Scherbius's death, a British-linked concern, Sun Vic Ltd., established Birka Regulator GmbH; thermostats and switches continued to be made at the Wannsee premises for decades.

#### Enigma's architecture and security

At a simplified level, a keypress sent current through a plugboard in military models, then through several wired rotors, through a reflector, and back through the rotors to illuminate a letter. At least one rotor advanced, so the substitution changed continually. The reflector allowed the same settings to encrypt and decrypt but also ensured that a letter could never encrypt to itself, a property later exploited by cryptanalysts.

The often quoted military key-space number—about 159 quintillion settings—belongs to later plugboard military Enigma configurations, not to Scherbius's 1918 two-rotor demonstrator. Key-space size also did not equal operational security. Regular stepping, the reflector constraint, repeated or stereotyped message indicators, operator habits, cribs, captured key material, and intelligence about procedures all reduced the practical problem.

### Business structure, income, and property

- **Early income:** Patent licences from motor-control inventions, including payments from foreign electrical firms. Stable-currency receipts were especially valuable during German inflation.
- **Capital commitment:** Those receipts financed Scherbius's share in Scherbius & Ritter and supported continued cipher development before substantial sales existed.
- **Control:** Transfer of the cipher portfolio to Securitas in 1921 gave him manufacturing help and an equity interest but reduced his direct control. The Dutch-financed ChiMaAG structure diluted it further.
- **Sales:** Civilian Enigma sales were small. State testing was prolonged; the costly Post Office project failed; reliable military orders arrived only late in his life.
- **Dividends/wealth:** ChiMaAG carried old losses and did not generate the personal fortune sometimes implied by Enigma's later ubiquity. The Army order improved prospects but did not erase debts before his death.
- **Property:** The Scherbius & Ritter complex and family house stood at/near Königstraße 40, Berlin-Wannsee. The marital property settlement assigned the home to Elisabeth, but posthumous business collapse forced its loss.
- **Aftermath:** Scherbius & Ritter was converted to a limited partnership on 1 July 1929, entered insolvency proceedings in 1930–1931, and lingered legally until dissolution in 1936. ChiMaAG's business ultimately passed to Heimsoeth & Rinke in 1934–1935.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political and religious record

No party membership, electoral office, ideological manifesto, or documented political activism by Scherbius was found. He served the Imperial German war apparatus, negotiated with Weimar ministries, and sought military and postal contracts. Those facts show a state-dependent engineering business, not a demonstrated party ideology. His professional network crossed military, civil-service, Centre Party, trade-union, and industrial circles largely because those people controlled finance or procurement.

No reliable source establishes his religion or confessional practice. The national biography leaves confession unspecified. Assertions about belief would therefore be conjectural.

### Intellectual profile

His publications show three durable interests:

1. **Regulation and efficiency:** hydraulic governors, induction-motor speed control, thermostats, and power transmission.
2. **Electromagnetic theory:** the 1919 monograph sought physical and technical limits of frequency conversion in coils, transformers, and brushless machines.
3. **Automated secrecy:** he treated ciphering as an engineering service for modern communications rather than as a purely linguistic art. His 1923 papers linked radio's speed and reach with the necessity for mechanized secrecy.

Later family recollection portrays him as musically enthusiastic and prone to jot calculations on his shirt cuffs during concerts. This is plausible testimony about habits, not a contemporary source for a specific invention.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Chiffriermaschinen AG and the Barmat/Höfle investigations

**Documented facts:** ChiMaAG was embedded in a speculative inflation-era financing network; investors disputed capital movements and governance; lawsuits occurred; its reports understated or omitted important losses and proceedings; Post Minister Höfle was arrested in a wider corruption case; and the Reichstag Barmat Committee questioned ChiMaAG transactions in July 1925.

**Allegations/investigation:** The surrounding network used loans, board positions, and gifts to cultivate political and official access. Company dealings with the Reichspost were scrutinized. Newspapers associated the cipher company with the scandal.

**What is not established:** The sources consulted do not show Scherbius being indicted, tried, convicted, or judicially found to have bribed an official, embezzled money, falsified accounts, laundered funds, or defrauded investors. He was an inventor-shareholder and business participant in the troubled structure, not a proven criminal merely by association.

### Marital separation of property

The 1925 transfer gave Elisabeth the house and two accounts, excluding Arthur's business holdings. Its timing during financial distress can support an inference that household assets were being insulated from business risk. No located judgment invalidates it or establishes fraudulent conveyance. It should be reported as a financially consequential transaction, not as a proved fraud.

### Secrecy and possible treaty evasion

The use of Dutch entities and the concealment of military-relevant development have led historians to suggest that the structure helped evade Allied scrutiny under the Versailles settlement. German military interest, foreign patent vehicles, and secrecy are documented. An explicit order stating that ChiMaAG or Dutch Securitas existed for illegal treaty evasion has not been produced in the sources reviewed. The motive remains a supported historical hypothesis, not a court finding.

### Security claims

Advertising and institutional optimism helped create the idea that Enigma was unbreakable. Yet Schauffler had already described a theoretical route to attack in 1923, and later German evaluators identified procedural risks. There is no evidence that Scherbius perpetrated a scientific fraud about mathematical proof. Commercial rhetoric, extremely large nominal key counts, and confidence in operational difficulty gradually hardened into the later “unbreakable” legend.

### Criminality and intelligence connections

No personal criminal record, imprisonment, formal charge, or intelligence-service membership has been found. His wartime telegraph and procurement service and his postwar work for commissions gave him security-sensitive government connections. Calling him an “intelligence agent” goes beyond the evidence.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “Scherbius died by suicide after Enigma failed”

1. **Story:** A printed secondary account says he took his own life after business failure.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** The claim appears in Bengt Beckman's *Arne Beurling und Hitlers Geheimschreiber* and has occasionally migrated into derivative discussions.
3. **Evidence for:** His firms were financially distressed, which gives the story superficial plausibility. No suicide note, police finding, death certificate description, or contemporaneous report supporting suicide has been identified.
4. **Evidence against:** The national biography records an accident. Friedrich L. Bauer transmitted the account that an out-of-control horse-drawn vehicle pressed Scherbius against a wall, causing severe internal injuries. A local reconstruction based on family history gives the same mechanism. A contemporary *Hannoverscher Kurier* death notice dated 17 May 1929 confirms the death but does not name the cause.
5. **Effect:** The suicide version converts a contingent accident into a morality tale about a ruined inventor. On current evidence, it is an erroneous or at least wholly unsubstantiated printed claim; the carriage accident is the well-supported account.

The contemporaneous notice can be viewed in the [digitized *Hannoverscher Kurier*](https://digitale-sammlungen.gwlb.de/content/73496076X_HannoverscherKurier_19290517_01/73496076X_HannoverscherKurier_19290517_01.pdf), while the accident account appears in Bauer's [“Scherbius und die ENIGMA”](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-85790-7_10).

### 2. “Hugo Koch invented Enigma; Scherbius only commercialized it”

1. **Story:** Dutch engineer Hugo Koch supposedly invented the rotor machine in 1919, and Scherbius acquired or copied it before building Enigma.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** It appeared in an influential U.S. National Security Agency historical brochure and was repeated in popular histories.
3. **Evidence for:** Koch did file a Dutch rotor-cipher patent, and ChiMaAG acquired his patent rights in 1927.
4. **Evidence against:** Scherbius's independent German application is dated 23 February 1918, earlier than the Koch filing usually invoked. No evidence connects Koch's design causally to Scherbius's first machine. Acquisition in 1927 is better explained as patent consolidation or defensive protection.
5. **Effect:** It displaced priority from Scherbius and generated decades of incorrect genealogy. Bauer documented the error in [“An Error in the History of Rotor Encryption Devices”](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-85790-7_27).

### 3. “Arthur Scherbius single-handedly invented the wartime Enigma”

1. **Story:** A lone inventor created, essentially complete, the plugboard Enigma later used by the Wehrmacht.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Museum captions, short biographies, news features, and “great inventor” narratives often compress the lineage this way.
3. **Evidence for:** His 1918 patent is the foundational patent of the German Enigma line; he built prototypes, recruited partners, financed development, and advocated the machine to the state.
4. **Evidence against/qualification:** The 1918 design differed materially from the wartime device. Ritter, Bernstein, Korn, Rinke, Heimsoeth, company mechanics, and government evaluators made major contributions. The reflector, ring settings, military plugboard, manufacturing redesign, and procedures were collective or later additions.
5. **Effect:** The simplification makes Scherbius more visible but erases the engineering organization that converted a principle into a service weapon.

### 4. “Enigma was mathematically unbreakable”

1. **Story:** Its astronomical setting count made decryption impossible without the key.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Commercial promotion, German wartime confidence, and postwar popular culture all reinforced it.
3. **Evidence for:** The number of nominal military settings was enormous; when procedures were disciplined, traffic could be extremely difficult to solve with contemporary resources.
4. **Evidence against:** Polish mathematicians broke military Enigma beginning in 1932; later British, French, and allied teams exploited structural properties, indicators, operator habits, cribs, captured material, and electro-mechanical search. Scherbius-era Foreign Office testing had already treated the machine as theoretically attackable.
5. **Effect:** The myth amplified both German confidence and the later heroic stature of Allied cryptanalysis. It also obscures that security depended on the whole system—machine, keys, procedures, operators, and counterintelligence—not just a key-space number.

### 5. “Turing alone broke Enigma”

1. **Story:** Alan Turing personally solved the machine's secret from first principles.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** English-language films and popular biographies, especially narratives centered on Bletchley Park.
3. **Evidence for:** Turing made indispensable advances against Naval Enigma and helped design British Bombe methods.
4. **Evidence against:** Marian Rejewski reconstructed the military machine mathematically in 1932 with French-supplied intelligence; Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski developed Polish methods; Poland shared machines and knowledge with Britain and France in July 1939. Gordon Welchman and a large Bletchley Park organization, operators, engineers, linguists, traffic analysts, and captured documents were also essential. The [Bletchley Park account](https://www.bletchleypark.org.uk/our-story/enigma/) and [Imperial War Museums overview](https://www.iwm.org.uk/history/second-world-war/intelligence/how-alan-turing-cracked-the-enigma-code) describe this larger process.
5. **Effect:** It turns a multinational, institutional achievement into a lone-genius counter-myth mirroring the lone-inventor Scherbius story.

### 6. “The name Enigma came to Scherbius during music”

1. **Story:** A musically absorbed Scherbius conceived ideas at concerts, possibly drawing the name from Elgar's *Enigma Variations* or the Greek word for riddle.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Later family recollections reported by his nephew Helmuth Heimsoeth and speculative commentary by Friedrich L. Bauer.
3. **Evidence for:** Heimsoeth remembered frequent concertgoing and calculations written on cuffs; “Enigma” is an obvious learned name for a cipher product.
4. **Evidence against/limits:** No contemporary naming memorandum, diary, or letter attributes the word to Scherbius, Elgar, or a particular concert. The earliest established trade use is in 1923, after corporate rights had been transferred.
5. **Effect:** It gives the machine a culturally attractive origin story. It should remain a recollection plus hypothesis, not a fact about the naming event.

### 7. “His death prevented him from seeing his invention shape the war”

This statement is substantially true but can mislead if taken too absolutely. Scherbius lived long enough to see naval purchases, the 400-machine Army order, and a clear shift from failed civilian product to military equipment. He did **not** live to see the formal 1930 service introduction, mass Wehrmacht deployment, Polish break, Allied exploitation, or the machine's postwar symbolic fame.

### 8. Assassination or secret-service elimination

No evidence of assassination, deliberate vehicle attack, Allied operation, German political violence, or industrial murder was found. The accident's dramatic timing can invite conspiracy, but no identifiable early conspiracy narrative or supporting document emerged in the consulted sources. It is best classified as an unsupported possibility occasionally implied by sensational retellings, not a historical allegation with an evidentiary base.

The specifications listed in the patent table are legally published technical works but not conventional articles. ## Death

On **13 May 1929**, Scherbius was involved in a horse-drawn-carriage accident in Berlin. In the account preserved by Friedrich L. Bauer, the vehicle became uncontrollable and pressed him against a wall. He suffered severe internal injuries and died the same day. The exact street, time, hospital, vehicle ownership, and immediate medical diagnosis have not been established from a police or civil death record in the consulted material.

He was buried in the cemetery on Lindenstraße in Berlin-Wannsee. The grave no longer survives. A death notice appeared in the *Hannoverscher Kurier* on 17 May.

The adoption he and Elisabeth had initiated remained incomplete. His partnership and patent interests passed into a difficult estate and corporate unwinding. Scherbius & Ritter was reorganized shortly after his death and then became insolvent. Elisabeth described the result in family correspondence as the “catastrophe of Arthur's foundations.”

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate corporate and military afterlife

The Reichswehr formally introduced Enigma I into service on **1 June 1930**, just over a year after Scherbius's death. ChiMaAG continued but did not resolve all debts. In 1934–1935, **Heimsoeth & Rinke** acquired the business and became the principal German manufacturer and supplier. Tens of thousands of Enigma-family machines were eventually produced for German armed forces and state organizations, with different services adopting different rotors, procedures, and enhancements.

### Cryptanalytic afterlife

In December 1932, Polish mathematician **Marian Rejewski** reconstructed the military Enigma's internal logic by combining permutation theory with material obtained by French intelligence from German source Hans-Thilo Schmidt. Rejewski, **Jerzy Różycki**, and **Henryk Zygalski** developed methods and devices to recover keys. In July 1939 Poland shared its knowledge and replica machines with France and Britain.

At Bletchley Park, Alan Turing, Gordon Welchman, Dilly Knox, Hugh Alexander, Joan Clarke, engineers, Wrens, linguists, traffic analysts, and many others expanded machine-assisted attacks. Captured codebooks, weather messages, operator habits, and changes in German procedure affected success. Some Enigma networks remained unread for periods; others were read regularly. Any claim that “Enigma was broken” at one moment oversimplifies a continuing contest across multiple systems.

The intelligence derived from these decrypts, codenamed **ULTRA**, influenced the Battle of the Atlantic, North African and Mediterranean operations, preparations for D-Day, and other campaigns. Exact claims that it “shortened the war by X years” are counterfactual estimates, not demonstrable measurements, and should be identified as such.

### Engineering legacy beyond cryptography

The Scherbius cascade remains part of the history of adjustable-speed electric drives. The Birka/Scherip line contributed to thermostat and heating-control manufacture in Wannsee after his death. These achievements are regularly eclipsed by Enigma but were commercially and technically central during his lifetime.

### Reputation over time

1. **During his life:** Known in electrical engineering for motor regulation; in government and specialist circles as a persistent cipher-machine entrepreneur. The public Enigma product was more conspicuous as an expensive commercial novelty than as a future strategic weapon.
2. **1930s–1945:** His name receded behind military secrecy and the manufacturer Heimsoeth & Rinke. Operators knew the machine and model designations, not necessarily the inventor.
3. **Early postwar:** Allied secrecy about ULTRA restricted public understanding. Captured and surplus Enigmas circulated, sometimes to governments unaware of the extent of Allied wartime success.
4. **From 1967 onward:** David Kahn's *The Codebreakers* and later declassification made Enigma a central object in histories of cryptology and computing. Scherbius re-emerged as “the inventor of Enigma,” sometimes with the complexities of multiple inventors and corporate development removed.
5. **Present:** Museums, collectors, auction houses, historians of computing, and cryptologic institutions treat surviving machines as major artifacts. Popular culture concentrates on Turing and Bletchley Park; specialist research increasingly restores Polish priority, multinational work, and the obscure commercial prehistory. Scherbius is consequently both more famous and more carefully qualified: originator of the German Enigma lineage, not sole author of every wartime feature.

No state award, honorary doctorate, named public office, or lifetime decoration specific to Scherbius has been identified. His doctorate was earned, not honorary. Posthumous recognition is primarily historical, museum-based, and technical rather than an official honors tradition.
