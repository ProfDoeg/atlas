## Works by Virgil

Virgil's secure canon is small and was composed slowly: three poems across roughly thirty years, the last left unfinished at his death. A larger body of short poems circulated under his name in antiquity and after, almost all of it now judged spurious or doubtful by modern scholars. This file treats the three authentic works and the problem of the so-called *Appendix Vergiliana* separately, since they belong to entirely different categories of evidence.

### The Eclogues (Bucolics)

Ten short pastoral poems, composed roughly between 42 and 38 BCE against the backdrop of the land confiscations that followed the civil wars and the Battle of Philippi. Virgil adapted the Greek bucolic tradition of Theocritus into Latin, but where Theocritus's herdsmen mostly sing of love and song contests in a timeless Sicilian countryside, Virgil let contemporary history intrude: displaced farmers, soldiers seizing land, and the uncertain protection of well-connected patrons appear alongside singing matches and unrequited love. The poems are arranged with a deliberate symmetry, alternating dialogues and monologues around a central fifth poem. The most famous of the set, the fourth, departs from pastoral convention entirely to announce the coming of a golden age tied to the birth or coming-of-age of an unnamed child; its ambiguity (candidates have included a child of Octavian's sister Octavia, of Virgil's patron Pollio, or a purely symbolic figure) made it available centuries later to Christian readers, beginning with Emperor Constantine's circle, who read it as an unwitting pagan prophecy of Christ. That reading has no basis in the poem's own historical context but shaped its medieval reputation enormously. The collection survives complete.

### The Georgics

A four-book didactic poem, usually dated to about 37–29 BCE and dedicated to Virgil's patron Maecenas. On its surface it instructs a Roman landowner in farming: Book 1 covers tillage, weather signs, and the hardships of agricultural labor; Book 2 treats vines, olives, and trees, including a well-known passage praising the Italian countryside; Book 3 covers livestock breeding, ending in a grim account of a cattle plague; Book 4 describes beekeeping and bee society as a model of ordered communal life. The poem is not really a farming manual. Its instructions are repeatedly undercut by images of labor failing, animals dying, and violence intruding on rural order, reflecting the generation of civil war the poem was written within. Book 4 interrupts its own didactic frame for the story of Aristaeus, a beekeeper who loses his hive and must descend to the sea-depths of his mother Cyrene to learn the cause; nested inside that episode is the tragic account of Orpheus's failed attempt to bring his wife back from the underworld, among the most influential short narrative poems to survive from antiquity. Tradition holds that Virgil and Maecenas recited the finished poem to Augustus in 29 BCE. The work survives complete; ancient commentary, especially that of Servius, preserved information about Virgil's sources and possible later revisions to individual passages.

### The Aeneid

Virgil's unfinished epic, composed over roughly the last decade of his life (29–19 BCE) and left incomplete at his death. Twelve books in dactylic hexameter follow the Trojan prince Aeneas from the fall of Troy through a Mediterranean voyage (Books 1–6, echoing the wandering structure of Homer's *Odyssey*) and then through a war in Italy against the Latin peoples and their champion Turnus (Books 7–12, echoing the battle narrative of Homer's *Iliad*). Along the way Aeneas forms and abandons a relationship with Dido, queen of Carthage, who takes her own life after his departure; descends to the underworld, where he is shown a vision of Rome's future greatness; and ultimately kills Turnus in the poem's final lines, in an act whose morality ancient and modern readers have both defended and questioned. The poem fuses Trojan legend with Roman and Augustan political history, tracing a line from Aeneas through the legendary kings of Alba Longa to the founding of Rome and, by extension, to the Julian family and Augustus himself.

**Transmission and the unfinished state.** The poem shows clear signs of incompleteness: a number of lines break off mid-verse (the so-called "half-lines" or tibicines, literally "scaffolding," which ancient tradition says Virgil left as structural placeholders to be finished later), and certain episodes read as inconsistent or unresolved, most famously the controversial image of the "gate of false dreams" through which Aeneas leaves the underworld in Book 6, and the final killing of Turnus, both of which scholars still debate as either intentional or evidence of planned revision. Ancient biography reports that Virgil set out for Greece and Asia around 19 BCE intending to spend three years revising the poem on location before devoting himself to philosophy; that he fell ill at Megara after encountering Augustus returning from the East; and that he died shortly after landing at Brundisium. The same tradition says that before leaving Italy, and again on his deathbed, Virgil asked that the manuscript be burned if he did not survive to finish it, and that his request was refused. Augustus is reported to have personally ordered the poem's publication and instructed Virgil's literary executors and friends, Lucius Varius Rufus and Plotius Tucca, to edit the text with the least possible alteration — adding nothing and removing nothing substantial. Whether Virgil actually demanded the poem's destruction cannot be verified independently of this later biographical tradition, but the unfinished verses themselves support the claim that the text had not received a final revision.

The *Aeneid* was composed and circulated in writing rather than through oral performance, which gave it an unusually stable transmission for an ancient text. Surviving late-antique manuscripts include the richly illustrated *Vergilius Vaticanus* and *Vergilius Romanus*, along with fragments such as the first-century Hawara papyrus from Egypt, attesting to the poem's wide and early circulation. No manuscript from Virgil's own lifetime survives.

### The Appendix Vergiliana

A heterogeneous group of shorter Latin poems transmitted under Virgil's name since antiquity, assembled under this collective title by the sixteenth-century scholar Joseph Scaliger. It includes the *Catalepton* (short epigrams, some plausibly connected to Virgil's youth and his teacher Siro, though the collection as a whole is widely doubted); the *Culex* ("The Gnat," a miniature epic ancient tradition claimed Virgil wrote at sixteen, but which modern scholars generally regard as later or pseudonymous); the *Ciris* (an episode about Scylla's betrayal of her father, now generally considered non-Virgilian); the *Moretum* (a vignette of a farmer preparing a simple meal); the *Copa* (a tavern-keeper's invitation to a traveler); the *Dirae* (curses over confiscated land, attractive to later readers because of its echo of Virgil's own biography); and the astronomical-volcanic poem *Aetna*, among others. None of these is now accepted by mainstream scholarship as genuinely Virgil's work; some, such as the *Elegiae in Maecenatem*, can be proven spurious outright because they commemorate events after Virgil's death. Their persistent attribution to Virgil says more about his posthumous authority as a poetic brand than about his actual output.

### Lost and projected works

Ancient biography mentions an early poem on a figure called Ballista and an abandoned or unfinished project on Roman or Alban royal history, but neither survives or can be independently confirmed. No letters, speeches, or working drafts in Virgil's own hand have come down to us.
