## Works by Publius Vergilius Maro

### Secure poetic works

- **c. 42–38 BC:** *Bucolica* (*Eclogues*). An architecturally arranged book of ten pastoral poems in hexameters, not merely ten unrelated “texts”; the dates and original sequence of individual poems remain debated. ([Wiley Encyclopedia of Political Thought](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781118474396.wbept1025); [Cambridge University Press](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/32137/sample/9780521832137ws.pdf))

- **37–29 BC:** *Georgica* (*Georgics*). A four-book hexameter poem about agriculture, husbandry, nature, labor, politics, and the condition of Italy: formally didactic, but not reducible to a practical farming protocol. ([Wiley Encyclopedia of Political Thought](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781118474396.wbept1025))

- **29–19 BC:** *Aeneis* (*Aeneid*). A twelve-book epic poem about Aeneas’s journey from Troy and the violent beginnings of a Roman future. Virgil died before completing his intended revision, reportedly sought its destruction, and Varius Rufus and Plotius Tucca prepared the surviving text for posthumous publication under Augustus’s instructions; it is therefore both an extant obra and an unfinished project. ([Wiley Encyclopedia of Political Thought](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781118474396.wbept1025); [Suetonius–Donatus, *Life of Virgil*](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/de_Poetis/Vergil%2A.html))

### The Aeneid's unfinished state and early transmission

Beyond the fact of incompleteness, the poem carries physical marks of it: a number of lines break off mid-verse, the so-called "half-lines" or *tibicines* (literally "scaffolding"), which ancient tradition says Virgil left as structural placeholders to be finished later. Certain episodes also read as unresolved, most debated among them the "gate of false dreams" through which Aeneas leaves the underworld in Book 6 and the final killing of Turnus in the poem's last lines — both still argued over as either intentional or evidence of planned revision. Ancient biography reports that Virgil set out for Greece and Asia around 19 BC intending three years of on-site revision before turning to philosophy, fell ill at Megara after encountering Augustus returning from the East, and died shortly after landing at Brundisium. Composed and circulated in writing rather than by oral performance, the poem had an unusually stable early transmission: surviving late-antique manuscripts include the richly illustrated *Vergilius Vaticanus* and *Vergilius Romanus*, and fragments such as the first-century Hawara papyrus from Egypt attest to early and wide circulation. No manuscript from Virgil's own lifetime survives. (Wikipedia, "Aeneid")

### The Fourth Eclogue's afterlife

The fourth of the ten *Bucolica* departs from pastoral convention to announce a coming golden age tied to the birth or coming-of-age of an unnamed child; candidates proposed for the child have included a son of Octavian's sister Octavia, a child of Virgil's patron Pollio, or a purely symbolic figure, and the ambiguity is original to the poem rather than a later corruption. Centuries afterward, beginning with Emperor Constantine's circle, Christian readers took it as an unwitting pagan prophecy of Christ's birth — a reading with no basis in the poem's own historical context, but one that shaped its medieval reputation far beyond its original nine companion poems. (Wikipedia, "Eclogues")

### Attested early or abandoned projects

- **Youth; exact date unknown:** *Epigram on Ballista*. A two-line funerary satire said by the ancient biography to have been Virgil’s first poetic composition; it survives only through that biographical quotation, so its authenticity cannot be independently established. ([Suetonius–Donatus, *Life of Virgil*, §17](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/de_Poetis/Vergil%2A.html))

- **Before the *Bucolica*; exact date unknown:** *Roman history*. The ancient biography reports that Virgil began a poem or project on Roman historical material but abandoned it because he considered himself unequal to the subject. No text or fixed title survives: what can be listed is the frustrated project, not a completed obra. ([Suetonius–Donatus, *Life of Virgil*, §19](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/de_Poetis/Vergil%2A.html))

- **Reported as youthful; exact date unknown:** *Epigrammata*. Donatus’s list names epigrams separately among Virgil’s early compositions, but no securely identifiable independent collection survives; the title may overlap with poems later assembled as the *Catalepton*. It resists treatment as a recoverable work rather than a biographical notice. ([Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.621); [Suetonius–Donatus, *Life of Virgil*, §17](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/de_Poetis/Vergil%2A.html))

### Poems traditionally attributed to Virgil

The following survive in the heterogeneous collection conventionally called the *Appendix Vergiliana*. That collection is not itself a work made by Virgil: its modern name dates from Scaliger’s 1573 edition, its contents have varied, and modern scholarship generally regards the poems as works of uncertain or diverse authorship rather than a secure Virgilian oeuvre. ([Oxford Bibliographies](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195389661-0271); [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.621))

- **Date unknown; traditionally assigned to Virgil’s youth:** *Dirae*. A hexameter curse poem directed against an appropriated estate, transmitted together with *Lydia* but distinguishable from it in subject and voice. Donatus attributes *Dirae* to Virgil, yet modern scholarship does not securely accept that attribution. ([Oxford Bibliographies](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195389661-0271); [Suetonius–Donatus, *Life of Virgil*, §17](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/de_Poetis/Vergil%2A.html))

- **Date unknown:** *Lydia*. A pastoral-erotic lament conventionally attached to *Dirae* in transmission; whether the pair forms one composite work or two poems is itself uncertain, and Virgilian authorship is unproved. ([Oxford Bibliographies](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195389661-0271))

- **Date unknown; ancient tradition calls it youthful:** *Culex* (*The Gnat*). A mythological miniature epic, or epyllion, about a gnat that saves a shepherd and subsequently describes the underworld. The ancient biography assigns it to the sixteen-year-old Virgil, but its date and authorship are heavily disputed. ([Suetonius–Donatus, *Life of Virgil*, §§17–18](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/de_Poetis/Vergil%2A.html); [Oxford Bibliographies](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195389661-0271))

- **Date unknown:** *Aetna*. A hexameter didactic poem explaining the volcano and rejecting mythological accounts of its eruptions. Even the ancient biographical tradition marked its Virgilian authorship as disputed, and no secure date or author is known. ([Suetonius–Donatus, *Life of Virgil*, §19](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/de_Poetis/Vergil%2A.html); [Oxford Bibliographies](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195389661-0271))

- **Date unknown:** *Copa* (*The Barmaid*). A short elegiac invitation voiced by a dancing tavern woman: a dramatic genre-piece rather than a book. Servius, not Donatus, adds it to the Virgilian list, and its authorship remains anonymous. ([Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.621); [Goodyear, “The Copa: A Text and Commentary”](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2041-5370.1977.tb00373.x))

- **Date unknown:** *Ciris*. A mythological epyllion recounting Scylla’s betrayal of her father Nisus and her transformation into a bird. Donatus lists it as youthful Virgil, but its authorship and literary chronology remain contested. ([Suetonius–Donatus, *Life of Virgil*, §17](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/de_Poetis/Vergil%2A.html); [Oxford Bibliographies](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195389661-0271))

- **Date unknown:** *Priapea*. Three short poems addressed to or voiced by Priapus, transmitted within the appendical tradition. Donatus names youthful *Priapea*, but it is uncertain whether his title denotes these particular poems, another collection, or a category of compositions. ([Oxford Bibliographies](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195389661-0271); [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.621))

- **Date unknown:** *Catalepton*. A transmitted miscellany of fifteen or sixteen short poems—epigrams, invectives, dedications, and occasional verse—rather than a single generically unified poem. Some individual pieces have attracted arguments for Virgilian authorship, but the collection as received cannot securely be called something Virgil made. ([Oxford Bibliographies](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195389661-0271); [review of Clausen et al., *Appendix Vergiliana*](https://www.vergiliansociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/03/PVS5_1965-66.pdf))

- **Date unknown:** *Quid hoc novi est?* (*Priapeum*). A separate Priapic poem preserved alongside the *Catalepton* and the three *Priapea*. Its inclusion in the appendical corpus records attribution and manuscript association, not established authorship by Virgil. ([Oxford Bibliographies](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195389661-0271); [review of the Oxford Classical Text](https://www.persee.fr/doc/antiq_0770-2817_1967_num_36_2_2672_t1_0670_0000_2))

- **Date unknown:** *Moretum*. A hexameter narrative or miniature epic describing a poor farmer making a herb-and-cheese dish. It entered the medieval Virgilian corpus, but Donatus does not attribute it to Virgil and its author remains unknown. ([Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.621); [Oxford Bibliographies](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195389661-0271))

### Works transmitted with the Virgilian corpus but not plausibly made by Virgil

- **After 8 BC:** *Elegiae in Maecenatem*. Two elegiac poems mourning Maecenas. Because Maecenas died eleven years after Virgil, these are necessarily post-Virgilian creations despite their medieval association with his corpus. ([Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.621))

- **Late antiquity:** *De institutione viri boni*. A short gnomic poem included by later manuscripts and modern editions of the *Appendix*, but identified as a work of Ausonius rather than Virgil. Its presence is part of the history of the Virgilian textual assemblage, not Virgil’s making. ([Oxford Bibliographies](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195389661-0271))

- **Late antiquity:** *De est et non*. A brief didactic or grammatical poem transmitted in the expanded *Appendix* but belonging to Ausonius. It is therefore a manifestation of the later Virgilian corpus, not a Virgilian obra. ([Oxford Bibliographies](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195389661-0271))

- **Late antiquity; exact date unknown:** *De rosis nascentibus*. A poem on newly blooming roses included in some later manuscripts and critical editions of the *Appendix*. It is not accepted as Virgil’s and has itself been attributed, uncertainly, to Ausonius. ([Oxford Bibliographies](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780195389661-0271); [review of the Oxford Classical Text](https://www.persee.fr/doc/antiq_0770-2817_1967_num_36_2_2672_t1_0670_0000_2))
