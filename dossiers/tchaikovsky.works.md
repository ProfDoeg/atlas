## Works by Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky

### Operas and operatic projects

- **1854:** *Hyperbola*. Project for a one-act lyrical opera; no completed score is known.
- **1864–67?:** *The Storm*. Projected opera, distinct from the completed orchestral overture after Ostrovsky.
- **1867–68:** *The Voyevoda*, Op. 3. Opera in three acts and four scenes; Tchaikovsky destroyed the score, so the surviving work is fragmentary and later performances depend on posthumous reconstruction.
- **1867–68?:** *The Palm*. Surviving sketches for a projected opera.
- **1868:** *Alexander of Macedon*. Projected opera that did not reach a finished score.
- **1869:** *Undina*. Opera in three acts; never staged and largely destroyed by Tchaikovsky in 1873, although portions survive or were reused.
- **1869–70:** *Mandragora*. Project for a one-act fantastic opera; only the *Chorus of Flowers and Insects* was completed.
- **1870:** *Raimond Lully*. Projected opera.
- **1870–72:** *The Oprichnik*. Opera in four acts.
- **1874:** *Vakula the Smith*, Op. 14. Comic-fantastic opera; later radically revised as *Cherevichki*, making the two scores related but distinct manifestations.
- **1875–76:** *Ephraim*. Project for a four-act opera.
- **1876:** *Francesca da Rimini*. Projected opera, distinct from the completed orchestral fantasia.
- **1876:** *Dobrynya Nikitich*. Project for a grand opera in four acts.
- **1876–77:** *Othello*. Project for a four-act opera.
- **1877–78:** *Yevgeny Onegin*, Op. 24. “Lyrical scenes” in three acts rather than a conventional grand opera.
- **1878:** *Taras Bulba*. Project for an opera in five acts.
- **1878–81?:** *Romeo and Juliet*. Operatic project represented by sketches; distinct from the orchestral overture-fantasia.
- **1878–93:** *Les Caprices de Marianne*. Repeatedly considered but unrealized operatic project.
- **1878–93:** *Undina*. A later projected opera, separate from the destroyed 1869 score.
- **1878–79:** *The Maid of Orleans*. Grand opera in four acts.
- **1881–82:** *Vanka the Steward*. Projected opera.
- **1881–83:** *Mazepa*. Opera in three acts.
- **1884:** *The Gypsies*. Projected opera.
- **1885–87:** *The Enchantress*. Opera in four acts.
- **1885–88:** *The Captain’s Daughter*. Recurrent operatic project that remained unrealized.
- **1886–87:** *Undina*. Projected ballet treatment of the subject; its intended medium makes it distinct from both opera projects.
- **1887:** *Song of Triumphant Love*. Operatic sketches rather than a completed work.
- **1888:** *The Bayadere*. Project for an opera in three acts.
- **1888–91:** *La Courtisane*, also called *Sadia*. Projected three-act opera whose unstable title reflects its unfinished status.
- **1889–93:** *Béla*. Project for a four-act opera.
- **1890:** *The Queen of Spades*, Op. 68. Opera in three acts and seven scenes.
- **1891:** *Watanabe*. Projected four-act opera-ballet: a hybrid project resisting a clean separation between opera and ballet.
- **1891:** *Iolanta*, Op. 69. One-act lyrical opera.
- **1893:** *The Sad Fortunes of the Reverend Amos Barton*. Projected opera after George Eliot.
- **1893:** *Mr Gilfil’s Love Story*. Projected opera after George Eliot.
- **1893:** *Adam Bede*. Projected opera after George Eliot.
- **Unknown:** *An Intricate Case*. Unidentified operatic sketches; neither date nor intended final form is secure.

### Ballets and choreographic works

- **1870:** *Cinderella*. Project for a ballet in four acts; no completed score survives.
- **1875–76:** *Swan Lake*, Op. 20. Ballet in four acts.
- **1882:** *Swan Lake Suite*. Project for an orchestral suite from the ballet; Tchaikovsky apparently did not complete or authorize the familiar published suite.
- **1888–89:** *The Sleeping Beauty*, Op. 66. Ballet in a prologue and three acts.
- **1890:** *The Sleeping Beauty Suite*. Projected suite from the ballet; no definitive suite by Tchaikovsky survives.
- **1891–92:** *The Nutcracker*, Op. 71. Ballet in two acts.
- **1892:** *The Nutcracker Suite*, Op. 71a. Orchestral suite selected and arranged by Tchaikovsky from the still-unperformed ballet; it is a separate concert manifestation of the larger score.

### Music for plays, spectacles, and tableaux

- **1864:** *The Storm*. Overture to Ostrovsky’s play; an independent orchestral composition rather than incidental music for a production.
- **1867:** *Dmitry the Pretender and Vasily Shuysky*. Introduction and mazurka for Ostrovsky’s play.
- **1867:** *The Tangle*. Music for a vaudeville; lost.
- **1868:** *Le Domino noir*. Additional recitatives and an introductory chorus for a Russian production of Auber’s opera; intervention in another composer’s work rather than an autonomous opera.
- **1872:** *The Barber of Seville*. Couplets for Count Almaviva in a production of Beaumarchais’s comedy.
- **1873:** *The Snow Maiden*, Op. 12. Extensive incidental music for Ostrovsky’s spring fairy tale, combining orchestral, choral, and solo-vocal forms without becoming an opera.
- **1879?:** *La Fée*. Cradle song and waltz for Octave Feuillet’s comedy; lost.
- **1880:** *Montenegro*. Music for a tableau vivant; lost.
- **1886:** *The Voyevoda*. Melodrama for Ostrovsky’s play, unrelated as a made object to the destroyed opera and later symphonic ballad of the same title.
- **1891:** *Hamlet*, Op. 67b. Incidental music for a stage production, incorporating and transforming material from the earlier overture-fantasia.

- **1892:** *Symphony in E-flat major*. Abandoned symphonic project; its first movement became the Third Piano Concerto, while other material was completed posthumously by Sergey Taneyev as the *Andante and Finale*. Later “Symphony No. 7” performances are reconstructions, not a finished Tchaikovsky score.
- **1893:** *Symphony No. 6 in B minor, “Pathétique,”* Op. 74. Symphony whose title was adopted immediately before the premiere.
- **1893:** *March on Belorussian and Ukrainian Folk Tunes*. Sketches for an orchestral march; unfinished.

### Music-theory books and pedagogical protocols

- **1871:** *Guide to the Practical Study of Harmony*. Textbook and instructional protocol for harmony study.
- **1875:** *A Short Manual of Harmony*. Adaptation of the earlier textbook for Russian church-music study; a distinct pedagogical manifestation rather than merely a reprint.

### Music criticism, autobiography, and public prose

- **1868–76:** *Music-critical articles for the Contemporary Chronicle, Russian Register, and other periodicals*. A corpus of sixty-five catalogued reviews, chronicles, essays, polemics, and notices, including the recurring “Musical Chronicle” reports and writings on Glinka, Dargomyzhsky, Mozart, Beethoven, Wagner, Russian opera, Italian opera, the Russian Musical Society, and the Bayreuth Festival. These pieces are individual journalistic texts, but the standard catalogue also makes sense of them as an obra of sustained critical practice; several appeared anonymously or under pseudonyms.
- **1872:** *A Musical Note*. Critical article; distinct from later articles with the same generic title.
- **1873:** *A Musical Note*. Critical article.
- **1874:** *A Musical Note*. Critical article.
- **1888:** *Autobiographical Account of a Tour Abroad in the Year 1888*. Autobiographical article first published in German; it is a constructed public self-account rather than a private diary.
- **Date uncertain:** *Beethoven and His Time*. Essay.
- **Date uncertain:** *Wagner and His Music*. Essay.
- **Date uncertain:** *About the Cefaut Clef*. Music-theoretical note.
- **Date uncertain:** *Dictionary of the Russian Language*. Contribution or lexicographical intervention whose status as an autonomous authored work is limited.
- **Date uncertain:** *Obituary: Ivan Osipovich Shramek*. Memorial article.
- **Date uncertain:** *Endorsement of Thomas Edison’s Phonograph*. Public testimonial: a text that performs endorsement and therefore approaches a manifesto or speech-act rather than detached description.

### Diaries, letters, notebooks, and documentary traces

- **1873, 1881, and 1884–90:** *Diaries*. A discontinuous series of dated private journals rather than a single designed book; the surviving diaries cover only particular months and years and should not be mistaken for a continuous life record.
- **1848–93 and undated:** *Correspondence*. An obra comprising 5,374 presently known letters addressed to 379 correspondents; the catalogue’s term “letter” also includes telegrams, visiting-card notes, and texts sent to newspaper editors, so this corpus resists classification as a single literary genre.
- **Dates various:** *Notebooks and sketchbooks*. Working repositories containing musical sketches, verbal notes, accounts, itineraries, and other documentary matter; many entries are evidence for projects rather than autonomous works.
