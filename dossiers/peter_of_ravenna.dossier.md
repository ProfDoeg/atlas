# Peter of Ravenna Research Dossier

**Pietro Francesco Tomai / Tomasi — Petrus Ravennas — Pietro da Ravenna — “Petrus a Memoria”**  
**c. 1448, Ravenna — 1509–1510, probably Worms**  
Italian jurist, university professor, poet, public mnemonist, author of *Phoenix sive artificiosa memoria*.

---

## Research note and evidentiary method

Peter of Ravenna is unusually difficult to reconstruct because the most vivid evidence about him is also self-promotional. His *Phoenix* contains autobiographical anecdotes, lists of pupils, claims of prodigious feats, named witnesses, courtly testimonials, and the text of the Venetian printing privilege. These are primary evidence for what Peter said about himself and for the existence of the appended testimonials; they are not automatically independent verification of every feat. University, civic, bibliographical, and polemical records provide a firmer external framework.

Modern biographical reconstruction also corrects several long-repeated shorthand claims:

- Peter was born **around 1448**, not on a securely known day.
- Modern Italian and German reference scholarship places his death **in 1509–1510, probably at Worms**, rather than simply “1508 at Mainz.” Older catalogues and biographies often give 1508 or 1509.
- The colophon of the first *Phoenix* says **10 January 1491**. Because Venetian dating began the year on 1 March, incunable catalogues commonly normalize this as **10 January 1491/92**.
- *Phoenix* was **not securely the first printed work on artificial memory**. Jacobus Publicius's *Oratoriae artis epitome* (1482) already contained a printed *ars memorativa*. Peter's book was, however, one of the earliest important printed treatments, an unusually successful early standalone manual, and a major vehicle for the European diffusion of the classical *ars memoriae*.
- Peter's Venetian privilege was an important early author-centered printing privilege, but it was **not literally the first copyright**. Venice had granted earlier privileges, including one to Marcantonio Sabellico in 1486. Calling Peter the inventor or first holder of “copyright” is anachronistic unless carefully qualified.

The most dependable modern biographical anchors used here are Francesco Lucioli's entry in the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* (2019), Dieter Girgensohn's *Neue Deutsche Biographie* entry (2001), the Harvard Ames Foundation's canon-law bibliography, Luis Merino Jerez's studies of the *Phoenix*, and surviving or digitized early prints. Older narratives by Tiraboschi, Eisenhart, Muther, Heidenheimer, and others remain important because they preserve the reception history and sometimes cite records no longer easily accessible, but their character judgments and dramatic retellings are treated as such.

## Basic Identifying Information

### Names and forms

The man's personal and family name is transmitted in many forms:

- **Pietro Francesco Tomai**
- **Pietro Francesco Tomasi**
- **Pietro Francesco Tommai**
- **Pietro da Ravenna**
- **Petrus Ravennas**
- **Petrus Ravennatis**
- **Petrus Ravennatus**
- **Petrus de Ravenna**
- **Petrus Thomai**
- **Petrus Thomasius**
- **Thomasius Petrus Franciscus**
- **Pedro de Ravena**
- **Pierre de Ravenne**
- **Petrus von Ravenna**
- **Petrus a Memoria**
- **Pietro da la Memoria / Pietro da Memoria**

The German biographical tradition warns that the family name is not completely secure. The older *Allgemeine Deutsche Biographie* argued that “Thomai/Thomasi” should not automatically be converted into a family “Thomasini,” and rejected an old conjecture that he was named Giovanni Battista. Modern Italian scholarship nevertheless normally catalogues him as **Pietro Francesco Tomai (Tommai, Tomasi, Tommasi)**.

### Titles and self-descriptions

During his career he was described or described himself as:

- **doctor iuris utriusque** — doctor of both civil and canon law;
- **iuris consultus** — jurist / legal consultant;
- professor or reader of canon and civil law;
- **memoriae magister** — master of memory;
- **Petrus a Memoria** — Peter “of Memory”;
- **eques auratus / miles auratus** — knight of the golden spur or “golden knight,” an imperial honor;
- humanist poet and occasional court poet;
- Franciscan tertiary late in life.

His most durable public identity fused two professions that were normally separable: university jurist and virtuoso mnemonist.

### Birth

- **Date:** approximately **1448**.
- **Place:** **Ravenna**, then within the political orbit of the Papal States/Venetian-Adriatic Italy.
- No exact birthday has been established in the consulted sources.

### Death

- **Date:** probably **1509 or 1510**.
- **Place:** probably **Worms**, in the Holy Roman Empire.
- The older date “1508” survives widely in catalogues and summaries because he left Cologne and reached Mainz in that year; modern reconstruction follows evidence that he was still active later, including at Worms.
- **Cause of death:** not securely documented.
- A later polemical letter by Johann Reuchlin said that Peter died *prae maerore*, “from grief,” after Jacob Hoogstraten drove him from Cologne. This is a partisan posthumous characterization, not a medical record.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father

Peter's father was **Gioacchino**, who was dead by about 1467. No secure profession, ancestry, wealth profile, or office for Gioacchino emerged from the consulted material.

### Mother

Peter's mother's name is unknown in the principal modern biographies.

### Social origin

The family does not appear to have belonged to the most prominent patrician houses. During his later quarrel with Jacob Hoogstraten, whose Latinized name could be rendered *de Alta Platea* (“of the high street”), Peter jokingly styled himself *de bassa platea* (“of the low street”). The nineteenth-century German biography turned this pun into an inference of humble birth. It is safer to treat it first as polemical wordplay; the surviving evidence nevertheless does not show a great noble lineage behind him.

### Wives

Peter married at least twice.

1. **First wife:** name unknown.
2. **Second wife:** **Lucrezia Azzoni**, from **Este**.

The wording of the modern Italian and German biographies indicates that his numerous children were born across these two marriages. No reliable evidence located here supports a third wife.

### Children

The exact number of children is unknown. The documentary record establishes at least:

- **Vincenzo (Vincentius) Tomai**, born about **1475**, a jurist who studied or worked at Padua, Greifswald, Wittenberg, and later Rome. He accompanied his father into the German university world and is the best documented child.
- **Giambattista**, matriculated at the University of Greifswald in **1502**.
- **Margherita**, daughter, died in **1502** during an epidemic that killed several of Peter's children.

Peter's large household had direct financial consequences. At Padua his salary was augmented in 1492 by an additional **50 ducats annually**, specifically because of his many children.

### Epidemic losses

While the family was in the German north, an epidemic killed several of Peter's children, including Margherita. The surviving accounts do not securely identify the disease. The family losses contributed to Peter's desire to return to Italy. He obtained permission to depart with Lucrezia and the surviving sons Giambattista and Vincenzo, but his career was redirected to Wittenberg instead.

## Childhood and Early Life

Very little is securely known about Peter's childhood in Ravenna. The later adult constructed a powerful retrospective narrative in which extraordinary memory had already appeared in youth. The surviving evidence does not permit reconstruction of childhood schooling, siblings, household finances, or a specific Ravenna teacher.

His own *Phoenix* repeatedly emphasizes youthful self-invention. He says that he had **no teacher in artificial memory** and, with divine assistance, discovered the rules through labor. Modern scholarship therefore describes him as claiming to be **self-taught in the ars memoriae**. This does not mean he lacked access to the older rhetorical tradition. His mature method belongs plainly to the ancient and medieval system of *loci* and *imagines* associated with the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Cicero, Quintilian, scholastic mnemonic practice, and the fifteenth-century recovery and publication of rhetorical arts.

The intellectual geography of his early life was therefore double:

- **Ravenna** supplied birthplace, civic identity, and the classical prestige implied by “ancient Ravenna.”
- **Padua** supplied legal formation, institutional certification, teachers, audiences, and the setting in which he first made memory into a public identity.

## Education and Formation

### Padua and Alessandro Tartagni da Imola

Peter moved to **Padua** to study law. His major teacher was **Alessandro Tartagni da Imola** (Alexander de Tartagnis / Alexander Imolensis), one of the leading fifteenth-century jurists.

The chronology is reconstructed as follows:

- By **1468**, Peter was already studying in Padua and later claimed a spectacular public demonstration there.
- In **1471**, the *Neue Deutsche Biographie* records that he received a **licence in Roman law**.
- By **1472**, he had become **doctor iuris utriusque**, doctor of civil and canon law. The *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* uses 1472 for the doctorate.
- From **1474**, German biographical reconstruction places him among the ordinary professors and additionally within a judicial college.

The distinction between a 1471 licence and a 1472 doctorate resolves what can otherwise look like contradictory dates.

### The celebrated 1468 demonstration

The most famous formative story is dated to **25 November 1468**, the feast of St Catherine, when Peter was about twenty.

According to Peter's autobiographical tradition, later elaborated by the *Allgemeine Deutsche Biographie*, he publicly claimed to know the civil-law corpus from memory. Arbitrary passages were proposed; he allegedly reproduced the text and then supplied Bartolus's summaries, glosses, opinions of doctors, and contrary views. His teacher Alessandro da Imola was said to have been stupefied and to have crossed himself “as if warding off evil,” while others embraced the young prodigy.

**Status of the story:**  
- The feat is primarily known through Peter's own promotional memory narrative and later retellings.
- Named witnesses occur in *Phoenix* for related Padua feats.
- No independent verbatim university protocol of the full miracle-like episode was located in the consulted sources.
- The story is historically important because it became the foundational legend of “Pietro da Memoria.”

### Legal and mnemonic formation as one practice

Peter did not treat memory as an isolated party trick. Legal education in the age of glossators and commentators required command of massive bodies of authorities. His mature mnemonic program specifically maps:

- books of Roman law by color and object;
- the *Decretum* and papal *Decretals*;
- glosses and commentators;
- legal rubrics;
- arguments and contrary arguments;
- canon-law distinctions and questions;
- numbers and citations.

His “artificial memory” was therefore both a rhetorical art and a technology of professional legal information retrieval.

## Early Career

### 1472: *Oratio pro patria*

In 1472 Nicolas Jenson printed at Venice Peter's **_Oratio pro patria ad illustrissimum principem Nicolaum Tronum_**, addressed to Doge **Nicolò Tron**. The Glasgow incunabula catalogue dates it after 25 February 1472 and describes an extremely short print of two leaves.

This is important for two reasons:

1. Peter was already using print at the beginning of his professional life, almost two decades before *Phoenix*.
2. His public identity was not limited to law lectures; it included civic-humanist oratory.

### 1470s: teaching in several Italian universities

The sources do not agree perfectly on sequence. The best reconstruction is:

- legal teaching at **Padua** after qualification;
- work or lectures in **Pavia** and **Bologna**;
- transfer to the Florentine university when it was operating at **Pisa**;
- association with **Pistoia** from the late 1470s into 1481.

The older German biography says he taught at Pisa from **1477 to the end of 1479** for a stipend of **355 florins/gulden** and participated in work connected with the first volume of university statutes published in 1480. Modern Italian biography condenses the period as teaching in Pavia, Bologna, and Pistoia, with residence in Pistoia **1477–1481**. These accounts are not necessarily mutually exclusive because the Florentine Studio moved and Peter's teaching life was itinerant.

### Pistoia citizenship, 1480

A civic letter dated **12 September 1480**, reproduced with *Phoenix*, granted Peter citizenship in **Pistoia** and extended it to his descendants. The privilege included eligibility for public offices and a lengthy exemption — described in the translated text as thirty years — from certain civic payments or taxes.

This is one of the strongest pieces of documentary evidence for his growing social value. His mnemonic reputation could be converted into civic honor and material privilege.

### A mobile professional strategy

Peter's career never settled into a single city for long. His movement between universities, courts, and public demonstrations was a feature rather than an accident. An older anecdote says that when friends warned him against wandering, he answered that he had less difficulty than others because he “carried all his possessions with him.” In *Phoenix* he applies the same phrase to the memory system: as a traveler through the cities of Italy, he could say that he carried everything with him because his intellectual storehouse was internal.

The line is both practical and self-mythologizing: the itinerant professor turns memory into portable property.

## Chronological Life History

## c. 1448–1467: Ravenna

- Born around 1448 in Ravenna to Gioacchino and an unidentified mother.
- No secure evidence survives here for siblings, primary schooling, or childhood wealth.
- Father Gioacchino died before 1467.
- Peter later claimed that the techniques underlying his artificial memory were his own discovery rather than a craft learned from a named memory master.

## 1468–1472: Padua — legal formation and the making of “Pietro da Memoria”

- Studies law under Alessandro Tartagni da Imola.
- **25 November 1468:** later accounts place his major public memory demonstration at Padua.
- Peter's own narrative presents legal memorization as spectacular performance: passages from the *Corpus iuris civilis*, summaries, glosses, learned opinions, and contraries could supposedly be produced on demand.
- **1471:** licence in Roman law.
- **1472:** doctorate in both laws.
- **1472:** *Oratio pro patria* printed at Venice by Nicolas Jenson.

The combination of degree, early print, and public mnemonics gave Peter a distinctive scholarly brand unusually early in his career.

## 1474–1481: professor, judge, Pisa/Pistoia period

- From about **1474**, held ordinary teaching responsibilities and participated in a judicial college at Padua according to the German reference tradition.
- Taught or lectured in multiple northern and central Italian centers.
- **1477–1479:** older evidence places him at the Florentine Studio in Pisa with a stipend of 355 florins/gulden.
- **1477–1481:** strong modern biographical tradition associates him with Pistoia.
- **12 September 1480:** Pistoia grants citizenship, civic eligibility, and a long tax/payment immunity to Peter and his descendants.
- His memory demonstrations are said to have accompanied academic travel rather than occurring only at Padua.

## 1482/83–1497: Padua — established professor, court connections, *Phoenix*

Peter returned to Padua by **1482–1483** and occupied a chair in **canon law**.

### Salary

- Initial salary: **80 ducats**.
- **1484:** raised to **150 ducats**.
- **1492:** another **50 ducats annually** because of his many children.

These are unusually concrete financial data for Peter. They show that his university income was significant enough to be negotiated upward and that household burden was recognized institutionally.

### Poetry and imperial distinction

- About **1485:** a collection of **_Carmina_** was printed at Padua by Matthaeus Cerdonis.
- He also wrote or circulated funeral poems connected with **Pietro Fortiguerra**.
- He bore the title **eques auratus / miles auratus**. Modern German reconstruction associates the honor with Emperor **Frederick III** and notes it as documented by 1487.

### Bonifacio of Montferrat, 1487

A testimonial dated **23 September 1487** from **Bonifacio, Marquis of Montferrat**, praises Peter's extraordinary memory and calls him a Caesarean or imperial knight. It asks authorities to allow Peter and his party safe passage with servants, horses, money, goods, and baggage.

The document shows that mnemonic celebrity was backed by the forms of court patronage and mobility ordinarily extended to valued learned men.

### Lancellotto Decio, 1488

A letter from **Lancellotto Decio** at Pavia, dated in November **1488**, praises Peter in the language of prodigious memory. It belongs to the network of jurists and academics through which Peter's reputation circulated.

### Cassandra Fedele

During one of his Venetian appearances Peter met **Cassandra Fedele**, the celebrated Venetian humanist. The modern *Dizionario Biografico* includes this meeting among the evidence of his memory tours. The consulted sources do not establish a sustained correspondence or friendship, so the connection should not be enlarged beyond an encounter unless further archival evidence emerges.

### Ferrara and the Este court

Peter demonstrated his art in **Ferrara**, where he cultivated the court of **Ercole I d'Este** and **Eleonora of Aragon (Eleonora of Naples)**. He claimed to have recited sermons from memory in their presence.

A ducal testimonial dated **10 October 1491** praises him as a knight and distinguished jurist with exceptional memory, receives him among the duchess's retainers or household protection, and requests free passage for him, his servants, up to eight horses, baggage, books, silver, weapons, and other possessions without ordinary duties or obstructions.

This is not simply praise. It is evidence of the logistical world of a traveling professor: people, horses, books, valuable objects, and formal protection across jurisdictions.

## 1491/92: *Phoenix sive artificiosa memoria*

### Printing

*Phoenix* was printed at Venice by **Bernardinus de Choris of Cremona**.

The text contains two dates:

- Venetian privilege: **3 January 1491**.
- Colophon: **10 January 1491**.

Modern incunabula catalogues usually normalize the colophon as **10 January 1491/92**, because Venice counted the new year from 1 March. Scholarship often continues to cite the book simply as “1491.”

### The privilege

Peter petitioned the Venetian authorities on the ground that he had spent his life in vigils and labor acquiring the art and had composed *Phoenix* for public benefit. The decree prohibited anyone in Venice and its dominions from printing the work or selling copies printed elsewhere except the printer chosen by Peter. The sanction included forfeiture and a monetary penalty per copy.

This is an important early **printing privilege** centered on an author and his chosen printer. It resembles later intellectual-property protection but belongs to the privilege system of the early print economy, not to modern statutory copyright.

### Commercial and reputational result

*Phoenix* was repeatedly printed across Europe and remained in circulation into the seventeenth century. It became Peter's most durable work and eventually eclipsed his legal writings in modern memory studies.

## 1497–1503: departure from Italy and Greifswald

### Recruitment by Bogislaw X

In **November 1497**, **Bogislaw X, Duke of Pomerania**, selected Peter to teach civil and canon law at the **University of Greifswald**.

An older narrative says that Bogislaw, returning from pilgrimage to the Holy Land, heard Peter praised while staying in Venice and sent trusted men to investigate him at Padua. Whether every detail of the recruitment anecdote can be independently confirmed, the appointment itself is secure.

### Innsbruck and Maximilian I

Before arriving in Greifswald, Peter visited **Emperor Maximilian I at Innsbruck**. The German biographical tradition says he performed poetry and legal allegations before the emperor. This continued the pattern of converting scholarly virtuosity into court access.

### Arrival in Greifswald

Peter reached **Greifswald in April 1498** with his family.

He taught **both civil and canon law** and was recruited in part to strengthen or reform legal study. His son **Vincenzo** also entered the German academic network.

### Rectorate

Sources differ in emphasis:

- the modern Italian biography states that he served as rector beginning in **1501**;
- German biographical reconstruction records rectoral service in **1498 and 1501**.

The safest statement is that he held the rectorate at Greifswald, certainly in 1501 and possibly also in 1498.

### Northern travel: Hamburg and Lübeck

Peter traveled or stayed in **Hamburg** and **Lübeck** while attached to the northern German world.

In **1499**, a legal work, **_Repetitio C. inter alia de immunitate ecclesiae_**, was printed at Lübeck. The book concerns ecclesiastical immunity.

A bibliographical footnote sometimes encountered in research on Lübeck printing records a **404 Lübeck-mark debt** and a court restriction in July 1500. That debt belongs to the printer **Lucas Brandis**, not to Peter. It should not be transferred to Peter's financial biography.

### *Aurea opuscula*, 1502

At Leipzig, **Wolfgang Stöckel** printed the **_Aurea opuscula_** in 1502. The collection included *Phoenix* and additional Latin poems or shorter writings. The title demonstrates the bundling of Peter's mnemonic celebrity with his legal and literary identity.

### Danish invitation

Peter was invited to the court of **King John of Denmark**. He declined. The record shows how far his reputation had traveled in northern Europe but does not establish a sustained Danish political relationship.

### Family deaths and plan to return to Italy

An epidemic killed several of Peter's children, including **Margherita**. Peter obtained permission to return to Italy with Lucrezia and the surviving sons **Giambattista** and **Vincenzo**.

The intended return did not occur. A new patron intervened.

## 1503–1506: Wittenberg and Frederick the Wise

**Frederick III, Elector of Saxony (“Frederick the Wise”)**, recruited Peter to the new **University of Wittenberg**.

Peter held a law chair there from **1503 to 1506**. This placed him in an institution founded only in 1502 and thus in the formative generation of a university that would soon become central to the Reformation, although Peter's Wittenberg career predated Luther's famous conflict by more than a decade.

### Wittenberg-period publications

Important works include:

- **_Lectio de potestate pontificis maximi et romani imperatoris_** (1503), on the powers of pope and emperor;
- **_Compendium iuris civilis_** (1503);
- **_Clypeus contra doctorem Caium_** (1503);
- **_Compendium pulcherrimum iuris canonici_** (1504–1506);
- **_Sermones extraordinarii et pulcherrimi_** (1505).

These works show that *Phoenix* did not replace his legal profession. Through the last decade of his life he continued producing compendia, lectures, polemics, and legal teaching texts.

## 1506–1508: Cologne — academic success, Franciscan affiliation, theological-legal conflict

### Flight from plague

Peter left Wittenberg during a plague and went to **Cologne** in **1506**.

He taught law there from **1506 to 1508**. Merino Jerez's reconstruction says that he initially received a warm welcome from professors, humanists, and prominent citizens.

### Franciscan Third Order

In **1507**, Peter and his wife **Lucrezia** entered the **Franciscan Third Order**. This is the clearest documentary statement about his late-life religious affiliation.

### Agrippa

The young **Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa von Nettesheim** was in the intellectual world of Cologne. Later sources and modern scholarship identify Peter as an important teacher of Agrippa or state that Agrippa heard his lectures. The Cambridge *Memory Arts* volume says Agrippa boasted that Peter had been his teacher. Because the exact pedagogical relationship is reconstructed retrospectively, “teacher or lecturer heard by Agrippa” is more precise than assuming a long master-disciple apprenticeship.

Agrippa later mentioned Peter among writers on memory even while criticizing the vanity or limitations of memory arts.

### 1508 legal publications

At Cologne, **Heinrich Quentel** printed:

- **_Compendium ... in materia consuetudinum feudorum_**, a feudal-law compendium;
- **_Alphabetum aureum_**, a large legal reference work arranged alphabetically.

The *Alphabetum* became entangled with the most serious controversy of Peter's career.

## The Cologne controversy, 1507–1508

### Issue 1: burial of executed criminals

Peter attacked a German practice by which the bodies of executed criminals could remain hanging on gallows or displayed on wheels rather than receiving burial even after repentance and priestly absolution.

Peter's proposition, as reconstructed by Merino Jerez and Nauert, was that rulers who permitted such bodies to remain unburied committed **mortal sin**.

The controversy was simultaneously:

- legal — whether established territorial custom had authority;
- theological — treatment of the penitent dead;
- institutional — whether a jurist was intruding into theological determination;
- political — Peter's claim implicitly criticized German princes.

The theologians **Gerardus (Gherardus) de Zutphania** and **Jacob van Hoogstraten**, a Dominican, defended German rulers and local custom using legal and theological authorities.

### Issue 2: “Italian students cannot live without prostitutes”

Peter was also accused of having said:

> *scholares Itali non poterant vivere sine meretricibus*

that Italian students could not live without prostitutes.

Peter's defense was that the line was spoken **_pro facetia et ioco_**, as a jest. He reported that enemies answered with cries of *crucifige, crucifige*.

Hoogstraten did not accept the defense. He accused Peter of encouraging sinful fornication and argued that the proposition could be scandalous, offensive to morals, or supportive of heresy.

**Evidentiary status:** the utterance is not merely a later rumor; it is discussed in Peter's own apologetic response and in hostile contemporary polemic. What remains disputed is how it was intended and whether theological censure properly followed from it.

### Issue 3: erotic and comic mnemonics

*Phoenix* openly recommended emotionally stimulating images. Its most famous instruction says that Peter commonly used **beautiful girls** as alphabetic images and most frequently placed **Juniper of Pistoia**, “most dear to me when I was a youth,” in his loci.

He tells the reader that beautiful virgins “greatly excite” his memory and calls this a secret he had long kept silent from modesty. Other examples involve naked figures, bodily gestures, confessions, comic inversions, ethnic or factional gestures, and deliberately ridiculous scenes.

In the **1508 _Additiones quaedam ad artificiosam memoriam_**, Peter retreated from some of this material. He claimed that words about young women had entered print without his consent and replaced the recommendation with the safer rule of using images of people toward whom one feels affection.

Merino Jerez regards the excuse as implausible because the controversial material had survived through editions for years. The retractation is therefore best treated as a documented revision accompanied by a disputed explanation, plausibly shaped by the Cologne pressure.

### Formal status of the accusations

The consulted sources show:

- hostile theological scrutiny;
- a faculty commission warning Peter that propositions could be punishable;
- charges framed in the language of scandal and possible heresy;
- printed polemics by Hoogstraten and others;
- Peter's printed counterattacks.

They do **not** establish that Peter was formally convicted of heresy by an inquisitorial court. “Accused of heresy” is accurate; “convicted heretic” is not supported by the present evidence.

### The polemical literature

Peter answered his opponents with works including:

- **_Contra Gherardum de Zutphania et fratrem Jacobum theologie professores_**;
- **_Valete cum perpetuo silentio ad ... Jacobum de Alta Platea_**.

The latter title makes a pun on Hoogstraten as *de alta platea* and Peter as *de bassa platea*. The older German biography describes Peter's polemical style as intentionally rough, with Peter himself ironically arguing that a rough opponent deserved rough treatment.

Hoogstraten responded with works including:

- **_Iustificatorium principum Alamaniae_** (1508);
- **_Defensio scholastica principum Almaniae_**.

The quarrel belongs to the pre-Reformation Cologne world that would shortly become famous through the Reuchlin controversy and the satirical *Epistolae obscurorum virorum*.

## 1508: Mainz

Peter left Cologne in **April 1508** and moved to **Mainz**, where he continued teaching canon law.

At Mainz, **Johann Schöffer** printed Peter's polemical **_Contra Gherardum de Zutphania et fratrem Jacobum theologie professores_**.

Peter also used a public sermon or address at the **Carmelite convent** to counter the allegation that he condoned fornication. According to his own account, he spoke before a large audience that included the **Cardinal of Santa Croce**, condemned fornication, and improvised from the Epistle to the Hebrews. He complained that Hoogstraten had followed the controversy there.

The event illustrates his lifelong reliance on public performance: even theological defense became a demonstration of extemporaneous speech and memory.

## 1509–1510: Worms and death

Peter still hoped to return to Italy. Instead he moved onward to **Worms**.

Modern German reconstruction places him there in **1509**, active as a lawyer at or around the **Reichskammergericht** while continuing to lecture or speak. This later evidence is the main reason that “died 1508 in Mainz” should no longer be treated as secure.

He died **between 1509 and 1510**, probably at Worms.

No contemporary medical cause of death has been established.

## The *Phoenix*: Contents, Method, and Claims

## What Peter meant by “artificial memory”

Peter's method belongs to the classical art of memory: information is converted into striking **images** and placed in an ordered sequence of familiar **locations**. The practitioner later mentally traverses the places and retrieves the images.

He compares the system implicitly to writing:

- the **places** are like the page or writing surface;
- the **images** are what is written on it.

His contribution was not invention of the basic *loci-and-images* system. His importance lies in vernacularizing its practical logic for lawyers and students, publicizing unusually concrete tricks, and turning personal virtuosity into an advertisement for a reproducible method.

## Selecting places

Peter advises the student to accumulate many loci in:

- churches;
- monasteries;
- walls;
- familiar architectural spaces.

The places should be mentally rehearsed. He recommends reviewing them **three or four times a month**.

For material that must be delivered daily — arguments, stories, sermons, or reasoning — he proposes dedicating specific churches or monasteries as memory structures.

## The claim of 110,000 loci

Peter says that as a young man he created **100,000 memory places** and later added **10,000 more**.

The figure is a first-person claim, not independently audited. It is central to his persona because it converts mnemonic capacity into a countable intellectual estate.

## Living images and known people

For names, Peter recommends using people the mnemonist actually knows. The familiar person should be imagined **doing something characteristic**.

This is a psychologically concrete rule: personal familiarity and distinctive action make an image easier to recover.

## Alphabetic people

His “golden conclusion” assigns people to letters:

- A might be represented by Antonius;
- B by Benedictus;
- and so on.

The initial letter of the familiar person's name acts as the alphabetic key.

This allows language to be broken into letters or syllables and then represented by combinations of persons and objects.

## Syllables

Peter describes ways to encode three-letter syllables by combining:

- a person whose name supplies one letter;
- a moving object or action whose word supplies another sound;
- spatial order to distinguish sequence.

Examples include Benedict with turnips or frogs for *bra*, Thomas for *tra*, or Raymond striking with a staff.

The point is not the specific Latin pun but a general principle: transform abstract phonetic units into concrete, active, ridiculous, familiar scenes.

## Grammar encoded on the body

For Latin grammatical cases Peter assigns parts of the body:

- head — nominative;
- right hand — genitive;
- left hand — dative;
- right foot — accusative;
- left foot — vocative;
- belly or chest — ablative.

Number can be indicated by the state or clothing of the represented figure.

This is a striking example of using the body as a spatial grammar.

## Legal books encoded by visual attributes

Peter gives lawyers a specialized system:

- *Digestum Vetus* — white covering;
- *Digestum Novum* — red;
- *Infortiatum* — black;
- *Codex* — green;
- *Volumen* — multicolored;
- *Institutiones* — a small book;
- *Authenticum* — a notary with a large instrument;
- feudal law — a count of a castle;
- *Decretum* — an old man writing;
- *Decretals* — the pope on a throne;
- *Clementines* — a woman named Clementia.

Legal bibliography becomes a cast of people, colors, costumes, objects, and actions.

## Rubrics and arguments

For legal rubrics he constructs scenes that embody the underlying action. A rubric on settlements can become two long-quarreling people making peace.

For arguments he advises remembering only two or three **principal words**, from which the whole argument can be reconstructed. This is closer to cue-based retrieval than to verbatim memorization.

## Numbers

Peter uses a small inventory of numerical images and finger positions. His examples include signs for tens and gestures associated with Guelphs, Ghibellines, and Jews.

Some of these examples preserve contemporary factional and anti-Jewish gesture culture. They are historical evidence for the social imagery available to a fifteenth-century Italian mnemonist; they should not be modernized out of the text.

## Ridiculous, erotic, and emotionally charged imagery

Peter repeatedly exploits a principle already present in the classical tradition: extraordinary, comic, violent, beautiful, sexual, or otherwise emotionally intense images remain memorable.

His most famous case is **Juniper of Pistoia**. He says that she was dear to him in youth and that he frequently placed her image in memory loci. He recommends beautiful girls and, in several examples, nudity.

The material has generated modern scholarship on memory and sexuality because it exposes what many mnemonic manuals leave abstract: the art works by attaching thought to affect.

## Reuse and maintenance

Peter distinguishes between material placed for immediate recitation and longer-term knowledge stored in more durable loci. He recommends continued rehearsal of the spatial framework itself.

The system therefore has three layers:

1. a stable architecture of locations;
2. a changing population of vivid images;
3. rules for indexing and reconstructing words, citations, arguments, and numbers.

## The “Chaos”

Peter says he planned or used a larger method organized around alphabetic topics, called **“Chaos.”** It appears in *Phoenix* as a projected or larger intellectual system. No securely identified complete work of that title is known from the consulted bibliography.

## Peter's Claimed Memory Performances

The following feats should be read as **Peter's own claims, sometimes accompanied by named witnesses**, rather than as independently measured cognitive records.

### Padua legal demonstrations

He claimed that before age twenty he could:

- recite the civil-law code;
- reproduce passages selected for him;
- give Bartolus's summaries;
- add glosses;
- report opinions of doctors;
- state contrary arguments.

He names witnesses to related feats, including **Joannes Franciscus Pasqualicus**, **Sigismundus de Capitibus Lista**, and **Monaldinus de Monaldinis**, and invokes the reaction of Alessandro da Imola.

### Reversing lectures

Peter claimed that he could retain a lecture by Alessandro da Imola, reproduce it, and repeat material **backward**, sometimes converting parts into verse.

### Sermons

He reports memorizing and reproducing sermons by preachers including:

- Michael of Milan;
- Antonius the Hermit;
- Matthew of Verona;
- Blasius of Piombino;
- Marianus the Hermit.

In one example he says he retained **135 authorities** cited by Michael of Milan on the immortality of the soul.

### Legal citations and biblical authorities

At the end of *Phoenix*, Peter itemizes an enormous storehouse, claiming approximately:

- **20,000** legal citations from civil and canon law;
- **7,000** sacred-scriptural items;
- **1,000** verses of Ovid;
- **200** authorities of Cicero;
- **300** sayings of philosophers;
- parts of Valerius Maximus;
- extensive material on animals and other topical subjects.

For canon law he gives similarly large sub-counts from the *Decretum*, *Decretals*, *Liber Sextus*, Clementines, glosses, and singular legal propositions.

These numerical inventories are central to his rhetoric of abundance. Their exact empirical accuracy cannot be established.

### Rapid recitation and reversal

He says that when a letter of the alphabet was proposed, he could immediately produce legal, scriptural, literary, and natural-history citations beginning from that letter and then repeat the series backward.

### Simultaneous-task stories

Older biographical literature amplifies Peter's repertoire with stories involving chess, dice, dictated letters, lists, and numerical material handled simultaneously and repeated in reverse order. Some versions derive ultimately from Peter's own exhibition narrative; later retellings increase the aura of impossible multitasking. They belong in the reception history unless an independent contemporary protocol is located.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Peter did not operate a “company” in the modern sense. His economic and institutional world consisted of universities, courts, printers, civic privileges, professional salaries, and intellectual-property privileges.

### Universities and teaching institutions

Documented or strongly attested affiliations include:

- University of Padua;
- Florentine Studio / Pisa;
- Pistoia;
- Pavia;
- Bologna;
- University of Greifswald;
- University of Wittenberg;
- University of Cologne;
- University of Mainz.

His mnemonic teaching also took place in cities where he may not have held a formal chair, including Ferrara, Brescia, Cremona, Venice, and perhaps Ravenna.

### Legal office

At Padua he was associated with a judicial college from the 1470s. Near the end of his life he appears in the environment of the **Reichskammergericht** at Worms.

### Salary and monetary privileges

Known figures:

- Padua canon-law salary: **80 ducats** initially.
- Increased in **1484** to **150 ducats**.
- Additional **50 ducats annually** from **1492** because of his many children.
- Older German biography reports a **355-florin/gulden** stipend for the Pisa period.
- Pistoia citizenship included a long exemption from certain civic charges.
- Court testimonials secured exemptions from tolls or obstacles for Peter, his party, horses, books, silver, arms, and baggage.

No reliable net-worth figure, estate inventory, banking account, investment portfolio, or major landholding was found in the consulted sources.

### Print as technology and business structure

Peter used print aggressively:

- Jenson printed his oration in 1472.
- Bernardinus de Choris printed *Phoenix*.
- Stöckel, Quentel, Schöffer, Marschalk, Trebelius, and other printers issued legal and polemical works.
- *Phoenix* was packaged into collections and copied into encyclopedic contexts.

The printing privilege of January 1491 gave Peter control over the authorized Venetian printer and attempted to exclude competitors and imported copies from the Venetian market. It is one of the clearest examples of a Renaissance author treating print rights as an economic asset.

### No verified criminal finance

No evidence located in the consulted material supports claims of fraud, corruption, money laundering, illicit financial enterprise, or criminal enrichment by Peter. A Lübeck court record for a 404-mark debt sometimes encountered near his 1499 *Repetitio* concerns the printer **Lucas Brandis**, not Peter, and should not be attributed to him.

## Political / Religious / Intellectual / Artistic Development

## Political position

Peter was not a party politician in the modern sense. His political significance came through law:

- the relation of imperial and papal power;
- university jurisdiction;
- civic citizenship;
- princely custom;
- ecclesiastical immunity;
- criminal bodies and burial;
- feudal custom.

His patrons included rulers across competing jurisdictions, so his career depended more on learned mobility than on fixed factional loyalty.

## Pope and emperor

The **_Lectio de potestate pontificis maximi et romani imperatoris_** shows sustained concern with the relationship between the highest spiritual and temporal authorities. A full doctrinal analysis requires comparison with the surviving edition; the title alone is sufficient to show the subject's importance in his Wittenberg teaching.

Later secondary literature also attributes to Peter an expansive position on papal power in matrimonial dissolution. That point is repeated in modern summaries, often through James Brundage, but the present research did not independently trace it to a secure passage in Peter's own text. It should therefore be cited as a scholarly attribution rather than as a reconstructed centerpiece of his thought.

## Ecclesiastical immunity

The 1499 **_Repetitio ... de immunitate ecclesiae_** belongs to the technical canon-law treatment of church immunity and jurisdiction.

## Burial and salvation

Peter's Cologne proposition that leaving absolved executed criminals unburied constituted mortal sin joined legal custom to the spiritual status of the penitent dead. His opponents defended princely practice; Peter denied that custom could legitimize what he regarded as violation of divine and natural law.

## Franciscan tertiary

His and Lucrezia's entry into the Franciscan Third Order in 1507 is evidence of lay religious affiliation late in life. It did not turn him into a cloistered friar; he remained a married professor and polemicist.

## Humanism

Peter wrote Latin poetry, civic oratory, court verses, and humanist paratexts, moved among learned courts, and cultivated classical exempla. At the same time, his legal work remained embedded in the commentator and gloss tradition. Modern historians therefore vary in how strongly they call him a “humanist jurist.”

It is more precise to identify overlapping practices:

- scholastic and post-glossator legal learning;
- humanist Latin display;
- courtly poetry;
- rhetorical memory;
- print self-fashioning;
- alphabetical legal compendia.

## Memory as intellectual technology

Peter's most original historical role lies not in inventing loci but in **operationalizing** them for the printed age.

His method treats memory as:

- architectural;
- embodied;
- affective;
- indexed;
- professional;
- portable;
- teachable.

It is a human cognitive technology designed to turn books into internally searchable spatial arrays.

## The Art-of-Memory Line: Simonides, Peter, Camillo, Bruno

## Simonides: the ancient origin myth

The canonical story of artificial memory begins with **Simonides of Ceos**. At a banquet, the building collapses and crushes the guests beyond recognition. Simonides can identify the bodies because he remembers where each person had been sitting.

The story, transmitted through classical rhetorical literature, establishes the rule:

**order in place makes absent or destroyed bodies mentally recoverable.**

Whether every detail is historical is less important to the tradition than the story's function as an origin myth for *loci*.

## Peter of Ravenna: printed, portable, legal memory

Peter stands at a different historical moment:

- memory moves from manuscript and classroom traditions into print;
- the art becomes publicly demonstrated as virtuosity;
- its imagery is individualized and sometimes erotic or comic;
- legal books and citations become its major professional objects;
- the mnemonist presents his mental architecture as portable property.

His *Phoenix* is therefore a bridge between medieval rhetorical memory and the sixteenth-century explosion of printed memory arts.

## Giulio Camillo

**Giulio Camillo Delminio** transformed artificial memory into the famous **Memory Theatre**, a monumental spatial encyclopedia in which the spectator would stand where the actors normally stand and look outward at an ordered theater of images, planetary structures, mythological figures, and universal knowledge.

Peter and Camillo belong to the same Renaissance history of spatial memory, but the current evidence does **not** establish a direct master-disciple relation or a documented personal meeting. The line Peter → Camillo should therefore be understood as a historical and conceptual succession, not as a proven chain of personal transmission.

The contrast is useful:

- Peter: portable loci, legal citation, personal images, rapid retrieval.
- Camillo: total theater, cosmic classification, encyclopedic visualization.

## Giordano Bruno

The link to **Giordano Bruno** is much firmer.

Modern memory scholarship reports Bruno's own statement that he had studied Peter's memory writings when young. Cambridge's *Memory Arts in Renaissance England* likewise notes the influence, and studies of Bruno's mnemonic “seals” identify principles related to *Phoenix*.

Bruno did not merely reproduce Peter. He fused artificial memory with:

- Lullian combinatorics;
- wheels and permutations;
- Hermetic and cosmological symbolism;
- a far more ambitious philosophy of images and cognition.

But Peter formed part of the printed mnemonic library from which Bruno learned.

## Agrippa

Peter's line also passes through **Cornelius Agrippa**, who encountered him or his teaching in Cologne. Agrippa later listed Peter among writers on memory while adopting a more skeptical stance toward the claims of the art.

Thus a historically defensible network is:

**classical loci tradition → fifteenth-century printed rhetorical mnemonics (Publicius and others) → Peter's exceptionally successful *Phoenix* → sixteenth-century memory writers, Copland, Reisch transmission, Romberch, Agrippa, Spanish and French rhetoricians → Bruno and the expanded Renaissance mnemonic systems.**

Camillo belongs centrally in the same sixteenth-century transformation but not, on currently located evidence, as a directly documented pupil of Peter.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

## Cologne theological proceedings

**Documented:** a dispute with Cologne theologians; hostile pamphlets; a theological commission; warnings that propositions could attract punishment; departure for Mainz.

**Alleged by opponents:** scandalous or potentially heretical teaching, especially in relation to burial and sexual remarks.

**Not established:** a formal heresy conviction.

## The burial of executed criminals

**Peter's position:** rulers who left the bodies of repentant, absolved criminals unburied committed mortal sin.

**Opponents' position:** German legal custom and authorities justified continued exposure of the bodies.

**Outcome:** the controversy damaged Peter's position in Cologne and generated printed polemic. It was not a criminal prosecution of Peter in the modern sense.

## Sexual remark concerning Italian students

**Documented as a controversy:** Peter acknowledged the phrase and said it was a joke.

**Opponents' interpretation:** it encouraged fornication and could be scandalous or doctrinally dangerous.

**Peter's defense:** *pro facetia et ioco*.

No evidence found here establishes that Peter himself was prosecuted for prostitution, sexual violence, or a sex crime.

## Erotic mnemonics and the 1508 retractation

**Documented:** the 1491 *Phoenix* recommends beautiful girls, including Juniper of Pistoia, as memorable images.

**Documented:** the 1508 additions modify the advice.

**Peter's claim:** problematic phrases had been printed without his consent.

**Modern scholarly assessment:** Merino Jerez finds this explanation difficult to believe because the material had persisted across editions. The revision is likely connected to the hostile Cologne atmosphere.

## Coarse polemical style

Peter's *Valete cum perpetuo silentio* uses aggressive wordplay against Hoogstraten. The older German biography reports that Peter defended rough treatment of a rough opponent.

This is a controversy of scholarly manners and institutional power, not evidence of criminal conduct.

## Finance

No substantiated accusation of fraud, corruption, embezzlement, money laundering, illicit business, or tax evasion was found.

Known financial matters are ordinary academic and privilege evidence:

- salary negotiations;
- civic tax exemptions;
- safe conducts;
- printing rights.

A **404 Lübeck-mark debt** sometimes surfaced in the surrounding bibliography but belongs to printer Lucas Brandis. It is not Peter's debt.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## 1. “He knew the whole civil law corpus by heart”

### Story
At about twenty Peter could allegedly reproduce the entire body of Roman civil law, plus Bartolus, glosses, opinions, and contraries on command.

### Appearance
The core is found in Peter's own autobiographical narrative and became standard in later biographies, especially Tiraboschi and Eisenhart.

### Circulation
Peter himself, his admirers, courtly witnesses, and later biographers.

### Evidence
Peter names witnesses to related feats and had genuine contemporary fame as a mnemonist. No independent test surviving in the consulted sources verifies the whole quantitative claim.

### Reputation effect
This story made “Peter of Memory” more famous than Peter the canonist.

## 2. Alessandro da Imola crossed himself

### Story
Peter's teacher was so astonished by the demonstration that he crossed himself as if confronting something uncanny.

### Appearance
The story is prominent in the nineteenth-century German biography and reflects the miracle-like rhetoric already present around Peter's demonstrations.

### Evidence
The reaction is not independently verified by a neutral contemporary protocol.

### Reputation effect
It transformed mnemonic virtuosity into the borderland between scholarship, miracle, and feared supernormal ability.

## 3. “Phoenix” as unique creature and unique book

### Story
Peter explains that just as there is one Phoenix, there is one such book; therefore it should be called *Phoenix*.

### Appearance
In the book itself.

### Circulation
Authorial self-branding, later repeated by printers and memory historians.

### Evidence
Fully documented as Peter's own explanation.

### Reputation effect
The title became a durable personal myth of singularity.

## 4. “First printed memory manual”

### Story
*Phoenix* is often called the first printed manual of memory.

### Evidence against
Modern scholarship points to **Jacobus Publicius's 1482 printed rhetorical work**, which included an *ars memorativa*, before Peter's 1491/92 *Phoenix*.

### More precise formulation
*Phoenix* was among the earliest printed memory treatises, one of the early major standalone manuals, and probably the most successful fifteenth-century printed manual in the sixteenth-century reception.

### Reputation effect
The “first” claim compresses a more complex print history into a convenient origin point.

## 5. “First copyright”

### Story
Venice granted Peter the first copyright in history.

### Evidence against
The January 1491 privilege is early and unusually author-centered, but Venetian printing privileges predate it. R. R. Bowker's early history of copyright itself notes the 1486 privilege for Marcantonio Sabellico.

### More precise formulation
Peter received one of the earliest famous author-centered **printing privileges**, controlling authorized printing and sale in Venetian territory.

### Reputation effect
Modern histories of copyright frequently use Peter as an emblematic ancestor of intellectual property.

## 6. Juniper of Pistoia

### Story
Peter's most memorable internal image was a beautiful young woman, **Juniper**, dear to him in his youth. He repeatedly used her in loci.

### Appearance
Directly in *Phoenix*.

### Evidence
The literary claim is primary and secure. Independent biographical evidence identifying Juniper, her family, or the nature of the relationship has not been established here.

### Reputation effect
Juniper became the best-known human figure inside Peter's memory palace and a central example in scholarship on sexuality and mnemonic affect.

## 7. The “unauthorized” erotic passage

### Story
In 1508 Peter said that certain wording about attractive young women had entered print without his consent.

### Evidence against
The passage had remained stable through the long transmission of the work, making accidental or unauthorized insertion difficult to explain.

### Competing interpretation
The disclaimer may have been a defensive retractation after the Cologne controversy.

### Reputation effect
It provides a rare case in which an early modern mnemonic author publicly revises the emotional and sexual mechanics of his own system.

## 8. Death “from grief” after Hoogstraten

### Story
Johann Reuchlin wrote in 1518 that the “divine” Peter perished from grief after Hoogstraten drove him from Cologne.

### Appearance
Reuchlin's letter to Cardinal Achille de Grassis, 1 November 1518, quoted in the older German biography.

### Evidence
The statement is a partisan retrospective in a conflict where Hoogstraten was also Reuchlin's enemy.

### Against literal reading
Peter continued to Mainz and Worms after leaving Cologne. No medical evidence identifies grief as cause of death.

### Reputation effect
The story made Peter an earlier victim of the Cologne “obscurantists,” fitting him into the later humanist campaign against Hoogstraten.

The Harvard Ames Foundation's canon-law bibliography also records or associates Peter with:

- **_Singularia iuris_**
- **_Enarrationes in titulo de consuetudine_**
- the *Repetitio* on ecclesiastical immunity
- the canon-law and civil-law compendia.

The exact boundaries among separate pamphlets, collected *opuscula*, reprints, and appended matter vary by edition.

### Reported or bibliographically uncertain titles

#### *De corpore suspensi in patibulo an remanere debeat*
A title of this form appears in older bibliographies and modern lists in connection with the Cologne dispute over the bodies of executed criminals. The underlying controversy is securely documented. The precise status of this phrase as a distinct independently printed work, rather than a disputation or material incorporated into another polemic, requires edition-level verification.

#### *Constitutio de statutis*
The older German biography reports that a work under Peter's name appeared only long after his death, first at Königsberg in **1574** and again at Venice in **1584**. It should therefore be categorized as **posthumously transmitted/attributed**, not as a lifetime publication without further manuscript evidence.

#### *Chaos*
A projected or larger mnemonic/topical system mentioned by Peter. No complete surviving publication securely identified.

## Editions, Translations, and Transmission of *Phoenix*

A complete census of every impression requires specialist incunable and sixteenth-century catalogues. The following are significant milestones documented in the consulted scholarship.

- **Venice, 1491/92**, Bernardinus de Choris — princeps.
- **Bologna, c. 1492**, Bazalerius — early reprint.
- **Erfurt, 1500**, Wolfgang Schenck.
- **Leipzig, c. 1503–1506**, Wolfgang Stöckel or associated printing tradition.
- **1504:** a composite work titled *Ars memorativa S. Thomae, Ciceronis, Quintiliani, Petri Ravannee* demonstrates that Peter had already been grouped with canonical authorities of memory.
- **1508:** Peter's memory doctrine appears in a reworked/unauthorized form in Gregor Reisch's *Margarita philosophica nova* printed by Johannes Grüninger.
- **Cologne, 1508**, in the context of Peter's collected legal/mnemonic publications.
- **Lyon, 1511**, and subsequent Lyonnais transmission.
- **1533, Venice:** surviving copies of *Foenix* printed by Giovanni Antonio Nicolini da Sabbio for Melchiorre Sessa; one Wellcome copy was later bound with Johannes Romberch's *Congestorium artificiose memorie*.
- **Vienne, 1541**, Macé Bonhomme — Latin edition reported in rare-book cataloguing.
- **French, 1545:** *L'art de memoire qui est aultrement inscript le phenix...*, associated with Nicolas Buffet.
- **English, 1545 or c. 1548:** Robert Copland's *An Art of Memory That Otherwise Is Called the Phoenix*. Bibliography varies between a 1545 imprint and c. 1548 dating; Cambridge's critical anthology uses 1548.
- **Vicenza, 1600**, Pietro Bertelli — *Phoenix sive artificiosa memoria* still circulating more than a century after the first printing.
- **Cologne, 1608:** another late printing in the long afterlife of the text.

Merino Jerez argues that the work also circulated “on the back” of the *Margarita philosophica* tradition and through authors who excerpted or adapted its precepts.

## Death / Later Life / Present Status

Peter's final sequence is:

1. Cologne until spring 1508.
2. Mainz from April 1508.
3. Worms by 1509.
4. Death in **1509–1510**, probably Worms.

The common older formula “died 1508 at Mainz” is weaker than the modern reconstruction.

No securely documented terminal illness, injury, addiction, suicide, execution, or violent death was found.

The epidemic that killed several of his children affected his family but is not documented as his own cause of death.

Reuchlin's claim that Peter died of grief is polemical posthumous rhetoric and cannot substitute for a medical cause.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

## As jurist

During his life Peter was first and continuously a jurist. He held chairs in major universities and produced compendia of civil, canon, and feudal law. His legal writings were repeatedly printed.

Later memory studies have reversed the balance: the legal professor is now remembered mainly for a short mnemonic manual.

## As “master of memory”

*Phoenix* gave Peter a durable place in the history of:

- rhetoric;
- pedagogy;
- legal information management;
- mnemonic techniques;
- early print culture;
- history of cognition;
- histories of copyright and printing privileges.

Its success did not depend on being the first, most complete, or most systematic memory book. Merino Jerez argues that its success came from the combination of Peter's public reputation and unusually memorable rules involving humor, affect, eroticism, and personal familiarity.

## European transmission

Peter's method influenced or was used by:

- French memory writers such as **Le Lièvre**;
- **Robert Copland** in England;
- Spanish rhetoricians and mnemonists including **Juan de Aguilera**, **Francisco Sánchez de las Brozas**, and **Velázquez de Azevedo**;
- humanist rhetoric through **Juan Lorenzo Palmireno**;
- the Spanish preacher **Miguel de Salinas**, who in 1541 advised readers without Latin to obtain a vernacular rendering of Peter's art;
- compilers associated with the *Margarita philosophica*;
- later theorists including **Giordano Bruno**.

## In England

Robert Copland's English *Art of Memory* brought Peter's method into Tudor print culture. It belongs to the English mnemonic tradition later represented by Guglielmo Gratarolo, Hugh Plat, John Willis, Henry Herdson, and others.

## In Bruno

Bruno's acknowledged early study of Peter is especially important because it joins a practical fifteenth-century mnemonic manual to the far more ambitious sixteenth-century philosophical art of memory.

Peter is not the sole source of Bruno, nor does he explain Bruno's Lullian and Hermetic structures. He is one identifiable component of Bruno's mnemonic education.

## In the historiography of copyright

The Venetian privilege made Peter a favorite figure in histories searching for the prehistory of copyright. The most accurate legacy is not “first copyright holder” but **early author who explicitly asked the state to prevent others from harvesting the fruits of his intellectual labor and obtained an exclusive printing-and-sale privilege**.

## In the history of self-fashioning

Peter is a remarkable early print self-promoter. *Phoenix* combines:

- technical instruction;
- autobiography;
- named witnesses;
- princely testimonials;
- poems of praise;
- quantified feats;
- travel history;
- a legal privilege;
- a claim of uniqueness embedded in the title.

The book is both a memory manual and a manufactured dossier of its author's credibility.

## In memory theory

Peter's most modern-seeming insight is not the numerical extravagance but the dependence of memory on **salience**. His loci are effective because images move, surprise, disgust, amuse, attract, embarrass, or recall intimate acquaintances. The 1508 retractation is historically revealing precisely because it shows the technique colliding with religious and institutional boundaries.

## Open Questions for Further Archival Research

1. **Exact birth record:** no baptismal or civic birth record establishing a day or year beyond c. 1448 was located.
2. **Mother and first wife:** both remain unnamed in the principal modern biographies.
3. **Full list of children:** Peter had “many”; only Vincenzo, Giambattista, and Margherita are securely named here.
4. **Exact Pisa/Pistoia sequence:** older and modern biographies organize 1477–1481 differently.
5. **Rectorate in 1498:** German scholarship records 1498 and 1501; modern Italian biography emphasizes 1501.
6. **Exact grant of eques auratus:** imperial association is strong, but a complete edition of the granting instrument should be checked.
7. **Independent records of individual memory performances:** Peter names witnesses, but a systematic search of local civic and university archives could test the autobiographical narrative.
8. **Identity of Juniper of Pistoia:** no independent prosopographical identification was found here.
9. **“Chaos”:** whether material survives under another title or in manuscript remains open.
10. **Standalone status of *De corpore suspensi in patibulo*:** bibliography should be resolved against surviving copies and VD16/ISTC/CERL records.
11. **Matrimonial doctrine:** secondary literature attributes a broad view of papal power over marriage to Peter; the precise primary legal passage should be established before making it a central claim.
12. **Exact death:** Worms 1509–1510 is the strongest modern reconstruction, but no death certificate or contemporary obituary has been located in the consulted sources.
13. **Burial place:** unknown in the present dossier.
14. **Estate and property:** no probate inventory, real-estate record, or complete personal library inventory was located.
15. **Manuscript transmission:** surviving manuscript copies and annotations of *Phoenix* remain an important field for tracing actual users before and after print.

## Atlas Connections

### Cicero

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** In *Phoenix* (Venice, 1491/92), Peter states that he had arranged **two hundred authorities of Cicero** within his mnemonic loci and could retrieve them alphabetically. This is direct written engagement by Peter with the earlier Roman author, although *Phoenix* does not systematically analyze Cicero’s life or philosophy. [English translation of *Phoenix*, pp. 11–12](https://artofmemory.com/pdf/the-phoenix-peter-of-ravenna.pdf)

### Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Agrippa identified Peter as one of his former teachers—“an old teacher of mine”—providing first-person evidence of a personal pedagogical relationship, conventionally placed during Peter’s Cologne period, **1506–1508**. The evidence supports Peter having taught Agrippa, though not necessarily an extended private apprenticeship. [Morley, *Life of Henry Cornelius Agrippa*, vol. II](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/44/Cornelius_Agrippa._The_life_of_Henry_Cornelius_Agrippa_von_Nettesheim%2C_doctor_and_knight%2C_commonly_known_as_a_magician_%28IA_corneliusagrippa02morliala%29.pdf)

- **[T1+] Documented fact:** In *De incertitudine et vanitate scientiarum* (**1530**), Agrippa’s chapter on the art of memory names *Petrus Ravennas* among recent writers on the subject. Because Agrippa wrote this after Peter’s death, Peter is the earlier, written-about figure. Agrippa’s surrounding discussion is notably skeptical of professional mnemonic display. [Agrippa, *De vanitate*, chapter on memory](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/34/Henrici_Cornelii_Agrippae_ab_Nettesheym%2C_De_incertitudine_%26_vanitate_omnium_scientiarum_%26_artium_liber_%28IA_henricicorneliia00agri%29.pdf)

### Giordano Bruno

- **[T1+] Documented fact:** Bruno stated in his *Explicatio triginta sigillorum* (**1583**) that, while very young, he studied Peter of Ravenna’s writings on memory. This establishes explicit posthumous textual engagement with Peter, not a personal meeting. Later scholarship treats *Phoenix* as one component of Bruno’s mnemonic formation, without reducing Bruno’s Lullian and philosophical systems to Peter’s method. [Cambridge University Press, “Peter of Ravenna, *The Phoenix*”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/memory-arts-in-renaissance-england/peter-of-ravenna-the-phoenix-1548/71CC72FEFA21816B49FD216EE313AB50); [Boulting, *Giordano Bruno*](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/acn414b2393959.pdf)

### Johannes Reuchlin

- **[T1+] Documented fact:** In a letter of **1 November 1518** to Cardinal Achille de Grassis, Reuchlin invoked the deceased Peter while attacking their mutual adversary Jacob Hoogstraten. Reuchlin called Peter “divine” and asserted that he had died from grief after Hoogstraten drove him from Cologne. The letter is documented; its claim about Peter’s cause of death is a **partisan allegation**, not independently established medical fact. [*Allgemeine Deutsche Biographie*, “Petrus Ravennas”](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/ADB:Petrus_Ravennas)

### Martin Luther

- **[T1+] Documented fact:** Luther named Peter in his **1518** *Resolutiones disputationum de indulgentiarum virtute*, presenting him among learned men allegedly persecuted for rightly held views. The mention is a documented posthumous interpretation rather than evidence of personal contact. Older legal-biographical scholarship disputes Luther’s characterization, noting that Peter remained aligned with the papal legal order and that his Cologne conflict was not a proto-Lutheran doctrinal campaign. [*Allgemeine Deutsche Biographie*, “Petrus Ravennas”](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/ADB:Petrus_Ravennas)

### Frances Yates

- **[T1+] Documented fact:** Yates devoted several pages of *The Art of Memory* (**1966**) to Peter and *Phoenix*, describing its publication, international transmission, practical mnemonic rules, self-advertising apparatus, and role in carrying artificial memory into a wider lay readership. Peter is therefore a directly discussed historical subject in Yates’s work. [Yates, *The Art of Memory*, pp. 111–114](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/mnemosyne/fq/yates_memory.pdf)

No documented personal or textual crossing was established with other tempting roster candidates such as **Giulio Camillo, Simonides of Ceos, Ramon Llull, Johannes Trithemius, or Thomas Aquinas**. Their placement near Peter in histories or compilations of memory arts demonstrates reception context or a shared tradition, not by itself one of the defined Atlas connections.
