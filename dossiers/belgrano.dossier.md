# Manuel Belgrano: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs Manuel Belgrano’s life from documentary editions, archival portals, institutional chronologies, his surviving writings, scholarly studies, bibliographic catalogues, and selected later reporting on his material legacy.

Evidentiary labels used:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary document, official record, surviving correspondence, institutional archive, or closely documented scholarly edition.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally reliable later source but not independently established here from the underlying contemporary record.
- **Disputed claim:** supported by competing interpretations or incomplete evidence.
- **Rumor or myth:** traditional, propagandistic, folkloric, or otherwise unverified.
- **Attributed quotation:** transmitted by later witnesses or historians; exact wording may not be securely contemporary.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Father: Domingo Belgrano Peri/Pérez**

Domingo came from Oneglia, Liguria, then within the dominions of the House of Savoy. His surname appears in variant forms—including Belgrano Peri, Pérez, or Peri y Pérez—owing to Hispanization and inconsistent colonial recording. He became a prominent Buenos Aires merchant and accumulated substantial wealth through Atlantic and internal trade. The family’s commercial standing made it possible to send Manuel and his brother Francisco to Spain.

Domingo’s finances were damaged by a late-colonial customs prosecution. He was implicated in an investigation concerning customs official Francisco Ximénez de Mesa and allegedly irregular commercial operations. His property was attached or restricted during proceedings. Manuel’s later correspondence from Spain reflects concern about the family’s finances. The existence of the proceedings is a **documented fact**; descriptions of Domingo simply as a convicted “smuggler” overstate what can safely be concluded without reconstructing the complete judicial file. The family retained sufficient resources and connections to educate its sons and later recovered at least part of its economic position.

**Mother: María Josefa González Casero**

María Josefa belonged to an established Buenos Aires family with roots extending into Santiago del Estero. Genealogical literature has traced her ancestry to early colonial settler Domingo Martínez de Irala and, through that lineage, to Guaraní ancestry. This is a **reported genealogical finding**, not something established in this research through complete parish-by-parish reconstruction.

### Siblings

Manuel belonged to a very large family, conventionally described as having fifteen or sixteen children, depending on whether infancy deaths and variant parish entries are counted. Frequently identified siblings include:

- María Florencia
- Carlos José
- José Gregorio
- María Josefa Anastasia
- Francisco José María
- Joaquín Cayetano
- Miguel José Félix
- Juana María
- Dominga Francisca
- José María
- Domingo Estanislao
- María del Rosario
- María Mercedes
- Francisco Antonio

Several siblings or their spouses formed part of the mercantile, ecclesiastical, military, and administrative network of late-colonial and revolutionary Buenos Aires. His brother Domingo Estanislao became a clergyman and was entrusted in Manuel’s testamentary arrangements with matters concerning Manuela Mónica.

Juan José Castelli, a cousin, acted as lawyer in family business and inheritance matters before becoming Belgrano’s colleague in revolutionary politics.

### Partners and children

#### María Josefa Ezcurra

**Documented relationship with some private details reconstructed retrospectively.** María Josefa Ezcurra was married to Juan Esteban de Ezcurra, who left the Río de la Plata and did not participate in the revolutionary movement. She accompanied or met Belgrano during the northern campaign. Their son:

- **Pedro Pablo**, born 30 July 1813, was baptized at Santa Fe as the child of unidentified parents.
- He was raised within the household of Juan Manuel de Rosas and Encarnación Ezcurra, María Josefa’s sister.
- He later used the name **Pedro Pablo Rosas y Belgrano**.
- Accounts differ on whether he was formally told his paternity at age twenty or twenty-one and on which intermediary delivered Belgrano’s keepsakes. His biological paternity is widely accepted in modern Belgranian genealogical work.
- Pedro later became a frontier landholder, justice of the peace, and military-political figure at Azul. [Belgranianos del Azul family summary](https://www.belgranianos.com.ar/)

Because María Josefa remained legally married, the relationship and child were concealed. The characterization of the liaison as scandalous reflects contemporary marital and legitimacy law, not doubt that the relationship occurred.

#### María Dolores Helguero y Liendo

Belgrano met the Tucumán-born María Dolores Helguero during his northern service. Their exact mutual intentions are less securely documented than the relationship itself. Later narratives say that they contemplated marriage, but military service and possibly family intervention prevented it. María Dolores was eventually married to another man, although the chronology and legal status of that marriage differ among accounts.

Their daughter:

- **Manuela Mónica del Corazón de Jesús Belgrano**, born in Tucumán on **4 May 1819**.
- Belgrano referred indirectly to her welfare in his testamentary arrangements, entrusting his brother Domingo to provide for an unnamed young girl.
- Manuela later married Manuel Vega Belgrano and became the principal source of Belgrano’s acknowledged line of descendants.

Belgrano never publicly established a conventional marital household with either woman and was separated from both children by military, political, and social circumstances.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1770–1786

Belgrano grew up in an affluent mercantile household in Buenos Aires. The city was transformed during his childhood by the creation of the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata in 1776 and the commercial regulations of 1778. These changes expanded administrative employment and Atlantic trade while sharpening conflict over monopoly, contraband, customs enforcement, and the distribution of commercial privilege.

In approximately 1776 he attended the first-letters school associated with Santo Domingo parish. Dominican connections remained important throughout his life: at death he asked to be buried in a Dominican habit.

He entered the Real Colegio de San Carlos, a principal institution for the sons of the regional elite. The institutional chronology records:

- Logic under Luis José de Chorroarín, completed in December 1783.
- Physics, completed in November 1784.
- Philosophy, completed in November 1785.

The college taught scholastic subjects but was increasingly permeated by Enlightenment-era natural philosophy and administrative rationalism. Belgrano formed lasting connections among students and teachers who would later occupy revolutionary or clerical offices.

---

## Education and Formation in Spain, 1786–1794

Belgrano departed for Spain on **16 June 1786** and arrived in October. His intended field was law.

### Salamanca and Valladolid

Because he had not completed moral philosophy at San Carlos, he had to appeal for admission and appear before examiners. On 18 June 1787 he obtained matriculation in the Faculty of Laws at Salamanca. He completed first- and second-year requirements in 1787 and 1788.

On 11 February 1789 the University of Valladolid certified him as a bachelor in laws. He qualified before the Royal Chancery of Valladolid on 31 January 1793, with the corresponding certificate issued on 6 February.

Belgrano did not pursue the doctorate. In a letter of 8 December 1790 he called the doctoral distinction a “patarata”—an empty display that would consume time better spent on useful studies. This is important evidence of his early preference for applied knowledge over academic rank.

### Intellectual environment

His own autobiography says that he devoted less energy to conventional legal training than to:

- Living languages
- Political economy
- Public law
- Agricultural improvement
- The circulation of Enlightenment and reformist ideas

In 1790 Pope Pius VI granted him permission to possess and read books otherwise prohibited by ecclesiastical controls. He also presided over an academy of Roman law, forensic practice, and political economy at Salamanca.

Important influences included:

- **Physiocracy**, especially François Quesnay and the idea that agriculture was a foundational source of wealth.
- **Antonio Genovesi**, whose political economy connected commerce, education, moral philosophy, and public happiness.
- **Pedro Rodríguez de Campomanes** and **Gaspar Melchor de Jovellanos**, representatives of Spanish Bourbon reformism.
- Discussions of free trade and the limits of monopoly.
- The need for technical, commercial, agricultural, nautical, and female education.
- The example of European economic societies and academies.

Belgrano was not a doctrinaire physiocrat. His later writings advocate agriculture but also manufacturing, technical training, internal commerce, navigation, infrastructure, population growth, and selective public intervention. A UCA study describes him as part of the Spanish–Río de la Plata network through which political-economic knowledge was adapted to American conditions. [Study of economic-intellectual networks](https://repositorio.uca.edu.ar/handle/123456789/19940)

### Appointment to the Consulate

In December 1793 minister Diego de Gardoqui informed him that the Crown intended to establish a commercial consulate at Buenos Aires. Belgrano was appointed lawyer of the Royal Councils and perpetual secretary of the new institution. He returned to Buenos Aires on **7 May 1794**.

---

## Early Career: The Royal Consulate, 1794–1810

The Royal Consulate of Buenos Aires combined the functions of:

- Commercial tribunal
- Corporate body for merchants
- Advisory institution on economic development
- Sponsor of educational and technical projects

Belgrano became its intellectual and administrative engine, although he repeatedly complained that entrenched merchants resisted proposals that threatened immediate private advantage.

### Economic program

His surviving annual consular memoirs and known titles addressed:

- Agricultural improvement
- Industry and manufacturing
- Commerce and navigation
- Roads, ports, river markers, and geographical surveys
- Lino and hemp cultivation
- Tanneries
- Population
- Statistical knowledge
- Commercial education
- Drawing, mathematics, and nautical science

In his autobiography he later described the merchants around him as principally concerned with monopoly and profit and said that many did not understand the broader duties of the institution. This is his own retrospective testimony, valuable but not neutral.

### Educational projects

Belgrano promoted or helped create:

- **School of Drawing**, opened in 1799 under Juan Antonio Hernández.
- **Nautical Academy**, opened in November 1799 under Pedro Cerviño.
- Proposals for schools of commerce, agriculture, mathematics, architecture, and technical crafts.
- Education for girls, particularly in useful work, literacy, and domestic economy.
- Public elementary schooling tied to citizenship and economic development.

The Drawing School was not conceived simply as fine-art instruction. It was intended to support architecture, surveying, design, machinery, construction, crafts, and manufacturing. Spanish authorities later closed or restricted some of these experiments, judging them unauthorized or too costly.

### Journalism and associations

Belgrano collaborated with or encouraged:

- Francisco Antonio Cabello y Mesa’s *Telégrafo Mercantil, Rural, Político-Económico e Historiógrafo del Río de la Plata*.
- Juan Hipólito Vieytes’s *Semanario de Agricultura, Industria y Comercio*.
- Plans for a Patriotic, Literary, and Economic Society.
- The *Correo de Comercio*, launched on 3 March 1810 and edited under his direction.

These newspapers were vehicles for applied economics, statistics, education, public morality, and reform rather than merely political news.

### Health

Belgrano requested leave in 1795 and again received authorization in 1796 to travel for his health. The precise illness at this stage is not securely established. Later biographers have proposed chronic digestive, hepatic, malarial, or venereal conditions, but retrospective diagnosis is uncertain.

## British Invasions and Militarization, 1806–1807

Belgrano’s formal military experience began comparatively late.

On 25–26 June 1806, during the first British invasion, he reported to the fort and participated in the failed defense. Unwilling to swear allegiance to British commander William Carr Beresford, he obtained leave from the Consulate and crossed to the Banda Oriental.

After the reconquest he was appointed sergeant major of the urban Patrician militia. He acknowledged that he knew little of military science and attempted to study it. During the 1807 invasion he served as aide to quartermaster-general César Balbiani.

These invasions demonstrated that locally raised forces could determine political power. They also weakened viceregal authority and brought figures such as Belgrano, Cornelio Saavedra, Castelli, Martín de Álzaga, and others into militarized political competition.

---

## Political Development Before May 1810

### Opposition to Álzaga

On 1 January 1809 Belgrano opposed Álzaga’s movement against Viceroy Santiago de Liniers. The struggle combined institutional, militia, commercial, and factional rivalries and cannot be reduced to a simple Spanish-versus-Creole division.

### Carlotism

After Napoleon’s intervention in Spain and Portugal’s royal court’s move to Brazil, Belgrano supported **Carlotism**: the proposal that Infanta Carlota Joaquina, sister of Ferdinand VII, exercise sovereign authority in Spanish America.

Belgrano, Castelli, Vieytes, Nicolás Rodríguez Peña, and associates sent a memorandum to Carlota on 20 September 1808. Belgrano continued corresponding with her in 1809. His autobiography explains that he hoped to prevent American territories from remaining subject either to Napoleonic Spain or to self-interested colonial officials.

**Documented fact:** Belgrano supported a Carlotist constitutional project.

**Later simplification:** Carlotism is sometimes presented either as straightforward loyalism or as a fully formed independence plot. It could function as both a dynastic claim and a vehicle for American self-government. Its meaning changed with circumstances.

### Religion and monarchy

Belgrano’s Catholicism did not prevent him from supporting popular sovereignty, constitutional government, or revolution. Nor did his participation in revolution make him consistently republican. His political thought moved among:

- Dynastic legitimacy
- Retroversion of sovereignty to the people
- Representative institutions
- Constitutional monarchy
- American independence
- Social and economic reform

## The May Revolution, 1810

In May 1810 news arrived that the Central Junta of Seville had collapsed. Belgrano and Saavedra pressed municipal authorities for an open cabildo. Belgrano participated in the Cabildo Abierto of 22 May and supported removing Viceroy Baltasar Hidalgo de Cisneros.

A dramatic statement attributed to him on 24 May threatens to throw Cisneros from the fort’s window if he had not resigned by the following afternoon. It appears in institutional and later historical narratives, but the exact words should be treated as an **attributed quotation**, not a stenographically verified utterance.

On 25 May he became one of the voting members of the **Primera Junta**, alongside Saavedra, Castelli, Miguel de Azcuénaga, Manuel Alberti, Domingo Matheu, and Juan Larrea; Mariano Moreno and Juan José Paso were secretaries.

The Junta formally governed in Ferdinand VII’s name while asserting that, with legitimate peninsular authority absent, sovereignty reverted to the people. Historians differ over when individual Junta members embraced complete separation from Spain. Belgrano’s flag actions in 1812 and later correspondence demonstrate movement toward explicit independence.

## Paraguay and the Littoral Campaign, 1810–1811

### Appointment and route

On 22 September 1810 the Junta appointed Belgrano commander of forces destined for Paraguay after an earlier plan involving the Banda Oriental. He had no advanced command training.

His route took him through:

- San Nicolás
- Santa Fe
- La Bajada, present-day Paraná
- Entre Ríos and Corrientes
- Misiones
- Paraguayan territory

He founded or reorganized Curuzú Cuatiá and Mandisoví in November 1810.

### Regulation for the peoples of Misiones

On 30 December 1810 Belgrano issued a thirty-article regulation for the Indigenous inhabitants of Misiones. It invoked their rights to:

- Liberty
- Property
- Security
- Local offices
- Commerce
- Land and agricultural improvement
- Relief from exploitative labor and administrative abuses

The regulation is a **documented primary text** and an important statement of revolutionary social policy. Enforcement was limited by military defeat, institutional instability, and the continued power of local elites. The text should not be treated as proof that the promised rights were fully realized.

### Military operations

- **Campichuelo, 19 December 1810:** Patriot victory.
- **Paraguarí, 19 January 1811:** Belgrano initially gained ground but was defeated after confusion, desertion, and Paraguayan recovery.
- **Tacuarí, 9 March 1811:** Defeat by Manuel Cabañas; an armistice followed on 10 March.

Belgrano wrote that he had come not to conquer Paraguayans but to assist them. The expedition nevertheless entered Paraguay as an armed force demanding recognition of Buenos Aires’s Junta. Paraguayan resistance contributed to a separate Paraguayan revolutionary process rather than submission to Buenos Aires.

### Trial and diplomatic return

Belgrano was removed from command, returned to Buenos Aires, and faced a military proceeding between June and July 1811. Residents and soldiers submitted testimony in his support. The government absolved him on 9 August and restored his rank and honors.

In October 1811 he returned to Asunción as a diplomat with Vicente Anastasio Echevarría. A treaty of friendship, trade, assistance, and cooperation was signed on 12 October, recognizing Paraguay’s autonomy from Buenos Aires.

**Failure and consequence:** Militarily the expedition failed. Politically, its proclamations and contacts may have contributed to the atmosphere surrounding Paraguay’s break with Spanish authority, but claims that Belgrano “liberated Paraguay” are patriotic interpretation, not a literal description.

---

## Patricios and the Motín de las Trenzas, 1811

Belgrano was appointed colonel of the reorganized First Regiment of Patricios in November 1811. He renounced half his salary.

In December the regiment mutinied in the episode known as the **Motín de las Trenzas**, provoked by military reorganization, political faction, disciplinary grievances, and an order concerning the soldiers’ traditional braids. The government violently suppressed the mutiny and executed alleged leaders.

Belgrano commanded the unit but was not the principal political author of the crisis. Interpretations differ over his share of responsibility for the discipline imposed. It remains part of the coercive history of revolutionary state-building and should not be omitted from accounts that portray him only as educator and reformer.

---

## Creation of the Flag, 1812–1816

Sent to defend the Paraná at Rosario, Belgrano asked the Triumvirate on 13 February 1812 to establish a common cockade. The government approved white and light blue on 18 February.

On **27 February 1812**, at batteries named Libertad and Independencia, Belgrano reported:

> “Being necessary to raise a flag, and not having one, I ordered it made white and light blue, in conformity with the colors of the national cockade.”

That sentence is the principal documentary evidence for the original colors. The surviving report does not describe:

- Number of stripes
- Horizontal or vertical disposition
- Exact shade
- Maker
- Person who physically raised it

The government, still publicly acting in Ferdinand VII’s name and concerned about foreign diplomacy, ordered him on 3 March to conceal the new flag and use the established Spanish standard. Belgrano had departed and did not receive the order promptly. He presented another flag to the Army of the North at Jujuy on 25 May and was again sharply reprimanded.

The Congress of Tucumán made the light-blue-and-white flag a national emblem in July 1816. The sun was added to the war flag in 1818.

## Army of the North: Jujuy, Tucumán, and Salta, 1812–1813

Belgrano received the Army of the North from Juan Martín de Pueyrredón at the Yatasto posting station on 26 March 1812. The force was demoralized, poorly paid, inadequately equipped, and retreating from Upper Peru.

### Discipline and reconstruction

Belgrano:

- Reorganized units and supply.
- Enforced religious observance and military discipline.
- Attempted to curb desertion.
- Requested recruits, arms, uniforms, and money.
- Used proclamations to mobilize civilians.
- Made the new flag an emblem of collective loyalty.

His discipline could be severe. Revolutionary armies used flogging, imprisonment, capital punishment, requisition, forced enlistment, and coercive evacuation. No reconstruction of his command should substitute later humanitarian ideals for early-nineteenth-century military practice, but neither should patriotic narrative obscure its compulsory character.

### The Jujuy Exodus

Ordered to retreat toward Córdoba before Pío Tristán’s royalist advance, Belgrano directed the population of Jujuy to evacuate on **23 August 1812**, removing livestock and supplies and destroying resources that could assist the enemy.

- **Documented fact:** The evacuation and scorched-earth order occurred.
- **Consequences:** Civilian property and livelihoods were sacrificed; compliance was enforced under wartime authority.
- **Later memory:** Argentine commemoration represents it as a voluntary heroic communal exodus. There was genuine popular participation, but the legal form was a military command backed by threatened punishment. [Government account of the Jujuy Exodus](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/node/212858)

### Las Piedras and Tucumán

Belgrano won at Las Piedras on 3 September. At Tucumán he decided not to continue retreating to Córdoba, contrary to the central government’s strategic instruction. Supported by Tucumán residents and troops, he fought Tristán on **24 September 1812**.

The battle was confused, affected by smoke, fire, terrain, cavalry movements, and breakdowns in control. Belgrano was not always aware of every sector’s position. The result was nevertheless a decisive patriot victory that preserved the revolution’s northern territories.

He attributed the victory to the protection of the Virgin of Mercy and later presented her with his command baton, proclaiming her general of the army. This was an authentic part of his religious and military culture, not solely later hagiography.

### Salta

On **20 February 1813** Belgrano defeated Tristán at Salta. The royalist army capitulated. Belgrano released its officers and soldiers after requiring an oath not to bear arms again against the United Provinces.

This clemency was celebrated, but many released men subsequently returned to royalist service after ecclesiastical authorities or commanders treated the oath as nonbinding. Critics therefore regarded the decision as strategically costly.

The Assembly of Year XIII awarded Belgrano a sword and **40,000 pesos**. He refused personal enrichment and directed the money to four schools in:

- Tarija
- Jujuy
- Tucumán
- Santiago del Estero

His May 1813 regulations prescribed instruction in reading, writing, arithmetic, Christian doctrine, and civic rights and duties. Payment and construction were repeatedly delayed for generations; later governments ceremonially “fulfilled” portions of the bequest under very different monetary conditions. The donation was real, but the familiar story of four immediately founded Belgrano schools is false.

## Upper Peru: Vilcapugio and Ayohuma, 1813–1814

Following Salta, Belgrano advanced through Jujuy into Upper Peru and was received at Potosí. Women of Potosí presented him with an elaborate gold-and-silver ceremonial tarja naming him “Protector of the Peoples of the American Continent.”

The strategic position deteriorated through:

- Poor communications
- Dispersed patriot forces
- Supply shortages
- Uncertain local political allegiance
- Royalist reinforcement
- Failure to coordinate fully with insurgent forces

### Vilcapugio, 1 October 1813

Belgrano’s army was defeated after an initially favorable phase. Confused signals, royalist recovery, and loss of cohesion contributed.

### Ayohuma, 14 November 1813

A second defeat forced abandonment of the campaign and retreat from Potosí.

These defeats led to investigation and removal from command. Belgrano transferred the army to José de San Martín in January 1814. San Martín defended Belgrano’s integrity and usefulness and opposed abruptly separating him from troops who trusted him.

Belgrano was arrested or placed under restraint during the investigation, spending time at Luján and later a San Isidro property because of illness. The proceeding produced no serious sustainable accusation and ended without conviction.

## San Martín, Command Transfer, and the “Yatasto” Problem

Traditional paintings and textbooks place Belgrano and San Martín’s celebrated meeting at the **Posta de Yatasto**. The better-supported reconstruction distinguishes two events:

- Belgrano had received command from Pueyrredón at Yatasto in March 1812.
- Belgrano and San Martín’s first documented personal meeting was probably at **Algarrobos**, south of the Juramento River, on **17 January 1814**.

Thus the iconic “embrace of Yatasto” is principally **patriotic visual mythology**, though the two men certainly met, collaborated, and transferred command in that region.

Their surviving relationship included:

- Belgrano’s enthusiastic welcome of San Martín.
- San Martín’s defense of Belgrano during the inquiry.
- Correspondence on discipline, religion, local custom, strategy, and the flag.
- Shared support for declaring independence.
- Strategic complementarity: San Martín’s Andean plan and Belgrano’s later northern holding role.

---

## Diplomatic Mission to Europe, 1814–1816

Belgrano and Bernardino Rivadavia left Buenos Aires on 28 December 1814. They traveled through Rio de Janeiro and then to Britain.

### Rio de Janeiro

They dealt with:

- Lord Strangford, the British diplomat
- Portuguese court officials
- Spanish representative Andrés Villalba
- The political circle surrounding Carlota Joaquina

### Britain

They arrived at Falmouth on 7 May 1815 and worked in London with Manuel de Sarratea. Europe had entered the post-Napoleonic restoration, interrupted by Napoleon’s Hundred Days.

The mission explored projects intended to secure recognition and prevent Spanish reconquest. One scheme proposed a constitutional kingdom in the Río de la Plata under **Francisco de Paula**, son of the former Spanish king Charles IV. Belgrano, Rivadavia, and Sarratea signed or participated in related documents.

This was not an aberration concealed by otherwise consistent republicanism. It was part of Belgrano’s continuing view that constitutional monarchy might obtain international legitimacy and restrain civil conflict.

Belgrano quarreled with the Count of Cabarrús over mission expenses and documentary accounting. On 3 November 1815 they came close to a duel before Rivadavia intervened. The episode is a **documented personal and financial dispute**, not evidence that Belgrano misappropriated mission funds. On the contrary, his correspondence demanded receipts down to the smallest expenditure.

Belgrano left London in November 1815 and reported to the government in February 1816. The mission did not obtain recognition or a European monarch.

## The Inca Monarchy Proposal and Congress of Tucumán, 1816

Belgrano appeared before the Congress of Tucumán in secret session on **6 July 1816**. He reported that European powers favored monarchy and recommended a **constitutional monarchy headed by a descendant of the Incas**, with its political center probably at Cuzco.

The proposal attempted to combine:

- Internationally recognizable monarchy
- Constitutional limits
- Mobilization of Indigenous and Upper Peruvian legitimacy
- South American unity
- A capital outside Buenos Aires
- Reconciliation of the revolutionary state with the demographic and symbolic weight of the Andes

San Martín and Martín Miguel de Güemes supported or viewed the proposal favorably. Some Upper Peruvian deputies supported it. Buenos Aires critics mocked it as a “chocolate king” project and resisted relocation of the capital and political power.

**Documented fact:** Belgrano proposed an Inca constitutional monarchy.

**Unresolved question:** He did not leave a definitive, operational nomination of a particular claimant. Juan Bautista Túpac Amaru is often named retrospectively, but firm evidence that Belgrano formally selected him is insufficient.

**Later propaganda:** Federalist, Indigenous, nationalist, liberal, and revisionist writers have each used the plan to claim a different “true” Belgrano. The proposal is better understood as a real 1816 political project shaped simultaneously by European restoration and Andean legitimacy.

## Final Military Command, 1816–1819

Belgrano became brigadier general and again commander of the Army of the North in August 1816, replacing José Rondeau.

The army no longer attempted a major conventional invasion of Upper Peru. Its roles included:

- Supporting Güemes’s frontier war.
- Guarding against royalist invasion.
- Supplying or indirectly supporting San Martín’s continental strategy.
- Maintaining order in interior provinces.
- Responding to revolts and civil conflict.

### Collaboration with Güemes

In 1817 Belgrano recommended promotion and honors for Güemes and met him near the Juramento/Pasaje River to coordinate mountain warfare. Their relationship involved strategic cooperation but also the chronic tension between a poorly supplied regular army and Güemes’s provincial gaucho forces.

### Juana Azurduy

In 1816 Belgrano forwarded or awarded Juana Azurduy’s promotion to lieutenant colonel in recognition of her military services. She and Manuel Ascencio Padilla’s forces had operated in Upper Peru, including in the broader campaigns associated with Belgrano’s army. [Government biographical notice on Azurduy](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/node/205102)

### María Remedios del Valle

María Remedios del Valle accompanied the Army of the North as nurse, camp follower, combatant, and later captain. Later records used to support her pension describe her service under Belgrano and her wounds and capture. Belgrano’s reported designation of her as “Captain” or “Mother of the Nation” belongs to a documentary tradition assembled later from military and legislative testimony. Her service is a **documented fact**; the exact moment and wording of every title are less certain.

### Civil war assignments

The central government repeatedly moved the Army of the North away from the royalist frontier to intervene in provincial conflicts:

- Córdoba in 1816–1817.
- The Littoral against forces associated with federal autonomy and Artigas.
- Santa Fe in 1819.

Belgrano obeyed orders but complained of undefined objectives, misery, lack of pay, nakedness, hunger, and the futility of operations. He supported the April 1819 Armistice of San Lorenzo.

This phase complicates the image of a war fought only against Spain. Under Belgrano’s command, national troops were also instruments in internal political struggle, even when he personally preferred negotiation.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political economy

Belgrano’s program consistently linked public prosperity to:

- Agriculture
- Technical education
- Manufacturing
- Navigation
- Internal and external trade
- Population growth
- Statistics
- Infrastructure
- Property rights
- Public virtue
- State-supported education

He criticized idle landholding, monopoly, ignorance, luxury imports, and commercial practices that exported raw wealth without building productive capacity.

Labels such as “first Argentine economist,” “liberal,” “protectionist,” “physiocrat,” or “developmentalist” illuminate only portions of his thought. His writings combine European political economy with the administrative paternalism and moral language of Bourbon Catholic reformism.

### Education and women

Belgrano regarded women as central educators of children and proposed schooling that would include literacy, spinning, sewing, and productive skills. These proposals challenged neglect of female education but remained situated within gendered assumptions about domestic and economic roles.

### Indigenous policy

His Misiones regulation and Inca monarchy proposal show sustained engagement with Indigenous political status. He condemned official “rapine” and promised property and security.

Limitations include:

- He still operated within a tutelary state-building framework.
- His army requisitioned resources and imposed military authority in Indigenous regions.
- The Misiones regulation was never fully implemented.
- Later claims that he anticipated modern plurinational or Indigenous-sovereignty programs go beyond the texts.

### Slavery

Belgrano lived in a slaveholding society, and the wealthy Belgrano household possessed enslaved servants. A document circulated in archival discussions records his manumission of an enslaved person in 1808. His revolutionary activity supported measures that gradually weakened slavery, but he did not produce a complete abolitionist program comparable to later emancipation movements. Claims presenting him either as simply an enslaver or as a modern abolitionist require more qualification.

### Religion

Documented features include:

- Permission to read prohibited books while remaining Catholic.
- Oath in defense of the Immaculate Conception at Salamanca.
- Marian devotion.
- Dedication of the Tucumán victory to the Virgin of Mercy.
- Use of Catholic moral and educational language.
- Request for burial in a Dominican habit.

The frequent portrayal of Enlightenment and Catholicism as mutually exclusive distorts his intellectual world.

### Constitutional monarchy and republican sources

Belgrano translated George Washington’s Farewell Address, praising its warnings against faction and foreign influence, while also supporting constitutional monarchy. This was not necessarily contradictory to him: Washington offered civic conduct for an independent society; monarchy offered a possible constitutional structure and diplomatic solution.

## Journalism, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions

Belgrano worked in or helped develop:

- Real Consulate of Buenos Aires
- School of Drawing
- Nautical Academy
- Proposed School of Commerce
- Patriotic, Literary, and Economic Society
- *Telégrafo Mercantil*
- *Semanario de Agricultura, Industria y Comercio*
- *Correo de Comercio*
- Primera Junta
- Montepío administration
- Patricios Regiment
- Army of the North
- Diplomatic commission to Europe

### Businesses and investments

No evidence located in the consulted sources establishes a major independent company or investment structure personally controlled by Belgrano. His financial base derived from:

- Family mercantile wealth and inheritance.
- Salary as Consulate secretary.
- Military and public salaries, often delayed.
- Family property and claims.

He was deeply involved in commercial policy but should not be described as the proprietor of the Consulate’s projects.

### Technologies and applied science

Belgrano did not obtain known patents. His technological interests were institutional:

- Nautical training
- Cartography
- River navigation
- Buoys for Río de la Plata hazards
- Drawing for machines, architecture, and crafts
- Agricultural processing
- Hemp and flax
- Tanneries
- Mathematics
- Proposed scientific and topographical journeys

### Personal finances

Belgrano came from wealth but died with severe liquidity problems. Important elements were:

- Family losses during his father’s prosecution.
- Public service over private commerce.
- Renunciation of half his Patricios salary.
- Donation of the 40,000-peso Salta award.
- Government arrears in salary and expenses.
- War-related personal expenditures and disrupted inheritance.
- Debts and medical costs in 1820.

The claim that he “died poor” is substantially supported, but it should mean cash-poor, indebted, and unpaid—not necessarily that every possible family or testamentary property interest had legally disappeared. In April 1820 he reported obligations of 6,200 pesos and an urgent need for another 2,000. The provincial government eventually supplied only 300 pesos.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Juan José Castelli

Cousin, family lawyer, fellow Enlightenment-oriented revolutionary, Carlotist collaborator, and Primera Junta member. Their political careers diverged geographically when Castelli led the first Upper Peru expedition and Belgrano went to Paraguay.

### Mariano Moreno

Junta secretary and radical revolutionary associate. Belgrano corresponded with him during the Paraguay expedition. Later historiography frequently creates rigid “Morenista” and “Saavedrista” camps; individual alliances were more fluid.

### Cornelio Saavedra

Militia leader and president of the Primera Junta. He and Belgrano jointly pressed for the open cabildo, though they did not share all political priorities.

### Juan Hipólito Vieytes

Journalistic and economic collaborator; Carlotist associate; promoter of agricultural and industrial knowledge.

### Nicolás Rodríguez Peña

Carlotist and revolutionary associate.

### Bernardino Rivadavia

Diplomatic colleague in Europe and political associate with significant tactical differences. Rivadavia prevented Belgrano’s duel with Cabarrús.

### Juan Martín de Pueyrredón

Transferred the Army of the North to Belgrano in 1812; as Supreme Director later appointed or supported him in northern command. The relationship connected Belgrano to the central government’s war strategy and its civil-war interventions.

### José de San Martín

Military successor, correspondent, defender, and strategic ally. Their relationship is among the best documented in Belgrano’s later career.

### Martín Miguel de Güemes

Northern military collaborator. Belgrano supported his promotion and coordinated defensive strategy with him.

### Pío Tristán

Royalist opponent at Tucumán and Salta. They knew one another from Spain according to later accounts, but the depth of any prewar friendship is uncertain. Belgrano released Tristán under the Salta capitulation.

### Joseph James Thomas Redhead

Scottish or British physician, translator, adviser, and companion. He helped Belgrano complete the translation of Washington and attended him during his final illness.

### Manuel de Sarratea

Revolutionary diplomat and collaborator in the European monarchical projects; later a person from whom the dying Belgrano sought financial assistance.

### María Josefa Ezcurra and María Dolores Helguero

Partners and mothers of his children. Their roles were long minimized because conventional patriotic biography separated the celibate public hero from his private relationships.

## Controversies, Investigations, Coercion, and Financial Questions

### His father’s customs case

- **Status:** Documented investigation and property consequences.
- **Disputed representation:** Later accounts sometimes call Domingo Belgrano a convicted contrabandist without reconstructing the disposition of every charge.
- **Effect:** Family finances and reputation were damaged; Castelli participated in the legal defense.

### Paraguay command

- **Status:** Military defeat followed by formal inquiry.
- **Outcome:** Belgrano was absolved and restored.
- **Controversy:** Whether the expedition was liberation, invasion, or a failed attempt to export revolution depends on political perspective; operationally it was an armed incursion ordered by Buenos Aires.

### Motín de las Trenzas

- **Status:** Documented mutiny and violent suppression.
- **Question:** The precise degree of Belgrano’s responsibility for subsequent executions and punishments requires a fuller study of orders and tribunal records.

### Unauthorized flag

- **Status:** Documented disobedience or premature symbolic innovation.
- **Government finding:** The Triumvirate reprimanded him and ordered concealment of the flag.
- **Later reputation:** What was initially an embarrassment to the government became his defining patriotic act.

### Scorched earth in Jujuy

- **Status:** Documented military order.
- **Effect:** Forced mass displacement and destruction or removal of property.
- **Competing characterization:** Heroic sacrifice in national memory; coercive wartime evacuation in social history. Both elements can coexist.

### Salta capitulation

- **Status:** Documented clemency and oath.
- **Controversy:** Former prisoners’ later return to royalist service made the decision appear strategically naïve to critics.

### Vilcapugio and Ayohuma inquiry

- **Status:** Formal military proceeding following defeats.
- **Outcome:** No serious charge sustained; case effectively closed without conviction.
- **San Martín’s position:** Belgrano’s knowledge, character, and standing with the troops made him valuable despite defeat.

### European mission expenditures and Cabarrús

- **Status:** Documented dispute.
- **Evidence:** Belgrano demanded itemized accountability from Cabarrús and nearly fought him.
- **No established finding:** No evidence consulted establishes Belgrano’s personal corruption or embezzlement.

### Repression of provincial autonomy

- **Status:** Documented deployment of his army against interior and Littoral rebellions.
- **Qualification:** He obeyed central orders while repeatedly questioning objectives, reporting the army’s ruin, and supporting armistice.

### Arrest in Tucumán, 1819

After a revolt against Governor Feliciano de la Motta, Captain Abraham González arrested the gravely ill Belgrano. An attempt was reportedly made to fit shackles to his swollen legs, prevented by his physician. Congress later demanded treatment appropriate to his rank.

### No established criminal conviction

No consulted evidence shows that Belgrano was convicted of corruption, fraud, treason, or a conventional crime. His two major command inquiries ended without conviction.

## Illnesses and Medical Questions

Belgrano suffered recurrent illness from at least the 1790s. Later accounts associate his condition with:

- Digestive or hepatic disease
- Malaria contracted or aggravated in the northern campaigns
- Dropsy or edema
- Cardiovascular failure
- Possible syphilis

### What is documented

- Repeated requests for health leave.
- Serious illness after the Upper Peru defeats.
- Progressive edema and swollen legs in 1819.
- A contemporary diagnosis of **hydropsy/dropsy**, a symptom rather than a modern etiological diagnosis.
- Final attendance by Joseph Redhead, with assistance attributed to John Sullivan.
- Incapacity severe enough to surrender command and travel home.

### Syphilis claim

The claim that Belgrano suffered from or died of syphilis appears in later medical and popular biographies. Symptoms and retrospective testimony have been interpreted in that direction, but no modern laboratory confirmation exists, and dropsy could arise from liver, cardiac, renal, or infectious disease.

**Classification:** **Disputed retrospective diagnosis**, not an established cause of death.

### Cause of death

The safest formulation is:

> Belgrano died following a prolonged illness characterized by severe edema or dropsy, probably reflecting advanced systemic, hepatic, cardiac, or renal disease; the precise underlying diagnosis cannot now be established.

## Death and Burial

Belgrano left Tucumán on 6 February 1820 in an old carriage called *La Golondrina* and reached the family house in Buenos Aires on 1 April.

He repeatedly requested money from the government. On 25 May he dictated his testament. His arrangement concerning his daughter was discreet, reflecting her illegitimate legal status.

On 19 June he gave Redhead his gold watch, saying in the institutional account that he had nothing else with which to reward him. The watch’s precise donor before it came into Belgrano’s possession is disputed: different accounts associate it with the British crown or with a Portuguese/Brazilian royal gift. The transfer to Redhead is well established; stories about its royal origin require caution.

Belgrano died at approximately **7:00 a.m. on 20 June 1820**. The phrase **“¡Ay, patria mía!”** is traditionally reported as his final utterance. It is an **attributed deathbed quotation**, not independently verifiable.

Buenos Aires was immersed in the political crisis later called the Anarchy of the Year XX. His death received little immediate newspaper attention; the cleric Francisco de Paula Castañeda’s *Despertador Teofilantrópico* is commonly identified as the only contemporary paper to mark it promptly.

He was buried in the Santo Domingo atrium in a Dominican habit. A piece of marble from a family washstand or chest was reportedly used for the modest original marker. The funeral observances began several days later.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Belgrano invented the exact modern Argentine flag”

- **Story:** He designed the present three-stripe flag with its precise colors and arrangement.
- **Evidence:** His 27 February report says only that he ordered a white-and-light-blue flag corresponding to the cockade.
- **Against:** No contemporary description securely fixes the original stripe pattern. Surviving Macha flags use different arrangements.
- **Effect:** Reduced a complex revolutionary career to a single schoolbook scene.
- **Classification:** Core act documented; exact modern design attribution is **later simplification**.

### Inspiration from the sky

- **Story:** Belgrano looked at the blue sky and white clouds and selected the colors.
- **Evidence:** None in his contemporary report.
- **Alternatives:** The colors of the national cockade; Bourbon and Order of Charles III colors; Marian iconography; existing revolutionary badges.
- **Effect:** Became an easily taught, apolitical origin myth.
- **Classification:** **Rumor or patriotic myth**.

### María Catalina Echevarría sewed the first flag

- **Story:** Belgrano asked María Catalina Echevarría de Vidal, sister of his friend Vicente Anastasio Echevarría, to make it.
- **Evidence:** Strong Rosario local tradition and plausible social connection, but no known contemporary order, receipt, or eyewitness record.
- **Effect:** Restored a woman’s role to the creation narrative and became an official commemorative tradition.
- **Classification:** **Reported tradition/hypothesis**, not conclusively documented. [Educational cartography study](https://www.bnm.me.gov.ar/giga1/libros/3-cartografia-belgrano.pdf)

### Cosme Maciel first raised the flag

- **Story:** Rosario civilian Cosme Maciel physically hoisted it.
- **Evidence:** Later local tradition, not identified in Belgrano’s surviving official communication.
- **Classification:** **Reported tradition**.

### The Yatasto embrace

- **Story:** Belgrano and San Martín met and embraced at the Posta de Yatasto.
- **Evidence:** Their meeting and command transfer are documented, but the first meeting is better located at Algarrobos on 17 January 1814.
- **Circulation:** Paintings, textbooks, monuments, film, patriotic ceremony.
- **Effect:** Produced an iconic scene of harmonious succession between founding fathers.
- **Classification:** **Mythic relocation of a documented relationship**.

### “Belgrano was not a soldier”

- **Story:** He was an untrained civilian forced into command and therefore militarily incompetent.
- **Evidence:** He lacked professional academy training and admitted initial ignorance, but accumulated militia service from 1797/1806 onward, studied military subjects, rebuilt armies, and won Tucumán and Salta.
- **Effect:** Used alternately to magnify his sacrifice or explain his defeats.
- **Classification:** **Misleading simplification**.

### Unblemished pacifist humanitarian

- **Story:** Belgrano’s clemency at Salta and educational program demonstrate uniformly non-coercive conduct.
- **Against:** He enforced military discipline, ordered scorched earth and compulsory evacuation, participated in suppression of mutiny, and commanded troops in civil conflict.
- **Classification:** **Hagiographic selection of documented traits**.

### Proto-socialist, laissez-faire liberal, protectionist, or modern developmentalist

Each label draws on real passages but imposes later ideological systems. He promoted free circulation in some contexts, state sponsorship and manufacturing in others, property rights, agricultural primacy, education, public virtue, and regulation against monopoly.

- **Classification:** **Retrospective ideological appropriation**.

### The Inca plan as joke, betrayal, or revolutionary decolonization

- **Hostile version:** An absurd scheme for a “chocolate king.”
- **Nationalist-republican version:** A monarchical retreat from revolution.
- **Revisionist/Indigenous version:** A fully decolonial program to restore Indigenous sovereignty.
- **Evidence:** It was a constitutional-monarchical proposal intended to obtain legitimacy, broaden Andean allegiance, and relocate political gravity.
- **Classification:** Proposal documented; later totalizing readings are **interpretive propaganda**.

### “Died forgotten and completely penniless”

- **Evidence for:** Unpaid claims, urgent requests for money, small government relief, modest burial, limited press attention.
- **Qualification:** Family attended him, he retained testamentary claims and personal objects, and funeral observances followed. “Completely forgotten” exaggerates.
- **Classification:** **Substantially based in fact but rhetorically amplified**.

### Final words: “¡Ay, patria mía!”

A defining component of his death legend. The words fit his known anxieties about civil disorder but lack a contemporaneous stenographic record.

- **Classification:** **Attributed quotation/traditional report**.

### The stolen teeth

At the 4 September 1902 exhumation, Interior Minister Joaquín V. González and War Minister Pablo Riccheri each took a tooth from the remains, allegedly intending to show them to Bartolomé Mitre. Newspaper criticism forced their return.

- **Classification:** **Documented posthumous scandal**, not myth.
- *Caras y Caretas* mocked the officials as “ministerial dentists” and depicted Belgrano protesting that they were taking even his teeth. [Buenos Aires legislative cultural account](https://cultura.legislatura.gob.ar/cultura_posts/4-de-septiembre-de-1902-el-insolito-robo-de-los-dientes-del-gral-manuel-belgrano945.html)

### The stolen watch

The watch associated with Belgrano’s payment or gift to Redhead eventually entered the Museo Histórico Nacional. It was stolen in June 2007 and has not been reliably recovered in the sources consulted.

- **Classification:** **Documented modern theft**. [Contemporary report](https://elpais.com/diario/2007/07/04/internacional/1183500004_850215.html)

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Nineteenth-century construction of the national hero

Belgrano’s immediate death was overshadowed by civil war. His national canonization accelerated through:

- Publication of memoirs and correspondence.
- Bartolomé Mitre’s biography.
- Public anniversaries of Tucumán and Salta.
- The equestrian monument in Plaza de Mayo, inaugurated in 1873.
- Incorporation into public-school history.
- Association of 20 June with the flag.

Mitre used Belgrano’s life as a narrative axis for a history of Argentine independence. This framework emphasized civic virtue, national unity, military sacrifice, and the transition from colonial monarchy to republic.

### Mausoleum and the 1902–1903 scandal

His remains were exhumed in 1902 for transfer to a monumental mausoleum by Italian sculptor Ettore Ximenes. The removal of teeth by ministers González and Riccheri caused a press scandal; the teeth were returned. The mausoleum was inaugurated in 1903.

The event demonstrates that relic culture, state ceremony, satire, and political criticism all participated in creating Belgrano’s posthumous cult.

### Flag Day

Law 12,361, enacted in 1938, established 20 June as Flag Day. This fixed his public identity primarily as creator of the flag, often at the cost of obscuring his economic writings, journalism, diplomacy, Indigenous regulation, constitutional monarchy, and civil-war service.

### Economic and educational remembrance

He is commemorated through:

- Schools and universities
- Instituto Nacional Belgraniano
- General Belgrano Railway and Belgrano Cargas
- Avenida Belgrano and numerous towns, departments, streets, and institutions
- Currency, stamps, statues, and military units
- Día del Graduado en Ciencias Económicas, associated with his Consulate work
- Schools claiming descent from the 40,000-peso endowment

### Historiographical reinterpretations

Successive traditions have emphasized different Belgranos:

- **Liberal-national:** civic republican virtue, flag, national unity.
- **Catholic:** Marian general, providential patriot, Dominican death.
- **Military:** self-taught commander and victor of Tucumán and Salta.
- **Revisionist-nationalist:** opponent of monopoly and advocate of productive development.
- **Social-democratic or left-national:** public education, regulation, labor, Indigenous rights.
- **Indigenous/decolonial:** Misiones regulation and Inca monarchy.
- **Federalist critique:** officer of Buenos Aires governments deployed against provincial autonomy.
- **Gender and family history:** partner of Ezcurra and Helguero, father of concealed children, advocate of female education within contemporary limits.
- **Material-culture history:** flags, tarja, command baton, watch, books, grave, teeth, portraits, and monuments.

No single tradition exhausts the documentary record.

## Atlas Connections

Only intersections supported by the consulted sources are listed. Shared nationality, general influence, or mere contemporaneity is not treated as a connection.

### José de San Martín — documented fact

- First securely located personal meeting: Algarrobos, south of the Juramento River, 17 January 1814.
- San Martín replaced Belgrano as commander of the Army of the North.
- San Martín defended Belgrano during the inquiry into Vilcapugio and Ayohuma.
- They corresponded about the army, religion, discipline, the flag, independence, and continental strategy.
- Both supported independence in 1816; San Martín regarded the Inca-monarchy proposal favorably.
- The familiar Yatasto embrace is a mythologized location of their documented relationship.

### Martín Miguel de Güemes — documented fact

- Military and political collaborator on the northern frontier.
- Belgrano recommended his promotion and decoration in May 1817.
- They met near the Pasaje/Juramento River in June 1817 to coordinate mountain warfare.
- Their forces divided the work of frontier defense: Güemes’s provincial gaucho war and Belgrano’s regular army.

### Juan Martín de Pueyrredón y O’Doggan — documented fact

- Transferred command of the Army of the North to Belgrano at Yatasto in March 1812.
- As Supreme Director, recalled Belgrano to Tucumán and supported his renewed command in 1816.
- Connected Belgrano’s army to the wider strategy supporting San Martín, while also ordering deployments in internal conflicts.

### Carlos María de Alvear — documented fact

- Political contemporary and Supreme Director during preparation of Belgrano’s European diplomatic mission.
- Alvear’s government formed part of the diplomatic framework in which Belgrano, Rivadavia, Sarratea, and Manuel José García pursued differing European and British options.
- No close friendship is established in the consulted material.

### Juana Azurduy de Padilla — documented fact

- Operated in the Upper Peruvian theater connected to the Army of the North.
- Belgrano recognized her military service and forwarded or issued her promotion to lieutenant colonel in 1816.
- Her forces participated in the broader struggle that sustained resistance after conventional patriot defeats.

### María Remedios del Valle — documented fact with partially reported details

- Served with the Army of the North in campaigns commanded by Belgrano.
- Later pension proceedings documented her work as nurse, camp follower, combatant, and wounded veteran.
- Belgrano is traditionally credited with naming her captain; her service is documented, while exact wording and date of the title depend on later testimony.

### Juan Manuel José Domingo Ortiz de Rozas y López de Osornio — documented family connection

- Better known as Juan Manuel de Rosas.
- Rosas and Encarnación Ezcurra raised Pedro Pablo, the son of Belgrano and María Josefa Ezcurra.
- Pedro later adopted the surname Rosas y Belgrano.
- Belgrano and Rosas were therefore linked through concealed biological paternity, guardianship, and the Ezcurra family, not through a documented close political partnership.

### George Washington — documented intellectual connection; no personal meeting

- Belgrano translated Washington’s Farewell Address into Spanish.
- An initial version was reportedly lost at Tacuarí; he reconstructed it with Joseph Redhead’s assistance and sent it to the government in February 1813.
- Belgrano explicitly wished Washington’s political lessons to circulate among his fellow citizens.
- This is an acknowledged intellectual connection, not a personal one.

### Adam Smith — reported intellectual influence, not a documented personal connection

- Belgrano studied European political economy in Spain and worked within intellectual networks in which Smith’s writings circulated.
- His surviving work shows engagement with themes also associated with Smith, including commerce, productive labor, education, and political economy.
- The consulted sources more directly document Quesnay, Genovesi, and Spanish reformism; therefore a specific decisive Smithian influence should not be overstated.

### Napoleon Bonaparte — documented contextual influence; no personal connection

- Napoleon’s invasion of Spain created the dynastic crisis underlying Carlotism and the May Revolution.
- Belgrano learned of Napoleon’s return during the Hundred Days when he arrived in Britain in May 1815.
- No meeting, correspondence, or personal relationship existed.

### Bernardo O’Higgins Riquelme — reported strategic-political connection

- Belgrano and O’Higgins belonged to the connected South American independence movement, and Belgrano’s relationship with San Martín placed him within the strategy that liberated Chile.
- The sources consulted here did not establish a specific meeting or substantial direct correspondence, so no stronger claim is made.

### Simón Bolívar and Antonio José de Sucre — no documented direct connection found

Belgrano died before Bolívar and Sucre completed the liberation of Peru and Upper Peru. They operated in the same continental independence process, but the consulted sources did not establish a meeting, correspondence, or direct collaboration.

### Domingo Faustino Sarmiento — posthumous intellectual connection only

Sarmiento later developed an expansive public-education program in Argentina and operated within a tradition that retrospectively treated Belgrano as an educational precursor. They could not have met: Sarmiento was born in 1811 and was a child when Belgrano died.

### Other roster findings

No securely documented personal intersection was found with Abraham Lincoln, Alexander von Humboldt, Francisco de Miranda, Simón Rodríguez, Manuela Sáenz, or the other atlas figures merely because they were contemporaries or participants in broader Atlantic political and intellectual movements. Absence of an entry is not evidence that no document exists; it means no connection meeting the stated evidentiary threshold was located in the sources actually consulted.
