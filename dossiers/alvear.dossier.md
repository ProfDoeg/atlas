# Carlos María de Alvear: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs the life of Carlos María de Alvear from archival guides, published primary documents, official records, contemporaneous or near-contemporaneous histories, later scholarly studies, and specialist catalogues. Short biographical summaries were used chiefly for orientation and bibliographic discovery.

Labels used for disputed material:

- **Documented fact:** supported by primary documents, official records, or mutually corroborating sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a generally reliable secondary source but not independently confirmed here from the underlying record.
- **Allegation or theory:** a substantive claim advanced by identifiable participants or historians but disputed or incompletely demonstrated.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story whose documentary basis is weak, late, contradictory, or genealogically implausible.

Dates follow the Gregorian calendar. Offices and political terms retain their contemporary Río de la Plata meanings.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full baptismal or birth name:** Carlos Antonio del Santo Ángel Guardián de Alvear y Balbastro.

**Names commonly used:** Carlos María de Alvear; Carlos de Alvear; General Alvear.

**Title and rank:** Brigadier general; Supreme Director of the United Provinces of the Río de la Plata; minister of War and Navy; minister plenipotentiary.

**Birth:** 25 October 1789.

**Birthplace:** Santo Ángel Guardián, one of the former Jesuit mission settlements in the Misiones Orientales, then within the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata. The site lies in present-day Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil. Older biographies sometimes simplify the birthplace as “Misiones,” “Santo Ángel,” or Argentina, but sovereignty and modern national geography should not be projected backward without qualification.

**Death:** 3 November 1852.

**Place of death:** New York City, United States.

**Occupation:** Professional military officer, revolutionary organizer, legislator, political leader, military theorist, army commander, minister, diplomat, and polemical writer.

**Political orientation:** His career moved through several configurations: Spanish royal service; Hispano-American revolutionary secret organization; Buenos Aires-centered revolutionary government; concentrated directorial government; alliance with anti-directorial federal armies in 1819–1820; Rivadavian and later broadly Unitarian politics; and, finally, diplomatic service under Juan Manuel de Rosas and Justo José de Urquiza. Simple labels such as “unitarian” or “federalist” therefore describe only particular periods.

**Principal residences and stations:** Misiones Orientales; Buenos Aires; Spain, especially Cádiz and Madrid; London as a detainee or displaced survivor in 1804–1805; Montevideo; Rio de Janeiro; England and the United States during diplomatic missions; Bolivia/Upper Peru; the Banda Oriental and Rio Grande do Sul during the Brazilian War; and Washington and New York during his final diplomatic appointment.

**Burial:** His remains were ultimately placed in the Alvear family mausoleum in the Recoleta Cemetery, Buenos Aires. The present monumental family tomb was designed by Alejandro Christophersen in the early twentieth century.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

Carlos was the son of **Diego Estanislao de Alvear y Ponce de León** (1749–1830), a Spanish naval officer, surveyor, frontier commissioner, and later brigadier. Diego came from Montilla, Córdoba, Spain. His paternal family belonged to the proprietors associated with the **Alvear wine enterprise**, founded in Montilla in 1729 by Diego de Alvear y Escalera.

Diego’s parents were Santiago de Alvear and Escolástica Ponce de León. Later family histories claimed descent through the Ponce de León line from medieval or early-modern nobility. The immediate Montilla genealogy is documented; the more distant aristocratic claims depend heavily on family genealogical literature and should not be treated as established without a full examination of parish and notarial records.

Diego spent roughly three decades in South America. He participated in the Spanish-Portuguese boundary commissions established after the Treaty of San Ildefonso and acquired extensive knowledge of the Río de la Plata borderlands. This military, scientific, and administrative environment formed Carlos’s earliest family setting. A modern biographical study of Diego is available through CONICET, while the Real Academia de la Historia provides a concise account of his career.

### Mother and siblings

Carlos’s mother was **María Josefa Balbastro**, a woman from Buenos Aires whom Diego married in 1781. Sources usually state that the couple had nine children, although the exact enumeration and spelling of every child’s name vary among genealogical accounts.

On 5 October 1804, María Josefa and most of Carlos’s siblings were killed when the Spanish frigate *Nuestra Señora de las Mercedes* exploded during a British attack off Cape Santa María, Portugal. Carlos and his father survived because they were aboard another frigate in the convoy, usually identified as the *Medea*. Some popular retellings say that Carlos had been transferred to his father’s ship because of unruly behavior. The deaths and his presence on the *Medea* are documented; the explanation involving misbehavior is a family anecdote and cannot be treated with equal certainty.

Diego later married the Englishwoman **Louisa Rebecca Ward**. Their children—including Carlos’s half-sister **Sabina de Alvear y Ward**, author of a substantial biography of Diego—became important in the continued management and commercialization of the family’s Montilla wine interests. Carlos did not become the principal manager of the Spanish business; his career and household became centered in the Río de la Plata.

### Marriage

Carlos married **María del Carmen Sáenz de la Quintanilla y Camacho**, commonly known as Carmen Quintanilla, in Cádiz on **26 July 1809**. She was born in Jerez de la Frontera in 1793, the daughter of Juan Sáenz de la Quintanilla, a municipal financial official associated with Burgos, and Joaquina María Camacho.

Official Argentine biographical material credits the couple with **ten children**. Lists generally include:

1. María Carlota de Alvear.
2. Emilio Marcelo de Alvear.
3. Camilo Patricio Juan de Alvear.
4. Carlos F. de Alvear.
5. Torcuato Antonio de Alvear.
6. María Joaquina de Alvear.
7. Virginia Mercedes de Alvear.
8. Diego Federico de Alvear.
9. Juan Nepomuceno Camilo de Alvear.
10. León Juan de Alvear.

The completeness and ordering of this list require confirmation from parish registers and the Alvear family archive. Some children died young or left little public record.

The best-known son was **Torcuato Antonio de Alvear** (1822–1890), later the first mayor or *intendente* of Buenos Aires. Through Torcuato, Carlos was grandfather of President **Marcelo Torcuato de Alvear**.

Carmen survived her husband and died in 1867.

### Household consequences of political exile

Alvear’s repeated exiles and military appointments separated him from his family for substantial periods. The surviving archival collection contains both public and family papers, making it possible—though beyond what has been digitized—to reconstruct household finances, remittances, education of the children, and marital correspondence in more detail. No reliable evidence examined for this dossier establishes a second spouse or an acknowledged extramarital family.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1789–1804

Carlos spent his earliest years within the military-administrative world of the eastern missions and the Spanish-Portuguese frontier. His father’s boundary work meant movement among settlements rather than a single uninterrupted childhood residence.

He was entered into the **Regiment of Dragoons of Buenos Aires** while still a boy and is described as having served as a cadet and standard-bearer. This was not unusual for sons of officers, but it gave him an unusually early institutional military identity.

In 1804 Diego received permission to return to Spain with his family. They joined a four-frigate convoy—*Medea*, *Mercedes*, *Clara*, and *Fama*—carrying passengers, official funds, and private property. Britain and Spain were not yet formally at war when a British squadron intercepted the convoy.

On **5 October 1804**, the British action off Cape Santa María destroyed the *Mercedes*. Carlos, aboard the *Medea* with his father, survived; his mother and siblings aboard the *Mercedes* did not. The surviving ships were taken to Britain.

The episode had several lifelong consequences:

- destruction of Carlos’s immediate childhood household;
- removal to Britain under coercive circumstances;
- litigation and diplomatic controversy over the seized cargo;
- intimate exposure to British power, language, and institutions;
- eventual relocation to Spain rather than return to the missions.

The tragedy later became a central element in Alvear family memory. It is factual in its essentials but increasingly romanticized in retellings.

## Education and Formation in Britain and Spain

No complete school record for Alvear has been located in the consulted sources. His education appears to have combined:

- private instruction appropriate to an officer’s son;
- practical military formation;
- familiarity with Spanish, French, and probably English;
- experience in the professional Spanish army;
- exposure to liberal, constitutional, and American independence circles in Cádiz and London.

Following release from British custody, father and son went to Spain. Carlos entered the **Royal Brigade of Carabineers**, an elite mounted formation, as an ensign in late 1807.

### Peninsular War

He fought for Spain against Napoleonic forces during the Peninsular War. Biographical and military summaries associate him with the battles or actions of:

- Tudela;
- Tarancón;
- Uclés;
- Talavera;
- Los Yébenes;
- Mora;
- Consuegra.

The exact extent of his personal participation in every listed action should be checked against his service file at the Archivo General Militar de Segovia. His Spanish service record is nevertheless secure in general outline: he was a trained officer with combat experience before joining the Río de la Plata revolution.

### Cádiz political and secret-society networks

In Cádiz he associated with American-born officers and political activists who wanted constitutional reform and, increasingly, independence for Spanish America. He is connected to the **Society of Rational Knights** (*Caballeros Racionales*), a secret political organization using forms resembling Freemasonry.

Whether this organization was technically a regular Masonic lodge has been debated. Contemporary revolutionary lodges borrowed degrees, oaths, secrecy, and terminology from Masonry but served immediate political purposes. It is safest to describe Alvear’s affiliation as documented participation in a secret revolutionary society with Masonic forms.

Among his associates were José de San Martín, José Matías Zapiola, José Chilavert, Eduardo Kailitz or Holmberg, and other American officers.

## Return to the Río de la Plata and Revolutionary Organization, 1812–1813

Alvear sailed from Europe aboard the British merchant vessel **George Canning** with San Martín and other American officers. They arrived at Buenos Aires on **9 March 1812**.

The First Triumvirate accepted their military services, although suspicions about European affiliations and British transportation persisted. Alvear received the rank of lieutenant colonel and worked with San Martín, Zapiola, Julián Álvarez, and others in establishing the organization generally remembered as the **Lautaro Lodge**.

### Revolution of 8 October 1812

The lodge and allied military units helped organize the movement of **8 October 1812**, which removed the First Triumvirate. San Martín and Alvear appeared with troops in the Plaza de la Victoria, while civilian petitioners demanded governmental change.

The resulting Second Triumvirate called a general constituent assembly. This was both a constitutional opening and an extension of the influence of Alvear’s network.

### Assembly of the Year XIII

Alvear represented Corrientes in the **General Constituent Assembly of 1813** and became its first president. His election for a province with which he had limited direct connection illustrates the managed character of some contemporary representation.

The Assembly abolished or restricted several colonial institutions and symbols, adopted national emblems, legislated freedom of womb for children born to enslaved women, ended noble titles and certain forms of coerced Indigenous labor, and asserted sovereignty in practice. It neither formally declared independence nor enacted a constitution.

Alvear’s precise authorship of individual reforms is difficult to isolate from collective decisions. He was, however, a principal organizer of the political coalition dominating the Assembly.

### Military writing

In 1813 Alvear prepared or published **_Elementos de táctica terrestre, o evoluciones de un ejército en campaña_**. It reflects his European professional formation and was intended for the rapidly expanding revolutionary forces. Manuscript material survives in the Alvear collection.

### Rise of the Alvear faction

The revolutionary coalition fractured. San Martín favored a sustained continental military strategy and increasingly operated from a separate political base. Alvear cultivated influence in the Assembly, army, and government. His maternal relative **Gervasio Antonio de Posadas** became Supreme Director in January 1814.

The division between “San Martín’s party” and “Alvear’s party” was real, but later biographies often render it as a purely personal quarrel. It also involved appointments, strategic priorities, political centralization, and control of the secret organization.

## Montevideo Campaign, 1814

Montevideo remained the principal Spanish naval and military base in the Río de la Plata. José Rondeau commanded the land siege, while Guillermo Brown’s naval victories isolated the city.

Posadas replaced Rondeau with Alvear shortly before the final capitulation. Alvear took command in May 1814 and negotiated the surrender with the Spanish governor, **Gaspar de Vigodet**. Montevideo capitulated on **20 June 1814**.

### Success

The capture eliminated the principal royalist base near Buenos Aires and yielded large quantities of arms, ships, stores, and prisoners. Alvear received promotion and public credit.

### Disputes over credit and capitulation

His enemies accused him of arriving only after Brown and Rondeau had made victory inevitable and of appropriating their achievement. Vigodet accused him of violating terms connected with the surrender; Alvear replied in a printed exposition.

The documentary conclusion is mixed:

- Alvear did not conduct the whole siege.
- Brown’s blockade and naval victories were indispensable.
- Rondeau had commanded the besieging army for an extended period.
- Alvear held command when the city surrendered and bore responsibility for the final negotiations and occupation.
- Claims that he had no meaningful role are polemical; claims crediting him alone are likewise untenable.

### Conflict with Artigas

After taking Montevideo, the directorial government attempted to suppress **José Gervasio Artigas** and the eastern federal movement. Alvear issued proclamations, deployed forces, and treated Artigas’s followers as rebels. A surviving October 1814 decree threatened confiscation of the property of inhabitants who continued to follow Artigas or Fernando Otorgués.

This policy intensified the civil conflict between Buenos Aires centralism and the League of Free Peoples. The Archivo Artigas preserves correspondence and orders bearing on this campaign.

---

## Supreme Director, January–April 1815

Posadas resigned amid political and military reverses. The Assembly selected Alvear as Supreme Director on **9 January 1815**.

His administration lasted approximately three months.

### Governing position

Alvear inherited:

- military reverses in Upper Peru;
- strengthening federal opposition;
- Artigas’s ascendancy in the littoral and Banda Oriental;
- fears that Ferdinand VII would send a reconquest expedition;
- factional hostility within Buenos Aires;
- uncertain external recognition.

He concentrated authority, relied on military supporters, attempted to impose directorial commanders, and prepared operations against the federal provinces. Opponents portrayed the government as personalist and coercive.

### Attempt to command the Army of the North

Alvear sought command of the Army of the North, but its officers resisted. José Rondeau remained influential. San Martín, then governor of Cuyo, retained his own military base. These refusals exposed the limited reach of Alvear’s authority.

### British-protection letters

In February 1815 Alvear sent **Manuel José García** to Rio de Janeiro with confidential communications for British ambassador Lord Strangford and Foreign Secretary Lord Castlereagh. The papers declared that the Río de la Plata provinces could not sustain themselves alone and proposed placing them under British protection, using language that could be read as accepting British sovereignty.

Evidentiary distinctions are essential:

- **Documented fact:** letters bearing Alvear’s signature existed; Nicolás Herrera is commonly identified as their drafter; García carried them to Rio.
- **Documented fact:** García showed or communicated their substance to Strangford.
- **Reported fact:** Strangford discouraged formal delivery of the original formulations because British policy had changed after the restoration of Ferdinand VII.
- **Historical interpretation:** nationalist and revisionist writers called the episode treason or an offer to turn the country into a colony.
- **Competing interpretation:** defenders describe it as a desperate, unauthorized diplomatic maneuver intended to prevent Spanish reconquest, comparable to other contemporary searches for foreign protection.
- **Unresolved legal point:** there was no final treaty, formal British acceptance, or judicial conviction of Alvear for treason.

The letters remain among the strongest documentary foundations for hostile assessments of Alvear. Their existence should neither be denied nor expanded into an accomplished transfer of sovereignty.

### Collapse

Alvear ordered Colonel Ignacio Álvarez Thomas to march against Artigas. At **Fontezuelas** on 3 April 1815, Álvarez Thomas and his officers revolted against him. Buenos Aires’s Cabildo, military units, and political opponents joined the movement.

Alvear resigned on **15 April 1815**. The Assembly and Directorio associated with his faction were dissolved. He initially sought protection aboard a British vessel and left the country.

## Exile and Anti-Directorial Politics, 1815–1821

### Rio de Janeiro

Alvear spent much of his first exile in Rio de Janeiro, then the seat of the Portuguese monarchy. His presence generated accusations that he encouraged Portuguese intervention against Artigas or supplied intelligence damaging to the Río de la Plata.

The documentary record shows contact with Portuguese officials and intense opposition to the Buenos Aires governments that succeeded him. It does not, by itself, prove every later accusation that he planned territorial annexation. The context is nevertheless politically significant: Portuguese forces invaded the Banda Oriental in 1816, ultimately defeating Artigas and annexing the territory as the Cisplatine Province.

### Montevideo and José Miguel Carrera

By 1818 Alvear was in Portuguese-occupied Montevideo. He formed a close political and military association with the exiled Chilean leader **José Miguel Carrera**, another enemy of San Martín’s political network. Carrera published newspapers and manifestos from Montevideo; Alvear issued pamphlets defending himself against accusations from Buenos Aires.

The two participated in a secret or semi-secret opposition network sometimes called **Caballeros Orientales**. Its precise institutional continuity with earlier lodges is debated, but their shared campaign against the Pueyrredón-San Martín order is documented.

### Publications of 1819

Alvear’s polemical writings included:

- **_Refutación de la calumnia intentada contra don Carlos Alvear_**, Montevideo, January 1819.
- **_Otras calumnias refutadas_**, Montevideo, March 1819.
- **_Observaciones sobre la defensa de la provincia de Buenos Aires_**, 1819.

These were political interventions, not detached memoirs. They should be read alongside hostile newspapers, official proclamations, and correspondence.

### Alliance with the littoral caudillos

In late 1819 Alvear and Carrera joined forces associated with Estanislao López of Santa Fe and Francisco Ramírez of Entre Ríos. The alliance opposed the central Directorio, although the participants had different aims:

- López and Ramírez defended provincial autonomy.
- Carrera sought forces with which to return to Chile.
- Alvear sought political rehabilitation or power in Buenos Aires.
- Artigas’s relationship with Ramírez was already deteriorating.

The federal army defeated Supreme Director José Rondeau at **Cepeda on 1 February 1820**, ending the national Directorio and Congress.

Alvear was not a signer of the Treaty of Pilar, signed by Manuel de Sarratea, López, and Ramírez on 23 February, but he participated in the surrounding military-political coalition.

### Attempt to obtain the Buenos Aires government

During the “Anarchy of the Year XX,” Alvear attempted to become governor of Buenos Aires. Veteran troops or a military assembly proclaimed him at one point, but civilian and military resistance prevented consolidation.

He accompanied López’s renewed invasion and fought in the campaign culminating at **Cañada de la Cruz** on 28 June 1820. López’s forces defeated Miguel Estanislao Soler. Alvear’s claim to govern nevertheless failed. Manuel Dorrego subsequently defeated or dispersed forces associated with López, Carrera, and Alvear near San Nicolás and Pavón.

He again left Buenos Aires. This episode is important because it contradicts any portrayal of his politics as consistently centralist: in 1820 he allied with federal provincial armies against former directorial colleagues. Whether this represented ideological conversion or tactical opportunism was debated even by contemporaries.

### Return under the Law of Oblivion

The Buenos Aires legislature’s **Law of Oblivion**, adopted in 1821, permitted political exiles including Alvear, Dorrego, Sarratea, and Soler to return. Alvear was back in Buenos Aires by 1822.

## Diplomatic Rehabilitation, 1823–1825

Governor Martín Rodríguez and minister Bernardino Rivadavia employed Alvear diplomatically.

### Mission to Britain and the United States

Alvear traveled to London in 1824. He met British foreign secretary **George Canning** on 22 July and discussed recognition, commerce, and the Banda Oriental, then held by Brazil.

He subsequently traveled to the United States and met President **James Monroe**. The talks concerned recognition and possible access to arms, ships, and supplies. Contemporary summaries report Monroe explaining that matériel could be acquired through commercial means despite Spanish objections.

The mission did not produce a British or American military guarantee for recovery of the Banda Oriental. It contributed to the broader effort for international recognition and illustrated Alvear’s value as an English-speaking, European-trained envoy.

### Mission to Bolívar and Sucre

In January 1825 Alvear was appointed minister plenipotentiary to the government headed by **Simón Bolívar**, with **José Miguel Díaz Vélez** as associate or secretary. He traveled through Upper Peru after the final collapse of Spanish power.

His objectives included:

- congratulating Bolívar and Antonio José de Sucre;
- securing cooperation against Brazil;
- discussing regional political organization;
- resolving the status of Tarija, claimed by the United Provinces but connected to the emerging Bolivian state.

Alvear reported sympathetically on Bolívar’s plans for a continental alliance. No durable union of the former Spanish-American territories resulted, and the Tarija dispute remained unresolved in Argentina’s favor.

---

## Minister of War and the Brazilian War, 1826–1827

After the creation of the presidency under Bernardino Rivadavia, Alvear became **minister of War and Navy on 8 February 1826**.

His tasks included rebuilding an army for war against the Empire of Brazil over the Banda Oriental. He oversaw mobilization, procurement, organization, and appointments before assuming field command.

### Commander in chief

Alvear became commander in chief of the Republican Army, a coalition of troops from the United Provinces and Oriental forces. Among his senior officers were:

- José María Paz;
- Juan Galo Lavalle;
- Juan Antonio Lavalleja;
- Lucio Norberto Mansilla;
- Federico Brandsen;
- Enrique Martínez;
- Tomás de Iriarte;
- Manuel Oribe and other Oriental officers.

Relations were difficult. Lavalleja challenged Alvear’s authority; Paz and Iriarte later criticized his conduct; rival national, provincial, and personal loyalties divided the command.

### Campaign in Rio Grande do Sul

Alvear invaded Brazilian territory late in 1826. The army fought at **Bacacay** and **Ombú** in February 1827. His operational plan used maneuver and withdrawal to draw the Imperial army, commanded by the Marquis of Barbacena, into unfavorable ground.

### Battle of Ituzaingó, 20 February 1827

At Ituzaingó, also known as Passo do Rosário, the Republican Army defeated the Brazilian force.

**Documented result:** a battlefield victory for Alvear’s army. Brazilian forces withdrew and abandoned equipment.

**Command controversy:**

- Alvear and his supporters attributed victory to the commander in chief’s strategic maneuvering.
- Paz, Iriarte, Lavalleja, and other critics emphasized decisions by subordinate commanders and accused Alvear of confused or inadequate battlefield control.
- Colonel Federico Brandsen died during a disputed cavalry attack; some accounts say Alvear ordered an impracticable charge despite Brandsen’s objection.
- Critics accused Alvear of failing to pursue and destroy Barbacena’s retreating army.
- Defenders cite exhausted horses, dispersed units, supply limitations, and the condition of the battlefield.

The most defensible conclusion is that Alvear deserves strategic command credit for bringing the enemy to battle and winning, while the execution depended heavily on subordinate formations and the failure to pursue prevented a decisive destruction of the Imperial army.

### Political consequences

The victory did not end the war. Logistics, finances, naval inferiority, and internal political crisis limited further operations.

When Manuel José García signed a preliminary agreement accepting Brazilian possession of the Banda Oriental, Alvear and senior officers publicly rejected it. Rivadavia’s government repudiated the agreement, but the scandal helped bring down the presidency.

Alvear was removed from command in July 1827, with José María Paz temporarily taking charge before Lavalleja’s appointment. He published a detailed defense:

**_Exposición que hace el general Alvear para contestar al mensaje del gobierno del 14 de septiembre de 1827_**, issued in 1827–1828 with supporting documents.

## Politics after the War, 1827–1837

After Rivadavia’s resignation, Alvear temporarily withdrew. Governor Manuel Dorrego’s government did not restore him to supreme field command.

The Unitarian military revolution of December 1828 returned him to political activity. Lavalle overthrew Dorrego and had him executed. Alvear belonged to the broader Unitarian camp, but the consulted evidence does not establish that he ordered or directly participated in Dorrego’s execution. Contemporary reconciliation proposals had even mentioned Alvear as a possible replacement governor.

During the civil wars he was periodically considered for military or diplomatic office. His earlier conflicts with San Martín, Artigas, Rondeau, and federal leaders continued to shape his reputation.

No comprehensive, reliable reconstruction of his property and income during these years was found online. He belonged to an affluent transatlantic family, but prolonged exile, a large household, and public service meant that family prestige cannot simply be equated with continuous liquid wealth.

## Minister Plenipotentiary in the United States, 1838–1852

Juan Manuel de Rosas appointed Alvear minister plenipotentiary of the Argentine Confederation to the United States. He departed in early 1838, during the French blockade of the Río de la Plata, and remained in the post until his death.

The Argentine Foreign Ministry’s archival guide identifies the Alvear mission in **AH/0019 and AH/0019 Bis** within the Rosas government papers. The Alvear family archive also contains his diplomatic correspondence.

### Relations with Rosas

The appointment illustrates Alvear’s political adaptability. A former directorial and Unitarian leader now represented Rosas’s confederation. His reports defended the Confederation’s position against French and British coercion and monitored diplomatic opinion in Washington.

There is no need to infer personal ideological conversion to every element of Rosismo. The documented relationship was an official diplomatic service maintained for roughly fourteen years.

### Subjects of his dispatches

His correspondence addressed:

- recognition and protocol;
- the French and Anglo-French blockades;
- United States commerce;
- European intervention in the Río de la Plata;
- Texas, Mexico, and United States expansion;
- the strategic vulnerability of Patagonia;
- hemispheric diplomacy.

Reports attributed to him warned that the United States was becoming expansionist and that its treatment of Mexico had departed from the republican restraint associated with George Washington. In the 1840s he urged vigilance regarding Patagonia and foreign territorial ambitions.

These writings complicate the older allegation that Alvear was invariably an agent of British interests. Whatever his 1815 policy, his later dispatches opposed Anglo-French coercion and warned against North Atlantic expansion.

### Final political transition

Rosas fell at Caseros on 3 February 1852. **Justo José de Urquiza** retained Alvear as representative in the United States, demonstrating that his diplomatic utility survived the regime change.

## Political, Ideological, and Intellectual Development

### Monarchy, republicanism, and independence

Alvear began as an officer of the Spanish monarchy. His subsequent revolutionary association sought American independence and constitutional government, but neither he nor the Lautaro network adhered consistently to one institutional formula. Revolutionary leaders considered republics, constitutional monarchies, foreign princes, and external protectorates at different moments.

His 1815 appeal for British protection conflicts sharply with a simple republican-nationalist image. It nevertheless arose before a definitive Argentine constitutional settlement and during credible fears of reconquest.

### Centralism

From 1812 through 1815 Alvear favored concentrating executive and military authority in Buenos Aires. His conflict with Artigas arose partly from incompatible claims:

- Buenos Aires asserted central control of war, customs, and diplomacy.
- Artigas and his allies defended provincial sovereignty and federal confederation.

Alvear’s alliance with López and Ramírez in 1820 did not erase his earlier centralism. It demonstrates that factional and personal calculations could override stable institutional alignments.

### Military thought

His *Elementos de táctica terrestre* and Brazilian War exposition show a professional conception of war based on European drill, maneuver, staff organization, written orders, and documentary justification. His critics considered him vain, theatrical, and overconfident; supporters considered him among the few Río de la Plata commanders with formal European training.

### Secret societies

Participation in *Caballeros Racionales* and the Lautaro organization is documented. Claims that these groups constituted a permanent British-controlled Masonic conspiracy are not established by the surviving evidence. They were secret, oath-bound political networks with Masonic forms and transatlantic contacts; that does not prove external command.

### Religion

Alvear came from a Catholic Spanish colonial family and operated in officially Catholic states. No substantial evidence located here supports a distinct theological program, conversion, atheism, or anticlerical philosophical system. Secret-society membership should not, by itself, be treated as proof of religious heterodoxy.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### José de San Martín

Initially a collaborator in Cádiz-associated revolutionary networks, the 1812 voyage, the Lautaro Lodge, and the overthrow of the First Triumvirate. By 1813–1814 they became political rivals. Alvear’s faction sought to remove or contain San Martín; San Martín developed an independent power base in Cuyo. Carrera’s later alliance with Alvear deepened the hostility.

### Gervasio Antonio de Posadas

Maternal relative, political ally, and patron. Posadas’s appointment as Supreme Director and Alvear’s military promotions were integral to the rise of the Alvear faction. Charges of nepotism were therefore grounded in a genuine family-political relationship, though they do not alone determine the merits of each appointment.

### José Gervasio Artigas

Military and political adversary in 1814–1815. Alvear’s government attempted to suppress the Artiguist movement and threatened confiscation against its supporters. The relationship was later complicated when Alvear joined armies nominally connected to the federal struggle against the Directorio.

### Guillermo Brown

Naval collaborator in the campaign that captured Montevideo. Later narratives disputed the division of credit. Brown’s victory at sea made the capitulation practicable; Alvear commanded the final land phase.

### José Rondeau

Rival commander displaced by Alvear at Montevideo. The Army of the North resisted Alvear’s subsequent attempt to replace Rondeau. Rondeau’s government fell to the federal coalition with which Alvear was associated in 1820.

### José Miguel Carrera

Close collaborator in exile, anti-San Martín propagandist, military ally, and companion in the 1819–1820 campaigns. Their alliance combined Carrera’s Chilean restoration project with Alvear’s effort to return to Buenos Aires politics.

### Estanislao López and Francisco Ramírez

Temporary allies against the Directorio and Buenos Aires governments in 1819–1820. The coalition was tactical and did not make Alvear an enduring federal caudillo.

### Bernardino Rivadavia

Patron during Alvear’s diplomatic rehabilitation, appointing him to missions and later as minister of War and Navy. Their association culminated in the Brazilian campaign and ended amid the collapse of Rivadavia’s presidency.

### Simón Bolívar and Antonio José de Sucre

Diplomatic interlocutors during the 1825 mission. Alvear explored continental cooperation and a possible anti-Brazilian alignment but secured no permanent military alliance.

### José María Paz, Juan Antonio Lavalleja, Juan Galo Lavalle, and Tomás de Iriarte

Senior officers in the Brazilian War. They contributed materially to the campaign but later supplied some of the most influential criticisms of Alvear’s command.

### Juan Manuel de Rosas

Appointed and retained Alvear as minister in the United States. Their official relationship lasted from 1838 to Rosas’s fall in 1852, despite Alvear’s earlier Unitarian associations.

### Justo José de Urquiza

Confirmed Alvear after Caseros. Their documented connection was brief and administrative rather than a long-standing personal alliance.

## Offices, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Offices and appointments

- Cadet or youthful officer, Regiment of Dragoons of Buenos Aires.
- Ensign, Royal Brigade of Carabineers, Spain.
- Lieutenant colonel in the revolutionary forces, 1812.
- Deputy and first president, Assembly of the Year XIII.
- Colonel, Infantry Regiment No. 2.
- Commander of the besieging army at Montevideo, 1814.
- Brigadier general.
- Supreme Director, 9 January–15 April 1815.
- Diplomatic envoy to Britain and the United States, 1824.
- Minister plenipotentiary to Bolívar’s government, 1825.
- Minister of War and Navy, from 8 February 1826.
- Commander in chief, Republican Army in the Brazilian War.
- Minister plenipotentiary to the United States, 1838–1852.

### Family business and property

The Alvears were connected to the Montilla wine business later known as Bodegas Alvear. Carlos’s father and half-family retained substantial interests there. No evidence consulted establishes Carlos as an active long-term manager of the winery after his return to the Río de la Plata.

His father received compensation connected with the 1804 British seizure, but the distribution, inheritance consequences, and Carlos’s personal share require notarial and family-account research.

### Military projects

- Revolutionary secret organization and officer recruitment.
- Tactical manual for the new army.
- Reorganization and arming of the national army in 1826.
- Invasion plan and maneuver campaign in Rio Grande do Sul.
- Diplomatic monitoring of Patagonia and foreign expansion.

No patents, inventions, industrial enterprises, or corporate directorships are known. “Technology” in his career consisted principally of military organization, fortification, logistics, artillery, cavalry tactics, and diplomatic communications.

## Controversies, Allegations, Political Violence, and Financial Questions

### Removal of Rondeau and appropriation of Montevideo credit

**Documented fact:** Rondeau was replaced shortly before capitulation; Brown’s naval campaign was decisive; Alvear commanded at surrender.

**Allegation:** Alvear arranged the replacement to seize another commander’s victory.

**Assessment:** Political influence clearly aided his appointment. Intent to steal credit is plausible but not independently provable as a discrete fact.

### Conduct toward Vigodet and the surrendered royalists

**Documented dispute:** Vigodet accused Alvear of breaching the capitulation; Alvear published a defense.

**Status:** No neutral judicial determination settled every disputed provision. The conflicting printed accounts should be treated as adversarial evidence.

### Repression of Artiguists

**Documented fact:** Alvear directed military operations against Artigas’s movement and issued confiscatory threats against supporters.

**Historical characterization:** Federal and Uruguayan traditions frequently present this as an authoritarian war against provincial autonomy. Directorial sources framed it as suppression of rebellion and preservation of a common war effort.

### The British-protection letters

**Documented fact:** Alvear signed confidential letters proposing British protection in language potentially accepting foreign sovereignty.

**Allegation:** Treason and an attempted colonial surrender.

**Defense:** Emergency diplomacy designed to prevent Spanish reconquest.

**Legal status:** No completed transfer, indictment, trial, or conviction. The disputed issue is interpretation and intent, not the existence of the documentary episode.

### Portuguese connections

**Documented fact:** Alvear lived in Rio and Portuguese-occupied Montevideo and communicated within Portuguese political circles.

**Allegation or theory:** He encouraged Portugal’s invasion of the Banda Oriental or acted as a Portuguese instrument against Artigas.

**Status:** Some correspondence supports political cooperation and shared hostility toward his Buenos Aires enemies. A comprehensive documentary demonstration that he originated or controlled the Portuguese invasion has not been established here.

### Opportunism in 1820

**Documented fact:** the former centralist Supreme Director allied with López, Ramírez, and Carrera against the Directorio and attempted to obtain the governorship of Buenos Aires.

**Interpretations:** Political opportunism; revenge; tactical federalism; or participation in the destruction of an unsustainable central regime. These are interpretations rather than judicial findings.

### Ituzaingó and Brandsen’s death

**Documented fact:** Brandsen died leading cavalry at Ituzaingó.

**Reported allegation:** Alvear insisted on a charge over terrain Brandsen considered unsuitable and responded to his objection with language impugning his courage.

**Status:** Widely repeated from military memoir traditions, but exact dialogue is difficult to authenticate. The death and command dispute are historical; verbatim theatrical exchanges should be treated cautiously.

### Failure to pursue after Ituzaingó

**Documented fact:** no destructive pursuit eliminated Barbacena’s army.

**Dispute:** Critics called this incompetence or timidity; defenders cited exhausted mounts, shortages, and dispersed forces. It was a military controversy, not proof of betrayal.

### Dorrego’s overthrow and death

**Documented fact:** Alvear was politically associated with Unitarians active after Lavalle’s December 1828 coup.

**Unproven allegation:** direct responsibility for Dorrego’s execution. No consulted document establishes that Alvear ordered or procured it.

### Espionage or intelligence connections

Alvear engaged in confidential diplomacy, secret political societies, wartime intelligence, and communications with foreign governments. These activities make him an intelligence actor in the broad historical sense. No evidence examined establishes that he was a salaried British or Portuguese intelligence agent.

---

## Health, Injuries, and Death

No reliable record located here identifies a chronic illness, addiction, psychiatric diagnosis, or major disabling wound.

The 1804 destruction of his family was an extreme childhood trauma, but retrospective psychological diagnosis would be speculative.

Alvear died in New York on **3 November 1852**, only weeks after Urquiza confirmed him in office. The consulted sources do not provide a securely documented medical cause of death. Claims assigning a precise disease should therefore be withheld pending examination of consular, church, cemetery, newspaper, or family records.

His remains were returned to Buenos Aires. They now rest in the Alvear family mausoleum at Recoleta.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Alvear was simply a traitor who tried to sell Argentina”

**Story:** Alvear offered the Río de la Plata to Britain as a colony and was throughout his life a British agent.

**Appearance and circulation:** Built around the authentic 1815 letters and developed in nationalist, federal, and revisionist histories; also repeated in popular biographies.

**Evidence for:** signed letters proposing British protection in exceptionally submissive terms; use of Manuel José García and Lord Strangford as intermediaries.

**Evidence against the maximal version:** Britain did not accept sovereignty; García apparently did not formally deliver the papers as drafted; later Alvear defended the Confederation against British and French coercion. No payroll, recruitment document, or continuous chain of British direction has been produced.

**Reputational effect:** Central to the hostile image of Alvear as an elite, foreign-aligned antihero.

### “He stole the victory of Montevideo”

**Story:** Alvear used family influence to replace Rondeau only after victory was assured and then claimed the honors earned by Rondeau and Brown.

**Evidence:** the timing of his appointment, Posadas’s kinship and patronage, and the indispensable prior work of Rondeau and Brown.

**Counterevidence:** Alvear did assume command, negotiate capitulation, occupy the city, and take responsibility for the final operation.

**Effect:** Reinforced accusations of vanity and careerism.

### “He alone won Ituzaingó”

**Story:** Official commemoration converted Alvear into the singular hero of the battle.

**Origin:** official victory narratives, Alvear’s own exposition, family prestige, military commemoration, and the later monument.

**Counterevidence:** memoirs of Paz, Iriarte, Lavalleja’s supporters, and other officers credit subordinate actions and criticize Alvear’s control.

**Effect:** Produced a counter-myth in which Alvear did nothing and his subordinates won despite him. The evidence supports neither extreme.

### The *Mercedes* “naughty child” rescue story

**Story:** Carlos survived because his mischievous behavior caused his transfer from the *Mercedes* to his father’s ship immediately before the battle.

**Evidence:** family-derived narrative repeated in later histories.

**Status:** The placement aboard the *Medea* is factual; the disciplinary cause is an anecdote not independently established.

**Effect:** Turned catastrophic survival into a providential childhood legend.

### José de San Martín as Carlos’s half-brother

**Story:** Diego de Alvear fathered José de San Martín with a Guaraní woman, often named **Rosa Guarú**, making San Martín an older half-brother of Carlos María de Alvear.

**Where it appears:** chiefly in twentieth-century circulation based on a manuscript attributed to Carlos’s daughter María Joaquina de Alvear; later promoted through genealogical publications, press debate, and political proposals for DNA testing.

**Evidence offered:** the daughter’s late family assertion; supposed physical resemblance; Diego’s presence in the missions; perceived silences in the San Martín family record.

**Evidence against:** no contemporary baptismal, correspondence, support-payment, acknowledgment, or legal record; chronological and geographical questions remain; the claim appears generations later; the manuscript’s reliability and mental condition of its author have been challenged.

**Status:** **Rumor or genealogical theory**, not documented fact.

**Effect:** It has become one of the most persistent modern legends involving both families and periodically renews demands for genetic testing. It should not be used to describe San Martín and Alvear as brothers in factual genealogy.

### Aristocratic descent

**Story:** the Alvears descended directly from major Ponce de León noble lines.

**Evidence:** family genealogies and the documented surname.

**Problem:** a shared surname and family tradition do not establish every claimed medieval link. Immediate ancestry is firmer than remote lineage.

**Status:** documented family tradition; precise noble descent requires specialized genealogical proof.

### Masonic control and British conspiracy

**Story:** the Lautaro Lodge was a branch of British Freemasonry controlling independence movements for Britain.

**Evidence:** British travel, London and Cádiz contacts, Masonic forms, secrecy, and overlapping personnel.

**Counterevidence:** political societies often borrowed Masonic structures without being subordinate regular lodges; no single British command archive has established operational control of Alvear’s organization.

**Status:** participation in a secret lodge is documented; centralized British control is an allegation or conspiracy theory.

## Works by Carlos María de Alvear

The bibliography is complicated because many of Alvear’s writings survive as manuscripts, official documents, pamphlets, later edited compilations, or letters embedded in documentary collections.

### Authored or issued during his lifetime

1. **_Elementos de táctica terrestre, o evoluciones de un ejército en campaña_** (Buenos Aires, 1813).  
   Military treatise or instructional manual. Manuscript material is preserved in the Alvear archive. Genuine authorship is generally accepted.

2. **_Exposición que hace el Sr. Brigadier D. Carlos Alvear, General en Jefe del Ejército Sitiador de Montevideo, de su conducta en la rendición de esta plaza, vulnerada por las falsas imputaciones de su gobernador D. Gaspar Vigodet_** (Buenos Aires, 1814).  
   Polemical documentary defense of his conduct at Montevideo.

3. **_El Supremo Director del Estado a los ejércitos de la patria_** (Buenos Aires, 1815).  
   Official proclamation issued upon or during his Supreme Directorship.

4. **Letters to Lord Strangford and Lord Castlereagh** (1815).  
   Confidential official communications, usually attributed in draftsmanship to Nicolás Herrera but signed and politically authorized by Alvear. They are genuine Alvear governmental documents, not necessarily personally composed prose.

5. **Proclamations and decrees concerning Artigas, Otorgués, Montevideo, and the eastern campaign** (1814–1815).  
   Official documents scattered through the *Archivo Artigas*, government gazettes, and documentary collections.

6. **_Refutación de la calumnia intentada contra don Carlos Alvear_** (Montevideo: Imprenta de John Griswold, January 1819).  
   Political self-defense.

7. **_Otras calumnias refutadas_** (Montevideo: Imprenta de John Griswold, March 1819).  
   Continuation of the polemical defense.

8. **_Observaciones sobre la defensa de la provincia de Buenos Aires_** (Imprenta de Mayo, 1819).  
   Military-political pamphlet.

9. **Proclamations of the commander in chief of the Republican Army** (1826–1827).  
   Includes addresses to the army before and after operations in Brazil.

10. **Official report of the Battle of Ituzaingó** (headquarters at São Gabriel, 1827).  
    An official military dispatch subsequently reproduced in government documentary collections.

11. **_Exposición que hace el general Alvear para contestar al mensaje del gobierno del 14 de septiembre de 1827_** (Buenos Aires, 1827–1828).  
    Extended defense of his conduct in the Brazilian War, published with notes and documentary appendices.

12. **Diplomatic dispatches from London, Washington, Upper Peru/Bolivia, and the United States** (1824–1825; 1838–1852).  
    Genuine official correspondence, much of it unpublished or distributed among archival and edited collections.

13. **Private and political correspondence** with family members, military officers, Carrera, government ministers, Rosas, and foreign officials.  
    Survives principally in the Fondo Carlos María de Alvear and related national archives.

### Posthumous editions and attributed compilations

14. **_El general Alvear a propósito de las memorias del general Iriarte_** (Buenos Aires: Emecé, 1986).  
    Posthumous editorial publication derived from Alvear papers. It is not a book issued by him in 1986; editorial provenance and manuscript selection should be consulted in the volume.

15. **Documents in Gregorio F. Rodríguez, _Historia de Alvear_** (1909) and **_Contribución histórica y documental_** (1921).  
    Posthumously edited primary materials mixed with the editor’s historical argument.

16. **Documents in _Biblioteca de Mayo_, Archivo General de la Nación publications, and military-document collections.**  
    Posthumous reproductions rather than separately authored books.

17. **Letters and proclamations reproduced on Wikisource or Internet Archive.**  
    Digital transcriptions or scans; authenticity depends on the underlying printed or archival edition.

No diary written continuously by Alvear is securely identified in the online catalogue. The AGN description mentions a military diary and extensive public and family documentation, but each item’s authorship should be verified from the inventory.

### Value and bias of the literature

The literature divides broadly into:

- **Mitrist and Sanmartinian narratives**, frequently hostile to Alvear;
- **family and commemorative literature**, generally defensive;
- **federal and Artiguist accounts**, emphasizing repression and foreign alignment;
- **military studies**, divided over Ituzaingó;
- **modern revisionist defenses**, challenging the “traitor” archetype;
- **archival studies**, which provide the strongest basis for moving beyond inherited reputational camps.

None should be used alone.

### Monument and visual legacy

Argentina commissioned **Antoine Bourdelle** to create a monumental equestrian Alvear. The commission dates to 1913; Bourdelle produced dozens of studies. The completed work was delivered during the 1920s and inaugurated in Buenos Aires on **16 October 1926**.

It consists of an equestrian figure above a massive granite pedestal, accompanied by allegories of Victory, Liberty, Strength, and Eloquence. The pedestal was designed by Alejandro Bustillo. The monument materially elevated Alvear in Buenos Aires’s civic landscape and counterbalanced the hostile image found in much nineteenth-century historiography.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Alvear’s reputation has never stabilized.

### Nineteenth-century hostility

Sanmartinian, federal, and liberal-national histories depicted him as:

- ambitious;
- aristocratic;
- factional;
- personally jealous of San Martín;
- repressive toward Artigas;
- dependent on foreign powers;
- responsible for political instability.

Bartolomé Mitre’s authority strongly influenced this tradition.

### Family rehabilitation

The later prominence of the Alvear family—especially Torcuato de Alvear and President Marcelo T. de Alvear—supported documentary collection, commemorations, monuments, and defensive biographies. Critics sometimes treated all rehabilitation as dynastic propaganda, while defenders accused Mitrist history of constructing a caricature.

### Military commemoration

Ituzaingó remains his clearest institutional claim to honor. Streets, towns, military units, monuments, and public spaces bear the Alvear name. The **Marcha de Ituzaingó**, taken as a trophy from the Brazilian army according to military tradition, became a ceremonial Argentine march.

### Modern reinterpretation

Recent work tends to treat him neither as an uncomplicated national hero nor as a one-dimensional traitor. Major subjects of reinterpretation include:

- the transatlantic character of revolutionary networks;
- contingency surrounding independence;
- diplomatic appeals for foreign protection;
- the relationship between secret societies and formal Masonry;
- factional construction of heroic reputations;
- professional military practice in Ituzaingó;
- his unexpectedly long and effective diplomatic service under Rosas.

### Documentary significance

The survival of an extensive personal archive makes Alvear unusually valuable for studying:

- revolutionary elite networks;
- political propaganda;
- military professionalization;
- exile;
- early Argentine diplomacy;
- the changing meaning of “unitarian,” “federal,” and “national” allegiance.

## Chronology

- **25 October 1789:** Born at Santo Ángel Guardián in the Misiones Orientales.
- **1790s:** Childhood in the military-border environment of the missions; entered the Buenos Aires Dragoons as a youthful cadet.
- **1804:** Family departed South America for Spain.
- **5 October 1804:** Survived the British attack off Cape Santa María; his mother and most siblings died aboard the *Mercedes*.
- **1804–1805:** Taken to Britain with his father; subsequently released.
- **Late 1807:** Entered the Royal Brigade of Carabineers in Spain as an ensign.
- **1808–1811:** Served against Napoleonic forces in the Peninsular War.
- **26 July 1809:** Married Carmen Sáenz de la Quintanilla in Cádiz.
- **Before 1812:** Participated in the Cádiz-associated Society of Rational Knights.
- **9 March 1812:** Arrived in Buenos Aires aboard the *George Canning* with San Martín and other officers.
- **Mid-1812:** Helped establish the Lautaro Lodge.
- **8 October 1812:** Participated in the military-political overthrow of the First Triumvirate.
- **31 January 1813:** Opening of the Assembly of the Year XIII; Alvear became its first president.
- **1813:** Produced *Elementos de táctica terrestre*; became colonel of Infantry Regiment No. 2.
- **January 1814:** His relative and ally Posadas became Supreme Director.
- **May 1814:** Replaced Rondeau in command of the siege of Montevideo.
- **20 June 1814:** Montevideo capitulated.
- **Late 1814:** Directed operations and punitive measures against Artigas’s supporters.
- **9 January 1815:** Became Supreme Director.
- **February 1815:** Sent García to Rio with letters proposing British protection.
- **3 April 1815:** Álvarez Thomas revolted at Fontezuelas.
- **15 April 1815:** Resigned and entered exile.
- **1815–1818:** Lived principally in Rio de Janeiro.
- **1818–1819:** Based in Portuguese-occupied Montevideo; allied with José Miguel Carrera.
- **1819:** Published pamphlets defending himself and discussing Buenos Aires’s defense.
- **Late 1819:** Joined the coalition of López, Ramírez, and Carrera.
- **1 February 1820:** Federal victory at Cepeda destroyed the Directorio.
- **June 1820:** Participated in the campaign associated with Cañada de la Cruz; failed to consolidate his claim to govern Buenos Aires.
- **1820–1821:** Renewed exile after military defeat.
- **1821–1822:** Law of Oblivion enabled his return.
- **1824:** Diplomatic mission to Britain and the United States; met Canning and Monroe.
- **1825:** Mission to Bolívar and Sucre in Upper Peru/Bolivia.
- **8 February 1826:** Appointed minister of War and Navy.
- **Late 1826:** Took command of the Republican Army against Brazil.
- **13 February 1827:** Action at Bacacay.
- **16 February 1827:** Action at Ombú.
- **20 February 1827:** Victory at Ituzaingó.
- **July 1827:** Removed or replaced in field command.
- **1827–1828:** Published his defense of the Brazilian campaign.
- **December 1828:** Returned to political activity during the Unitarian revolution.
- **Early 1838:** Departed as Argentine minister plenipotentiary to the United States.
- **1838–1852:** Represented the Argentine Confederation in Washington; reported on the blockades, Mexico, United States expansion, and Patagonia.
- **3 February 1852:** Rosas defeated at Caseros.
- **1852:** Urquiza confirmed Alvear in his diplomatic post.
- **3 November 1852:** Died in New York.
- **Later nineteenth century:** Remains transferred to the family mausoleum in Recoleta; family and historical disputes over his reputation intensified.
- **1908–1913:** Movement to erect a national monument; Bourdelle received the commission.
- **16 October 1926:** Bourdelle’s equestrian monument inaugurated in Buenos Aires.
- **1986:** Posthumous edition *El general Alvear a propósito de las memorias del general Iriarte* published.
- **Present:** Remembered simultaneously as captor of Montevideo, victor of Ituzaingó, controversial Supreme Director, foreign-protection petitioner, and long-serving diplomat.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted record are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### José de San Martín — **Documented fact**

Alvear and San Martín were fellow Spanish-service officers connected to revolutionary circles in Cádiz; traveled to Buenos Aires aboard the *George Canning* in 1812; organized the Lautaro Lodge; and cooperated in the revolution of 8 October 1812. They became political rivals by 1813–1814. Alvear’s faction attempted to restrict San Martín’s influence, while San Martín consolidated power in Cuyo. Their rivalry was intensified by Alvear’s later alliance with José Miguel Carrera.

### Juan Martín de Pueyrredón y O’Doggan — **Documented fact**

Pueyrredón’s directorial administration represented the political order opposed by Alvear during exile. From Montevideo, Alvear and Carrera campaigned against the Pueyrredón-San Martín network. The connection was chiefly adversarial and political rather than a documented close personal relationship.

### Manuel Belgrano — **Reported fact**

Both served the revolutionary government and belonged to the upper military-political network of the United Provinces. Alvear’s attempt to assume control of the Army of the North occurred within the command structure in which Belgrano had been a central figure. No well-documented intimate friendship or direct personal rivalry was established in the sources consulted, so the connection should not be overstated.

### Simón Bolívar — **Documented fact**

Alvear was appointed minister plenipotentiary in 1825 and met Bolívar’s government in Upper Peru. They discussed continental cooperation, the emerging Bolivian state, Tarija, and possible support in the coming conflict with Brazil.

### Antonio José de Sucre — **Documented fact**

Alvear met or negotiated with Sucre’s government during the 1825 mission in Upper Peru/Bolivia. The relationship was diplomatic and concerned territorial organization and an anti-Brazilian alignment.

### Bernardo O’Higgins Riquelme — **Reported fact**

Both belonged to the larger independence network associated with San Martín and the Lautaro organizations. Alvear’s later alliance with O’Higgins’s rival José Miguel Carrera placed him politically against the San Martín–O’Higgins settlement in Chile. The consulted sources support a shared political network and antagonistic alignment, but not a securely documented major personal meeting.

### Torcuato Antonio de Alvear — **Documented fact**

Torcuato was Carlos María de Alvear’s son, born in Montevideo in 1822. He later became the first *intendente* of Buenos Aires and played a central role in the family’s continued public prominence.

### Alejandro Christophersen — **Documented fact**

Christophersen designed the Alvear family mausoleum in Recoleta, where Carlos María de Alvear’s remains rest. This is a posthumous architectural connection, not a lifetime meeting.

### Napoleon Bonaparte — **Documented indirect connection**

Alvear served in the Spanish army against Napoleonic forces during the Peninsular War. No personal meeting is claimed.

### George Washington — **Documented intellectual-symbolic reference**

In diplomatic dispatches from the United States, Alvear reportedly contrasted contemporary United States expansionism with the modest republican nation associated with Washington. This was a rhetorical and political reference, not a personal connection.

### Absence finding

No documentary crossing was found with the remainder of the atlas roster. Several roster members lived in the same broad nineteenth-century Atlantic world, but shared geography, profession, or historical period is insufficient evidence of a connection.
