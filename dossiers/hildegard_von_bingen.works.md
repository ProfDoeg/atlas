## Works by Hildegard von Bingen

### Visionary Gesamtkunstwerke

- **1141–1151:** *Scivias*. A three-part visionary-theological work combining dictated visions, doctrinal interpretation, lyrics, dramatic writing, and—at least in its celebrated lost Rupertsberg manuscript—thirty-five illuminations; Hildegard’s precise role in designing those images remains disputed, so the illustrated manuscript is better understood as a collaborative Gesamtkunstwerk than as her unaided artwork.

- **1158–1163:** *Liber vitae meritorum*. A six-part visionary dialogue between the cosmic figure of the Man and personified virtues and vices, organized as moral theology rather than as a conventional narrative.

- **c. 1163–1173/74:** *Liber divinorum operum*. Hildegard’s final visionary summa, joining cosmology, anthropology, salvation history, ethics, and eschatology in ten visions. Its wording passed through scribal revision, especially under Guibert of Gembloux, so “authorship” here includes dictated revelation and collaborative textual manufacture.

### Music, poetry, and sacred drama

- **principally 1151–1158, with earlier and later additions:** *Symphonia armonie celestium revelationum*. A manuscript constellation of seventy-seven sacred lyric compositions—antiphons, responsories, sequences, hymns, two pieces designated *symphonia*, an alleluia, a kyrie, and other chants—for which Hildegard made both words and melodies. Four texts survive without music, some pieces have variant forms, and scholars caution that the surviving collection may not represent a final or complete authorial corpus.

- **c. 1151–1158:** *O cruor sanguinis*. A chant surviving only as a fragment; its incompleteness is part of the object’s status, not evidence for a recoverable “full” work.

- **c. 1151–1158:** *O frondens virga*. A Marian chant whose text survives but whose melody has been lost.

- **c. 1151–1158:** *O fili dilectissime*. A sacred lyric surviving without musical notation.

- **c. 1151–1158:** *O factura Dei*. A sacred lyric surviving without musical notation.

- **c. 1151–1158:** *O magna res*. A sacred lyric surviving without musical notation.

- **c. 1151, revised later:** *Ordo virtutum*. A sung morality drama for the Soul, sixteen personified Virtues, a chorus, and the Devil, whose part is spoken or shouted rather than sung; an early unnotated form concludes *Scivias*, while a longer independently transmitted version preserves eighty-two melodies. It is neither merely a text nor merely a collection of songs, but a liturgical-theatrical Gesamtkunstwerk and performance protocol.

### Natural knowledge and medicine

- **probably 1150s; extant redactions are later:** *Liber subtilitatum diversarum naturarum creaturarum*. Hildegard names a work of this kind, but its original unity and form are lost; the surviving medical and natural-historical books appear to be later redactions of that project.

- **probably 1150s; surviving manuscripts 13th–16th centuries:** *Physica*, also called *Liber simplicis medicinae*. A nine-book inventory of plants, elements, trees, stones, fish, birds, animals, reptiles, and metals together with their properties and applications. It is transmitted only in substantially varying later manuscripts, so no single surviving text can simply be equated with Hildegard’s finished original.

- **probably 1150s; surviving manuscript mid-13th century:** *Causae et curae*, also called *Liber compositae medicinae*. A medical-cosmological compilation covering the body, disease, sexuality, diagnosis, prognosis, and remedies. Its Hildegardian authorship, unity, and redaction history remain disputed: Laurence Moulinier has argued for later Hildegardian compilation and interpolation, while other scholars defend substantial or nearly complete authorship by Hildegard.

### Homilies and doctrinal protocols

- **probably c. 1158–1170:** *Expositiones evangeliorum*. A collection of fifty-eight homiletic expositions on twenty-seven Gospel passages, apparently arising from Hildegard’s oral preaching and written down within her community. Because spoken performance, communal recording, and later arrangement produced the surviving object, it resists treatment as a single deliberately authored “book.”

- **date uncertain, probably mid-12th century:** *De regula sancti Benedicti* or *Explanatio regulae sancti Benedicti*. A selective explanation of the Benedictine Rule, framed as responses to questions rather than as a continuous commentary; it functions partly as a protocol for monastic life.

- **c. 1168:** *Explanatio symboli sancti Athanasii*. A doctrinal exposition of the Athanasian Creed, probably developed for oral instruction within Hildegard’s community.

- **after 1176:** *Triginta octo quaestionum solutiones*. Hildegard’s responses to thirty-eight biblical and theological questions sent by Guibert of Gembloux and the monks of Villers. It is simultaneously a treatise, correspondence, and collaborative questionnaire, and therefore resists classification as an independently conceived book.

### Saints’ lives

- **after 1162, perhaps c. 1170–1173:** *Vita sancti Ruperti confessoris*. A hagiographic life of the otherwise obscure patron of Rupertsberg, using sanctity to articulate the identity and sacred geography of Hildegard’s own foundation.

- **c. 1170:** *Vita sancti Disibodi episcopi*. A visionary hagiography of the patron and reputed founder of Disibodenberg. Hildegard presents the life as knowledge received through vision rather than as documentary biography.

### Invented language and script

- **probably 1150s:** *Lingua ignota per simplicem hominem Hildegardem prolata*. A glossary of roughly one thousand invented words supplied with Latin and sometimes German equivalents. It is less a complete usable language than the surviving manifestation of a linguistic project whose intended scope and function remain uncertain.

- **probably 1150s:** *Litterae ignotae*. An invented alphabet of twenty-three characters associated with the *Lingua ignota*. This is a graphic system rather than a prose text, and whether Hildegard designed it simultaneously with the vocabulary cannot be established securely.

### Correspondence, manifestos, and public interventions

- **c. 1146/47–1179:** *Epistolarium*. The critical edition contains 390 numbered documentary units, but this is an editorial corpus rather than one work: it combines Hildegard’s letters, letters addressed to her, petitions, replies, visions, sermons, and texts reworked by medieval compilers. Individual items often lack authorial titles, and the authenticity or integrity of some pieces—especially material uniquely preserved in the Riesencodex—is disputed.

- **1146/47:** Letter to Bernard of Clairvaux. An appeal for recognition and counsel concerning Hildegard’s visions; it acts as a request for authorization and is therefore closer to a legitimating manifesto than to private correspondence.

- **1147/48:** Letter to Pope Eugenius III. A prophetic declaration seeking papal recognition of her visionary project; its surviving form belongs to the mediated and sometimes editorially reshaped letter corpus.

- **1150s–1170s:** Admonitory letters to popes, emperors, bishops, abbots, abbesses, clergy, religious communities, and lay petitioners. These are individual interventions rather than chapters of a book, but most possess no fixed medieval titles; their dates and textual boundaries are often reconstructed from recipients, manuscript order, and historical context.

- **c. 1160–1170:** Records of Hildegard’s public preaching journeys. Several letters preserve or rework admonitions associated with sermons delivered at Mainz, Würzburg, Bamberg, Trier, Metz, Cologne, and elsewhere; they resist classification as securely transcribed speeches because the surviving texts may be retrospective written manifestations of oral performances.

- **1178–1179:** *Ad praelatos Moguntinenses*. Hildegard’s defense of her community during the interdict imposed after the burial of a man whom Mainz officials considered excommunicated. Although transmitted as a letter, it acts as a manifesto for monastic autonomy, visionary authority, and the indispensable sacramental force of music.

### Autobiographical and collaboratively transmitted manifestations

- **1150s–1170s; incorporated after 1179:** Autobiographical notices preserved in the *Vita sanctae Hildegardis*. Hildegard supplied accounts of her childhood, illnesses, visions, foundations, and conflicts, but she did not make the surviving *Vita* as a whole: Gottfried of Disibodenberg began it and Theoderic of Echternach completed it after her death.

- **1170s; recorded by Guibert of Gembloux:** *Visiones Hildegardis a Guiberto Gemblacensi exaratae*. Reports of Hildegard’s visionary experiences written down and shaped by Guibert. The critical edition includes them among the minor works, but they resist attribution as independent works “by Hildegard” because the extant textual form is Guibert’s manifestation of her oral testimony.

### Institutional projects

- **c. 1150:** Rupertsberg convent. Hildegard initiated and directed the transfer of her women’s community from Disibodenberg and the creation of an independent monastery near Bingen. The surviving institution and its lost buildings were collective works rather than objects made by Hildegard alone, but the foundation was one of her central realized projects.

- **1165:** Eibingen convent. Hildegard acquired and reorganized an existing Augustinian house across the Rhine as a second women’s community under her authority. Like Rupertsberg, it is an institutional obra—architectural, economic, liturgical, and communal—whose making cannot be reduced to personal authorship.
