## Works by Paul the Apostle

### Extant letters accepted as Pauline

- **c. 50–51 CE:** *First Letter to the Thessalonians*. An occasional congregational letter, jointly presented under the names of Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy; it is generally regarded as the earliest surviving Pauline letter and one of the earliest surviving Christian texts.

- **c. 53–54 CE:** *First Letter to the Corinthians*. A long intervention in the disputes, worship, ethics, and teaching of the Corinthian assembly, sent under the names of Paul and Sosthenes. It incorporates responses to reports and to a letter from Corinth, so it is less a treatise than one move in an extended correspondence.

- **c. 54 CE, or as early as c. 49–50:** *Letter to the Galatians*. A polemical circular letter—a near-manifesto asserting Paul’s gentile mission and opposing circumcision for gentile converts. Its date depends especially on whether its recipients are placed in northern or southern Galatia.

- **c. 54–56 CE:** *Second Letter to the Corinthians*. The canonical object is probably an editorial assemblage of several Pauline communications or fragments, although some scholars defend its unity; in that sense the surviving “letter” may not be the obra Paul originally made.

- **c. 53–62 CE:** *Letter to the Philippians*. A prison letter of friendship, exhortation, and thanks, sent under the names of Paul and Timothy. Its date depends on whether Paul’s unnamed imprisonment was in Ephesus, Caesarea, or Rome, and substantial scholarship treats the canonical text as a combination of two or three letters rather than a single original object.

- **c. 56–61 CE:** *Letter to Philemon*. A short prison petition, jointly presented by Paul and Timothy, concerning Onesimus; its exact legal and social purpose remains disputed. Like Paul’s other letters it was meant to act within a relationship, not merely describe one.

- **c. 56–57 CE:** *Letter to the Romans*. An occasional but unusually extensive letter dictated by Paul and physically written by the named secretary Tertius; it introduces Paul’s gospel and prepares for a projected mission to Spain. Its closing chapter has sometimes been suspected of separate circulation or later attachment, but the letter’s Pauline authorship is not seriously disputed.

### Lost or editorially reconstructed correspondence

- **c. 52 CE:** An earlier letter to Corinth, without a surviving title. Paul refers to it in 1 Corinthians 5:9, but the independent object is lost; proposals that a fragment survives in 2 Corinthians 6:14–7:1 remain conjectural.

- **c. 54–55 CE:** A collection letter concerning aid for Jerusalem. One influential reconstruction identifies 2 Corinthians 8 as what remains of this separate communication, but both its original independence and its boundaries are disputed.

- **c. 55 CE:** A defensive letter to Corinth. On a widely used partition theory, part of it survives in 2 Corinthians 2:14–7:4, excluding 6:14–7:1; this is a reconstructed textual object, not an independently preserved letter.

- **c. 55 CE:** A painful or “tearful” letter to Corinth, without a secure ancient title. Paul mentions such a communication in 2 Corinthians 2:3–4 and 7:8; scholars variously regard it as lost, identify it with 1 Corinthians, or locate much of it in 2 Corinthians 10–13.

- **c. 55–56 CE:** A letter of reconciliation to Corinth. Under a composite reconstruction, its remains are 2 Corinthians 1:1–2:13, 7:5–16, and 13:11–13; other reconstructions divide the canonical letter differently or deny this object’s separate existence.

- **c. 56 CE:** A further collection letter concerning Jerusalem. Some reconstructions treat 2 Corinthians 9 as an independent communication, while others understand chapters 8–9 as parts of the same letter.

- **Unknown, if it existed as a Pauline letter:** A letter associated with Laodicea. Colossians 4:16 mentions a letter “from Laodicea,” but the wording does not securely establish that Paul wrote it; identifications with Ephesians or with the much later apocryphal *Epistle to the Laodiceans* are disputed.

### Letters attributed to Paul but disputed

- **Early 50s or c. 70–95 CE:** *Second Letter to the Thessalonians*. It names Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy and closely imitates 1 Thessalonians; scholars divide between authorship during Paul’s lifetime and post-Pauline pseudepigraphy.

- **c. 60–62 or 65–80 CE:** *Letter to the Colossians*. A letter in Paul’s name, also naming Timothy, that some scholars accept as late Pauline work and others assign to a disciple developing his language and theology after his death.

- **c. 60–62 or 70–80 CE:** *Letter to the Ephesians*. Formally a circular exhortation or theological homily in letter form, heavily related to Colossians. Pauline authorship has defenders, but many scholars regard it as a post-Pauline composition; “in Ephesus” is absent from important early manuscripts, so even its destination is unstable.

- **c. 63–67 or 95–125 CE:** *First Letter to Timothy*. A protocol-like pastoral letter prescribing teaching, worship, offices, and household conduct. Although it speaks as Paul, most critical scholars assign it to a later author working in his name.

- **c. 63–67 or 95–125 CE:** *Second Letter to Timothy*. A testament-like pastoral letter staged as Paul’s final imprisonment and instruction to Timothy. Some scholars defend its authenticity or propose authentic Pauline fragments within it, but the prevailing critical attribution is post-Pauline.

- **c. 63–67 or 95–125 CE:** *Letter to Titus*. A pastoral protocol for appointing leaders and regulating communities in Crete. Its Pauline authorship is disputed, with most critical scholars grouping it with 1–2 Timothy as later pseudepigraphy.

### Projects and nonliterary works

- **c. 40s–50s CE:** Paul’s gentile assemblies. Paul describes himself as founding or cultivating communities in places including Galatia, Philippi, Thessalonica, and Corinth; these are collective social creations rather than individually authored objects, and their continuation depended on numerous named and unnamed collaborators.

- **c. 50s CE:** The collection for the poor among the Jerusalem believers. This was a transregional fundraising and reconciliation project organized among Paul’s gentile assemblies, documented especially in 1 Corinthians 16, 2 Corinthians 8–9, Galatians 2:10, and Romans 15:25–28; its final delivery and outcome are not securely known.

- **Undated, during his missions:** Products of Paul’s manual labor. Paul says that he worked with his hands to support himself, but his authentic letters neither name his craft nor identify any surviving object; Acts calls him a *skenopoios*, conventionally rendered “tentmaker,” an attribution that cannot become a catalogue of specific made things.

### Ancient attributions not accepted as works by Paul

- **c. 60–90 CE:** *Letter to the Hebrews*. This anonymous sermon or “word of exhortation” entered parts of the ancient tradition as Pauline, but it does not name Paul and is not regarded by modern scholarship as his work.

- **c. 170 CE:** *Third Letter to the Corinthians*. Part of the *Acts of Paul* tradition and once canonical in some Syriac and Armenian settings, it is a pseudonymous composition from long after Paul’s lifetime.

- **Fourth century CE:** *Correspondence of Paul and Seneca*. A set of fourteen pseudonymous letters connecting Paul with the Roman philosopher Seneca; its late date excludes Pauline authorship.

- **Fourth century CE or later:** *Epistle to the Laodiceans*. A brief Latin compilation made largely from phrases in canonical Pauline letters. It is not the uncertain letter mentioned in Colossians 4:16 and is not a work by Paul.
