## Works by Abu Karib As'ad

### Royal buildings and monumental ensembles

- **c. 375–376 CE:** *Mikrāb Barīk at Maʾrib* (Ja 856 = Fa 60). A sanctuary or prayer-house—often interpreted as a synagogue—built from foundation to summit by Malkīkarib Yuhaʾmin and a son whose restored name is probably Abīkarib Asʿad. Because the relevant royal name is lost, Asʿad’s participation remains hypothetical, not certain.

- **January 384 CE:** *Palace Kilānum* (Garb Bayt al-Ashwal 2). A royal palace at Ẓafār built and completed by Malkīkarib Yuhaʾmin jointly with his sons Abīkarib Asʿad and Dharaʾʾamar Ayman; its foundation inscription invokes the “Lord of the Sky,” making building and inscription together an early monotheistic royal manifestation.

- **January 384 CE:** *Palace Shawḥaṭān* (RÉS 3383). A second palace at Ẓafār, constructed by the same three rulers and recorded in another monotheistic foundation inscription dated to the same month. The damaged ancient name is variously transliterated *Sʾwḥṭn*, *Sʾwhlʾ*, or normalized as *Shawḥaṭān*.

- **Uncertain date, probably early fifth century:** *Palace or multistorey building at Ḍahr* (YM 327 = Lu 10 = Ja 520 = Rossi 24). A several-storey royal building, possibly itself named *Ḍahr*, erected by Abīkarib Asʿad while ruling with his brother Dharaʾʾamar Ayman and his sons Ḥaśśān Yuʾmin, Maʿdīkarib Yunʿim, and Ḥugr Ayfaʿ; neither its exact date nor its classification as a palace is secure.

- **c. 400–420 CE?:** *Unnamed palace at Ẓafār* (Garb Frammenti II, 7). A fragmentary construction inscription, probably belonging to Asʿad’s royal titulary phase, appears to commemorate a palace and mentions Israel. Both the building’s identity and its attribution to Asʿad’s group of co-rulers remain conjectural.

### Royal inscriptions and commemorative manifestations

- **First half of the fifth century CE:** *Ry 509 (Maʾsal 1)*. A monumental rock inscription commissioned by Abīkarib Asʿad and his son Ḥaśśān Yuhaʾmin in Wādī Maʾsal al-Jumḥ, commemorating their expedition into central Arabia, possession of Maʿadd, and installation of garrisons. It is simultaneously a made inscription and a performative claim to territory—a manifesto of conquest rather than a narrative composed for private reading.

- **Possibly 429 CE:** *MS Šiǧāʿ 1*. A fragmentary royal rock inscription authored by Abīkarib Asʿad to commemorate an ibex hunt in the mountains northeast of Ṣanʿāʾ and to relate that hunt to the cult of the Lord of Heaven. The proposed date, year 539 of the Ḥimyarite era, is uncertain; if correct, it and other inscriptions showing Asʿad alive in 432–433 require extending his reign beyond the atlas’s conventional endpoint of c. 420.

### Works attributed only by later tradition

- **No secure date:** *Kiswa for the Kaʿba*. Later Arabic tradition calls Asʿad the first ruler to cover the entire Kaʿba and variously describes successive coverings of leather or Yemeni textiles. No contemporary inscription confirms the attribution, rival traditions name earlier originators, and none of Asʿad’s alleged textile survives; this is therefore a remembered commission, not a securely attested object.

- **No secure date:** Untitled prophetic and admonitory verses attributed to Tubbaʿ Asʿad. Ibn Isḥāq/Ibn Hishām and later Yemeni legendary literature place poems or speeches in Asʿad’s mouth, including verses associated with his journey through Yathrib and an exhortation to his son Ḥassān. These resist classification as Asʿad’s works: they survive only in sources written centuries later, and scholarship treats the larger “Ballad of Asʿad’s Adventures” as later folk-poetry, possibly shaped or invented by early Islamic storytellers.
