# Rashi: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Note

**Subject identified:** Rashi, the medieval Jewish biblical and Talmudic commentator of Troyes, not any modern person using the name Rashi.

The surviving record is unusually rich for Rashi’s writings but sparse for his private life. No contemporary narrative biography, personal diary, financial ledger, portrait, grave marker, or securely identified autograph manuscript survives. Much biographical information comes indirectly from:

- statements embedded in his commentaries and responsa;
- reports transmitted by students and descendants;
- manuscripts copied generations later;
- works of Rashi’s school, including *Maḥzor Vitry*, *Sefer ha-Pardes*, *Sefer ha-Orah*, and *Siddur Rashi*;
- medieval scholarly references;
- later legends and genealogies.

Accordingly, this dossier distinguishes documented or strongly supported information from reconstruction and folklore.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Principal Hebrew name:** רבי שלמה בן יצחק, *Rabbi Shelomoh ben Yitsḥaq* (“Rabbi Solomon son of Isaac”).
- **Common name:** רש״י, **Rashi**.
- **Probable expansion of the acronym:** רבי שלמה יצחקי, *Rabbi Shelomoh Yitsḥaqi*.
- **Other forms:** Shlomo Yitzchaki; Shelomo ben Isaac; Solomon bar Isaac; Solomon ben Isaac of Troyes; Salomon de Troyes; French *Rachi*; German *Raschi*.
- **Honorific:** רבן של ישראל, *Rabban shel Yisrael*, “teacher/master of Israel,” a later reverential title.
- **Erroneous or secondary name:** **Jarchi/Yarḥi**. Early-modern writers derived this from *yareaḥ*, “moon,” and associated Rashi with Lunel. Modern scholarship regards this as a confusion with another Solomon and not an authentic surname or indication that Rashi came from Lunel.
- **Birth:** Traditionally 1040 CE; some manuscripts give Hebrew year 4800, while manuscripts judged stronger by Avraham Grossman indicate 4801, corresponding to **1040–1041**.
- **Traditional exact birthday:** 22 February 1040 appears in modern reference works but is not supported by a known contemporary record.
- **Birthplace:** Troyes, Champagne, in what is now the Aube department of France.
- **Death:** Thursday, **29 Tammuz 4865**, conventionally **13 July 1105**.
- **Deathplace:** Probably Troyes.
- **Age:** A student-derived notice says sixty-five.
- **Burial:** Almost certainly Troyes. The grave’s precise location is lost.
- **Primary roles:** Biblical commentator, Talmudic commentator, teacher, halakhic respondent, communal authority, liturgical poet.
- **Commonly asserted occupation:** Vintner, vineyard owner, wine producer, or wine merchant. This remains a plausible but unproved reconstruction.

The death notice is preserved in several later manuscripts, including Parma, Biblioteca Palatina, De Rossi 175. Its calendrical statement—29 Tammuz 4865 falling on a Thursday—supports its authenticity, although the manuscript witness itself is not contemporary.

## Historical Setting

Rashi lived in the eleventh-century County of Champagne, before the Champagne fairs reached their later thirteenth-century scale. Troyes was nevertheless a regional commercial and ecclesiastical center with a Jewish community connected to northern France and the Rhineland.

His lifespan overlapped:

- the consolidation of Norman power under William the Conqueror;
- the growth of rabbinic schools in Mainz and Worms;
- the aftermath of Rabbenu Gershom’s institutional influence;
- expanding Jewish-Christian commercial contact;
- the First Crusade and the massacres of Jewish communities in the Rhineland in 1096.

“France” in Rashi’s works normally denotes northern France or the lands of the French-speaking Jews, while “Lotharingia” and “Germany” refer to the Rhineland centers in which he studied.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father

**Documented fact:** Rashi’s father was named **Isaac/Yitsḥaq**. Rashi cites an interpretation from “my father, my teacher” in his commentary to Avodah Zarah 75a, evidence that Isaac possessed rabbinic learning.

Almost nothing else can be established about him. Later portrayals of Isaac as poor, a wine merchant, a gem dealer, or a traveler belong principally to biography and legend.

### Mother

Rashi’s mother’s name is not reliably preserved.

**Reported fact:** Rashi referred to **Rabbi Simeon the Elder** as a maternal relative, usually understood as his maternal uncle. The exact genealogy is uncertain.

### Wife

Rashi’s wife is historically obscure.

- Her name is not securely known.
- “Rivka/Rebecca,” widely repeated online, lacks solid medieval documentation.
- There is no reliable evidence about her ancestry, education, occupation, or dates.
- Women in medieval Ashkenaz sometimes managed household commerce, but applying that general pattern specifically to her would be inference.

### Children

Rashi had no known sons. Two daughters are securely documented; a third is probable, and a possible fourth is conjectural.

1. **Jochebed/Yocheved**

   - Called “the pious” in some traditions.
   - Married **Rabbi Meir ben Samuel of Ramerupt**.
   - Their children included the major Tosafists:
     - Samuel ben Meir, **Rashbam**;
     - Isaac ben Meir, **Rivam**;
     - Jacob ben Meir, **Rabbenu Tam**;
     - Solomon ben Meir, sometimes called Solomon the Grammarian.
   - Through this household, Rashi’s family became central to the Tosafist movement.

2. **Miriam**

   - Married Rashi’s student **Judah ben Nathan**, known by the acronym **Rivan**.
   - Rivan completed or transmitted portions of Rashi’s Talmud commentary, notably the end of tractate Makkot.
   - Miriam is cited in later halakhic literature as a transmitter of family ritual practice. This supports learning and participation in the household scholarly culture, but not every later claim about her.

3. **Rachel**

   - Her existence is probable but less secure than that of Jochebed and Miriam.
   - She is associated with a Rabbi Eliezer and a short-lived marriage or divorce in later reconstructions.
   - Exact dates and details cannot be established.

4. **Possible unnamed daughter**

   Avraham Grossman suggested that a responsum describing Rashi’s exceptional mourning for a young girl during a festival might refer to another daughter who died young. This is a scholarly inference, not a direct identification.

### Claimed Davidic ancestry

**Later genealogical claim:** Numerous families claim descent from Rashi and, through him, from the tanna Johanan ha-Sandlar and ultimately King David.

**Evidence assessment:** The continuous chain is not documented. Genealogies publicized in the early-modern period contain gaps of many centuries and generations. Solomon Luria’s relevant responsum does not supply a continuous Davidic pedigree for Rashi. Descent from Rashi through identifiable medieval rabbinic families may be plausible in particular cases, but the enormous number of undocumented genealogical bridges makes most modern claims unprovable.

## Childhood and Early Life, c. 1040–1055

No contemporary source describes Rashi’s childhood.

Later biographies portray his family as poor but learned and exceptionally pious. These portrayals may preserve communal ideals rather than biographical facts.

He acquired:

- biblical Hebrew and rabbinic Hebrew;
- Babylonian Jewish Aramaic;
- knowledge of Midrash and halakhah;
- vernacular Old French;
- some German vocabulary during his Rhineland residence.

Rashi did not receive a philosophical or scientific education comparable to that of Jewish intellectuals in Islamic Spain. His writings do not show systematic engagement with Greek philosophy, Arabic science, astronomy, medicine, or mathematics. His formation was concentrated in scripture, Talmud, Midrash, liturgy, custom, and legal practice.

---

## Education and Formation in the Rhineland, c. 1055–1065

Rashi left Troyes as a young man to study in the older academies of the Rhineland. Dates are reconstructed rather than directly recorded; scholars commonly place these years from his late teens or around age twenty until approximately twenty-five.

### Worms

His first major teacher was **Jacob ben Yakar**, whom Rashi repeatedly remembers with reverence. Jacob belonged to the scholarly tradition shaped by Rabbenu Gershom.

After Jacob’s death, Rashi studied under **Isaac ben Eliezer ha-Levi**, also called Isaac Segan Lewiyah. He learned not only texts but the rulings, liturgy, and customary practice of the Worms community.

### Mainz

Rashi also studied in Mainz under **Isaac ben Judah**, head of the academy there and later at Worms. Other associated teachers or senior scholars included **Solomon ben Samson**, apparently a relative.

### Relationship to Rabbenu Gershom

Rabbenu Gershom ben Judah died before, or near the beginning of, Rashi’s childhood. He was therefore not Rashi’s personal teacher. Rashi belonged to his intellectual lineage through Gershom’s pupils and the Mainz-Worms academies.

### Conditions of study

Later accounts say Rashi studied in poverty, leaving his wife in Troyes and returning periodically. Poverty is plausible but not securely documented. Medieval academies were generally small circles centered on a master rather than large, endowed institutions.

Rashi copied traditions, textual variants, legal decisions, and explanations associated with his teachers. His later writings retain disagreement as well as deference: he quotes teachers respectfully but does not follow them mechanically.

---

## Return to Troyes and Establishment of His School, c. 1065–1070

Rashi returned permanently to Troyes by approximately 1065–1070. The often-repeated date **1070** for founding his school is a reconstruction, not a dated foundation charter.

His *beit midrash* began as a small household-centered circle. A manuscript report that his maidservant purchased flour “for the young men” during a festival supports the picture of students boarding or eating in his household.

As the Rhineland teachers died, Troyes became an increasingly important center. Students came from northern France and possibly more distant communities. The school was not a salaried university and apparently had no permanent institutional building of the later kind.

### Students and close collaborators

Among those associated with Rashi’s school were:

- **Shemaiah of Soissons**, student, secretary, textual transmitter, and glossator;
- **Simḥah ben Samuel of Vitry**, compiler of *Maḥzor Vitry*;
- **Judah ben Nathan (Rivan)**, student and son-in-law;
- **Joseph Kara**, biblical exegete, associate, and younger contemporary;
- **Jacob ben Samson**;
- **Judah ben Abraham**;
- **Joseph ben Judah**;
- Rashi’s grandsons, especially **Rashbam**, who reportedly discussed exegetical method with him late in his life.

The relationship between Rashi and his students was collaborative. Students copied oral rulings, recorded customs, supplied manuscript readings, inserted glosses, and sometimes completed unfinished commentaries. This makes the boundary between Rashi’s own final text and his school’s recension difficult to draw.

## Livelihood, Property, and Financial Position

### The vintner tradition

Rashi is routinely described as a winegrower or vintner.

**Evidence for the reconstruction:**

- His writings display detailed familiarity with wine presses, barrels, fermentation, vineyard practice, and commerce.
- Responsa discuss Jewish and Christian handling of wine and commercial transactions.
- Jews in medieval northern France participated in viticulture and wine trade.
- Later biographies consistently connect Rashi to wine.

**Evidence against treating it as established fact:**

- No surviving deed, tax record, property inventory, or contemporary narrative calls Rashi a vineyard owner or vintner.
- Haym Soloveitchik and Mayer Gruber have emphasized that technical knowledge can derive from community life and legal consultation.
- Vineyards near Troyes existed, but local conditions do not prove Rashi owned one.
- Some relevant responsa concern other people’s barrels or vineyards.

**Assessment:** “Vintner” is a plausible traditional reconstruction, not a documented occupation. The more cautious description is that Rashi was familiar with, and issued rulings concerning, a wine-producing commercial society.

### Rabbinic income

No evidence shows that Rashi received a formal salary as rabbi. He likely exercised communal authority without holding a modern salaried office.

### Wealth

No reliable estimate survives. His household could accommodate students and servants, indicating some resources, but this does not establish wealth. Stories of severe lifelong poverty and stories of substantial vineyards are both unproved.

### Businesses, investments, patents, or financial structures

None is documented. Patents and corporations in the modern sense are inapplicable. His responsa document commercial relationships, debts, deposits, agency, partnership, inheritance, wine, animals, and relations with Christian traders, but usually as legal cases rather than his own investments.

## Biblical Commentary

Rashi wrote commentaries on the Torah and most of the Hebrew Bible. The precise order and dating remain uncertain.

### Method

His frequently cited programmatic statement says that he came to explain:

- the “plain sense” or contextual meaning, *peshuto shel miqra*;
- and aggadah that resolves difficulties in the wording.

His work is therefore neither purely literal nor merely a digest of Midrash. Its characteristic procedure is to identify a textual difficulty, supply a concise solution, and quote or reshape rabbinic tradition when it answers that difficulty.

Principal features include:

- attention to grammar, syntax, word order, and narrative sequence;
- close comparison of parallel verses;
- selective quotation of Midrash rather than indiscriminate reproduction;
- use of Old French glosses, usually introduced by *belaʿaz* (“in the vernacular”);
- explanations of objects, crafts, animals, plants, law, and social practice;
- willingness to say “I do not know” where no explanation satisfied him;
- pedagogical compression.

Approximately 3,000 Old French words appear in the commentarial corpus. They are valuable evidence for the history of medieval French, although scribal corruption complicates their reconstruction.

### Sources and influences

Rashi drew on:

- the Babylonian Talmud;
- Targum Onkelos and other Aramaic translations;
- Genesis Rabbah and other classical Midrash;
- *Seder Olam*;
- the Masorah;
- liturgical and exegetical traditions from Mainz and Worms;
- earlier northern French and Italian Jewish learning;
- Gaonic literature.

He did not ordinarily name every source and frequently condensed or altered a Midrash to fit the verse.

### Revision and textual growth

Medieval reports say Rashi revised some explanations. Modern manuscript research, however, does not support a simple theory of several complete authorial editions. Much variation arose through:

- students’ marginal additions;
- explanatory expansions;
- alternative readings;
- corrections;
- material from Joseph Kara and other exegetes;
- printing-house harmonization.

The Leipzig 1 manuscript, copied by a student of Rashi’s secretary Shemaiah, is especially important for distinguishing Rashi’s text from school glosses.

### Authorship problems in the biblical corpus

Rashi’s Torah commentary is substantially authentic, although individual glosses remain disputed.

Commentaries printed under his name on parts of the later biblical books are composite or non-Rashian. Scholarship has particularly questioned or rejected attribution of:

- Chronicles;
- Ezra and Nehemiah;
- portions of Job;
- portions of other Writings and prophetic books.

The Library of Congress manuscript description explicitly notes additions and sections by other authors, chiefly Joseph Kara.

## Talmud Commentary

Rashi’s commentary on the Babylonian Talmud was his most consequential scholarly work. It explains vocabulary, identifies speakers, reconstructs omitted argumentative steps, defines legal concepts, and gives the reader enough grammar and context to follow the discussion.

### Textual function

Rashi did more than explain a fixed Talmudic text. He selected readings and occasionally emended wording. Some of his preferred readings subsequently entered printed Talmuds, making it difficult to separate the Talmudic text available to Rashi from the text adjusted under his influence.

### Earlier foundations

He drew on commentaries and oral explanations of:

- Jacob ben Yakar;
- Isaac ha-Levi;
- Isaac ben Judah;
- Mainz and Worms traditions;
- Gaonic responsa and commentaries.

His commentary largely displaced earlier Ashkenazic Talmud commentaries, many of which subsequently disappeared.

### Incompletion and disputed attribution

The traditional printed corpus is not entirely by Rashi.

- He did not comment on Mishnah tractates lacking Babylonian Gemara in the same manner.
- The commentary printed on **Avot** is not his.
- The printed commentary on **Nedarim** is not his.
- The printed commentaries on **Nazir** and **Taʿanit** are generally rejected as his.
- Attribution of **Moʿed Katan** is disputed; an authentic alternative commentary has been identified and published.
- Parts of **Bava Batra** were not completed by him.
- The end of **Makkot** was completed by Judah ben Nathan.
- Several other tractates contain student continuations, recensions, or interpolations.
- The textual history of **Ḥullin**, **Sukkah**, **Ketubbot**, **Bava Kamma**, **Sanhedrin**, and **Zevahim** is complex.

The resulting printed “Rashi” should therefore be understood as an authorial corpus transmitted through an active school, not as a mechanically preserved autograph.

## Responsa, Halakhah, and Communal Activity

Approximately several hundred rulings and responsa are attributed to Rashi, but counts vary because of duplication, uncertain attribution, and incorporation into school collections.

Subjects include:

- marriage, divorce, levirate obligations, and inheritance;
- women’s property and ritual practice;
- commercial agency, debts, deposits, and partnership;
- kosher wine and commercial contact with Christians;
- synagogue ritual and prayer;
- mourning;
- festival observance;
- communal taxation and authority;
- disputed rabbinic rulings;
- treatment of apostates and coerced converts.

A surviving communal text associated with Troyes records Rashi’s refusal to use excommunication casually in a financial dispute. Such materials present him as a working legal authority embedded in ordinary community conflict.

### Women

Grossman argues from Rashi’s rulings and exegesis that he sometimes limited expansive claims of husbands’ control and showed concern for women placed at legal disadvantage. This is an interpretation of dispersed rulings, not a systematic theory written by Rashi.

His daughters’ learned household environment is well supported. More expansive modern claims—that they functioned as formally ordained rabbis, wrote anonymous tractate commentaries, or routinely wore tefillin—are not documented.

### Converts and apostates

The First Crusade produced forced conversions. Rashi’s school participated in the halakhic problem of receiving those who returned to Judaism. The surviving material generally treats coerced apostasy differently from voluntary abandonment. Individual attributions must be checked because later school collections intermingle authorities.

## The First Crusade, 1095–1099

Pope Urban II proclaimed the First Crusade in 1095. In 1096 crusading bands and local attackers devastated Jewish communities in Speyer, Worms, Mainz, Cologne, and elsewhere.

Rashi was in Troyes, not at the principal massacre sites. He likely lost teachers’ families, friends, relatives, or correspondents, but precise names are not securely recoverable.

### Silence and possible allusion

**Documented fact:** No undisputed prose passage by Rashi explicitly narrates the 1096 massacres.

**Scholarly interpretation:** Some researchers detect Crusade-era suffering in his comments on Isaiah, Psalms, and other texts, especially depictions of Israel as a persecuted servant. Others warn that such readings can be explained from older Jewish exegetical traditions and cannot be securely dated to 1096.

### Liturgical poetry

Penitential poems and laments are attributed to Rashi, some traditionally connected with the massacres. Attribution and dating vary. They should not automatically be treated as eyewitness reports.

## Intellectual, Religious, and Social Outlook

Rashi left no philosophical treatise or systematic creed. His outlook must be reconstructed from commentary, responsa, and poetry.

### Torah and rabbinic tradition

Torah study occupies the central place in his intellectual world. He regarded sages and transmitters of learning as essential to Jewish communal continuity.

### Innovation and conservatism

He was conservative in accepting the authority of scripture, Talmud, Midrash, and inherited custom, but innovative in pedagogy:

- he made difficult texts navigable;
- selected rather than merely accumulated Midrash;
- used vernacular explanation;
- clarified logical structure;
- produced texts usable by non-specialist learners.

### Peshat

Rashi’s *peshat* was not modern historical criticism. It sought contextual sense while assuming the unity and sanctity of scripture. Rashbam later claimed that Rashi acknowledged the need for renewed contextual readings and would have revised more had he lived longer. The report is important but comes through a grandson advocating a more strongly contextual method.

### Christianity and polemic

Rashi lived in an overwhelmingly Christian environment and knew Christian neighbors through commerce and daily life.

Scholarly positions differ:

- Earlier scholarship often read numerous comments—especially on Genesis, Psalms, Isaiah, and the Song of Songs—as deliberate anti-Christian polemic.
- More recent studies distinguish explicit polemic from interpretation that could serve a polemical purpose without having been composed primarily for that purpose.
- His collective interpretation of the servant in Isaiah 52–53 as Israel has figured prominently in Jewish-Christian debate.
- The claim that Rashi invented the collective Jewish reading of Isaiah 53 is false: collective and righteous-Israel interpretations predate him.
- The related modern claim that Jews removed a “forbidden chapter” from synagogue reading because of Christian missionary pressure is unsupported; the haftarah cycle and non-messianic readings cannot be reduced to Rashi’s influence.

### Non-Jews

His legal writings reflect practical trade and social interaction with Christians alongside religious boundary maintenance. Terms later rendered simply as “gentile,” “idolater,” or “Christian” can have distinct textual histories, and some printed passages were altered by Christian censorship.

## Language, Material Culture, and Technical Knowledge

Rashi’s comments preserve descriptions of:

- agriculture and viticulture;
- barrels, presses, ovens, mills, tools, textiles, dyes, and buildings;
- domestic labor;
- trade and transport;
- animal and plant names;
- Old French vocabulary.

This material is historically valuable but must be used cautiously. Some explanations derive from rabbinic tradition rather than observation, and medieval zoological or geographical identifications do not necessarily correspond to modern taxonomy.

There is no evidence that Rashi invented a technology or held anything analogous to a patent.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Teachers

- **Jacob ben Yakar:** principal early teacher at Worms; treated with exceptional reverence.
- **Isaac ben Eliezer ha-Levi:** teacher at Worms and transmitter of community customs.
- **Isaac ben Judah:** teacher at Mainz/Worms.
- **Solomon ben Samson:** senior scholar, teacher, and possible relative.

### Students and transmitters

- **Shemaiah of Soissons:** secretary, pupil, glossator, and major conduit of Rashi’s text.
- **Simḥah of Vitry:** student or close colleague; compiled material into *Maḥzor Vitry*.
- **Judah ben Nathan:** student, son-in-law, and continuator of the Talmud commentary.
- **Joseph Kara:** biblical exegete in Rashi’s circle whose interpretations entered some manuscripts.
- **Rashbam:** grandson, exegete, and critical continuer of the family tradition.
- **Rabbenu Tam:** grandson; never merely repeated Rashi and became a formidable independent Tosafist and legal authority.

### Rivals and opponents

No personal enemy is securely documented. Rashi reports disagreements with teachers and contemporaries, but the later scholarly opposition to his commentary was mostly posthumous.

### Patrons

No secular or ecclesiastical patron is known. There is no evidence that a count of Champagne financed his work.

## Controversies, Criticism, Allegations, and Adversarial Findings

A dedicated search was conducted using combinations of Rashi’s name with “controversy,” “scandal,” “allegations,” “lawsuit,” “fraud,” “accusations,” “conspiracy,” and “criticism,” including results from 2024–2026.

Because Rashi died in 1105, modern criminal, corporate, or financial categories largely produce false matches to living people named Rashi. No credible source revealed a lawsuit, criminal charge, fraud investigation, corruption inquiry, intelligence relationship, money-laundering allegation, sexual scandal, or political-violence allegation against Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki.

The meaningful contested matters are textual, theological, historical, and genealogical.

### 1. Authorship inflation

**Documented textual problem:** Works and commentaries not written by Rashi circulated under his name. This includes non-authentic tractate commentaries and portions of biblical commentary.

**Cause:** manuscript anonymity, school transmission, marginal glosses entering the text, reverence for Rashi’s authority, and early printing practices.

**Assessment:** This is not evidence that Rashi committed fraud. It is a posthumous attributional process.

### 2. “Rashi script”

**Popular misconception:** Rashi wrote in the semi-cursive typeface now called Rashi script.

**Finding:** The typeface was developed and standardized by Sephardic and Italian Hebrew printers centuries after his death to distinguish commentary from the square biblical or Talmudic text. It is not his handwriting.

### 3. Harsh criticism by later Jewish readers

Rashi’s canonical status was not uncontested.

- Rationalist readers criticized his reliance on Midrash.
- Some Sephardic and eastern Mediterranean scholars called particular explanations implausible or childish.
- Pseudo-Rabad composed strictures against the Rashi-rabbinic exegetical axis.
- Aaron Aboulrabi derided some interpretations as “girls’ fantasies” while accepting others as valuable Midrash.
- Abraham ibn Ezra, Ramban, Rashbam, and later supercommentators disagreed with individual readings, sometimes sharply.

Eric Lawee documents that Rashi’s eventual canonical position emerged through debate and resistance rather than unanimous acceptance.

### 4. Effects on the Talmudic text

**Scholarly criticism:** Rashi’s textual emendations and the incorporation of his preferred readings into printed Talmuds can obscure older variants.

**Finding:** His commentary is indispensable evidence, but it is not a neutral window onto a fixed original Talmud. Modern manuscript criticism sometimes rejects the received wording influenced by Rashi.

### 5. Christian censorship and textual alteration

From the later Middle Ages into the print era, ecclesiastical censorship affected Hebrew books. Polemical references were deleted, euphemized, or altered. Consequently, the absence or wording of an anti-Christian passage in a printed Rashi cannot always be projected back to the eleventh century.

No evidence shows Rashi himself participated in deception; the controversy concerns later transmission.

### 6. Scientific and zoological accuracy

Modern readers sometimes treat Rashi’s descriptions of cosmology, anatomy, plants, or animals as scientific propositions and identify errors.

**Assessment:** The comments combine observation, vernacular identification, Talmudic interpretation, and medieval natural lore. They are historically situated explanations, not a systematic scientific work. Disagreement over their factual accuracy is real, but allegations that one disputed identification invalidates the corpus are theological or polemical conclusions rather than historical findings.

### 7. Anti-Christian intent

**Disputed scholarly claim:** Rashi’s pursuit of *peshat* was primarily a defense against Christian allegory.

**Evidence for:** Some comments answer readings central to Christian proof-texting; Jewish-Christian controversy shaped his environment.

**Evidence against:** Much of his method develops internal Jewish exegetical and pedagogical needs, and no direct Christian target is named in many supposedly polemical passages.

**Assessment:** Explicit polemic exists or is probable in some places; a single anti-Christian explanation for the entire project is excessive.

### 8. Treatment of women

Modern discussions alternately present Rashi as unusually supportive of women or as a transmitter of patriarchal law.

**Documented basis:** His rulings operated within medieval rabbinic law, while some decisions limited husbands’ claims or protected women’s interests.

**Assessment:** Neither a modern feminist identity nor a blanket misogynist characterization is a documentary category supplied by Rashi himself.

### 9. No recent scandal

The 2024–2026 adversarial search produced recent work on anti-Christian readings, manuscript attribution, the daughters-and-tefillin legend, and modern reception. It did not uncover a new primary document establishing criminal or financial misconduct.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

### The jewel cast into the sea

**Story:** Rashi’s father possessed or found a precious jewel sought by Christians for use in an idol. Rather than enable idolatry, he threw it into the sea. A heavenly voice promised him a son who would illuminate Israel.

**Appearance and circulation:** Later Jewish hagiography and popular biographies.

**Evidence:** No contemporary evidence.

**Reputational function:** Portrays Rashi’s birth as divine reward for paternal self-sacrifice and rejection of idolatry.

### The opening wall in Worms

**Story:** While pregnant, Rashi’s mother was nearly crushed by a carriage in a narrow Worms street. A wall miraculously opened to shelter her, leaving a niche.

**Evidence against:** Rashi was born in Troyes; no reliable source places his pregnant mother in Worms. The physical “Rashi wall” and associated locations were identified long after his lifetime.

**Effect:** Created a sacred geography in Worms and connected Rashi’s birth to the city where he later studied.

### Rashi’s house, chair, chapel, and synagogue in Worms

**Story:** A building or room in Worms was Rashi’s personal school.

**Finding:** The building traditionally called his study house is of much later construction, especially associated with sixteenth-century rebuilding. It commemorates his presence as a student but is not an intact eleventh-century classroom.

### Extensive travels through Europe, Asia, and Africa

**Story:** Rashi traveled through Prague, Spain, North Africa, and the Near East.

**Evidence:** His securely recoverable travel consists essentially of Troyes–Rhineland movement. The grand tour lacks contemporary support.

**Effect:** Enlarged him from regional scholar into universal wandering sage.

### Meeting Maimonides

**Story:** Rashi met Maimonides or Rashi’s father consulted him.

**Evidence against:** Maimonides was born in 1138, thirty-three years after Rashi’s death.

**Classification:** Chronologically impossible legend.

### Marriage in Prague and burial there

**Story:** Rashi married, died, or was buried in Prague.

**Evidence against:** The strongest evidence places his life and death in Troyes. Rabbenu Tam described Troyes as the city of the ancestral graves.

### Encounter with Godfrey of Bouillon

**Story:** Crusade leader Godfrey of Bouillon consulted Rashi before departing. Rashi predicted that Godfrey would return with only three horses; Godfrey returned with four, but a falling stone killed one as he entered the city, fulfilling the prediction.

**Evidence:** No contemporary source; it resembles common prophet-and-ruler legends.

**Effect:** Converts Rashi’s vulnerability during the Crusade era into prophetic superiority over a Christian warlord.

### Barcelona and the destined companion in paradise

**Story:** A dream sent Rashi to Barcelona to find the person destined to sit beside him in paradise.

**Evidence:** Late devotional legend with no documentary confirmation.

### His daughters wore tefillin

**Story:** Rashi’s daughters were so learned that they wore tefillin.

**Evidence for:** Medieval Ashkenaz knew some women who voluntarily performed time-bound commandments; Rabbenu Tam permitted women to bless over certain voluntary commandments.

**Evidence against:** No medieval source says Rashi’s daughters wore tefillin. The story appears to be modern or very late. Reasoning backward from Rabbenu Tam’s legal view to his mother’s practice is not evidence.

**Effect:** The legend became symbolically important in modern arguments about women’s ritual participation.

### His daughters wrote the commentary on Nedarim

**Story:** The non-Rashian commentary printed on Nedarim was composed by his daughters.

**Evidence:** No manuscript attribution supports the claim.

**Classification:** Modern legend derived from the genuine facts that the commentary is not Rashi’s and that his daughters were learned.

### Dictation to a daughter

A responsum has been read as saying that Rashi, weakened by illness, dictated to “my daughter.”

**Textual dispute:** Scholars have proposed reading “the son of my daughter,” meaning his grandson. David Golinkin argues that the matter should not be declared closed without examining the manuscripts and notes that learned medieval Jewish women did transmit law.

**Assessment:** The received reading is disputed; it cannot securely prove that a daughter served as his scribe.

### The final word “pure”

**Story:** Rashi died while commenting on Makkot 19b, and his final word was *tahor*, “pure.”

**Evidence:** The commentary breaks off and was completed by Rivan, but the symbolic death-at-the-word story is later embellishment.

### Flies never entered his former home

**Story:** Troyes butcher shops erected where Rashi’s house had stood were miraculously free of flies.

**Evidence:** Reported as local folklore centuries later.

### Descent of innumerable families

Because Rashi’s daughters married into prolific rabbinic families, many genuine lines of descent are historically plausible. Claims that almost every Ashkenazic rabbinic family, or millions of named individuals, can document an unbroken line to him generally exceed the surviving records.

## Illness, Aging, and Death

Late responsa suggest physical weakness that sometimes required dictation, although the identity of the scribe is disputed.

No securely documented diagnosis, addiction, psychological crisis, serious accident, or cause of death survives.

He died on Thursday, 29 Tammuz 4865, probably 13 July 1105, at about sixty-five. A student notice describes him as having been “summoned to the heavenly yeshiva.” The literal medical cause is unknown.

His grave was probably in the medieval Jewish cemetery at Troyes. Its location disappeared through later destruction and urban change. A modern memorial marks his association with the city but is not an authenticated tomb.

## Works by or Attributed to Rashi

Dates below are approximate composition periods; most texts circulated in manuscript before print.

### Secure or substantially secure works

1. **פירוש רש״י על התורה — *Perush Rashi ʿal ha-Torah***  
   Commentary on the Pentateuch; composed and revised during the later eleventh century. Substantially genuine, with numerous later glosses and textual variants.

2. **Commentaries on most of the Hebrew Bible**  
   Commentaries on the Former and Latter Prophets and much of the Writings. Authenticity varies by book and passage.

3. **פירוש רש״י על התלמוד הבבלי — Commentary on the Babylonian Talmud**  
   Composed over much of his mature life. Authentic core extensive; incomplete in places and supplemented or replaced by student and later commentaries.

4. **תשובות רש״י — *Teshuvot Rashi***  
   Responsa written or dictated in individual cases, not assembled by Rashi as a single book. Modern editions collect material preserved across manuscripts and school compilations.

5. **Piyyutim and seliḥot**  
   Liturgical poems survive under his name. Individual attribution and Crusade-era dating require case-by-case evaluation.

6. **Commentaries on liturgical poems**  
   Some explanations of *piyyut* are attributed to him or transmitted through his school.

### Works of the school, compilations, or disputed attribution

1. **מחזור ויטרי — *Maḥzor Vitry***  
   Compiled principally by Simḥah ben Samuel of Vitry. Contains Rashi’s rulings, customs, and responsa alongside earlier and later material. Not authored wholesale by Rashi.

2. **ספר הפרדס — *Sefer ha-Pardes***  
   Halakhic-liturgical compilation from Rashi’s school, built partly from his rulings. Attribution of the entire book to him is inaccurate.

3. **ספר האורה — *Sefer ha-Orah***  
   School compilation transmitting rulings associated with Rashi; not a securely author-written monograph.

4. **סידור רש״י — *Siddur Rashi***  
   Prayer-law compilation by an unidentified student or school compiler, containing traditions and responsa from Rashi.

5. **Likkutei ha-Pardes**  
   Derivative compilation related to the *Pardes* tradition.

6. **Commentary on Pirkei Avot**  
   Printed under Rashi’s name but not accepted as his.

7. **Commentary printed on Nedarim**  
   Not Rashi’s.

8. **Printed commentaries on Nazir and Taʿanit**  
   Generally not accepted as his.

9. **Commentary on Moʿed Katan**  
   Attribution disputed; the standard printed commentary is not securely his.

10. **Commentaries on Ezra, Nehemiah, and Chronicles**  
    Largely or wholly non-Rashian in the form traditionally printed under his name.

11. **Portions of Job and other later biblical books**  
    Composite, continued, or disputed.

### Early printing history

- **Reggio di Calabria, 1475:** Rashi’s Torah commentary, printed by Abraham ben Garton. It is the first dated printed Hebrew book known, although earlier undated Hebrew printing may have existed.
- **Bologna, 1482:** Torah with Rashi in the margin.
- **Bologna, c. 1484:** Five Scrolls with commentary.
- **Naples, 1487:** Five Scrolls, Daniel, Ezra, and Nehemiah.
- **Venice, 1517 and 1525:** Major Rabbinic Bible editions.
- **Venice, 1520–1523:** Daniel Bomberg’s complete Babylonian Talmud established the enduring page architecture with Gemara centrally placed and Rashi and Tosafot around it.

“Rashi script” is a printer’s type, not an authorial script.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Tosafist inheritance

Rashi’s daughters, sons-in-law, students, and grandsons created the immediate institutional bridge to the Tosafists. Tosafot frequently question, qualify, or reject Rashi while presupposing his explanation as the starting point.

### Canonization

By the thirteenth century, studying the weekly Torah portion with Rashi could be treated by some authorities as fulfilling the traditional requirement to read it with an explanatory translation. More than 150 supercommentaries were eventually written on his Torah commentary.

### Influence on Jewish education

For centuries, Rashi became the first major commentator encountered by children learning Torah and the constant guide for advanced Talmud study. His language entered sermons, legal argument, literature, and ordinary Jewish speech.

### Influence on Christian scholarship

Christian Hebraists consulted and translated Rashi, sometimes through polemical intermediaries such as Raymond Martini’s *Pugio Fidei*. Nicholas of Lyra and later Christian exegetes drew, directly or indirectly, on Jewish interpretation associated with Rashi. Complete or partial Latin translations appeared in the early-modern period, including Johann Friedrich Breithaupt’s eighteenth-century edition.

### Printing and page design

The visual organization of the standard Talmud page made Rashi physically inseparable from the text for generations of readers. This layout amplified both his authority and the danger of confusing commentary, emendation, and base text.

### Linguistic legacy

His Old French glosses are a major corpus for Romance philology and the history of Jewish French.

### Modern religious interpretation

Hasidic, Orthodox, Conservative, Reform, academic, feminist, literary, and Christian readers have all appropriated Rashi differently. The Lubavitcher Rebbe’s extensive public talks on Rashi popularized a theory that every comment answers a difficulty perceptible to a five-year-old beginning scripture.

### Historical reinterpretation

Modern scholarship has progressively replaced the solitary-genius portrait with that of:

- a product of Mainz-Worms learning;
- leader of a household school;
- an author revised by students and copyists;
- a scholar working in a Jewish-Christian commercial environment;
- the center of a family and textual network.

His canonical status remains immense, but manuscript studies have made attribution more exact and exposed the diversity obscured by printed editions.

The atlas roster contains many figures separated from Rashi by centuries or connected only through later intellectual reception. Mere shared subject matter, geography, or inclusion in Jewish tradition has not been counted.

### Maimonides

- **Nature:** Supposed personal meeting in later legend.
- **Dates and places claimed:** Versions place an encounter during one of Rashi’s fabulous journeys, sometimes in Spain or elsewhere.
- **Label:** **Myth; chronologically impossible.**
- **Evidence:** Rashi died in 1105; Maimonides was born in 1138.
- **Historical importance:** The story symbolically joins the two most influential medieval Jewish explicators of Bible, Talmud, and law.

A genuine posthumous intellectual relationship exists: Maimonidean rationalist culture became one pole in later disputes over Rashi’s Midrash-centered Torah commentary. This was a reception-history conflict, not personal contact.

### Saadia Gaon

- **Nature:** Earlier Gaonic intellectual influence mediated through Gaonic literature and the Franco-German academies.
- **Dates and places:** Saadia died in 942, about a century before Rashi’s birth; no meeting was possible.
- **Label:** **Documented intellectual transmission in general; exact direct borrowing requires passage-by-passage demonstration.**
- **Evidence:** Rashi used Gaonic explanations, responsa, and textual traditions. The degree of his direct access to particular works of Saadia is less certain than his dependence on the broader Gaonic inheritance.

### Abraham, Moses, David ben Jesse, King Solomon, and other biblical persons on the roster

- **Nature:** Subjects of Rashi’s biblical commentary and rabbinic interpretation.
- **Label:** **Documented textual/exegetical relationship, not a biographical crossing.**
- These figures were central to Rashi’s intellectual world, but they cannot be presented as historical meetings, correspondence, or social relationships.

### William the Conqueror

- **Nature:** Contemporary European ruler.
- **Label:** **No documented connection.**
- No source consulted establishes a meeting, correspondence, patronage, political relationship, or acknowledged influence between William and Rashi.
