## Works by Aristotle

### Surviving logical and methodological treatises

- *Categories*. A compact classificatory treatise transmitted as the opening of the *Organon* (Bekker 1a–15b); its Aristotelian authorship has repeatedly been questioned but remains defensible.
- *On Interpretation*. A treatise on statements, contradiction, and modality (16a–24b); already in antiquity its authenticity was disputed.
- *Prior Analytics*. A two-book treatise establishing Aristotle’s theory of syllogistic inference (24a–70b).
- *Posterior Analytics*. A two-book treatise on demonstration, scientific knowledge, definition, and first principles (71a–100b).
- *Topics*. An eight-book protocol for constructing and testing dialectical arguments from generally accepted premises (100a–164b).
- *Sophistical Refutations*. A treatise identifying fallacious arguments and prescribing methods for answering them (164a–184b); transmitted as the ninth book of the *Topics*.

### Surviving natural philosophy, psychology, and zoology

- *Physics* (*Lectures on Nature*). An eight-book assemblage on change, causation, nature, place, time, infinity, and motion (184a–267b); its present unity is editorial rather than that of a conventionally composed book.
- *On the Heavens*. A four-book cosmological treatise on celestial bodies, the terrestrial elements, and the structure of the universe (268a–313b).
- *On Generation and Corruption*. A two-book investigation of coming-to-be, destruction, elemental transformation, and mixture (314a–338b).
- *Meteorology*. A four-book collection on atmospheric, terrestrial, and related phenomena (338a–390b); all or part of Book IV is often treated as non-Aristotelian or as a separate work.
- *On the Soul*. A three-book inquiry into soul as the form and actuality of living bodies, including sensation and intellect (402a–435b).
- *On Sense and Sensible Objects*. A short physiological-psychological treatise on the senses (436a–449a), later grouped within the *Parva Naturalia*.
- *On Memory and Recollection*. A short treatise distinguishing memory from deliberate recollection (449a–453b).
- *On Sleep and Waking*. A short treatise explaining sleep and waking as conditions of embodied perceivers (453a–458a).
- *On Dreams*. A short inquiry into dream images and their bodily causes (458a–462b).
- *On Divination in Sleep*. A skeptical inquiry into whether dreams can foretell events (462b–464b).
- *On Length and Shortness of Life*. A short biological treatise on longevity and mortality (464b–467b).
- *On Youth and Old Age, Life and Death*. A compact biological treatise whose paired titles may describe sections rather than independently made works (467a–470b).
- *On Respiration*. A biological investigation of breathing, bodily heat, and the heart (470b–480b).
- *History of Animals*. A large empirical and classificatory zoological project transmitted in ten books (486a–638b); Book X is generally rejected as non-Aristotelian, and the surviving arrangement may incorporate distinct research collections.
- *Parts of Animals*. A four-book explanatory study of animal anatomy and the purposes served by bodily parts (639a–697b).
- *Movement of Animals*. A short theoretical investigation of the common principles of animal movement (698a–704b).
- *Progression of Animals*. A study of animal locomotion and bodily organization, also translated as *Gait of Animals* (704a–714b).
- *Generation of Animals*. A five-book biological treatise on reproduction, heredity, embryology, and sexual differentiation (715a–789b).

### Surviving metaphysics, ethics, politics, rhetoric, and poetics

- *Metaphysics*. A fourteen-book assemblage concerning being, substance, causation, actuality, and divine intellect (980a–1093b). The title and present architecture are post-Aristotelian editorial constructions, and several books originated as relatively independent projects.
- *Nicomachean Ethics*. A ten-book practical-philosophical course on happiness, character, action, justice, friendship, and contemplation (1094a–1181b). Its relation to Nicomachus and the extent of later editorial intervention are uncertain.
- *Eudemian Ethics*. An eight-book ethical course associated in transmission with Eudemus (1214a–1249b); three books are also transmitted within the *Nicomachean Ethics*, so the boundaries of the two works resist clean separation.
- *Politics*. An eight-book investigation of constitutions, citizenship, political communities, education, and the best practicable regimes (1252a–1342b); its book order and completeness remain disputed.
- *Rhetoric*. A three-book protocol and analysis of civic persuasion, proofs, emotion, character, and style (1354a–1420b).
- *Poetics*. The surviving portion of a project on mimetic arts, chiefly tragedy and epic (1447a–1462b). Ancient evidence and internal references indicate that the work once extended further, probably including a treatment of comedy, so the extant text is incomplete.

### Constitutional and documentary research

- *Constitution of the Athenians*. A historical and institutional account of Athens, preserved principally on papyrus and absent from Bekker’s original corpus. It belongs to the Lyceum’s constitutional project; whether Aristotle personally composed it, supervised it, or delegated it within his school remains disputed.
- *Constitutions of Cities*. A collaborative research project collecting accounts of Greek and non-Greek political systems. Ancient catalogues give incompatible totals—158 or 171—and only the Athenian constitution survives substantially, so the project cannot safely be reduced to 158 individually authored books.
- *Olympic Victors*. A chronological documentary compilation, reportedly made with Callisthenes; it is lost except for testimonia.
- *Pythian Victors*. A documentary list of victors at the Pythian games, now lost.
- *Victors in the Musical Contests at the Pythian Games*. A specialized historical register, now lost; ancient catalogue wording may overlap with *Pythian Victors*.
- *Victories at the City Dionysia*. A documentary record of dramatic victories, now lost.
- *Didascaliae* (*Records of Dramatic Performances*). A research compilation recording theatrical productions and performers, preserved only through fragments and later use.
- *On Tragedies*. A lost documentary or historical study of tragic drama; its relation to the *Didascaliae* is uncertain.
- *List of Plays*. A lost dramatic catalogue; it may have belonged to the same theatrical research enterprise rather than being a separate authored treatise.
- *Proverbs*. A lost collection of proverbial expressions, better understood as a research compilation than a continuous book.
- *Customs or Decrees of Cities*. A lost documentary compilation whose ancient title is transmitted in variant forms.
- *Laws*. A four-book collection or comparative study of laws, now lost.
- *Law of Assembly*. A lost work of uncertain character, possibly a protocol or documentary constitutional text rather than discursive philosophy.

### Lost dialogues and public-facing works

- *On Justice*. A four-book dialogue or exoteric work, surviving only in fragments and reports.
- *On Poets*. A three-book dialogue or literary-critical work, now fragmentary.
- *On Philosophy*. A three-book dialogue on philosophical history and first principles, reconstructible only conjecturally from fragments.
- *On the Statesman*. A two-book dialogue, now lost.
- *Gryllus, or On Rhetoric*. A lost dialogue traditionally associated with Aristotle’s early criticism of rhetorical handbooks.
- *Nerinthus*. A lost dialogue whose subject and even title remain obscure.
- *Sophist*. A lost dialogue, not to be confused with Plato’s work of the same translated title.
- *Menexenus*. A lost dialogue; its relationship to Plato’s *Menexenus* is unknown.
- *Eroticus*. A lost dialogue or discourse on love.
- *Symposium*. A lost dialogue set in, or concerned with, a banquet.
- *On Wealth*. A lost dialogue or exoteric treatise.
- *Protrepticus*. A lost exhortation to philosophy, substantially but imperfectly reconstructed from later authors, especially Iamblichus. Its reconstructed text is a scholarly hypothesis rather than a surviving work.
- *On the Soul*. A lost exoteric dialogue distinct from the surviving technical treatise bearing the same title.
- *On Prayer*. A lost dialogue or exoteric work known from fragments.
- *On Noble Birth*. A lost dialogue investigating ancestry and nobility.
- *On Pleasure*. A lost dialogue or exoteric work; the ancient catalogues may preserve more than one Aristotelian project under this title.
- *Alexander, or On Colonists*. A lost work addressed to or framed around Alexander; its alternative title and genre remain uncertain.
- *On Kingship*. A lost political work, probably public-facing or advisory in character.
- *On Education*. A lost exoteric work on paideia.
- *On the Good*. A lost three-book engagement with Platonic first principles.
- *Extracts from Plato’s Laws*. A lost three-book epitome or research digest rather than an independently argued treatise.
- *Extracts from Plato’s Republic*. A lost two-book digest of Platonic material.
- *On Friendship*. A lost work whose relationship to the surviving ethical discussions of friendship cannot be established.
- *On Being Affected*. A lost work also transmitted under wording equivalent to *On Having Been Affected*.
- *On Sciences*. A lost epistemological work.
- *On the Better*. A lost work of uncertain form and subject.
- *On the Idea*. A lost critical engagement with the Platonic theory of Forms.
- *On the Choiceworthy and the Incidental*. A lost practical or dialectical work whose title is textually uncertain.
- *On Emotions, or On Anger*. A lost work preserved under alternative ancient titles.
- *On the Voluntary*. A lost ethical work; its catalogue title is sometimes translated *On Power* because of textual corruption.
- *On the Fine*. A lost ethical or aesthetic work.
- *On Pleasure* (second catalogue entry). A lost work separately listed in the ancient catalogue; it may duplicate the earlier dialogue rather than represent another made thing.

### Lost logical, dialectical, and methodological projects

- *Discussions*. A lost two-book dialectical work.
- *Solutions of Disputed Questions*. A lost two-book collection of resolutions or exercises.
- *Sophistical Divisions*. A lost four-book classification of sophistical arguments.
- *On Contraries*. A lost logical or metaphysical treatise.
- *On Species and Genera*. A lost classificatory treatise.
- *On Property*. A lost logical work on predicates peculiar to a subject.
- *Argumentative Commentaries*. A lost three-book collection of dialectical arguments.
- *Propositions Concerning Virtue*. A lost three-book collection of theses.
- *Objections*. A lost collection of dialectical counterarguments; later catalogues assign it differing lengths.
- *On Things Said in Many Ways*. A lost disambiguating lexicon or methodological treatise, also catalogued under a longer alternative title.
- *Ethics*. A lost five-book work whose relationship to the surviving ethical treatises is unknown.
- *On Elements*. A lost three-book investigation.
- *On Science*. A lost epistemological treatise.
- *On Principle*. A lost work on beginnings or first principles.
- *Divisions*. A large lost classificatory project listed as seventeen books by early catalogues and twenty-six by Ptolemy’s catalogue.
- *On Divisible Things*. A lost classificatory work that may duplicate an entry titled *Division*.
- *Questions and Answers*. A lost two-book dialectical protocol.
- *On Motion*. A lost work distinct from, or an antecedent of, discussions incorporated into the *Physics*.
- *Propositions*. A lost collection whose title recurs several times in the catalogues and may denote multiple notebooks.
- *Contentious Propositions*. A lost collection of eristic material.
- *Syllogisms*. A lost logical work; ancient catalogues list more than one collection under this title.
- *Earlier Analytics*. A lost nine-book or eight-book analytical project that cannot be identified straightforwardly with the surviving two-book *Prior Analytics*.
- *Greater Posterior Analytics*. A lost two-book analytical work, possibly related to the surviving *Posterior Analytics*.
- *On Problems*. A lost methodological collection, not securely identical with the transmitted pseudo-Aristotelian *Problems*.
- *Methodics*. A lost eight-book methodological project, sometimes associated with the material now called *Topics*.
- *Definitions for the Topics*. A lost seven-book definitional collection.
- *Syllogisms and Definitions*. A lost combined logical handbook.
- *Items before the Topics*. A lost preliminary manual.
- *Topics Concerning Definitions*. A lost two-book dialectical collection.
- *Affections*. A lost logical or psychological notebook; the title does not identify its subject securely.
- *Mathematical*. A lost methodological or philosophical work concerning mathematics.
- *Definitions*. A lost thirteen-book collection; later catalogues give sixteen books, suggesting revision, duplication, or catalogue corruption.
- *Epicheirematics*. Collections of dialectical arguments listed in two, thirty-nine, and single-book forms. These entries resist being treated as one securely bounded work.
- *Epicheirematic Theses*. A twenty-five-book argumentative collection.
- *Erotic Theses*. A four-book collection of disputational propositions concerning love.
- *Theses on Friendship*. A two-book dialectical collection.
- *Theses on the Soul*. A one-book dialectical collection.
- *Division According to Plato*. A lost six-book classificatory project attested by Ptolemy’s catalogue.
- *Division of Hypotheses*. A lost six-book methodological project.
- *On Axioms*. A lost logical work.
- *On the Defining Art*. A lost methodological treatise.
- *Instructions*. A lost four-book protocol of uncertain subject.
- *Notebooks*. Several ancient catalogue entries—of one, two, sixteen, and unspecified numbers of books—record working materials rather than clearly finished works. Their resistance to the category “work” is part of their status.

### Lost rhetoric and literary criticism

- *Collection of Arts*. A lost two-book survey or compilation of earlier rhetorical handbooks.
- *On the Art of Rhetoric*. A lost two-book rhetorical work whose relation to the surviving *Rhetoric* is uncertain.
- *Art*. A lost rhetorical handbook.
- *Another Art*. A lost two-book handbook distinguished by the catalogue from *Art* but otherwise unidentified.
- *Methodic*. A lost one-book rhetorical manual.
- *Compendium of the Art of Theodectes*. A lost digest of a rhetorical handbook associated with Theodectes; whether Aristotle wrote the underlying “art” is disputed.
- *Treatise on the Poetic Art*. A lost or partly preserved two-book project. The surviving *Poetics* may represent its first book, but the identification is not certain.
- *Rhetorical Enthymemes*. A lost collection of persuasive arguments.
- *On Magnitude*. A lost rhetorical work, possibly concerning amplification.
- *Divisions of Enthymemes*. A lost classificatory handbook.
- *On Style*. A lost two-book rhetorical treatise; some of its material may correspond to the surviving *Rhetoric* III.
- *On Advice*. A lost rhetorical work concerning deliberative speaking.
- *Compendium*. A lost two-book collection of uncertain contents.
- *Homeric Problems*. A lost multi-book project resolving difficulties in Homer; catalogues give six or ten books, and surviving fragments do not establish a single fixed title or extent.
- *On Poetry*. A lost literary-critical work distinct in the early catalogue from both *On Poets* and *Poetics*.

### Letters, poems, and personal texts

- *Letters to Philip*. A lost correspondence or ancient collection; individual authenticity cannot be established.
- *Letters of the Selymbrians*. A lost group of letters whose authorship and addressee structure are obscure.
- *Letters to Alexander*. Four letters in the early catalogue; later collections transmitted additional letters under Aristotle’s name, most or all of doubtful authenticity.
- *Letters to Antipater*. Nine letters in the early catalogue; surviving items attributed to this correspondence are textually and historically disputed.
- *Letter to Mentor*. A lost individual letter.
- *Letter to Ariston*. A lost individual letter.
- *Letter to Olympias*. A lost individual letter.
- *Letter to Hephaestion*. A lost individual letter.
- *Letter to Themistagoras*. A lost individual letter.
- *Letter to Philoxenus*. A lost individual letter.
- *Letter to Democritus*. A lost and chronologically implausible attribution, since Democritus belonged to an earlier generation.
- *Collected Letters*. Ancient catalogues mention collections in eight and twenty sections assembled by Artemon or Andronicus. These are editorial manifestations of correspondence, not securely Aristotle-made works.
- *Poems*. An ancient catalogue records a book of verse under Aristotle’s name; only fragments and titles survive, and attribution of individual poems is uncertain.
- *Elegies*. A separately catalogued book of elegiac verse, now fragmentary and of disputed extent.
- *Hymn to Virtue*. A lyric composition for Aristotle’s deceased friend Hermias, preserved through quotation and musical-hymnic tradition; its authorship is generally accepted.
- *Epigram for Hermias at Delphi*. A dedicatory epigram associated with Aristotle’s memorial for Hermias; it is an inscribed or inscription-like commemorative object rather than simply a “book.”
- *Will*. Aristotle’s testament is preserved by Diogenes Laertius. It is a juridical protocol that disposes of people and property and therefore acts rather than merely describes.

### Works transmitted under Aristotle’s name but generally rejected

- *On the Cosmos*. A cosmological-theological treatise transmitted in the corpus (390a–401b), generally dated after Aristotle.
- *On Breath*. A short physiological treatise (481a–486b), generally considered non-Aristotelian.
- *On Colours*. A treatise on colour and vision (791a–799b), probably produced within the Peripatetic tradition rather than by Aristotle.
- *On Things Heard*. A treatise on sound and acoustics (800a–804b), generally rejected as Aristotle’s work.
- *Physiognomonics*. A treatise correlating bodily appearance and character (805a–814b), probably assembled from more than one Peripatetic source.
- *On Plants*. The surviving botanical work (814a–830b) is a retroversion through Arabic and Latin of a work associated with Nicolaus of Damascus, not Aristotle’s lost botanical treatise.
- *On Marvellous Things Heard*. A paradoxographical collection (830a–847b) accumulated after Aristotle; it is a manifestation of the Aristotelian tradition, not securely his creation.
- *Mechanics*. A collection of mechanical problems (847a–858b), probably Peripatetic but not generally accepted as Aristotle’s own composition.
- *Problems*. A large, layered collection of scientific questions (859a–967b) produced and expanded within the Peripatetic school; portions may preserve Aristotelian material, but the collection as transmitted is not a single Aristotle-made work.
- *On Indivisible Lines*. A mathematical polemic (968a–972b), usually assigned to a later member of the school.
- *Positions and Names of the Winds*. A brief meteorological catalogue (973a–b), generally treated as non-Aristotelian.
- *On Melissus, Xenophanes, and Gorgias*. A doxographical-polemical treatise (974a–980b), conventionally called *MXG* and generally rejected as Aristotle’s.
- *Magna Moralia* (*Great Ethics*). A two-book ethical compilation (1181a–1213b); scholars dispute whether it is an early Aristotelian work, a student’s record, or a later Peripatetic synthesis.
- *On Virtues and Vices*. A short ethical epitome (1249a–1251b), generally considered post-Aristotelian.
- *Economics*. A two-book work on household and estate management (1343a–1353b); neither book is generally accepted as Aristotle’s, and the two books probably have different origins.
- *Rhetoric to Alexander*. A rhetorical handbook (1420a–1447b), now usually attributed to Anaximenes of Lampsacus rather than Aristotle.
- *Secret of Secrets*. A medieval Arabic mirror-for-princes presented as Aristotle’s advice to Alexander. It is pseudepigraphic and therefore not something Aristotle made.
- *Book of Causes*. A late antique Arabic-Latin adaptation of Proclus transmitted under Aristotle’s name. It is pseudepigraphic.
- *Book of the Apple*. A medieval philosophical death dialogue attributed to Aristotle. It is pseudepigraphic and should not be confused with a lost classical dialogue.
- *Theology of Aristotle*. An Arabic adaptation of material from Plotinus, not a work by Aristotle despite its title.
