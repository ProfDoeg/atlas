# Matteo Ricci: Research Dossier

## Scope and Identification

This dossier concerns the Italian Jesuit missionary, scholar, translator, cartographer, and founder of the enduring Jesuit mission in China—not the modern Italian politician or other persons of the same name.

Evidentiary labels used:

- **Documented fact:** supported by primary documents, contemporaneous testimony, institutional records, or strong historical consensus.
- **Reported fact:** supported by credible secondary literature but not independently confirmed here from a surviving primary document.
- **Allegation or theory:** an accusation, interpretation, or hypothesis advanced by an identifiable person or group.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story that circulated without adequate supporting evidence or that substantially enlarges the documentary record.

The birth date of 6 October 1552 is supported by modern archival scholarship and the Vatican decree. Older reference works sometimes give 7 October. That appears to be a transmission error rather than a viable competing date. The principal modern biographical synthesis is R. Po-chia Hsia’s entry in the *Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani* and his critical biography, *A Jesuit in the Forbidden City*.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Documented fact:** Ricci was the son of Giovanni Battista Ricci and Giovanna Angiolelli.

Giovanni Battista was an apothecary or pharmacist and occupied a respectable position in Macerata’s urban society. Some later biographies call the family “noble.” The safest formulation is that it belonged to Macerata’s established and prosperous civic elite; claims of high aristocratic rank are less securely demonstrated.

Giovanna Angiolelli is commonly described as belonging to a noble local family. The surviving biographical record contains far less information about her individual life than about her husband.

### Siblings

**Reported fact:** Matteo was the firstborn of a large family. One institutional biography specifies seven brothers and four sisters, implying twelve children including Matteo. Other sources merely call him the eldest of “numerous” children. The precise sibling list is not consistently supplied in modern biographies and should not be treated as fully reconstructed.

### Relations with his family

Giovanni Battista intended Matteo for a legal and possibly papal-administrative career. Matteo’s decision in 1571 to enter the Society of Jesus was made against, or at least without, his father’s approval. His departure for Asia in 1577 was permanent. He did not return to Macerata to take leave of his family before sailing.

Ricci’s surviving letters show that the separation remained emotionally significant. He continued to communicate with family members and Jesuit contacts in Europe, but the scale and duration of travel made reunion effectively impossible.

### Marriage, partners, and children

**Documented fact:** None. As a professed Jesuit priest, Ricci remained celibate. No credible documentary evidence identifies a spouse, sexual partner, or biological child.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1552–1568

Ricci grew up in Macerata, then a city of the Papal States. He first received instruction from the priest Niccolò Bencivegni or Bencivenga. In 1561 he entered the Jesuit college at Macerata.

The college exposed him to the humanist curriculum of grammar, rhetoric, Latin literature, religious instruction, and disciplined exercises that characterized Jesuit education. The Society of Jesus was still a comparatively young order. Its combination of scholarship, centralized discipline, education, and overseas mission supplied the institutional model that shaped the rest of his life.

No dependable evidence documents unusual childhood illnesses, psychological crises, criminal activity, or youthful scandals. Later devotional biographies sometimes retrospectively portray his missionary vocation as evident from childhood; contemporary evidence supports early Jesuit influence but not a fully formed childhood plan to evangelize China.

## Education and Formation, 1568–1578

### Legal studies in Rome

In 1568 Giovanni Battista sent Matteo to Rome to study law. Ricci attended the university commonly identified with La Sapienza. His father apparently expected him to enter a legal or administrative career.

After roughly two years, Ricci abandoned legal study. On 15 August 1571 he entered the Jesuit novitiate of Sant’Andrea al Quirinale.

### Jesuit novitiate and Collegio Romano

Ricci took his first vows in 1572. His formation included:

- Latin and Greek literature;
- rhetoric;
- Aristotelian philosophy and natural philosophy;
- logic;
- theology;
- mathematics;
- astronomy;
- cosmography;
- mechanics and instrument-making;
- mnemonic techniques inherited from classical and Renaissance traditions.

His most important identified teachers and superiors were:

- **Alessandro Valignano:** novice master and later Jesuit Visitor of the Asian missions. Valignano developed the mission policy of linguistic and cultural adaptation that Ricci implemented in China.
- **Christopher Clavius:** the Collegio Romano mathematician closely associated with Gregorian calendar reform. Ricci studied mathematics, astronomy, geography, Euclidean geometry, and scientific instrumentation under him and maintained contact with him.

The Collegio Romano supplied Ricci with a late-Renaissance scientific curriculum rather than “modern science” in the later seventeenth-century sense. His astronomy remained geocentric. His mathematical training, however, was systematic and in several respects differed markedly from the learned traditions he encountered in Ming China.

### Decision to enter the Asian mission

In 1577 Ricci volunteered for the missions of the Portuguese *Estado da Índia*. He traveled through Genoa to Portugal and stayed at the Jesuit college in Coimbra while completing preparatory theological studies and learning Portuguese.

On 24 March 1578 he sailed from Lisbon with other Jesuits. The voyage followed the Portuguese route around the Cape of Good Hope, with a stop in Mozambique. He reached Goa on 13 September 1578 after nearly six months at sea.

## India and Ordination, 1578–1582

Ricci studied theology and taught classical literature at Jesuit institutions in Goa and Cochin. He was ordained a priest in 1580; sources associate his first Mass with Cochin on 26 July 1580.

His health was unreliable. He became ill in Cochin and returned to Goa. The precise diagnosis is not recoverable. His later letters repeatedly record illnesses, exhaustion, and the physical costs of climate, travel, and administrative work, but do not establish a chronic named disease.

During these years he observed Portuguese colonial society and Jesuit institutions in India. In a 1580 letter he criticized inaccuracies in European books and maps concerning India and Japan. He also argued that Asian students should have access to philosophy and theology, suggesting that he had already begun to question purely European control of advanced religious education.

Portuguese maritime power provided the ships, institutions, funding, and jurisdictional framework that carried Ricci to Asia. He was therefore structurally connected to the Portuguese imperial system. No evidence shows that he personally commanded military forces, participated in conquest, owned a colonial business, or held commercial investments.

## Macau and Preparation for China, 1582–1583

Valignano had concluded that a durable mission in China required mastery of Chinese language and etiquette. Michele Ruggieri had already begun that work. Ricci was summoned to assist him and reached Macau on 7 August 1582.

At Macau’s Jesuit residence, Ricci studied spoken and written Chinese. Ruggieri and Ricci, working with Chinese assistants, compiled an early Portuguese–Chinese dictionary. The surviving manuscript is untitled; its authorship and editorial history are collaborative rather than exclusively Ricci’s.

Their early vocabulary and pronunciation reflected contact with southern speech and the prestige language later conventionally described as late-Ming Mandarin. Ricci eventually became capable of writing literary Chinese, though his published texts depended heavily on Chinese collaborators, editors, scribes, and block-cutters.

## Chronological Life History in China

## Zhaoqing, 1583–1589

In September 1583 Ricci and Ruggieri received permission from the prefect Wang Pan to establish themselves at Zhaoqing in Guangdong.

### The “Western monks”

They initially shaved their heads and wore clothing that Chinese observers associated with Buddhist clerics. They described themselves as religious men from the West and displayed religious images, clocks, prisms, maps, and other objects.

Their residence was generally presented as a place for religious teaching rather than a European enclave. Permission remained dependent on local officials and could be withdrawn.

### First Chinese world maps

In 1584 Ricci prepared the *Yudi shanhai quantu* (輿地山海全圖, “Complete Map of the Mountains and Seas of the Earth”), adapting European geographic knowledge to a Chinese-language format. Wang Pan participated in editing or sponsoring it.

The original Zhaoqing printing is lost. Later maps derived from successive revisions. Consequently, claims that a single surviving “Ricci map” reproduces exactly what Chinese viewers saw in 1584 are not demonstrable.

### Catechism and religious activity

Ruggieri produced the *Tianzhu shilu* (天主實錄), an early Chinese catechism, with Ricci participating in revision and translation. The missionaries used *Tianzhu*, “Lord of Heaven,” as their preferred name for the Christian God.

The mission initially produced few converts. Chinese visitors were often more interested in clocks, maps, mathematical demonstrations, mnemonic displays, and foreign curiosities than in baptism.

### Qu Rukui and the alchemy story

Ricci became close to Qu Rukui, also known as Qu Taisu, an educated and socially connected man. Qu helped Ricci understand literati society and introduced him to wider networks.

**Documented fact:** Qu initially showed interest in the foreigners partly because he believed they might possess alchemical knowledge, including techniques for transforming metals. Ricci explicitly mentioned the widespread supposition that Europeans knew alchemy.

**Rumor or myth:** Ricci could manufacture gold or transmute metals. No evidence shows that he performed or claimed to perform metallic transmutation. The rumor mattered because it attracted visitors and illustrates how Chinese observers interpreted European instruments and secrecy.

### Expulsion from Zhaoqing

A change of officials undermined the mission’s position. In 1589 Ricci was ordered to leave Zhaoqing. The missionaries lost their residence and moved north to Shaozhou.

This was a significant institutional failure: six years of work had not secured permanent legal status, broad conversion, or reliable protection.

## Shaozhou/Shaoguan, 1589–1595

At Shaozhou, Ricci concentrated more intensively on classical Chinese learning and elite networks. He gradually concluded that association with Buddhist monks placed missionaries too low in the Ming social hierarchy and misrepresented their intellectual and religious identity.

### Transition to literati dress

Between approximately 1594 and 1595, with Valignano’s approval, Ricci and his companions stopped presenting themselves as Buddhist-style monks. They grew their hair and beards and adopted the robes, caps, etiquette, and transportation practices of Chinese literati.

**Documented fact:** The clothing change occurred and was consciously connected to access to scholar-official society.

**Interpretive dispute:** Supporters describe it as inculturation or mutual adaptation. Critics describe it as social camouflage concealing a proselytizing purpose. The documents establish both the missionary purpose and Ricci’s serious acquisition of Chinese learning; they do not reduce the relationship to either pure disinterested exchange or simple fraud.

Ricci’s brother in the mission, António de Almeida, died at Shaozhou in 1591. The small community endured illness, local hostility, burglaries, and administrative insecurity.

## Nanchang, 1595–1598

Ricci reached Nanchang in 1595 and entered the society of officials and literati through introductions supplied by Qu Rukui and others.

### *Jiaoyou lun* — *On Friendship*

In 1595 he composed the *Jiaoyou lun* (交友論), conventionally translated as *On Friendship*. It presented maxims drawn from classical and Renaissance European traditions in an idiom accessible to Chinese literati.

The text was not a literal translation of a single European book. It adapted sayings associated with Cicero, Seneca, Aristotle, Plutarch, Augustine, Erasmus, and other traditions, while being reshaped for a late-Ming audience. Chinese editors and patrons facilitated its circulation.

Its success strengthened Ricci’s identity as “Xitai,” a scholar from the Far West, and created relationships that proved more useful than his earlier monastic persona.

### Mnemonic performances and *Xiguo jifa*

Ricci demonstrated the ability to memorize long sequences of Chinese characters after limited exposure. He taught architectural mnemonic techniques—the construction of imagined palaces or rooms in which images could be stored.

He composed *Xiguo jifa* (西國記法, “Western Methods of Memory”) around 1596. The work circulated in manuscript and was printed only after his death, probably after 1625.

**Documented fact:** Ricci possessed and taught trained mnemonic methods.

**Legendary enlargement:** Popular retellings sometimes give him literally photographic or supernatural memory. His performances were remarkable but arose from learned mnemonic discipline, not evidence of paranormal capacity.

### Growth of the mission

Ricci gained patrons among Jiangxi literati and established a more stable residence. Christianity remained a small community rather than a mass movement. The attraction of foreign learning and the decision to accept baptism were not identical.

## First Attempt to Reach Beijing, 1598–1599

Valignano directed Ricci to seek access to Beijing, partly because imperial recognition could protect the mission throughout China.

Ricci departed Nanchang on 25 June 1598 with the Jesuit Lazzaro Cattaneo and Wang Zhongming. He traveled by water through Nanjing toward Beijing.

The war involving Ming China, Japan, and Korea made officials especially suspicious of foreigners. Ricci reached Beijing but could not establish himself there and stayed only about a month. Financial problems and the absence of a secure patron forced him to retreat.

He returned to Nanjing in February 1599.

## Nanjing, 1599–1600

Nanjing offered an exceptionally dense community of officials, scholars, printers, and religious specialists.

Ricci met and debated major intellectual figures, including the Buddhist monk Xuelang Hong’en. The encounter was remembered as sharp and antagonistic. Ricci’s developing critique of Buddhism was no incidental difference: he increasingly argued that ancient Confucian teaching contained traces of natural monotheism, while portraying Buddhism and much Daoism as later corruptions.

He prepared a new world map, *Shanhai yudi quantu* (山海輿地全圖), printed in Nanjing in 1600 with Chinese patronage. The printing is lost.

Ricci also met Xu Guangqi in Nanjing. Their collaboration became the most consequential intellectual relationship of his Chinese career.

## Final Journey to Beijing, 1600–1601

Ricci left Nanjing in May 1600 with Diego de Pantoja. They carried gifts intended for the emperor, including:

- mechanical clocks;
- religious paintings;
- prisms and scientific instruments;
- a clavichord or keyboard instrument;
- maps;
- books and other European objects.

At Linqing, the powerful eunuch Ma Tang detained the party and appropriated or controlled access to the gifts. Ricci spent months in a precarious condition. The gifts nevertheless came to official attention, and an imperial order eventually permitted the Jesuits to proceed.

They entered Beijing in January 1601. The court accepted the gifts and assigned stipends and residence to the missionaries.

### Did Ricci meet the Wanli emperor?

**Documented fact:** Ricci’s gifts were presented to the imperial court; the emperor took an interest in the clocks and ordered that missionaries or their associates maintain them. Ricci acquired permission to remain in Beijing and received financial support.

**Disputed claim:** That Ricci personally met or conversed with the Wanli emperor.

The emperor had largely withdrawn from direct ceremonial government and did not grant Ricci a conventional face-to-face audience. Ricci entered the palace environment and served court needs indirectly, but the familiar phrase “Ricci met the emperor” overstates the evidence. Accounts describing him as the emperor’s intimate adviser or personal friend are later legendary compression.

Ricci did not hold a regular mandarin office and was not formally incorporated into the civil-service bureaucracy.

## Beijing, 1601–1610

Beijing became the mission’s headquarters. Ricci maintained contacts with officials, scholars, eunuchs, converts, and fellow Jesuits and supervised residences elsewhere in China.

### Court service and clocks

The missionaries’ mechanical clocks required periodic winding and repair. This created continuing court demand for their technical presence. Musical instruction was supplied so that eunuchs could perform European music associated with the gifts.

This was an unusual form of patronage: Ricci had neither direct political power nor commercial independence, but technical usefulness gave him protected access, housing, and an imperial stipend.

### The 1602 world map

With Li Zhizao as sponsor and collaborator and Zhang Wentao associated with block production, Ricci issued the six-panel *Kunyu wanguo quantu* (坤輿萬國全圖, “Complete Geographical Map of All the Countries of the World”) in 1602.

Its geographic framework drew on European cartography, but its finished form was a collaborative Chinese printed artifact. It included Chinese annotations, information supplied or corrected by Chinese scholars, and extensive explanatory texts.

The map placed China near the visual center rather than at the far eastern edge, facilitating Chinese reading while still presenting a spherical world divided into continents and oceans. It circulated in China, Korea, and Japan.

Claims that it was “the first Chinese world map” are false if read literally: China possessed older maps of the world and of foreign regions. It was, more precisely, among the earliest and most influential Chinese-language maps to synthesize late-sixteenth-century European global cartography with Chinese geographic knowledge.

### Xu Guangqi

Xu Guangqi was baptized in 1603 under the Christian name Paul. Ricci and Xu worked together in Beijing, most famously on the first six books of Euclid’s *Elements*, published as *Jihe yuanben* (幾何原本) in 1607.

The collaboration was dialogic. Ricci orally explained or translated from the Latin edition and commentary of Clavius; Xu rendered, reformulated, and polished the text in literary Chinese. The work established durable Chinese terminology for geometry, including *jihe* as the title word for geometry.

Xu wished to continue beyond six books, but Ricci stopped. The reasons given include the difficulty of the undertaking and Ricci’s desire to see whether the first installment would generate demand. Completion of all thirteen books came much later through other translators.

Ricci and Xu also collaborated on works concerning measurement and mathematical methods. Xu’s interests extended beyond mission service to calendar reform, agriculture, hydraulics, military technology, and statecraft.

### Li Zhizao

Li Zhizao, baptized as Leo, sponsored the 1602 map and collaborated with Ricci on astronomy, arithmetic, cosmology, and instruments. Associated works include:

- *Hungai tongxian tushuo* (渾蓋通憲圖說), on an astronomical instrument;
- *Tongwen suanzhi* (同文算指), based substantially on Clavius’s arithmetic and published after Ricci’s death;
- cosmological and calendrical writings.

### Christianity and Confucianism

Ricci’s principal theological work, *Tianzhu shiyi* (天主實義, “The True Meaning of the Lord of Heaven”), appeared in a mature printed form in 1603.

Structured as a dialogue between a Western and a Chinese scholar, it argued:

- that one supreme, personal Creator exists;
- that the human soul is immortal;
- that moral accountability continues after death;
- that ancient Chinese terms such as *Shangdi* and *Tian* could refer to the true God;
- that early Confucian classics were compatible with natural theology;
- that Neo-Confucian monism, Buddhist rebirth, and aspects of Daoist teaching were mistaken.

Ricci’s approach was selective. He treated the ancient classics more favorably than contemporary Neo-Confucian metaphysics and rejected Buddhism increasingly sharply. He did not regard the “three teachings” as equally valid routes.

### Ancestor and Confucian rites

Ricci permitted Chinese Christians to participate in rites honoring ancestors and Confucius when construed as civil, familial, or commemorative rather than divine worship. He also used *Tianzhu*, while allowing carefully qualified reference to *Shangdi* and *Tian*.

These positions became the basis of the “Ricci rules” (*Li Madou guiju*) after his death.

### Other Beijing writings

Ricci produced or supervised works on:

- Christian doctrine;
- friendship and moral philosophy;
- Epictetan or Stoic ethical maxims;
- memory;
- Euclidean geometry;
- practical measurement;
- arithmetic;
- cosmology;
- astronomy and astronomical instruments;
- Roman-alphabet transcription of Chinese;
- music and songs prepared for court;
- world geography.

### Administration and converts

Ricci served as superior of the China mission. By the end of his life, Jesuit residences operated at Beijing, Nanjing, Nanchang, and Shaozhou. A modern Chinese-language publication history estimates more than 2,000 baptized Chinese by 1610, but figures vary with the definition of active adherent and the incompleteness of mission registers.

Ricci cultivated elite converts because they offered intellectual collaboration and political protection. The mission also included converts and employees of less exalted status, whose lives are less visible in literati and Jesuit records.

## Final Illness and Death, 1610

Ricci’s final years involved heavy correspondence, writing, visitor reception, personnel management, and negotiations with officials. On 3 May 1610, according to the chronology reconstructed from mission sources, he became seriously ill after returning from an audience or social obligation.

His condition worsened over approximately a week. He died at the Beijing Jesuit residence on 11 May 1610.

The sources describe fever, exhaustion, and terminal illness but do not establish a modern clinical diagnosis. Claims of a specific disease should therefore be treated as conjectural. There is no evidence of murder, poisoning, suicide, addiction, or violent death.

Fellow missionaries and Chinese Christians gathered around him. His death created an immediate legal problem because Europeans who died in China were ordinarily returned toward Macau rather than buried in the capital.

## Burial and Immediate Aftermath, 1610–1611

Diego de Pantoja and Chinese supporters petitioned for permission to bury Ricci in Beijing. The imperial government granted property formerly belonging to the condemned eunuch Yang and allowed its use as a cemetery and residence.

Ricci was interred at Zhalan in 1611. The grant was more than a personal honor: it created a legally recognized, continuing physical base for foreign missionaries in Beijing.

Later writers sometimes describe the grant as a direct act of intimate imperial gratitude. The permission was real, but it was mediated through officials and should not be converted into evidence of a personal friendship between Ricci and the emperor.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions

Ricci was connected with:

- the Society of Jesus;
- Sant’Andrea al Quirinale;
- the Collegio Romano;
- Jesuit colleges at Coimbra, Goa, and Cochin;
- St. Paul’s College and the Jesuit mission at Macau;
- residences at Zhaoqing, Shaozhou, Nanchang, Nanjing, and Beijing;
- the Portuguese Padroado and *Estado da Índia*, which supplied the maritime and ecclesiastical framework;
- networks of Ming scholar-officials, academies, printers, artisans, and court eunuchs.

### Technologies and scientific objects

He introduced, demonstrated, translated, or helped circulate:

- European mechanical clocks;
- prisms and optical curiosities;
- globes and world maps;
- astrolabe-related and armillary instruments;
- European arithmetic;
- Euclidean deductive geometry;
- geographic coordinates and spherical-earth cosmography;
- mnemonic systems;
- Roman-alphabet transcription;
- keyboard instruments and European musical notation.

Ricci did not invent clocks, Euclidean geometry, the astrolabe, or the cartographic projections he used. His historical role was primarily transmission, adaptation, explanation, translation, synthesis, and institutional deployment.

### Patents and proprietary inventions

No patents are known. Patent systems in the modern sense are inapplicable to his work in Ming China. The mission sometimes treated technical knowledge and access to instruments strategically, but Ricci did not establish a patent-holding company.

### Mission finances

The China mission depended on:

- Jesuit institutional resources;
- Portuguese imperial and ecclesiastical networks;
- gifts from patrons;
- local Chinese donations;
- imperial stipends after settlement in Beijing;
- residences and property granted or permitted by officials.

Ricci did not accumulate a documented private fortune. Property nominally associated with him generally belonged to, or was used by, the mission. No reliable evidence identifies personal investments, commercial partnerships, land speculation, hidden accounts, or private business ownership.

The mission’s use of expensive clocks and curiosities as gifts was deliberate patronage strategy. It was not bribery in a clearly prosecutable modern sense, but it participated in a court economy where valuable objects purchased access, attention, and protection.

## Political Activity

Ricci was not a party politician and held no electoral or civil office. His political activity consisted of:

- cultivating officials who could authorize residences and travel;
- seeking imperial recognition for the mission;
- preparing memorials and gifts for the court;
- using elite networks to protect missionaries;
- reporting on Ming government and society to Jesuit superiors;
- positioning Christianity as supportive of moral government and social hierarchy.

He praised aspects of the Ming examination system and literati government while criticizing corruption, eunuch power, legal practices, and what he saw as Chinese ethnocentrism or religious error.

His European account sometimes idealized Christian Europe, omitting or minimizing the Reformation, wars of religion, and internal European conflict. This was an apologetic and strategic presentation, not a neutral comparative ethnography.

No credible evidence establishes that Ricci acted as an intelligence officer for a European government, planned military conquest, transmitted invasion plans, or organized political violence. Missionaries did collect geographic and political information, and their reports inevitably had strategic value; that structural fact is not evidence of a clandestine espionage assignment.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Early Catholic and humanist formation

Ricci’s foundations were Tridentine Catholicism, Jesuit spirituality, Aristotelian natural philosophy, Renaissance humanism, and Clavian mathematics.

### Adaptation under Valignano

Valignano’s program required language study, respect for elite customs, and the formation of local clergy and scholars. Ricci expanded it through practical experience.

His development proceeded roughly as follows:

1. Adoption of a Buddhist-style religious persona in Guangdong.
2. Recognition that Buddhist status limited elite access.
3. Adoption of literati identity and Confucian terminology.
4. Increasing claim that ancient Confucianism was compatible with natural monotheism.
5. Increasingly explicit attack on Buddhism and Neo-Confucian metaphysics.
6. Use of science, ethical literature, and friendship as an indirect route to Christian teaching.

### Stoicism and moral writing

*Ershiwu yan* (二十五言, “Twenty-Five Sayings”) drew extensively on Epictetus’s *Enchiridion* and related Stoic moral traditions. Ricci selected and Christianized material rather than making a complete literal translation.

### View of Chinese civilization

Ricci admired Chinese literacy, printing, political administration, urban order, agriculture, crafts, and the prestige of learning. He also criticized:

- the perceived absence of a personal Creator in contemporary philosophy;
- Buddhism and Daoism;
- practices he regarded as superstition;
- ethnocentric geography;
- restrictions on foreigners;
- corruption and arbitrary official conduct;
- the status of women and certain familial practices.

His descriptions are valuable eyewitness testimony but remain missionary and early-modern European interpretations.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Alessandro Valignano

**Documented fact:** Teacher, superior, strategist, and institutional patron. Valignano authorized or encouraged language-centered accommodation and repeatedly shaped Ricci’s appointments.

### Christopher Clavius

**Documented fact:** Mathematics teacher and continuing intellectual influence. Ricci transmitted and adapted material from Clavius’s editions of Euclid and arithmetic manuals.

### Michele Ruggieri

**Documented fact:** Senior collaborator in Macau and Zhaoqing; co-worker on language study, the early dictionary, and catechetical materials. Their approaches later diverged, especially when Ricci replaced Ruggieri’s Buddhist-inflected presentation with literati-oriented methods.

### Lazzaro Cattaneo

**Documented fact:** Jesuit colleague and traveling companion. Cattaneo contributed to linguistic and musical analysis of Chinese tones and assisted the mission’s expansion.

### Diego de Pantoja

**Documented fact:** Companion on the final journey to Beijing, colleague in the capital, and principal petitioner for Ricci’s burial.

### Qu Rukui / Qu Taisu

**Documented fact:** Early Chinese friend, student, social broker, and collaborator in mathematical work. His initial interest included the alchemical rumor surrounding Europeans.

### Wang Pan

**Documented fact:** Zhaoqing official who permitted the first residence and sponsored or edited Ricci’s early map.

### Zhang Huang

**Documented fact:** Scholar and publisher whose engagement with classical texts influenced Ricci’s reading of Confucianism and helped circulate geographic knowledge.

### Wang Zhongming

**Documented fact:** Chinese associate who assisted Ricci’s northward travel and political introductions.

### Feng Yingjing

**Documented fact:** Official, patron, and contributor of a preface to *Jiaoyou lun*. He helped legitimize Ricci within literati publishing networks.

### Xu Guangqi / Paul Xu

**Documented fact:** Convert, friend, scholar-official, translator, and mathematical collaborator. Their *Jihe yuanben* became a landmark of Chinese mathematical translation.

### Li Zhizao / Leo Li

**Documented fact:** Convert, official, map sponsor, scientific collaborator, and later publisher. His 1628 *Tianxue chuhan* preserved numerous Ricci-related works.

### Ma Tang

**Documented fact:** Court eunuch who detained Ricci’s party and controlled or attempted to appropriate the gifts intended for the emperor. He functioned as an adversarial gatekeeper rather than a stable patron.

### Xuelang Hong’en

**Documented fact:** Eminent Buddhist monk who debated Ricci sharply in Nanjing in 1599. Their dispute contributed to Christian–Buddhist polemics.

### Yunqi Zhuhong

**Documented fact:** Leading Buddhist critic. Ricci and Zhuhong disputed questions including killing animals and Buddhist abstention from taking life. Zhuhong’s explicit refutations became especially visible after Ricci’s death.

### Wanli emperor

**Documented fact:** The court accepted Ricci’s gifts, enabled his residence, and supported the missionaries financially.

**Mythic enlargement:** Personal friendship, repeated private audiences, or a formal position as intimate imperial adviser.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Contested Questions

## Cultural accommodation or strategic disguise

**Documented facts:** Ricci changed clothing and social persona, adopted Chinese terminology, used classical texts, tolerated specified rites, and deployed scientific objects to gain elite access. His ultimate purpose included conversion to Christianity.

**Critical interpretation:** Some scholars describe the program as rhetorical camouflage or cultural imperialism—a way to enter Chinese elite culture while concealing the disruptive implications of conversion.

**Counter-interpretation:** Other scholars emphasize that Ricci’s decades of language study, dependence on Chinese collaborators, and changes in his own thought demonstrate reciprocal adaptation rather than a fixed European program imposed intact.

The evidence supports a consciously strategic mission and genuine intellectual exchange simultaneously.

## Hostility toward Buddhism

Ricci’s early monastic persona gave way to strong anti-Buddhist polemic. In *Tianzhu shiyi* he rejected rebirth, emptiness, monastic celibacy as he understood it, vegetarian abstention, and Buddhist metaphysics.

A modern scholarly criticism calls his choice of Buddhism as an enemy “unprovoked” and argues that he exaggerated difference to win Confucian allies. Buddhist writers including Zhuhong answered him. The controversy was theological and social, not merely a later academic construction.

Ricci defended human killing of animals for legitimate use as part of a divinely ordered hierarchy. Zhuhong and later Buddhist critics treated this as a failure of compassion and a misunderstanding of the prohibition on taking life.

## Confucianism reconstructed for Christian purposes

Ricci argued that the oldest Confucian classics testified to a supreme God and that later Neo-Confucianism had obscured this knowledge.

**Documented fact:** This is the argument of his writings.

**Disputed interpretation:** Whether ancient *Shangdi* can legitimately be identified with the Christian God. Chinese and European writers have disagreed for four centuries. Ricci’s reading was a theological reconstruction, not a neutral recovery universally accepted by Chinese scholars.

## Chinese rites

Ricci’s permission for ancestor and Confucian rites helped create a durable Chinese Christian community but later became the center of the Chinese Rites Controversy.

Dominicans, Franciscans, some Jesuits, and Roman authorities argued that the rites were religious worship incompatible with Christianity. Defenders argued that they were civil and familial ceremonies.

The dispute escalated after Ricci’s death and cannot be attributed to him alone. Papal prohibitions in the eighteenth century damaged the mission; Rome reversed the broad prohibition in 1939 under changed interpretations of the rites.

## Chinese accusations of sedition and dangerous doctrine

Around 1607, twenty-seven Nanchang degree-holders reportedly accused Ricci, João Soeiro, Manuel Dias, and other Jesuits of rebellion or dangerous teaching and sought their execution or expulsion. The plaintiffs lost, but their pamphlet later contributed to anti-Christian mobilization.

The 1616 Nanjing persecution occurred six years after Ricci’s death. Shen Que and allied officials accused missionaries of violating Confucian order, deceiving Chinese subjects, disrespecting the emperor, and presenting a security danger analogous to Iberian expansion elsewhere in Asia.

These were formal political accusations against Ricci’s successors and the system he built, not convictions of Ricci. They reflected genuine Ming concerns about foreign religious organization in the context of Portuguese and Spanish maritime expansion.

## Piracy, espionage, and conquest narratives

Some Ming observers associated Westerners generally with maritime piracy and the Spanish conquest of the Philippines. Anti-Christian memorials alleged that missionaries softened countries through religion before foreign seizure.

**Documented context:** Iberian conquest and missionary activity did overlap elsewhere; Jesuit reports transmitted geographic and political information.

**Unproven allegation:** Ricci was personally preparing an invasion, organizing espionage, or directing a conquest of China. No evidence found supports those claims.

## Science as conversion instrument

Ricci openly regarded science, clocks, maps, and mathematics as means of demonstrating the intellectual credibility of Christian Europe.

This was neither a secret nor necessarily a fraudulent claim. Controversy concerns whether it instrumentalized knowledge and Chinese friendship. The scientific translations can be technically and historically consequential while also serving an evangelizing strategy.

## The map and “European superiority”

Older narratives present Ricci as revealing the true world to an ignorant China. Modern scholarship qualifies this:

- China already had sophisticated cartography and mathematical astronomy.
- Ricci supplied a new global synthesis and unfamiliar geographic information.
- His maps depended on Chinese editors, sponsors, artisans, sources, and readers.
- European maps contained errors, speculative southern continents, and inherited legends.
- The encounter was not a simple transfer from total knowledge to total ignorance.

## Financial misconduct, fraud, lawsuits, and criminality

The dedicated adversarial search found no credible evidence that the Jesuit Matteo Ricci was charged with embezzlement, fraud, money laundering, sexual abuse, homicide, or another ordinary criminal offense.

Searches for recent “Matteo Ricci” corruption and peculation cases produce reports about the living Italian politician Matteo Ricci. They are unrelated and must not be imported into this biography.

Mission opponents accused the Jesuits of deception, illicit religious organization, and potential sedition. Those accusations are historically important but did not produce a documented personal criminal conviction of Ricci.

## Slavery and coerced labor

Ricci traveled within the Portuguese imperial world, where slavery was institutionalized. Sources also describe his use of servants and sedan-chair bearers after adopting literati status.

No evidence consulted establishes that Ricci personally owned enslaved people. “Servant” in mission and Chinese records cannot automatically be translated as “slave,” and the matter remains insufficiently documented for a stronger conclusion.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## “First European in China”

**Myth:** Ricci was the first European to enter China.

**Evidence against:** Europeans and West Asians had reached China centuries earlier, including Franciscan missionaries under the Yuan. Portuguese merchants and other missionaries preceded Ricci in the sixteenth century.

**Historically defensible reformulation:** He was a foundational figure in the permanent early-modern Jesuit mission and among the first Europeans to master literary Chinese sufficiently for sustained elite publication.

## “First European in the Forbidden City”

The phrase is common but ambiguous. Ricci obtained access within the Beijing court system and palace environment. It should not be understood as proof of a private imperial audience or unique physical entry into every restricted precinct.

## Personal friend of the Wanli emperor

**Mythic or hagiographic enlargement:** Ricci became the emperor’s personal confidant.

**Evidence:** The emperor accepted gifts, authorized residence, used the clocks, and supported the mission. He apparently knew Ricci through memorials and court intermediaries. There is no strong evidence of a face-to-face personal friendship.

**Reputational effect:** The story elevates Ricci from dependent foreign petitioner to equal partner in a civilizational encounter.

## Supernatural memory

**Legend:** Ricci could remember anything after seeing it once.

**Evidence:** He trained extensively in classical mnemonic architecture and performed striking demonstrations. His *Xiguo jifa* explains a technique rather than a miracle.

**Reputational effect:** The legend made him a sage-like or magician-like figure and drew visitors to the mission.

## Alchemist and maker of gold

**Rumor:** Ricci possessed the secret of transmutation.

**Origin and circulation:** Chinese visitors, including Qu Rukui, associated foreign learning with alchemy and marvelous technology.

**Evidence:** Ricci reported the rumor; no evidence confirms transmutation.

**Reputational effect:** It attracted curiosity but risked placing him among wonder-workers rather than scholars.

## Ricci as creator of Chinese geometry

**Myth:** He introduced geometry itself to China.

**Evidence against:** China possessed an ancient and extensive mathematical tradition, including geometric problems and proofs of various kinds.

**Accurate claim:** Ricci and Xu Guangqi introduced the Euclidean axiomatic-deductive text in Clavius’s recension and created influential Chinese terminology for that tradition.

## Ricci as sole maker of the 1602 map

**Myth:** A solitary European drew and printed the map for a passive Chinese audience.

**Evidence:** Li Zhizao sponsored and collaborated; Zhang Wentao was associated with block production; Chinese scholars supplied edits and geographic information; the artifact’s language and print technology were Chinese.

**Reputational effect:** Sole attribution supported heroic “great man” and unilateral diffusion narratives. Recent scholarship treats it as intercultural production.

## The conversion of China nearly achieved

Jesuit and Catholic hagiography sometimes depicts Ricci’s strategy as bringing the empire to the threshold of mass conversion, frustrated principally by later Roman errors.

By 1610 the community numbered in the low thousands, not millions, and remained vulnerable to official repression. Elite conversions were important, but imperial conversion was not demonstrably imminent.

## Friend of China versus colonial advance agent

Modern political narratives divide Ricci into two opposing symbols:

- a respectful “friend of China” and pioneer of equal dialogue;
- a culturally sophisticated advance agent of Western imperialism.

Both select real evidence. He respected and learned from Chinese civilization, while remaining committed to replacing religious beliefs he considered false and operating within Portuguese imperial networks. No surviving document reduces him completely to either symbol.

## Works by Matteo Ricci

Authorship classifications below distinguish original works, collaborations, translations or adaptations, and posthumous editorial constructions.

### Italian and Latin writings

#### Letters

Approximately fifty-four surviving letters are commonly attributed to Ricci. They were written principally in Italian and Portuguese to family members, Jesuit superiors, and colleagues between 1580 and 1609.

**Status:** Genuine correspondence, though modern editions normalize, select, and annotate the manuscripts.

Important editions include:

- Pietro Tacchi Venturi, ed., *Opere storiche del P. Matteo Ricci*, 2 vols., 1911–1913.
- Pasquale M. d’Elia, ed., *Fonti Ricciane*, 3 vols., 1942–1949.
- Francesco D’Arelli, ed., *Lettere (1580–1609)*.
- Brendan Gottschall, trans., *Matteo Ricci: Letters from China*, a modern English translation with commentary.

#### *Della entrata della Compagnia di Giesù e Christianità nella Cina*

Written principally in Beijing, approximately 1608–1610.

**Status:** Genuine Italian manuscript history by Ricci, left unfinished or unprepared for publication at his death.

It records the establishment of the Jesuit mission and describes Ming China. Nicolas Trigault translated, rearranged, expanded, and edited it as:

#### *De Christiana expeditione apud Sinas suscepta ab Societate Jesu*

Augsburg, 1615.

**Status:** Posthumous Latin adaptation by Nicolas Trigault based substantially on Ricci’s manuscript and papers. It should not be cited as a verbatim Latin work written solely by Ricci.

It became one of early modern Europe’s most influential books about China and was quickly translated into French, German, Spanish, and other languages.

#### Latin translation of the Confucian Four Books

Ricci reportedly prepared translations or paraphrases of the Four Books for Jesuit use.

**Status:** Genuine project attested by early Jesuit sources, but the complete text is lost or not securely identifiable. It was not a standard printed edition during his lifetime.

### Chinese religious and ethical works

#### 《天主實錄》 *Tianzhu shilu* — *True Record of the Lord of Heaven*

Zhaoqing, 1584; primarily associated with Michele Ruggieri.

**Status:** Collaborative translation and editorial involvement by Ricci; not solely his original work.

#### 《交友論》 *Jiaoyou lun* — *On Friendship*

Nanchang, 1595; later editions and revisions.

**Status:** Genuine Ricci composition in literary Chinese with Chinese editorial assistance; an adaptation of European classical and Renaissance maxims rather than a translation of one original.

#### 《西國記法》 *Xiguo jifa* — *Western Methods of Memory*

Composed around 1596; published after 1625.

**Status:** Genuine Ricci work; posthumously printed.

#### 《天主實義》 *Tianzhu shiyi* — *The True Meaning of the Lord of Heaven*

Mature printed edition, Beijing, 1603.

**Status:** Principal genuine theological work, created with significant Chinese linguistic and editorial assistance.

#### 《二十五言》 *Ershiwu yan* — *Twenty-Five Sayings*

Beijing, 1605.

**Status:** Genuine adaptation, based largely on Epictetus and Stoic ethical traditions and recast within Ricci’s Christian framework.

#### 《天主教要》 *Tianzhu jiaoyao* — *Essentials of the Teaching of the Lord of Heaven*

Beijing, 1605.

**Status:** Catechetical work attributed to Ricci; its relationship to earlier mission formularies is editorial and compilatory.

#### 《西字奇蹟》 *Xizi qiji* — *Miracle of Western Letters*

Beijing, 1605.

**Status:** Genuine samples of Romanized Chinese and religious text. Historically important in the development of alphabetic transcription of Chinese, though not the sole origin of romanization.

#### 《畸人十篇》 *Jiren shipian* — *Ten Discourses by a Man of Paradox*

Beijing, 1608; some sources date initial compilation to 1607.

**Status:** Genuine Ricci work in dialogue form, incorporating conversations with Chinese literati and Christian moral teaching.

#### 《西琴曲意八章》 *Xiqin quyi bazhang* — *Eight Songs for the Western Keyboard Instrument*

Prepared around 1601; printed as an appendix to *Jiren shipian* in 1608.

**Status:** Lyrics or moral songs attributed to Ricci and associated with court performance; the precise division between Ricci’s composition, translation, and musical arrangement is not entirely recoverable.

#### 《辯學遺牘》 *Bianxue yidu* — *Surviving Documents on the Debate over Learning*

Letters involving Ricci, Yu Chunxi, and Buddhist-Christian controversy; printed posthumously, often dated 1635 in collected form.

**Status:** Genuine correspondence preserved and arranged posthumously; not a lifetime unified book.

### Scientific and mathematical works

#### 《輿地山海全圖》 *Yudi shanhai quantu*

Zhaoqing, 1584.

**Status:** Ricci-directed Chinese world map with Wang Pan and other Chinese collaborators; original lost.

#### 《山海輿地全圖》 *Shanhai yudi quantu*

Nanjing, 1600.

**Status:** Revised collaborative map; original printing lost.

#### 《坤輿萬國全圖》 *Kunyu wanguo quantu*

Beijing, 1602.

**Status:** Major collaborative map associated with Ricci, sponsored by Li Zhizao and produced with Chinese editorial and artisanal participation.

#### 《兩儀玄覽圖》 *Liangyi xuanlan tu*

1603.

**Status:** Cosmographic or world-map project associated with Ricci and Li Ying-shi; attribution and surviving versions require edition-specific analysis.

#### 《經天該》 *Jingtian gai*

Beijing, approximately 1601.

**Status:** Astronomical work attributed to Ricci.

#### 《四元行論》 *Siyuan xing lun*

Nanjing, approximately 1599–1600.

**Status:** Cosmological discussion of the four elements, attributed to Ricci.

#### 《日球大於地球，地球大於月球》 *Riqiu dayu diqiu, diqiu dayu yueqiu*

Approximately 1606–1607.

**Status:** Short astronomical proposition attributed to Ricci.

#### 《幾何原本》 *Jihe yuanben* — Euclid’s *Elements*, Books I–VI

Beijing, 1607.

**Status:** Collaborative translation by Ricci and Xu Guangqi from Clavius’s Latin edition and commentary. Neither man should be treated as sole author.

#### 《勾股義》 *Gougu yi*

1607.

**Status:** Mathematical work associated with Ricci and Xu Guangqi; collaborative or derivative.

#### 《測量法義》 *Celiang fayi*

Published 1617.

**Status:** Posthumous work based on Ricci’s instruction and collaboration with Xu Guangqi. The printed text cannot be classified as a solely authored lifetime publication.

#### 《渾蓋通憲圖說》 *Hungai tongxian tushuo*

Beijing, 1607.

**Status:** Collaborative work with Li Zhizao on an astronomical instrument, drawing on Clavius.

#### 《圜容較義》 *Huanrong jiaoyi*

Beijing, 1609.

**Status:** Geometrical work associated with Ricci and Li Zhizao.

#### 《同文算指》 *Tongwen suanzhi*

Published 1613.

**Status:** Posthumous collaborative arithmetic text, primarily connected with Ricci and Li Zhizao and derived substantially from Clavius’s *Epitome arithmeticae practicae*.

#### Portuguese–Chinese dictionary

Compiled in Macau, approximately 1583–1588, by Ruggieri and Ricci with Chinese assistance.

**Status:** Collaborative manuscript; original untitled and unpublished during Ricci’s lifetime.

### Collected editions

#### 《天學初函》 *Tianxue chuhan*

Edited by Li Zhizao, 1628.

**Status:** Posthumous anthology containing ten Ricci-related religious and scientific works along with writings by other missionaries and Chinese collaborators. It is not an author-controlled “complete works.”

#### *Fonti Ricciane*

Edited by Pasquale M. d’Elia, 1942–1949.

**Status:** Modern critical documentary collection of Ricci’s history, letters, and related sources.

#### Modern Chinese collected works

Collections titled *Li Madou Zhongwen zhuyiji* or *Matteo Ricci: Writings and Translations in Chinese* assemble genuine, collaborative, derivative, and posthumously published texts. Individual editorial notes must be consulted before assigning sole authorship.

Important critical literature includes:

## Death, Burial Site, and Present Canonical Status

Ricci died in Beijing on 11 May 1610 and was buried at Zhalan in 1611.

The cemetery later held numerous Jesuits, including Johann Adam Schall von Bell and Ferdinand Verbiest. It passed through Jesuit, Vincentian, Orthodox, and Catholic administration after the suppression of the Society of Jesus.

### Destruction and restoration

- **1900:** The Boxer movement damaged and desecrated the cemetery; remains were dispersed and monuments broken.
- **1903:** The Qing government supplied funds for restoration pursuant to the post-Boxer settlement.
- **Mid-twentieth century:** Many tombstones were moved; the site became part of a government educational or administrative campus.
- **Cultural Revolution:** The cemetery and church structures suffered further damage.
- **1979 onward:** Ricci’s, Schall’s, and Verbiest’s monuments were restored; additional stones were re-erected in the 1980s.
- **2006:** The site received national-level protected cultural-relic status in China.

The visible tomb is therefore a historically restored memorial centered on an original inscribed monument, not an untouched seventeenth-century site.

### Cause of canonization

A diocesan cause began in Macerata and was transferred to Roman consideration. In December 2022 Pope Francis authorized the decree recognizing Ricci’s heroic virtues. The Catholic Church consequently calls him “Venerable.”

This is an ecclesiastical judgment about virtue, not a historical court finding. As of 1 September 2026, no beatification or canonization was identified in the sources consulted.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### In the Jesuit mission

Ricci’s successors preserved his writings, institutional network, and accommodation principles, though not without disagreement. His death exposed uncertainties about ancestor rites, terminology for God, direct versus indirect evangelization, and the treatment of Buddhism.

The later successes of Xu Guangqi, Li Zhizao, Yang Tingyun, Schall von Bell, Verbiest, and other missionaries were built partly on the legal and intellectual position Ricci secured.

### Chinese Rites Controversy

The “Ricci rules” became evidence in a dispute lasting into the eighteenth century. Opponents argued that Jesuits had compromised Christianity; defenders argued that Roman authorities misunderstood Chinese civil ritual.

The papal prohibition of the rites and Qing resistance to that intervention helped reduce the mission’s position. In 1939 the Catholic Church permitted participation in rites interpreted as civil homage, bringing official policy closer to Ricci’s broad position.

### European image of China

Trigault’s Latin adaptation disseminated Ricci’s descriptions widely. European readers learned of:

- the examination system;
- civil government by a literate elite;
- Chinese printing and education;
- urban scale and economic production;
- Confucian moral philosophy;
- China’s identification with Marco Polo’s “Cathay.”

His account contributed to later European admiration for China but also transmitted stereotypes and missionary judgments.

### Science and translation

The *Jihe yuanben* influenced Chinese mathematical vocabulary and proof culture. Ricci’s maps helped reshape geographic knowledge in China, Korea, and Japan. His romanization samples and dictionary work belong to the early history of European Chinese linguistics.

His astronomy was Ptolemaic and became outdated after the telescopic and theoretical transformations associated with Galileo and Kepler. Historical claims that Ricci delivered the entire Scientific Revolution to China are anachronistic.

### Modern Chinese commemoration

Modern Chinese institutions frequently commemorate Ricci as a pioneer of Sino-Western cultural exchange while treating his missionary religion more cautiously. Documentaries, museums, schools, academic conferences, and monuments emphasize science, geography, language, and friendship.

This selective civic image differs from Catholic veneration but draws on genuine parts of his career.

### Catholic reputation

Catholic institutions present Ricci as a model of inculturation, scholarship, perseverance, and dialogue. His 2022 recognition as Venerable formalized the hagiographic tradition without resolving historical criticism concerning his treatment of Buddhism or the politics of mission.

### Historiographical reinterpretation

The main shifts have been:

1. Seventeenth-century Jesuit founder and holy missionary.
2. Enlightenment source for an idealized learned China.
3. Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century missionary hero.
4. Mid-twentieth-century pioneer of intercultural understanding.
5. Late-twentieth-century subject of “conflict of cultures” and accommodation scholarship.
6. Recent emphasis on Chinese collaborators, printing networks, material objects, translation, and imperial context.
7. Postcolonial scrutiny of knowledge as a missionary and imperial instrument.
8. Renewed Catholic sanctity narrative after the 2022 decree.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supportable beyond mere contemporaneity or shared geography are included.

### Athanasius Kircher

**Evidentiary label: Documented posthumous intellectual connection.**

Kircher never met Ricci: he was born in 1602, eight years before Ricci died, and never traveled to China. In *China monumentis illustrata* (Amsterdam, 1667), Kircher relied heavily on Jesuit reports and books descending from Ricci’s mission. Ricci appears in the book’s imagery, sometimes alongside Xu Guangqi or Johann Adam Schall von Bell.

Nature of connection: posthumous use and mythologization of Ricci’s scientific and missionary legacy within a cross-generational Jesuit knowledge network.

### Marco Polo

**Evidentiary label: Documented textual-geographical connection, not a personal connection.**

Ricci inherited the European problem of whether Marco Polo’s “Cathay” was identical with China. Information collected by the Jesuit Bento de Góis and incorporated into the Ricci–Trigault history helped establish the identification for European readers.

Nature of connection: Ricci’s mission helped resolve and publicize a geographic question created by Marco Polo’s account. No meeting was possible; Polo died more than two centuries before Ricci’s birth.

### Euclid, Aristotle, Cicero, Epictetus, and other classical authors

These figures influenced Ricci’s mathematics, philosophy, and moral writings, but they are not members of the supplied atlas roster except Aristotle.

#### Aristotle

**Evidentiary label: Documented intellectual influence.**

Ricci’s Collegio Romano education was grounded in Aristotelian logic, natural philosophy, metaphysics, and psychology. His *Tianzhu shiyi* carried Aristotelian and Scholastic concepts into Chinese theological argument. This was a textual and institutional influence across centuries, not a personal connection.

### Galileo Galilei

**Evidentiary label: No documented personal connection found.**

Galileo and Ricci were Italian contemporaries and both had connections to Christopher Clavius’s intellectual world. The sources consulted did not establish a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, or explicit influence between them. A link based only on Italian origin, chronology, mathematics, or Clavius would be inferential and is therefore excluded.

### Johannes Kepler

**Evidentiary label: No documented direct connection found.**

Kepler and Ricci were contemporaries, but no meeting or correspondence was found. Ricci’s astronomical teaching remained largely within the Clavian-Ptolemaic framework. Mere participation in the same era of astronomy is not an atlas connection.

### Nicolaus Copernicus

**Evidentiary label: No securely documented direct influence established in the sources used.**

Ricci knew a European astronomical curriculum formed after Copernicus, but he did not introduce Copernican heliocentrism as his public cosmology in China. No personal connection was possible.

### Xuanzang

**Evidentiary label: No documented connection found.**

Both traveled across major cultural boundaries and became later symbols of intercultural transmission, but no source consulted establishes Ricci’s explicit reliance on Xuanzang or a historically transmitted institutional connection.

### Jesus of Nazareth, Paul the Apostle, and Francis of Assisi

Ricci’s Christian vocation necessarily invoked Jesus and Pauline missionary language; the Vatican decree explicitly uses Paul’s “all things to all people” formulation to interpret him. Francis of Assisi was part of the broader Catholic tradition, but no distinctive Franciscan influence was established.

For atlas purposes:

- **Jesus of Nazareth — documented religious allegiance and central object of Ricci’s preaching.**
- **Paul the Apostle — documented textual and missionary model; the connection is doctrinal, not personal.**
- **Francis of Assisi — no specific documented influence beyond shared Catholic tradition; excluded as a substantive connection.**

No reliable crossings were found with the remaining atlas figures. Absence is especially important here: Ricci’s actual networks were dominated by Jesuit superiors, Chinese scholar-officials, Buddhist opponents, court eunuchs, printers, and converts, most of whom are not on the supplied roster.

| Sept. 1583 | Entered the Chinese interior and established the Zhaoqing residence. |
| 1584 | Produced first Zhaoqing Chinese world map; worked on early catechetical texts. |
| 1583–1588 | Worked with Ruggieri and Chinese assistants on an early Portuguese–Chinese dictionary. |
| 1589 | Expelled from Zhaoqing; moved to Shaozhou. |
| 1591 | Death of colleague António de Almeida at Shaozhou. |
| c. 1594–1595 | Abandoned Buddhist-style dress and adopted literati clothing and etiquette. |
| 1595 | Moved to Nanchang; composed *Jiaoyou lun*. |
| 1596 | Composed *Xiguo jifa* on mnemonic techniques. |
| 25 June 1598 | Departed Nanchang on first major journey toward Beijing. |
| 1598 | Brief first stay in Beijing failed to secure residence. |
| 6 Feb. 1599 | Returned to Nanjing. |
| 1599 | Debated Xuelang Hong’en; met or deepened association with Xu Guangqi. |
| 1600 | Produced Nanjing world-map edition; departed again for Beijing with Diego de Pantoja. |
| 1600 | Detained at Linqing by eunuch Ma Tang. |
| Jan. 1601 | Entered Beijing; gifts accepted by the imperial court; permitted to reside there. |
| 1601 | Prepared songs and music associated with court gifts. |
| 1602 | Issued *Kunyu wanguo quantu* with Li Zhizao and Chinese collaborators. |
| 1603 | Published mature *Tianzhu shiyi*; Xu Guangqi baptized as Paul. |
| 1605 | Published *Ershiwu yan*, *Tianzhu jiaoyao*, and *Xizi qiji*. |
| c. 1607 | Nanchang degree-holders reportedly accused missionaries of rebellion or dangerous doctrine; accusation unsuccessful. |
| 1607 | Ricci and Xu Guangqi published Books I–VI of *Jihe yuanben*; scientific collaborations with Li Zhizao continued. |
| 1608 | *Jiren shipian* published; Ricci worked on the Italian history of the China mission. |
| 1609 | *Huanrong jiaoyi* published; final surviving correspondence period. |
| 3 May 1610 | Became seriously ill in Beijing. |
| 11 May 1610 | Died in Beijing, aged 57. |
| 1611 | Buried at Zhalan after an imperial land grant. |
| 1615 | Trigault published the Latin *De Christiana expeditione apud Sinas*. |
| 1616 | Nanjing persecution attacked the mission system Ricci had established. |
| 1628 | Li Zhizao’s *Tianxue chuhan* preserved numerous Ricci works. |
| c. 1630 | Giulio Aleni produced an early Chinese biography. |
| 1667 | Athanasius Kircher’s *China illustrata* incorporated and mythologized the Ricci mission’s legacy. |
| 1700s | Chinese Rites Controversy made the “Ricci rules” a central ecclesiastical dispute. |
| 1900 | Zhalan Cemetery desecrated during the Boxer movement. |
| 1903 | Qing-funded restoration undertaken. |
| 1911–1913 | Tacchi Venturi published major modern editions of Ricci’s historical works. |
| 1942–1949 | Pasquale d’Elia published *Fonti Ricciane*. |
| 1979 | Restoration of the principal Zhalan tombs began. |
| 1984 | Zhalan received Beijing municipal cultural-protection status. |
| 2006 | Cemetery designated a nationally protected cultural relic. |
| 2009–2012 | Major Italian and Chinese documentaries marked renewed public commemoration. |
| 2014 | Canonization cause advanced to Roman consideration. |
| Dec. 2022 | Pope Francis authorized recognition of Ricci’s heroic virtues; Ricci declared Venerable. |
| 2024 | A new four-part Chinese-produced documentary on Ricci was released or publicly promoted. |
| 1 Sept. 2026 | No beatification or canonization identified; canonical status remains Venerable. |
