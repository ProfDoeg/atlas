# Max Perlman / Max Perelman Research Dossier

**Canonical name used in this dossier:** Max Perlman  
**Documented variants:** Max Perelman; Max Perlmann; Maks Perelman  
**Birth name:** Meiers Perlmanis  
**Born:** 1909, Riga  
**Died:** 23 July 1985, Israel  
**Primary fields:** Yiddish actor, comedian, singer, songwriter/composer, dancer, theatrical impresario  
**Geographic centers:** Riga and Kovno/Kaunas; Central and Western Europe; Buenos Aires; South Africa; London; Israel; New York  
**Prepared:** 19 August 2026

---

## Scope and Identification Note

The person reconstructed here is the Yiddish entertainer **Max Perlman**, whose surname appears in Argentine and other records as **Perelman**, **Perlmann**, and, in the 1938 film *Mamele*, **Maks Perelman**. The University of Buenos Aires biographical dictionary of Jewish musicians exiled in Argentina identifies his original name as **Meiers Perlmanis**.

Perlman was born in Riga in 1909, trained in Yiddish and Russian theatre, became a professional performer in Latvia and Lithuania, toured extensively in Europe during the 1930s, appeared in the Yiddish feature film *Mamele* in 1938, and arrived in Buenos Aires with his wife and stage partner Gita/Gitta Galina on **7 June 1939**. The outbreak of the Second World War turned what appears to have been an engagement or visit into a prolonged Argentine base. Buenos Aires became the setting for an important body of immigrant popular song mixing Yiddish, Spanish and lunfardo. From the late 1940s onward his career became exceptionally transnational: South Africa, Britain, Israel, the United States, Argentina, Brazil, continental Europe and Australia all appear in the record.

The description of Perlman as a figure of "Argentine tango" is **partly supportable but requires qualification**. The Robert and Molly Freedman Jewish Sound Archive at the University of Pennsylvania explicitly catalogues his song **"Ven Du Lakhst"** as **Theatre/Tango**, with both words and music credited to Perlman. Argentine scholarship also places him within the Yiddish popular-song world that developed in the same Buenos Aires theatrical ecology as Yiddish tango. A modern promotional notice goes much further, claiming that "two of the best known Argentinian Tangos" were composed by Perlman. No strong source consulted for this dossier substantiates that larger claim or identifies the alleged two canonical Argentine tangos. Perlman is therefore best described as a **major Yiddish popular-theatre and song figure with a documented tango component**, rather than simply as a canonical Argentine tango composer.

A second identification problem concerns a **different Max Perlman** who worked for the American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee in North Africa and Italy during 1943–44. JTA and JDC archival material place that man in wartime relief administration in Tripoli/Bari and later returning to the United States. Those movements conflict with the actor's documented Buenos Aires theatrical activity in 1942–43 and his Argentine/South American career. No evidence found connects the entertainer to that relief official, military intelligence, or wartime clandestine operations.

### Evidence terminology used below

- **Documented:** supported by primary records or several mutually reinforcing archival/scholarly sources.
- **Strong secondary:** supported by a specialist scholarly or archival study but not independently verified here from the original record.
- **Oral history:** reported by a participant or witness; historically useful but identified as recollection.
- **Disputed / conflicting:** reliable sources give materially different versions.
- **Unverified promotional claim:** published, but not backed by evidence located here.
- **No evidence found:** the consulted record does not establish the proposition; this is not proof that it never occurred.

## Family and Ancestry

### Family background in Riga

The University of Buenos Aires biographical dictionary describes Perlman as born in **1909 in Riga into a middle-class Jewish family**. The source does not supply the names of his parents, their professions, his grandparents, or siblings. No birth certificate, synagogue register, civil record, passenger genealogy file, or family memoir identifying those relatives was located in the material consulted for this dossier.

The original form **Meiers Perlmanis** is consistent with a Latvian-form surname, while **Perlman/Perelman/Perlmann** became the professional forms used internationally. The record does not establish whether the change was a formal legal name change, a stage simplification, a transliteration choice, or a combination of these.

### Marriage and theatrical partnership: Gita Galina

Perlman's wife and long-term performing partner was the singer-actress most often called **Gita Galina**, with English-language sources also spelling her name **Gitta** or **Guita**. The UBA dictionary gives her original name as **Guta Israiley**.

There is a source conflict over her birthplace:

- The UBA biographical dictionary identifies her as born **ca. 1907 in Vitebsk, Belarus**.
- Veronica Belling's biography describes her more generally as also coming from Riga.

The UBA entry is the more specific of the two and is tied to Argentine immigration research and CEMLA documentation, so **Vitebsk ca. 1907 is the strongest presently available version**. A possible reconciliation is that Galina was born in Vitebsk but later worked or lived in Riga, causing later theatrical memory to associate her with Riga. That reconciliation remains an inference, not a proven fact.

Perlman and Galina were already a professional pair before emigrating to Argentina. They toured Europe together and entered Argentina together on the same ship in 1939. Their marriage was also a business and artistic unit: advertisements, theatre programs, touring companies, recordings, and later Israeli and American engagements repeatedly pair their names.

The precise date and place of their marriage have not been established.

### Later life and death of Gita Galina

Belling, drawing partly on Menora Zahav's oral history, reports that Galina died of **leukemia approximately two and a half years before Max**. A Wikidata entry, itself citing BillionGraves, gives **6 January 1983** as her death date. Because the underlying cemetery record was not independently inspected here, the exact date should be treated as **provisional**, although it is compatible with Belling's relative chronology.

Belling reports that Galina was buried in Holon and that Perlman was later buried beside her.

No consulted source identifies children of Max and Gita, adopted or biological. It would be an error to turn that silence into a positive assertion that they had none.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Riga, 1909–1920s

Perlman was born in Riga in 1909. According to the UBA dictionary and Belling, his performing life began extraordinarily early. At approximately **six years old**, he sang in the choir of **Hazan Rosovski/Rozovski** and took child roles.

The identification of the cantor's name varies in transliteration, as is common with Yiddish/Russian/Latvian names. The important point is that Perlman's earliest documented formation was not through a conservatory but through **Jewish liturgical and theatrical performance**.

His childhood coincided with an exceptionally unstable political period in the Baltic region: the final years of the Russian Empire, the First World War, revolution, civil conflict, and the emergence of independent Latvia. The consulted Perlman sources do not describe how these events affected his household directly, and no claims about displacement, political allegiance, or wartime experience should be inferred from the broader history alone.

### Peretz Club

Perlman later studied dramatic art at the **Riga Peretz Club**, named for the Yiddish writer I. L. Peretz. Sources describe his formation as encompassing both **Russian and Yiddish theatre**.

This dual formation is important for understanding his later style. He did not emerge solely as a folk singer or cabaret singer. He had theatrical training and developed as a performer capable of acting, dancing, comic timing, songs, revue and operetta. Later descriptions comparing his stage persona to figures such as Maurice Chevalier or Fred Astaire reflect this composite theatrical identity rather than a narrow specialization.

No conventional secondary-school, university, conservatory, or degree record has been found.

## Education and Formation

The reconstructable elements of Perlman's training are:

1. **Child choir and stage work** beginning around age six.
2. **Dramatic arts at the Riga Peretz Club**.
3. Experience in **Russian-language and Yiddish-language theatre**.
4. Professional apprenticeship through the **Riga Nayer Yidishe Teater / New Jewish Theater**.
5. Intensive formation through touring in the Baltic states and across European theatrical circuits.

No named acting teacher beyond the early association with Hazan Rosovski/Rozovski is clearly identified in the consulted sources. No formal dance teacher, voice teacher, or composer teacher has been located.

His later songwriting suggests practical musical literacy and an ear for urban popular forms, but the record does not establish conservatory composition training.

## Early Career

### Riga and Kovno/Kaunas, 1928–1934

By **1928** Perlman was working professionally. The UBA dictionary places him with the **Riga Nayer Yidishe Teater** and describes activity in **Kovno (Kaunas) and Riga from 1928 to 1934**.

The Baltic Yiddish theatre world was part of a wider transnational network. A performer could move among Riga, Kaunas, Warsaw, Vienna, Paris, London, Buenos Aires, New York, Johannesburg and Tel Aviv through personal invitations, touring companies and ethnic theatrical institutions. Perlman's later career is an unusually clear example of that network.

### European touring

During the 1930s he toured in:

- Vienna
- Czechoslovakia
- Poland
- France
- Belgium
- England

Belling supplies additional detail: Perlman and Galina appeared in British Yiddish-theatre centers including **London, Liverpool, Leeds, Manchester and Glasgow** and spent an extended period in **Paris and Belgium**. Perlman also appeared as a guest with Poland's **Skale Theater** and toured Poland before returning to Riga.

Exact dates for every stop have not been reconstructed. The evidence establishes repeated touring rather than one continuous, precisely dated itinerary.

### Relationship with Gita Galina

By this European phase, Gita Galina was his principal partner. Their shared theatrical career predates Buenos Aires. The surviving record emphasizes them as a performing couple rather than preserving details of their private domestic life.

## 1938: *Mamele* and the Polish Yiddish Film World

Perlman's most securely documented screen appearance is the 1938 Yiddish musical comedy **_*Mamele*_**, directed by **Joseph Green and Konrad Tom**, starring Molly Picon.

The National Center for Jewish Film identifies *Mamele* as a 1938 Polish Yiddish production. The NYU Second Avenue Online archive preserves a still/caption in which Max Perlman appears with Molly Picon and other cast members. Film databases credit him as **Maks Perelman** in the role of **Dawid (David) Samet**.

This screen credit is significant for several reasons:

- It proves that Perlman was already integrated into the international Yiddish entertainment world before Argentina.
- It predates his June 1939 arrival in Buenos Aires.
- It provides an additional surname rendering, **Perelman**, independently explaining why Argentine and later searches can split his documentary trail.
- It links him to the same interwar Polish-Yiddish film culture that would soon be destroyed or dispersed by Nazi occupation and the Holocaust.

No evidence found indicates that Perlman wrote the film's screenplay or music. His documented role was as a performer.

## Chronological Life History

## 1939–1947: Buenos Aires and the Southern Cone

### Invitation to Argentina

Belling identifies **Adolf Mide** as the person who invited Max Perlman and Gita Galina to perform in Buenos Aires in 1939. The UBA dictionary gives the immigration detail with unusual precision:

- **Arrival:** 7 June 1939
- **Ship:** *Oceanía*
- **Port of embarkation:** Trieste
- **Traveling together:** Max Perlman and Gita Galina

Their Argentine arrival only months before the outbreak of the Second World War transformed the meaning of the trip. Skura and Fiszman describe Perlman as arriving already internationally known and indicate that what had not necessarily been planned as permanent emigration became a prolonged stay because return to Europe was no longer straightforward.

### Compañía Israelita de Operetas

In Buenos Aires, Perlman and Galina joined the **Compañía Israelita de Operetas**. The principal venues associated with them were **Teatro Excelsior** and the wider Yiddish theatrical circuit that also included Teatro Mitre/Soleil in different periods.

Belling says the couple worked at the Excelsior for roughly three years. Argentine Jewish theatre was then large enough to sustain multiple professional Yiddish houses. Lloica Czackis's study of Yiddish tango notes that in **1939 Buenos Aires had five professional Yiddish theatres**, a figure that helps explain why an international performer could make Buenos Aires a serious career center rather than a peripheral stop.

### Buenos Aires as an immigrant musical laboratory

Perlman's Argentine work is especially important linguistically. Susana Skura and Lucas Fiszman studied approximately **thirty songs associated with Perlman** found in the collections of **Fundación IWO** and the **Jewish Museum of Buenos Aires**. At the time of their research, only a smaller subset was readily playable.

Their analysis describes a repertoire mixing:

- Yiddish
- standard Buenos Aires Spanish
- "Castidish" or hybrid Spanish-Yiddish usage
- lunfardo
- immigrant jokes
- gambling vocabulary
- automobiles and modernization
- courtship and sexuality
- economic aspiration
- changing Jewish social behavior

The songs are therefore evidence not merely of theatre entertainment but of **linguistic and social adaptation among Ashkenazi immigrants in Argentina**.

### Radio

Skura and Fiszman report Perlman's frequent participation in the Buenos Aires **Yidishe Sho / Yiddish Hour** radio program. The exact station and a complete broadcast log have not been reconstructed here. Radio would have amplified his reach beyond theatre audiences and helped circulate songs independently of stage productions.

### Jevel Katz and the "Jewish Gardel" repertoire

The entertainer **Jevel Katz** died in Buenos Aires in 1940. Argentine Yiddish scholarship associates Katz, not Perlman, with the nickname **"the Jewish Gardel."** After Katz's death, Perlman incorporated a number of Katz songs into his own repertoire. Belling and the Digital Yiddish Theatre Project mention this explicitly.

This relationship matters because later recollection can blur Katz and Perlman: both were comic Yiddish singers in Buenos Aires, both adapted local Argentine material, and Perlman performed Katz's songs. The evidence does **not** justify transferring Katz's "Jewish Gardel" epithet to Perlman.

### 9 May 1942: Teatro Excelsior concert

The UBA dictionary documents a performance on **9 May 1942** at Teatro Excelsior featuring **Max Perlman and Gita Galina**, with **Leo Schwarz** conducting, organized by the JKG.

This specific date is valuable because it anchors Perlman physically in Buenos Aires during the war. It is one of the facts that makes identification with the JDC relief official Max Perlman in North Africa/Italy untenable.

### 1943: *Rund um die Operette*

A UBA entry relating to musician Sándor Horvath records a 1943 Buenos Aires production, **_*Rund um die Operette. Una muestra de operetas de compositores judíos*_**, involving Max Perlmann, Gita Galina, Philipp Wenning and others. The program included excerpts from operettas by Jewish composers such as Offenbach, Paul Abraham, Kalman and others, with **Victor Schlichter** in musical direction and **André Laszlo** as regisseur.

Again, this provides a wartime Buenos Aires anchor.

### Songs of Argentine modernization

Several Perlman songs discussed by Skura and Fiszman are closely tied to Argentine urban life:

- **"Benzin"** ("Gasoline") uses the automobile and modern consumption as comic material.
- **"Rulete"** uses roulette/gambling as a social motif.
- **"Poker"** and **"A pokerl"** similarly draw on gambling.
- **"Madagaskar"** refers to the historical idea of Madagascar as a destination proposed for Jewish mass migration.

These songs are not all tangos. Their importance lies in the way they convert contemporary immigrant anxieties and aspirations into musical comedy.

### Regional touring through 1947

The UBA dictionary states that through approximately **1947** Perlman and Galina also performed in:

- Brazil
- Uruguay
- Chile

Buenos Aires remained their principal base during much of this period. Detailed city-by-city itineraries are not preserved in the sources consulted.

## 1948–1953: South Africa, London and the Expansion of the Touring Circuit

### First South African season: 1948–49

Sources differ slightly over whether Perlman's first major South African engagement should be dated **1948** or **1949**, probably because the touring season straddled years or because later recollections rounded the date.

Belling reports that a first musical revue at **His Majesty's Theatre in Johannesburg in 1948** ran for roughly **eight weeks** and drew extremely large Jewish audiences. Her broader narrative also describes South African visits beginning around 1949. An OpenEdition history of South African Yiddish theatre lists visits by Perlman and Galina in **1948, 1949 and 1953**.

The practical conclusion is that the couple entered the South African touring circuit in the **1948–49 period**.

### Sarah Sylvia

The actress and impresario **Sarah Sylvia** played an important role in bringing Perlman and Galina to South Africa. Her own career had included Buenos Aires in the 1920s and 1930s, illustrating how the South African and Argentine Yiddish theatre systems were connected by personnel rather than existing as isolated national cultures.

### Popular success and the "shund" debate

Perlman's South African success triggered or participated in a recurring argument within Yiddish culture: whether popular musical comedy, revue and sexually suggestive or vernacular entertainment constituted **shund**—commercial "trash"—as opposed to more prestigious literary or art theatre.

Belling documents critics and cultural gatekeepers who regarded Perlman's material as light, vulgar or insufficiently elevated, while audiences responded enthusiastically. Similar tensions appear in Skura and Fiszman's Argentine interviews, where some informants remembered the songs as gaudy, vulgar or full of double entendre even while acknowledging their popularity.

This is not a criminal or moral controversy but an important **aesthetic and class-cultural dispute** surrounding his reputation.

### 1951: South Africa and Menora Zahav

Actress-singer **Menora Zahav** later recalled that she first performed with Perlman in Johannesburg in **1951**, when she was approximately fifteen and a half.

Zahav had survived the Holocaust in France with her mother, actress **Niusia Gold**, and a younger sister, after being hidden by a French family. Her family later lived in Johannesburg. She became a recurring collaborator in Perlman's later career and eventually replaced Gita Galina in some ingénue roles as Galina aged.

Belling's detailed information about Zahav derives from interviews conducted with her on **24–25 December 2018**. Statements arising from those interviews should be treated as oral history rather than contemporaneous documentation.

### London

Perlman and Galina also appeared repeatedly in London, including at the **Grand Palais**. Belling identifies London appearances in **1949, 1951 and 1955** and names productions such as:

- *Here Comes Grandpa*
- *The Shlemmazl*
- *The American Litvak*

These engagements show that Perlman's postwar career did not move linearly from Argentina to Israel. It became a recurring circuit between diasporic Yiddish audiences.

### 1952: United States

The UBA dictionary says that Perlman worked in the **United States in 1952** and subsequently alternated American engagements with a home base in Israel. That final phrase appears to compress chronology, because Rachel Rojanski's research places Perlman's **first visit to Israel in 1953**. The safest reconstruction is:

- U.S. activity by 1952 is plausible and supported by the UBA biography.
- A permanent or semi-permanent Israeli home base developed only afterward.
- The UBA summary should not be read as proving Israeli residence before his first 1953 visit.

## 1953–1965: Israel, London and New York

### 1953: first documented visit to Israel

Rachel Rojanski's *Yiddish in Israel: A History* is the strongest source for this phase. She identifies **1953 as Max Perlman and Gita Galina's first visit to Israel**.

This corrects a potentially misleading statement in Skura and Fiszman that can be read as placing Perlman's emigration to Israel already in the 1940s. The evidence assembled by Rojanski, including period press and theatrical permits, supports **1953** as the first visit and the mid-1950s as the period when Israel became an increasingly important home base.

### Hebrew political satire: *Zehirut ha’derech be’tikun*

Soon after arriving, Perlman participated in the Hebrew-language Matate Theater production **_*Zehirut ha’derech be’tikun*_** ("Caution: Roadworks"), described by Rojanski as a **political satirical revue**. A *Davar* notice of 25 October 1953 is cited in her documentation.

Participation in a political satire does not establish a party affiliation. No consulted source identifies Perlman as a member or official of an Israeli political party.

### 1954: Te’atron Komedya

In **1954**, Perlman established his own Yiddish **"Te’atron Komedya" / Comedy Theater** in Israel. Rojanski describes its repertoire as melodramas and musical comedies with songs and jokes.

Named plays associated with this theatre in Israeli documentation include:

- *Der groyser fardiner*
- *Shlimazl*
- *Motl Mayers glikn*
- *Der yidisher gazlen*
- *Alts tsulib parnose*
- later *Yashke zukht a kale*
- later *Der Galitsyaner fun Meksike*

Perlman's theatre performed in Israel intermittently for roughly three decades because he continued to spend long periods abroad.

### Israeli touring geography

Belling reconstructs an extensive Israeli circuit. Places in which Perlman's company performed include, at different times:

- Tel Aviv
- Ramat Gan
- Jaffa
- Holon
- Haifa
- Bat Galim
- Kiryat Motzkin
- Lydda/Lod
- Netanya
- Tiberias
- Givatayim
- Pardes Hanna
- Petah Tikva
- Nahariya
- Tel Hanan
- Kiryat Hayovel in Jerusalem
- Afula
- Yehudia
- communities in Emek Yizrael

This geographical spread is evidence of sustained mass-audience touring beyond the elite Tel Aviv theatre world.

### Yiddish-language regulation in Israel

Rojanski places Perlman's theatre within the larger history of Israeli regulation of Yiddish and other foreign-language theatre. Surviving records include permits from the **Council for the Control of Films and Plays**, such as:

- Permit no. **1500**, 21 November 1960, for *Yashke zukht a kale*
- Theatre permit no. **1557**, 24 March 1961

These permits document the administrative environment in which Yiddish theatre operated. They should **not** be misrepresented as criminal proceedings or evidence that Perlman's theatre was uniquely censored or banned. They are licensing/censorship-era records.

### Ramat Gan apartment: patronage anecdote

Menora Zahav told Belling that **Avraham Krinitzi**, longtime mayor of Ramat Gan, gave Max and Gita a small ground-floor apartment in Ramat Gan, which became their Israeli home base.

This is an important story about civic patronage and the couple's material circumstances, but it is presently an **oral-history claim** in the sources consulted. No property deed, municipal resolution or tax record was independently examined here.

There is no evidence in the consulted material that the alleged gift was investigated as corruption or treated as illegal.

### 1955: return to Buenos Aires

The UBA dictionary documents a **26 August 1955** Buenos Aires performance of **_*Der Amerikanische Littwak*_**, directed by **Hermann Ludwig**, with Perlman and Galina at **Teatro Soleil**.

This demonstrates that even as Israel became a base, Perlman continued returning to Argentina.

### 1956 Argentine record registration

An Argentine **Boletín Oficial** record from 1957 preserves registration information for an Odeón disc produced in Buenos Aires in October 1956. It lists:

- **Face A: "No es como se quiere"** — letra y música: **Max Perelman**
- **Face B: "Dinero y más dinero"** — letra y música: **Max Perelman**
- Odeón disc no. 101
- Buenos Aires
- October 1956
- 300 copies reported in the entry

This is unusually strong primary evidence for Spanish-titled compositions published under the **Perelman** spelling.

The government entry does **not** identify their genre. They should therefore not automatically be called tangos merely because Perlman had a tango connection.

### 1960: *Oy! Vayber Vayber!*

The National Library of Israel preserves a Tel Aviv-Jaffa archival poster for **"Max Perlman and troupe – Oy! Vayber Vayber!"**, dated **11 December 1960**. The item demonstrates his continuing visibility as the named center of a touring company.

### 1961 South African visit

The National Library of Israel holds a group of photographs documenting Perlman during a **1961 South African visit with his troupe**. This provides archival corroboration beyond memoir and press recollection for the persistence of the South African circuit into the 1960s.

### 1962: New York, *Honeymoon in Israel*

In **1962** Perlman appeared at the **Roosevelt Theater** in New York in **_*Honeymoon in Israel*_**.

The Workers Circle's Mlotek Yiddish Song Collection identifies Perlman's **"Kh'vil nisht keyn sakh nor a bisele"** ("I Don't Want Much, Just a Little") as a song with **words and music by Max Perlman**, associated with *A Honeymoon in Israel* and published in **1962**.

### 1963: *Don't Worry, Brother*

Belling records Perlman at the Roosevelt Theater in 1963 in **_*Don't Worry, Brother*_**.

### 1964: *Good Luck*

In 1964 Perlman starred in **_*Good Luck*_** at New York's Anderson Yiddish Theater, with music by **Sholem Secunda**. Surviving NYU program material lists a cast including Max Perlman, Miriam Kressyn, Jacob Jacobs, Gita Galina, Fyvush Finkel, Seymour Rexite and others.

A useful cataloguing correction is necessary. One UBA entry appears to associate Perlman with the role **Zisel**, but the direct cast listing gives:

- **Max Perlman — Yosel Soloveitchik**
- **Gita Galina — Zisel**

The UBA role assignment is therefore very probably a simple role swap or transcription error.

The production mixed Yiddish and English. Belling describes it as approximately 75 percent Yiddish and 25 percent English, demonstrating Perlman's willingness to adapt to changing American Jewish audiences.

## 1966–1974: Argentina, Brazil, New York, South Africa and Europe

### 1966: Argentina

The UBA biography records another Argentine return in **1966**. This fits the broader pattern of Perlman repeatedly revisiting the city where his wartime and early postwar reputation had been established.

### 1967: Argentina and Brazil with Shimon Dzigan

In **1967**, Perlman performed in Argentina and Brazil with the celebrated Yiddish comedian **Shimon Dzigan**.

The pairing is especially interesting because critics and later observers sometimes contrasted their personas. Dzigan's comic image remained more visibly connected to Eastern European Jewish hardship, while Perlman cultivated a more polished, urban, cosmopolitan and at times "Americanized" stage personality.

The National Library of Israel also catalogues a **1967 Makolit** recording involving Perlman, *A Medine An’antikele*. Cataloguing details should be treated cautiously because language metadata in older sound catalogues can be inconsistent.

### *Der Galitsyaner fun Meksike*

Rojanski documents Perlman's staging of **_*Der Galitsyaner fun Meksike*_** in Tel Aviv in 1967, supported by permit/poster records. This further illustrates his use of transnational Jewish-geographical types as comic material.

### 1968: *It's Never Too Late for Happiness*

The NYU Second Avenue Online archive preserves a program for **_*It's Never Too Late for Happiness*_**, described as a remake of *The Lithuanian Yankee*, at the **Anderson Yiddish Theater** in New York in 1968.

The cast included:

- Max Perlman
- Gita Galina
- Leon Liebgold
- Jacob Jacobs
- Miriam Kressyn
- Seymour Rexite
- Diane Cypkin
- Thelma Mintz
- Bruce Adler
- Jacob Zanger
- Samuel Fershko
- Mordecai Yachson
- Fraydele Oysher

This cast list places Perlman squarely inside the mature New York Yiddish-theatre network.

### 1969: *Oh What a Wedding!*

Belling records Perlman in the 1969 Anderson Theatre production **_*Oh What a Wedding!*_**, associated with writer G. Yisraelev, music by **Murray Rumshinsky**, and lyrics by **Jacob Jacobs**.

By this stage **Menora Zahav** increasingly took over parts once performed by Gita Galina. The change appears to have been an age/role-type transition rather than a rupture between Perlman and Galina; Galina remained his wife and longtime partner.

### 1968–1974 international touring

The UBA dictionary summarizes **1968–1974** as another period of touring through:

- the United States
- South Africa
- Europe

The compressed biographical entry does not provide a complete itinerary.

### 1972 South Africa

Belling identifies another South African visit in **1972** and reproduces highly favorable contemporary critical responses. One critic, Chaim Lewis, treated Perlman as a consummate stage artist. Such notices confirm that several decades after his first Johannesburg season he was still a recognizable star for Yiddish-speaking audiences there.

## 1975–1985: Late Career

The documentary trail thins in the later 1970s, but Perlman continued operating through an international Yiddish performance circuit centered increasingly on Israel and the United States.

### 1981: *Dem shmids tekhter*

In **October 1981**, Perlman appeared in Peretz Hirshbein's **_*Dem shmids tekhter* / *The Blacksmith's Daughters*_** at New York's Norman Thomas Theater, in a production associated with **Ben Bonus and Mina Bern**.

Perlman played an elderly grandfather figure. This was a markedly different age type from the urbane young comic and romantic performer of earlier decades.

### Gita Galina's death, ca. January 1983

As noted above, Galina died before Perlman, reportedly of leukemia. A database-derived date of **6 January 1983** is plausible but has not been independently verified here from the original burial record.

Her death removed the partner who had accompanied virtually every major phase of Perlman's career since before Argentina.

### 1984: last New York tour

Belling identifies **1984** as Perlman's last New York tour, with the Shalom Yiddish Musical Comedy Theatre production **_*Oy Mama! Am I in Love*_**. Cast references include Mary Soreano/Soriano and other veterans of the Yiddish stage.

### 1985: final tour and illness

Perlman remained active into his final year. The UBA dictionary summarizes his 1985 activity as including appearances in the **United States, Australia and Israel** with Menora Zahav. Belling's reconstruction emphasizes a **final tour to Sydney, Australia**.

After returning to Israel exhausted, Perlman was found to have **advanced/final-stage cancer**. The sources consulted do not identify the cancer type, date of diagnosis, treating institution or details of therapy.

He died on **23 July 1985**.

Belling reports that he was buried in **Holon**, next to Gita.

## Political / Religious / Intellectual / Artistic Development

### Jewish cultural identity

Perlman's career was structurally rooted in Jewish/Yiddish culture from childhood: cantor's choir, Peretz Club, Yiddish theatre, Yiddish radio, Jewish immigrant audiences and later Israeli Yiddish theatre.

The record does not establish the degree of his personal religious observance. He should not be classified as Orthodox, secularist, atheist or otherwise on the basis of repertoire alone.

### Linguistic adaptation in Argentina

His Argentine songs are particularly valuable documents of **cultural hybridization**. Skura and Fiszman show that his comic language could move among Yiddish, Spanish and lunfardo. This was not merely translation. The language itself staged immigrant adaptation, aspiration, embarrassment, generational change and social mobility.

### The modern city

Recurring motifs include:

- automobiles
- gasoline
- traffic
- gambling
- money
- courtship
- work and livelihood
- the tension between old communal norms and modern consumer life

Rather than writing nostalgic "Old World" repertoire alone, Perlman made the modern urban environment itself the comic subject.

### Zionism / Israel

Several later songs and stage works use Israel as a setting or object of affection and satire. Belling describes a movement in his repertoire toward celebration of Jewish statehood, national self-confidence and self-defense.

This supports describing Perlman as participating artistically in **pro-Israel/Zionist cultural discourse**, especially after 1948. It does **not** establish formal party membership or ideological consistency on specific Israeli political questions.

### Political satire

His immediate participation in the 1953 Hebrew Matate political revue establishes that he was willing to perform current political satire. No evidence found shows that he held office, served as a political adviser, financed a party, or was employed by a government.

### Popularism versus high culture

Perlman's most persistent intellectual/artistic controversy was the debate over **shund**. Critics who wanted literary Yiddish theatre could regard musical comedy, topical jokes, mixed-language songs and sexual double meanings as debased commercial entertainment. Perlman's defenders and audiences treated those same features as immediacy, accessibility and vitality.

This opposition shaped his later canonization: he was widely popular but underrepresented in some histories that privileged literary theatre.

## The Tango Connection: Evidence, Context, and Limits

Because Perlman is sometimes described online through the lens of Argentine tango, the evidence should be separated carefully.

### 1. Direct evidence: "Ven Du Lakhst"

The **Robert and Molly Freedman Jewish Sound Archive** at the University of Pennsylvania catalogues:

**"Ven Du Lakhst"**
- Author: Max Perlman
- Composer: Max Perlman
- Genre: **Theatre/Tango**
- Known recordings include Perlman's own theatre/song albums and later performances by other artists.

This is direct archival evidence that Perlman wrote at least one work understood as a tango within the Yiddish-theatre repertoire.

### 2. Buenos Aires Yiddish tango context

Lloica Czackis's study of Yiddish tango describes 1930s Buenos Aires as a major center of Yiddish song and theatre in which Jewish musicians, lyricists, singers and publishers engaged deeply with tango. She includes Max Perlman among prominent figures of the Buenos Aires Yiddish stage.

The same context produced explicitly Yiddish tangos, parodies, translated tangos and songs that borrowed tango rhythm or imagery without entering the canonical Spanish-language tango repertory.

### 3. Spanish-language Perlman compositions

The Argentine Boletín Oficial documents Perlman/Perelman as lyricist and composer of:

- "No es como se quiere"
- "Dinero y más dinero"

Both were issued by Odeón in 1956. The primary record does **not** call them tangos. Their existence proves Spanish-titled Argentine composition, not tango genre.

### 4. What has not been established

A modern event promotion for **"Tango Nuevo: A Musical Exploration of Jews & Tango"** states that "two of the best known Argentinian Tangos were composed by Max Perlman."

The consulted promotion does not name the two songs or cite a source. No authoritative tango database, Argentine government record, scholarly biography, Yiddish-theatre archive or specialist study consulted here substantiates that formulation.

Accordingly:

- **Documented:** Perlman wrote at least one Yiddish theatre tango, "Ven Du Lakhst."
- **Documented:** he wrote/recorded songs in Buenos Aires and at least two Spanish-titled compositions were registered there.
- **Documented:** he belonged to the Buenos Aires Yiddish theatrical milieu in which tango was pervasive.
- **Unverified:** that he wrote "two of the best-known Argentine tangos."
- **Not established:** that he should rank with canonical Spanish-language tango composers solely on existing evidence.

### 5. Possible source of confusion: Jevel Katz

Jevel Katz, not Perlman, is the Buenos Aires Yiddish entertainer repeatedly described as **"the Jewish Gardel."** Perlman's performance of Katz songs after Katz's death may have helped blur their reputations in later memory.

## Namesake Problem: the Wartime JDC "Max Perlman"

A separate Max Perlman appears in wartime Jewish relief records.

JTA reported a **Max Perlman** heading a Joint Distribution Committee office in Bari, and JDC archival references place a man of that name in North African/Italian relief operations around **1943–44**. A history of Jewish displaced persons in Italy describes that Max Perlman returning to the United States by the end of 1944.

The entertainer, however, is specifically documented:

- at Teatro Excelsior in Buenos Aires on 9 May 1942;
- in the 1943 Buenos Aires operetta program *Rund um die Operette*;
- as based in Argentina/South America during this phase.

A simultaneous career as a JDC field administrator in wartime North Africa and Italy would require evidence that does not exist and conflicts with the theatrical chronology.

**Conclusion:** the JDC administrator should be treated as a **different Max Perlman** unless new primary evidence establishes otherwise. No intelligence, refugee-relief or military connection should be imported into the actor's biography from the namesake.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth / disputed narrative 1: "Max Perlman composed two of the best-known Argentine tangos"

**What the story says:**  
A modern promotional page for a Jewish-tango event states that two of the best-known Argentine tangos were composed by Max Perlman, who lived in Argentina from 1939 to 1942.

**Where it appears:**  
Promotional copy for "Tango Nuevo: A Musical Exploration of Jews & Tango."

**Who circulated it:**  
A modern cultural-event promoter; the page does not identify an original historical source for the claim.

**Evidence for:**  
Perlman undeniably lived and worked in Buenos Aires beginning in 1939; the Freedman archive classifies his "Ven Du Lakhst" as Theatre/Tango; Argentine records document his songwriting.

**Evidence against / missing:**  
The promotional page does not identify the alleged two tangos. Specialist Argentine/Yiddish tango scholarship consulted here does not repeat the claim. The official 1956 record entry does not describe his Spanish songs as tango.

**Reputational effect:**  
The claim can transform a major Yiddish theatrical songwriter into a supposedly canonical Spanish-language tango composer, obscuring the more interesting and better documented hybrid reality.

**Status:** **Unverified promotional claim.**

### Myth / disputed narrative 2: Perlman as "the Jewish Gardel"

**What the story says:**  
Later retellings can associate the "Jewish Gardel" image with Perlman's Argentine fame.

**Evidence:**  
Scholarship consulted here attributes the epithet to **Jevel Katz**, who died in 1940. Perlman subsequently sang Katz material.

**Status:** **Likely conflation.**

### Myth / disputed narrative 3: Argentina as his place of origin

South African and other diaspora memories sometimes present Perlman essentially as an "Argentine" entertainer because he arrived from Buenos Aires and carried Argentine-Yiddish material.

**Documentary correction:**  
He was born in Riga, trained in the Baltic Yiddish theatre, and had a European career before arriving in Buenos Aires at age about thirty.

**Historical significance:**  
Calling him Argentine is not wholly meaningless: Argentina profoundly shaped his repertoire and was a long-term professional base. But it describes a cultural formation, not his birthplace.

### Myth / disputed narrative 4: immigration to Israel in the 1940s

Skura and Fiszman's overview can be read as suggesting emigration to Israel in the 1940s.

**Competing documentation:**  
Rachel Rojanski, using Israeli press and archival permits, identifies **1953 as Perlman and Galina's first visit to Israel**.

**Status:** **Chronological conflict; 1953 is better documented.**

### Myth / disputed narrative 5: Gita Galina's birthplace

**Version A:** Vitebsk, ca. 1907 — UBA biographical dictionary.  
**Version B:** Riga — Belling's narrative.

**Status:** **Unresolved conflict; Vitebsk is presently the more specific documentary version.**

### Myth / oral legend 6: Krinitzi's apartment gift

Menora Zahav remembered that Ramat Gan mayor Avraham Krinitzi gave the couple a small apartment.

**Evidence:** oral testimony reported by Belling.  
**Missing:** deed, municipal file, property registration.  
**Status:** **Plausible oral history, not independently verified here.**

### Myth / oral legend 7: Golda Meir in the audience

Bruce Oppenheimer remembered Golda Meir attending a Jerusalem Perlman performance around 1971–72.

**Evidence:** witness recollection.  
**Status:** **Oral anecdote; no broader political inference warranted.**

### Myth / false lead 8: JDC/intelligence identity

The wartime relief official Max Perlman is a separate namesake on present evidence.

**Status:** **Rejected identification absent new evidence.**

## Books, Articles, and Major Works About Max Perlman

### Veronica Belling, "S'iz nisht dos vos amol iz geven: Max Perlman (1909–1985)"
Digital Yiddish Theatre Project, University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee, 2020.

This is the most substantial accessible narrative biography located. Its strengths are its integration of press history, theatre chronology, South African material, songs and **original oral-history interviews with Menora Zahav**. *Journal of Jewish Languages* 4 (2016), pp. 231–251.

The most important specialist study of Perlman's Argentine songs. It uses the Perlman corpus to analyze Yiddish-Spanish linguistic change, modernity, gambling, social mobility and immigrant acculturation.

The Centro de Estudios Migratorios Latinoamericanos is cited by the UBA biographical dictionary in connection with immigration data. The 7 June 1939 arrival is therefore not merely a theatrical anecdote but grounded in migration research.

### Argentine press

The UBA dictionary cites period material from **La Semana Israelita**, including notices corresponding to dated performances.

### Israeli State Archives

Rojanski cites theatre-permit files in the Israel State Archives, including permit numbers for Perlman's theatre and productions. These are key primary sources for reconstructing his Israeli repertory and the administrative environment of Yiddish theatre.

### South African archival material

Belling's work and other histories draw on South African Jewish press and theatre collections. The National Library of Israel's 1961 photographs provide an additional accessible visual record.

## Awards, Honors, Offices, Appointments, and Dismissals

No formal state decoration, major prize, elected office or official cultural appointment was securely identified.

His measurable forms of recognition were instead:

- repeated international bookings over nearly five decades
- large South African audiences
- ability to establish and sustain a named Yiddish comedy theatre in Israel
- repeated New York engagements
- extensive recording circulation
- continued posthumous inclusion in Yiddish humor and tango anthologies

The alleged apartment gift from Mayor Krinitzi may represent civic patronage but is not an "award" in the formal sense.

No documented dismissal from office or institution was found.

## Death / Later Life / Present Status

Perlman died **23 July 1985**, following a final period in which he was still touring internationally. The combination of UBA and Belling suggests he remained active in **Australia, Israel and possibly the United States** during his last year.

Belling's account is that the final Australian/Sydney tour left him severely exhausted, after which physicians discovered terminal cancer. He died soon afterward in Israel and was buried in Holon beside Gita.

No probate file, will, estate inventory or obituary containing family survivors was located in this research.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### 1. A transnational Yiddish entertainer rather than a single-country figure

Perlman's life resists national categorization:

- born in Riga
- professionalized in Baltic Yiddish theatre
- filmed in Poland
- toured Western and Central Europe
- transformed artistically in Buenos Aires
- became a major visitor to South Africa
- established a theatre in Israel
- repeatedly worked in London and New York
- returned to Argentina and Brazil
- ended his career after an Australian tour

His biography maps the global geography of twentieth-century Yiddish theatre.

### 2. Argentine Jewish linguistic history

His Argentine songs preserve a moment when Yiddish-speaking immigrants encountered Buenos Aires slang, mass consumption, cars, casinos, Spanish-speaking neighbors and new forms of Jewish identity.

For linguists and cultural historians, this may be his most distinctive legacy.

### 3. Tango

His place in tango history is real but narrower than some publicity claims:

- a documented Theatre/Tango composition exists;
- he belongs to the Buenos Aires Yiddish-tango environment;
- his repertoire participated in the Argentine popular-song ecosystem.

The evidence currently available does not justify turning him into the composer of unnamed "two best-known Argentine tangos."

### 4. The problem of "shund"

Perlman's relative absence from some high-literary histories is itself historically meaningful. His material was often popular precisely because it was topical, vulgar, hybrid, comic and commercially responsive—the qualities that high-cultural gatekeepers could use to exclude it from the canon.

The later recovery of his work by scholars of language, Yiddish theatre and Jewish tango reflects a broader expansion of what counts as historically significant culture.

### 5. Recorded legacy

Perlman's records survive in major Jewish music collections, including the National Library of Israel and the Freedman Jewish Sound Archive. His songs have been reissued and in some cases re-recorded by later performers.

### 6. Theatre-history legacy

Programs, posters and photographs preserved by NYU and Israeli archives make it possible to reconstruct an international career that would otherwise disappear into short biographical notices.

## Atlas Connections

### Gita Galina

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Galina was Perlman’s wife, longtime stage partner, and co-leader of their Israeli Yiddish Comedy Theater. They performed together across Europe before emigrating aboard the *Oceanía* to Buenos Aires on **7 June 1939**, and subsequently worked together in Latin America, South Africa, Britain, Israel, and the United States. Contemporary records specifically place them together at Buenos Aires’s Teatro Excelsior on **9 May 1942** and Teatro Soleil on **26 August 1955**. Their partnership continued until Galina’s declining health and death in 1983. [University of Buenos Aires biographical dictionary](https://publicaciones.filo.uba.ar/sites/publicaciones.filo.uba.ar/files/Diccionario%20tomo%20II_interactivo.pdf); [Digital Yiddish Theatre Project biography](https://uwm.edu/yiddish-stage/siz-nisht-dos-vos-amol-iz-geven-max-perlman-1909-1985/); [Rachel Rojanski, *Yiddish in Israel*](https://dokumen.pub/yiddish-in-israel-a-history-0253045142-9780253045140.html)

### Golda Meir

- **[T3] Reported fact:** Bruce Oppenheimer recalled that Prime Minister Golda Meir attended a Perlman-company Yiddish performance at Jerusalem’s **Mitchell Theatre in 1971 or 1972**, entering after the lights had gone down and sitting in the audience. This supports Meir seeing Perlman perform, but it remains a retrospective eyewitness account rather than a contemporaneously verified attendance record; it does not establish a personal meeting or political relationship. [Digital Yiddish Theatre Project biography](https://uwm.edu/yiddish-stage/siz-nisht-dos-vos-amol-iz-geven-max-perlman-1909-1985/)
