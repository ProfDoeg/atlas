# Henri Bergson — Research Dossier

**Henri-Louis Bergson (1859–1941)**  
French philosopher, professor, public intellectual, wartime diplomatic emissary, first president of the League of Nations' International Committee on Intellectual Cooperation, member of the Académie française, and recipient of the 1927 Nobel Prize in Literature.

> **Methodological note.** This dossier distinguishes documented fact from later report, recollection, interpretation, rumor, and myth. Bergson is unusually vulnerable to biographical legend because he ordered the destruction of much private material, because his celebrity generated contemporary folklore, and because later Catholic, Jewish, philosophical, political, and scientific narratives repeatedly recruited episodes from his life for their own purposes. Where strong sources disagree, the disagreement is retained rather than silently resolved.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Henri-Louis Bergson; generally published as **Henri Bergson**.
- **Alternative forms:** Henri Louis Bergson; occasionally “Henri-Louis Bergson” in reference works. No durable literary pseudonym is known.
- **Birth:** **18 October 1859**, Paris, France, in the 9th arrondissement; biographical sources place the family in the **rue Lamartine**.
- **Death:** **4 January 1941**, Paris, at his home at **47 boulevard de Beauséjour**, 16th arrondissement. A number of secondary sources give 3 January; the Nobel Foundation and the Académie française use 4 January, and 4 January is preferred here.
- **Burial:** cemetery of **Garches**, Hauts-de-Seine, west of Paris.
- **Nationality:** French. He became a French citizen in **1878**; because of his English mother and family connections he could have pursued British nationality but chose France.
- **Languages:** French and English from childhood; educated in Latin and Greek; wrote his supplementary doctoral thesis in Latin.
- **Principal occupations:** lycée teacher; university lecturer; professor at the École normale supérieure; professor at the Collège de France; philosopher and author; member and officer of learned academies; wartime cultural diplomat; international intellectual-cooperation official.
- **Best-known philosophical terms:** **durée** (duration), **intuition**, **mémoire** (memory), **multiplicity**, **élan vital**, the distinction between **closed/open morality and religion**, and the **closed/open society**.
- **Nobel Prize:** Nobel Prize in Literature for **1927**, formally awarded “in recognition of his rich and vitalizing ideas and the brilliant skill with which they have been presented.” Illness prevented his travel to Stockholm.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father: Michał / Michel Bergson

Bergson's father was the pianist and composer **Michał Bergson** (also Michel Bergson; surname forms include Bergsohn/Bereksohn), born in Warsaw. Sources disagree on the year: **1818** appears in Emily Herring's archival-based biography/excerpt, while **1820** appears in several reference traditions. The disagreement is retained here rather than silently harmonized. He trained and worked across Germany, Italy, France, and Switzerland. His compositions included operatic and instrumental music; the 1859 Paris operetta *Qui va à la chasse perd sa place* briefly enjoyed success.

The paternal family belonged to a notable Polish-Jewish commercial lineage. Modern genealogical and Jewish-historical sources connect the Bergson/Bereksohn line to **Berek (Berko) Sonnenberg/Bereksohn**, to the philanthropist **Temerl Bergson**, and farther back to **Szmul (Shmuel) Zbytkower**, an eighteenth-century Warsaw merchant and court supplier. The precise genealogical spelling varies across Polish, French, German, and Jewish sources, and later biographies sometimes compress several generations. YIVO's entry on Zbytkower provides independent documentation for the prominence of this commercial family.

The romanticized claim that Henri came from a uniformly prosperous upper-middle-class household is contradicted by Louis M. Greenberg's archival study. Michel's adult musical career was unstable. In **1863** he obtained a post at the Geneva Conservatory, but conflicts with the institution ended in resignation in **1867**. Conservatory minutes cited by Greenberg include severe criticism of his teaching and temperament. Michel even requested money to cover rent when leaving Geneva. The family subsequently experienced real financial insecurity.

### Mother: Katherine / Kate Levison

Bergson's mother was **Katherine (Kate) Levison**, born in England into an English-Jewish family; sources describe her father as a Yorkshire physician or doctor. She married Michel in Paris in **1856**. She spoke English to the children, making Henri effectively bilingual. Bergson remembered her with exceptional affection and described her intelligence, serenity, religious sensibility, goodness, and devotion. In private he reportedly called her “Sweet Mim.”

### Siblings

Archival work establishes that Michel and Katherine had **seven children — three sons and four daughters**. Henri was **the second child and eldest son**, not the first child, as many older biographies imply.

Children securely identified in the sources consulted include:

- **Juliette Bergson**, born about **1858**, Henri's elder sister; a London marriage record cited by Greenberg records her 1883 marriage to Theodor Cohn.
- **Henri-Louis Bergson**, born 1859.
- **Joseph Bergson**, a younger brother, born in the early 1860s; archival Paris birth records are cited by Greenberg.
- **Mina / Minna Bergson (1865–1928)**, later **Moina Mathers**, artist and occultist; born in Geneva on 28 February 1865.
- **Rachel Elizabeth Bergson**, born in Paris on **1 December 1867**, according to the civil record cited by Greenberg.
- A younger son, **Zaleq Philip Bergson**, is identified in Joyce scholarship and British records as the writer who used the name **Philip Beaufoy / Philip Beaufoy Barry**. Sources conflict on whether he was born in 1871 or 1878; the former is supported by later Joyce scholarship using civil evidence. He is noteworthy because the name “Philip Beaufoy” appears in James Joyce's *Ulysses*. This was apparently not a deliberate allusion to Henri.
- One further daughter is implied by the reliable three-sons/four-daughters count, but her identity was not established to the same standard in the web-accessible sources consulted for this dossier.

### Moina Mathers and the occult connection

Henri's sister Mina, who adopted the name **Moina Mathers**, became the wife and magical collaborator of **Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers**, one of the founders of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn. She was an artist trained at the Slade School, became an early initiate of the Golden Dawn, participated in its ritual work, and after MacGregor Mathers's death headed the Alpha et Omega successor organization.

This produces a striking family juxtaposition: Henri publicly approached psychical research through philosophical and quasi-scientific inquiry while his sister became a ritual occultist. There is **no good evidence that Henri was a Golden Dawn member or participant**. Any attempt to infer Golden Dawn influence on his philosophy from kinship alone belongs to speculation, not documented intellectual history. The siblings also appear to have been estranged for periods.

### Marriage, Marcel Proust, and daughter Jeanne

Bergson married **Louise Neuburger/Neuberger**, a relative of Marcel Proust. A common English-language date is 1891, but French chronology based on family/civil sources gives **7 January 1892**. The later date is stronger and is used here. **Marcel Proust served as a witness/best man** at the marriage, making Bergson and Proust cousins by marriage.

Their only child was **Jeanne Adèle Bergson**, an artist — draughtswoman, painter, and sculptor — who studied with **Antoine Bourdelle**. A widespread secondary-source date gives her birth as 1896, but Paris-based biographical and art-historical records give **16 March 1893**, Paris, and death **23 October 1961**. Jeanne was deaf; some older accounts describe her as deaf-mute. The 1893 date is preferred here, while the persistent 1896 date should be treated as a secondary-source error.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1859–1878

Henri was born into a mobile household. His father's career carried the family among **Paris, Geneva, and London**, while the son's academic ability eventually fixed him in Paris.

### Paris and Geneva

In **1863**, when Henri was about four, Michel moved the family to **Geneva** for his Conservatory appointment. Henri began his education there and showed exceptional ability. After Michel's professional collapse in Geneva, the family returned temporarily to Paris in difficult financial circumstances.

The **Chief Rabbi of Geneva, Joseph Wertheimer**, according to archival-based accounts, helped secure a scholarship enabling Henri to live at a Jewish boarding institution in Paris and attend what was then the Lycée Bonaparte, later renamed Lycée Fontanes and ultimately again **Lycée Condorcet**.

### Separation from his family

Around age nine or ten, a decisive split occurred. Michel could not re-establish a viable career in Paris, and the rest of the household relocated to **London**, where he supported the family principally through private music teaching. Henri remained behind in Paris for roughly a decade of schooling.

Greenberg's archival reconstruction is important here: Bergson's later reputation as a serenely bourgeois Parisian intellectual can obscure that his childhood involved paternal professional failure, unstable family finances, repeated migration, and an early separation from his mother and siblings. Bergson later said that before age ten he was already treated “as an adult” by the family. Psychobiographical claims connecting this rupture directly to his later philosophy of effort, creativity, duration, and personality are hypotheses, not demonstrable causal facts.

### Franco-Prussian War and Paris Commune

His Paris schooling overlapped the **Franco-Prussian War (1870–71)** and the **Paris Commune (1871)**. Later he recalled the repeated political renaming of his lycée — Bonaparte, Condorcet, Fontanes, and again Condorcet — as an early lesson in the instability of human affairs. Whether the violence of the Commune directly shaped his philosophy cannot be established; his own later recollection emphasized institutional and political mutability rather than a trauma narrative.

### School excellence and mathematics

At Condorcet/Fontanes Bergson distinguished himself in both humanities and mathematics:

- **1875:** first prize in rhetoric in the Concours général.
- **1876:** first prize in philosophy.
- **1877:** first prize in mathematics.

His solution to a mathematics problem posed for the **1877 Concours général** was published in the *Nouvelles annales de mathématiques* in **1878**, under the title “Question proposée au concours général de 1877, pour la classe de mathématiques élémentaires.” This is an important corrective to the simplification that Bergson was “anti-scientific”: his earliest published work was mathematical, and his philosophical arguments were made by someone with serious youthful mathematical training.

A famous classroom anecdote has his mathematics teacher lamenting that Bergson “could have been a mathematician” but would instead become “a mere philosopher.” The remark is transmitted through later biography and is best treated as a well-established anecdote rather than a stenographically documented utterance.

## Education and Formation, 1878–1889

### École normale supérieure

In **1878** Bergson entered the humanities section of the **École normale supérieure (ENS)** in Paris. His cohort placed him among figures who would become central to the Third Republic's intellectual culture, notably **Jean Jaurès** and **Émile Durkheim**.

At the ENS Bergson initially admired **Herbert Spencer**, whose evolutionary philosophy and attempt at systematic scientific synthesis became an important stimulus — eventually a target. Bergson later presented his mature conception of duration partly as an escape from what he saw as Spencer's tendency to spatialize time and treat genuine becoming as rearrangement of already-defined elements.

Among formative French philosophical figures were **Jules Lachelier** and **Félix Ravaisson**. Ravaisson's spiritualist philosophy, insistence on habit, and non-mechanistic account of life form part of the French background of Bergsonism. Exact teacher/student relations are sometimes overstated in summaries; the safest formulation is that Lachelier and Ravaisson were major institutional and intellectual influences in the philosophical milieu through which Bergson was trained.

### Agrégation and first appointment

Bergson completed the ENS course in **1881** and sat the highly competitive **agrégation de philosophie**. Strong sources place him **second**, with Jean Jaurès third. The identity/order of the first-ranked candidate varies in derivative sources, so this dossier does not repeat unverified rankings beyond Bergson's own second place. One examination lecture concerned the value of contemporary psychology.

Immediately afterward he entered the state teaching system.

### Angers, 1881–1883

Bergson taught philosophy at the lycée in **Angers** beginning in 1881. The Collège de France chronology also records teaching literature at the **École supérieure de jeunes filles d'Angers** in 1882. This early employment placed him inside the expanding educational apparatus of the Third Republic.

### Clermont-Ferrand, 1883–1888

In **1883** he moved to **Clermont-Ferrand**, teaching at the lycée and later lecturing at the local university faculty. These years were intellectually productive and more empirically oriented than the later stereotype of Bergson as a purely intuitive metaphysician suggests.

His work included:

- an edition/study of selected passages from **Lucretius**;
- close attention to contemporary psychology and medicine;
- observation of **hypnosis** and suggestion;
- the 1886 article **“De la simulation inconsciente dans l'état d'hypnotisme”**, an early scholarly publication on unconscious simulation in hypnotic states.

Bergson's interest in hypnosis foreshadowed his lifelong concern with memory, consciousness, dissociation, and evidence at the boundaries of ordinary psychology.

### Doctorate

In **1888** he submitted the two theses required for the French doctorate; the principal thesis appeared as a book in **1889**:

1. **Essai sur les données immédiates de la conscience** (1889), later translated as *Time and Free Will*.
2. **Quid Aristoteles de loco senserit** (1889), a Latin supplementary thesis on Aristotle's conception of place.

The *Essai* developed the distinction between measurable, spatialized time and lived **duration (durée)**. It also framed freedom not as an uncaused puncture in causal order but as action expressing the developing whole of a person.

## Early Career and Rise, 1889–1907

### Return to Paris

After the doctorate Bergson returned to Paris. He taught briefly at the **Collège Rollin** and then at the **Lycée Henri-IV**, where he remained through much of the 1890s. Students connected with these years include **Alfred Jarry** and later sociologist **Maurice Halbwachs**.

### Marriage and domestic life

Bergson married Louise Neuburger in January **1892**. Their daughter Jeanne followed in 1893. Compared with his sister Moina's public occult and bohemian life, Henri cultivated a private, controlled domestic existence. Surviving sources reveal comparatively little about the marriage because of the destruction and dispersal of private papers.

### *Matière et mémoire* (1896)

**Matière et mémoire. Essai sur la relation du corps à l'esprit** appeared in **1896**. Bergson had spent years studying neurological and psychological literature. The book argued against both reductive materialism and a simple substance dualism. The brain, in his model, is centrally an organ of selection and action rather than a warehouse in which memories are literally stored. He distinguished habitual/body memory from a more expansive memory of past experience and developed the famous image of the **cone of memory**.

Later neuroscience did not simply vindicate Bergson, and some empirical claims belong to nineteenth-century psychology. His importance lies partly in formulating a non-reductive problem of memory and brain function that continues to provoke philosophers of mind and historians of psychology.

### Dreyfus Affair: documented non-engagement

During the **Dreyfus Affair**, Bergson — Jewish by birth and already intellectually prominent — **did not join the public Dreyfusard campaign**. Stanford's biography and later scholarship state that he refused to participate in the public debate. This is a significant political fact because contemporaries such as Émile Zola, Lucien Herr, Charles Péguy, and Jean Jaurès made the Affair central to their public identities.

The reasons should not be invented. Later interpreters have variously read his silence as political reserve, assimilation, aversion to public faction, personal caution, or failure of solidarity. The documentary fact is non-participation; the psychological explanation remains interpretive.

### École normale supérieure and Collège de France

Bergson sought a higher academic chair more than once. In **1898** he was appointed to teach at the **École normale supérieure**, remaining there for approximately two years.

In **1900** he entered the **Collège de France**. The institutional sequence is slightly complicated:

- He competed for the chair of modern philosophy; **Gabriel Tarde** obtained it.
- Bergson instead succeeded **Charles Lévêque** in the chair of Greek and Latin/ancient philosophy.
- After Tarde's death, Bergson moved to the **chair of modern philosophy in 1904**, which he held until his retirement/relief from teaching duties around 1920–21.

### *Le Rire* (1900)

**Le Rire. Essai sur la signification du comique** was published as a book in **1900**, gathering essays first published in the *Revue de Paris*. It analyzed comedy as a social phenomenon: laughter corrects rigidity, automatism, or “the mechanical encrusted upon the living.” It became one of his most accessible and durable works.

### Académie des sciences morales et politiques

Bergson was elected to the **Académie des sciences morales et politiques** in **1901**, consolidating his place in the French institutional elite.

### “Introduction à la métaphysique” (1903)

In **1903** the *Revue de métaphysique et de morale* published **“Introduction à la métaphysique.”** It became a compact statement of his philosophical method. Bergson contrasted analytic knowledge, which uses symbols and perspectives, with an **intuition** intended to enter into the movement or duration of the thing studied.

“Intuition” here did not mean casual hunch or anti-rational impulse. It was a disciplined philosophical method intended to reverse habitual intellectual operations that cut continuous becoming into static units.

## International Celebrity and “Bergsonism,” 1907–1914

### *L'Évolution créatrice* (1907)

The publication of **L'Évolution créatrice** in **1907** transformed Bergson from a major French professor into an international celebrity. The book treated biological evolution as genuinely creative and temporally irreversible. Its most famous term was **élan vital** — roughly “vital impetus.”

Bergson opposed both a mechanical picture in which the future was entirely implicit in the past and a finalism in which evolution unfolds a pre-existing plan. Evolution for him was branching, inventive, wasteful, contingent, and creative.

### What *élan vital* was — and was not

Later polemics often turned *élan vital* into a cartoon “mystical life force.” Bergson did use vitalist language and argued that mechanistic explanation was insufficient for life, but the concept functioned primarily as part of a philosophical account of evolutionary creativity rather than as a measurable new physical force. It was criticized by biologists and philosophers who regarded it as explanatory obscurity. Other readers found it a way to articulate historical novelty that neither mechanism nor predetermined teleology captured.

### The Bergson boom

Between about 1907 and the First World War, Bergson's Collège de France lectures became extraordinary public events. Students, writers, foreign visitors, diplomats, and society figures crowded the lecture hall. Reports emphasize unusually large numbers of women, leading hostile journalists and polemicists to feminize both Bergson and his audience.

Popular nicknames for women followers included terms such as **“bergsoniennes,” “caillettes,” and “snobinettes.”** The allegation was often that fashionable women attended because Bergson was chic rather than because they understood philosophy. Modern historians, notably Emily Herring, have shown that this rhetoric tells as much about gendered policing of intellectual life as it does about Bergson's actual audience.

### Charles Péguy

**Charles Péguy** became one of the most important Bergsonian writers. Bergson's thought on duration, creativity, and freedom entered Péguy's literary-political universe, although Péguy was never simply a spokesman for Bergson. Their association also mattered during Catholic controversy over Bergson's influence.

### William James

Bergson and **William James** developed a major intellectual friendship. James admired Bergson's attack on intellectualism and helped carry Bergsonism into the English-speaking world. Their correspondence records mutual respect but not identity of doctrine: pragmatism and Bergsonism overlapped around experience, novelty, and criticism of rigid conceptualism while remaining distinct philosophies.

### Other readers and students

Among those who attended, studied, or engaged seriously with Bergson were **Étienne Gilson, Jean Wahl, T. S. Eliot**, and many younger French philosophers. His influence extended into literature and aesthetics. Later critics and interpreters associated him with Proust, modernism, Cubism, and new conceptions of cinematic time, though direct influence varies case by case and should not be assumed merely from thematic similarity.

### Opponents: Bertrand Russell and rationalist criticism

**Bertrand Russell** became one of Bergson's best-known philosophical critics, attacking his use of intuition and arguing that his approach weakened the authority of intellect. Russell's caricatures had enormous afterlife in Anglophone philosophy and contributed to the image of Bergson as a seductive but irrational literary thinker.

In France, critics came from several incompatible directions: rationalists, neo-Thomists, nationalist writers, positivists, and academic rivals. That ideological diversity is important: Bergsonism was not the proprietary philosophy of a single political camp.

### Sorel, politics, and appropriation

**Georges Sorel** drew on Bergsonian themes in developing his theory of myth, violence, and revolutionary syndicalism. The relationship was principally one of intellectual appropriation rather than proof that Bergson endorsed Sorel's political program. Bergson's concepts were also appropriated by writers on the nationalist right and by Catholic modernists, socialists, liberals, and avant-gardists. “Bergsonism” became a cultural vocabulary wider than Bergson's own politics.

### International lectures, 1911

Bergson's British lecture circuit in **1911** included:

- Oxford lectures on **“The Perception of Change”**;
- the **Huxley Lecture** at the University of Birmingham on “Life and Consciousness”;
- four lectures at the University of London on **“The Nature of the Soul.”**

Oxford awarded him an honorary doctorate.

### United States, 1913

Bergson made his first major American visit in **1913**, invited by **Columbia University**. Columbia librarians prepared *A Contribution to a Bibliography of Henri Bergson*, with an introduction by **John Dewey**, as a welcome gift. He arrived in New York in early February and lectured on subjects including **“Spirituality and Liberty”** and philosophical method. His American itinerary also brought him into contact with other universities and intellectual circles.

#### The “first Broadway traffic jam” story

A famous story says that the crowd for Bergson's Columbia lecture caused **the first traffic jam on Broadway**. The episode is repeated by the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, the 1914–1918 Online encyclopedia, and modern biographies. Contemporary enthusiasm and crowding are well documented; the stronger superlative “first traffic jam in Broadway history” is difficult to establish from the sources consulted. It is best treated as **celebrity folklore built around a real mass-attendance event**.

### Psychical research and the Society for Psychical Research

Bergson became president of the London-based **Society for Psychical Research (SPR)** and in 1913 delivered the presidential address **“Fantômes de vivants et recherche psychique” / “Phantasms of the Living and Psychical Research.”**

His interest was not a late eccentricity. It continued the earlier hypnosis work and his broader argument that ordinary brain-bound models might not exhaust the field of consciousness. He took testimony about telepathy and related phenomena seriously enough to demand investigation.

**Evidence status:** Bergson's participation in psychical research is documented. Claims that this establishes the reality of telepathy, survival after death, or spirit communication do not follow. Conversely, accounts that omit this work because it embarrasses a “respectable” philosophical image are historically incomplete.

### Gifford Lectures, 1914

In **May–June 1914** Bergson delivered the first series of eleven **Gifford Lectures** at the University of Edinburgh under the title **“The Problem of Personality.”** The First World War prevented the planned continuation. Full authorized lecture texts were not published in his lifetime; summaries, notes, and later reconstructions survive.

### Académie française

Bergson was elected to the **Académie française** in **1914**, succeeding the statesman and historian **Émile Ollivier**. His formal reception took place on **24 January 1918**. Some reference sources call him the first Jewish member of the Académie française; because “Jewish” can be defined by birth, confession, or self-identification and because earlier members require separate prosopographical checking, that superlative should be used cautiously.

### Vatican Index, 1914

On **1 June 1914**, the Roman Catholic **Congregation of the Index** placed major Bergson works on the *Index Librorum Prohibitorum*. Scholarship on the proceedings shows that the condemnation emerged from the Modernist controversy and anxieties over Bergson's treatment of evolution, intellect, metaphysics, and religion. The works targeted included his major philosophical books then in circulation.

This later sits beside the paradox that Bergson himself moved increasingly toward Catholic Christianity. The Church's institutional condemnation of his books and his private religious attraction are both documented and should not be collapsed into a single “Catholic Bergson” story.

## First World War: Propaganda, Diplomacy, and Politics, 1914–1919

The war marked Bergson's most direct entry into state politics.

### War rhetoric

From August 1914 Bergson publicly aligned himself with the French war effort. In speeches and articles he framed the conflict in philosophical terms as a struggle between creative/spiritual civilization and mechanized German imperialism.

Significant texts include:

- an August 1914 address to the Académie des sciences morales et politiques;
- **“La force qui s'use et celle qui ne s'use pas”** in the *Bulletin des Armées de la République* (4 November 1914);
- **“La signification de la guerre”**, delivered 12 December 1914 and translated as *The Meaning of the War: Life and Matter in Conflict* (1915);
- wartime essays later collected or republished in *Écrits et paroles* and *Mélanges*.

He explicitly described the conflict as a struggle of “civilization” against “barbarism.” These writings are primary examples of **intellectual mobilization and wartime propaganda**. They do not amount to secret intelligence work, but they do show a philosopher placing his prestige at the service of national strategy.

### Spain mission, 1916

France used prominent intellectuals in cultural diplomacy to influence neutral countries. Bergson traveled to **Spain in 1916** as part of a French mission that included major cultural and scientific figures. The group engaged Spanish elites, and Bergson had contact with **King Alfonso XIII**. A contemporaneous memoir records the mission's presence at the **Ateneo de Madrid**.

Some summaries date his first political mission to Spain in 1917. Research specifically on the Madrid lectures and mission gives **1916**, which is preferred here; the discrepancy illustrates how Bergson's diplomatic chronology has often been simplified.

### Secret United States mission, early 1917

In **January 1917**, the French government sent Bergson on a **confidential/secret diplomatic mission to the United States**. His task was to use his American reputation and access to **President Woodrow Wilson** in support of French objectives and American entry into the war.

Historian Geert Somsen's archival study shows that Bergson's utility lay precisely in his apparent universality: as a philosopher rather than conventional politician, he could present the French cause as the cause of civilization rather than naked national interest. Bergson met Wilson and appealed to the president's internationalist ambitions, including the possibility of a postwar league of nations.

The United States entered the war in April 1917. It would be an exaggeration to say Bergson “caused” American intervention. German unrestricted submarine warfare, the Zimmermann Telegram, financial and strategic considerations, Wilson's own changing policy, and numerous diplomatic efforts were decisive. Bergson was one component of a larger persuasion effort.

### Further American mission, 1918

Bergson returned to the United States in **1918**, during a longer mission lasting roughly from May into September according to wartime-biographical sources. He interacted with official and intellectual networks concerned with Allied cooperation and postwar organization.

### René Viviani and official networks

Bergson was connected to the French diplomatic mission led by **René Viviani** and later wrote a preface to Viviani's *La Mission française en Amérique*. His wartime network included French officials, diplomats, academics, and Wilsonian interlocutors.

### Intelligence connections?

No source consulted establishes Bergson as an intelligence officer, spy, covert operative in the professional sense, or participant in clandestine violence. His **1917 mission was secret diplomacy**, which is sometimes loosely retold as “intelligence work.” The accurate characterization is government-authorized confidential political and cultural diplomacy.

## Postwar Internationalism, Einstein, and Institutional Leadership, 1919–1925

### *L'Énergie spirituelle* (1919)

In **1919** Bergson published **L'Énergie spirituelle. Essais et conférences**, bringing together essays and lectures on consciousness, mind/body relations, dreams, memory, intellectual effort, and psychical questions. The English translation appeared as *Mind-Energy*.

### Withdrawal from regular teaching

By about **1919–1921**, Bergson withdrew from normal Collège de France teaching. In **1920** Cambridge awarded him a doctorate. The Collège de France relieved him of lecture duties so he could devote himself to the projected work on morality, religion, and society. **Édouard Le Roy** increasingly replaced him in teaching and later succeeded him institutionally.

### League of Nations and intellectual cooperation

Bergson became the first president/chair of the League of Nations' **International Committee on Intellectual Cooperation (ICIC/CICI)**, serving from **1922 to 1925**. Members included figures such as **Albert Einstein** and **Marie Curie**.

The committee aimed to restore and institutionalize international intellectual exchange after the destruction and nationalist mobilization of the war. It addressed scientific cooperation, education, scholarly exchange, intellectual property, and international cultural relations. It later gave rise to the **International Institute of Intellectual Cooperation** in Paris, an institutional ancestor — though not a simple one-to-one predecessor — of UNESCO.

There is a structural irony: Bergson had been a vigorous French wartime propagandist, then became a leading postwar organizer of intellectual internationalism. Historians such as Somsen interpret the ICIC partly as an attempt to rebuild transnational authority from the very networks that had been mobilized nationally during the war.

### Einstein encounter, 6 April 1922

On **6 April 1922**, during Einstein's visit to Paris, Bergson participated in a celebrated meeting of the **Société française de philosophie** devoted to relativity. Other participants included leading mathematicians and physicists such as Paul Langevin, Jacques Hadamard, Élie Cartan, Paul Painlevé, Jean Becquerel, and others.

Bergson did not simply reject relativity. He accepted the physical theory's mathematical achievements while disputing what he saw as philosophical extrapolations about lived time and simultaneity. The exchange with Einstein was brief but became emblematic of a supposedly absolute clash between “philosophy” and “physics.”

### *Durée et simultanéité* (1922)

Bergson published **Durée et simultanéité. À propos de la théorie d'Einstein** in 1922 and revised it through multiple editions. It attempted to reconcile or delimit physical relativity and philosophical duration while defending a sense in which a universal lived simultaneity remained meaningful.

The book generated sustained criticism from physicists and philosophers. Bergson later chose not to keep it in the standard corpus of works he wanted reprinted. Édouard Le Roy later claimed that Bergson admitted insufficient mathematical command of developments in general relativity and therefore preferred to drop the matter. Yet Bergson's will did not explicitly ban the book, and editors restored it posthumously.

A **November 1924 letter** is crucial: Bergson complained that relativist physicists had misunderstood him and explicitly called relativity “a very great advance” in both physics and philosophy. Thus “Bergson rejected Einstein” is too crude.

### The myth that Einstein simply “defeated” Bergson

A powerful twentieth-century narrative casts the Paris exchange as a duel in which Einstein scientifically defeated an obsolete metaphysician. Jimena Canales's *The Physicist and the Philosopher* challenged that memory by reconstructing the broader cultural dispute over what clocks, consciousness, physics, and philosophy are entitled to say about time. Physicists and historians continue to disagree over the technical merits of Bergson's arguments.

The historiographically secure points are:

1. Bergson accepted relativity as physics.
2. He believed philosophical questions about lived time were not exhausted by relativistic coordinate systems.
3. Several of his technical arguments were rejected by leading physicists.
4. He later ceased promoting the book.
5. The episode's later reduction to a single decisive “defeat” is itself part of the history of disciplinary boundary-making.

## Health, Honors, and Late Work, 1925–1934

### Rheumatoid arthritis

From the mid-1920s Bergson suffered increasingly severe **rheumatoid arthritis / deforming rheumatism**. A medical-historical note by Marcel-Francis Kahn specifically diagnoses his condition as rheumatoid arthritis. It progressively restricted movement and eventually left him severely disabled.

Sources record repeated efforts at treatment, including stays in clinics and thermal treatments, without durable relief. His health substantially reduced travel, lecturing, and public activity.

### Nobel Prize in Literature

The Swedish Academy awarded Bergson the **1927 Nobel Prize in Literature**. The award was publicly associated with the literary power of his philosophical prose as much as with the doctrines themselves. Because of illness, he could not travel to Stockholm for the ceremonies associated with the award.

The prize is sometimes dated **1928** because the 1927 prize was announced/presented in the following cycle. Official Nobel records designate him the **1927 laureate**.

### Legion of Honour and other honors

Bergson's honors accumulated over decades:

- Officer of the **Légion d'honneur** (1907).
- Honorary doctorate, **Oxford** (1911).
- Member of the **Académie française** (elected 1914; formally received 1918).
- Commander of the Légion d'honneur (1919).
- Honorary degree, **Cambridge** (1920).
- Grand Officer of the Légion d'honneur (1923).
- Nobel Prize in Literature (1927).
- Foreign honorary membership in major academies, including American learned bodies.
- **Grand Cross of the Légion d'honneur** (1930).

### *Les Deux Sources de la morale et de la religion* (1932)

After a long silence caused partly by illness and the scale of the project, Bergson published **Les Deux Sources de la morale et de la religion** in **1932**.

The book distinguished:

- **closed morality**, linked to social obligation, cohesion, and group preservation;
- **open morality**, associated with creative moral personalities and universal love;
- **static religion**, serving social and protective functions;
- **dynamic religion**, associated with mysticism and transformative contact with creative life.

Bergson treated major Christian mystics as empirical evidence of a kind of religious experience. The book intensified speculation that he was moving toward Catholicism.

The terms **open society** and **closed society** later became famous through Karl Popper, though Popper's political use should not simply be identified with Bergson's moral-religious distinction.

### *La Pensée et le mouvant* (1934)

His final major lifetime collection, **La Pensée et le mouvant. Essais et conférences**, appeared in **1934**. It brought together methodological essays including “Introduction à la métaphysique,” “Le possible et le réel,” and reflections on philosophical intuition, change, and the relation between metaphysics and science.

## Religious Development: Judaism, Catholicism, Mysticism

### Jewish background and assimilation

Bergson was born to Jewish parents and grew up in a Jewish family milieu, but his adult public philosophy was not confessional Judaism. He was highly assimilated into the French republican academic elite and generally avoided presenting himself as a Jewish communal thinker.

His non-participation in the Dreyfus campaign is often invoked in this context, though it cannot by itself prove indifference to Jewish identity.

### Attraction to Christianity

By the 1930s Bergson had moved intellectually and spiritually toward **Catholic Christianity**. His **1937 testamentary statement** says that his reflections had brought him increasingly close to Catholicism, which he regarded as the “complete fulfillment” or completion of Judaism. He said he would have converted had he not seen a great wave of antisemitism preparing to spread over the world; he wished to remain among those who would be persecuted.

This statement is central and must be handled exactly:

- **Documented:** Bergson expressed strong Catholic attraction and a belief that Catholicism completed Judaism.
- **Documented:** he deliberately refrained from baptism/conversion in the context of antisemitism.
- **Not securely documented:** a completed sacramental conversion before death.
- **Later claim/rumor:** some Catholic writers and oral traditions imply a secret conversion, baptism of desire, or essentially Catholic death. The documentary record does not establish formal baptism.

A later letter from Louise Bergson to Emmanuel Mounier reiterated that he had resolved **not to be baptized** because of antisemitic persecution.

### Funeral prayers

Accounts differ over the exact religious arrangements around Bergson's funeral. Catholic prayers may have been said, and Bergson's Catholic sympathies were known among intimates. Such rites do not by themselves establish formal conversion.

## Vichy France, Jewish Registration, and Final Months, 1940–1941

### Anti-Jewish law

After France's defeat in 1940, Bergson remained in occupied Paris. The German occupation authorities ordered the registration of Jews in the occupied zone in autumn 1940; the Vichy regime simultaneously promulgated its first **Statut des Juifs** on **3 October 1940**, excluding Jews from major areas of public life.

Bergson's international fame and honors made exceptional treatment possible. Contemporary Jewish reporting stated that he **refused exemption** from anti-Jewish restrictions and chose to share the legal status imposed on other Jews.

Later accounts say that, despite severe arthritis, he went or was taken to his local police station in Passy to register as Jewish. A widely repeated version has him entering on the form a string equivalent to: “Academic. Philosopher. Nobel Prize winner. Jew.” The exact wording should be treated as reported tradition unless the original registration form is produced.

### Did he renounce his honors?

Several later biographies state that Bergson resigned posts or returned honors rather than accept privileged exemption. The general pattern of refusing exemption is well attested; details about exactly which decorations or titles he formally returned vary and are not equally documented in the readily accessible primary record.

### The yellow-star error

Some otherwise reputable modern accounts say Bergson refused exemption from **wearing the yellow Star of David**. This is **chronologically impossible**. Bergson died in January 1941. The compulsory wearing of the yellow star by Jews in the German-occupied zone of France began in **June 1942**. The authentic episode concerns **registration and legal exemption under anti-Jewish measures**, not the star.

This error is an instructive case of later memory compressing separate stages of persecution into a single symbolic act.

### Did standing in the cold cause his death?

A persistent story says the elderly, arthritic Bergson waited in a cold queue to register as a Jew, caught the respiratory illness that killed him, and therefore effectively died as a consequence of refusing privilege.

**Evidence status:**

- His decision to register/refuse exemption is well attested.
- His severe arthritis is medically and biographically documented.
- He died shortly afterward from a pulmonary/respiratory illness.
- Stanford explicitly labels the cold-in-the-registration-line causal story a **rumor**.
- The Académie française's memorial states that he died of **“congestion pulmonaire”**; later accounts often say pneumonia or bronchitis.
- Direct medical evidence tying the fatal illness causally to the registration queue has not been established in the sources consulted.

The story therefore belongs in the dossier as **a powerful and plausible-sounding memorial narrative, not a proven causal chain**.

## Death

Henri Bergson died at his Paris home, **47 boulevard de Beauséjour**, on **4 January 1941**, aged eighty-one. The official Académie française memorial described the immediate cause as **pulmonary congestion**; later biographical sources use pneumonia or bronchitis. Severe rheumatoid arthritis had already left him frail and partially immobilized.

A small funeral followed under occupation conditions. **Paul Valéry**, speaking for the Académie française, delivered an official memorial tribute on **9 January 1941**. Bergson was buried at **Garches**.

His widow **Louise** survived him; their daughter Jeanne preserved at least part of his study and possessions. Louise died during the war years, according to French biographical chronologies; Jeanne died in 1961.

## Political / Religious / Intellectual / Artistic Development

### Intellectual trajectory in compressed form

1. **Mathematics and classical education** — adolescent excellence in mathematics; strong classical philosophical formation.
2. **Spencer and evolutionary mechanism** — early enthusiasm followed by dissatisfaction with spatialized models of time.
3. **Duration and freedom** — *Essai* (1889): lived time is qualitative, heterogeneous, cumulative.
4. **Brain, action, and memory** — *Matière et mémoire* (1896): perception and memory cannot be reduced straightforwardly to cerebral storage.
5. **Social comedy** — *Le Rire* (1900): laughter responds to mechanical rigidity in life.
6. **Intuition as method** — “Introduction à la métaphysique” (1903): philosophical access to movement from within.
7. **Creative evolution** — *L'Évolution créatrice* (1907): novelty is real; *élan vital* names the creative impetus of life.
8. **Psychical research** — consciousness may exceed ordinary models of brain function; extraordinary claims deserve disciplined investigation.
9. **War philosophy** — 1914–18: his categories were nationalized into a moral contrast between French/Allied creative spirit and German mechanism.
10. **Internationalism** — postwar: intellectual cooperation and League of Nations institutions.
11. **Relativity controversy** — 1922 onward: defense of philosophical/lived time against reduction to measured coordinate time.
12. **Mysticism and open morality** — *Deux Sources* (1932): religion, moral creativity, and mystical experience.
13. **Catholic convergence without formal conversion** — late testamentary attraction to Catholicism combined with deliberate Jewish solidarity under antisemitism.

### Political profile

Bergson was never a conventional party politician. He did not become a parliamentary figure like Jaurès. His political activity was episodic but at times consequential:

- silence during the Dreyfus Affair;
- wartime patriotic intellectual mobilization;
- official French diplomatic missions;
- support for an international postwar league;
- leadership of League of Nations intellectual cooperation;
- refusal of privileged exemption from anti-Jewish persecution in 1940.

Attempts to classify him simply as left, right, liberal, nationalist, conservative, socialist, or Catholic political thinker all miss the shifting uses made of his philosophy and his relatively sparse partisan commitments.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

No credible evidence was found that Bergson committed fraud, corruption, money laundering, criminal violence, or ordinary financial crime. The serious controversies surrounding him were intellectual, political, religious, and institutional.

### 1. Dreyfus Affair silence — documented

He did not publicly campaign for Dreyfus. This is documented and has been criticized by later writers. Motives remain uncertain.

### 2. Vitalism and science — documented controversy

Biologists and philosophers disputed whether *élan vital* explained anything or merely renamed the unexplained. Bergson's argument opposed reductionist mechanism but did not supply a testable biological force. Later histories of biology often treat the doctrine as scientifically superseded while historians of philosophy treat its account of novelty separately.

### 3. “Irrationalism” — polemical characterization

Russell and many later analytic philosophers portrayed Bergson as an enemy of reason. Bergson himself repeatedly defended precision and saw intuition as a disciplined supplement/correction to analytic intellect, not the abolition of reasoning. “Irrationalist” is therefore a **critical label**, not a neutral self-description.

### 4. Gendered celebrity backlash — documented cultural campaign

The feminization of Bergson's audience became a weapon against his philosophical legitimacy. Women attendees were portrayed as fashionable, eroticized, gullible, or intellectually unserious. Antisemitic and nationalist critics could combine this with depictions of Bergson as foreign, feminine, decadent, or un-French.

### 5. Action Française and nationalist hostility — documented

Writers around **Action Française** attacked Bergsonism as a threat to Cartesian rationality and French classical order, often in an environment where antisemitism and nationalism were explicit. These attacks are historically important regardless of the philosophical validity of separate arguments against Bergson.

### 6. Vatican Index — formal ecclesiastical condemnation

On 1 June 1914 major works were formally placed on the Catholic Index. This was a real institutional sanction, not rumor. It later coexisted with Bergson's own increasing Catholic attraction.

### 7. First World War propaganda — documented

Bergson used highly moralized national categories, including “civilization” versus “barbarism,” in support of the French war effort. Later scholarship places him within the broad wartime mobilization of European intellectuals. The source record does not justify converting this into allegations of war crime or operational intelligence activity.

### 8. Einstein and relativity — documented technical/philosophical dispute

Bergson's 1922 book was heavily criticized; he later withdrew it from active promotion. Whether this represents a decisive philosophical defeat, a technical error with surviving philosophical value, or a disciplinary misunderstanding remains contested. His own correspondence explicitly praised relativity.

### 9. Psychical research — documented participation, disputed phenomena

He investigated and defended the legitimacy of investigating telepathy and related phenomena. That participation is factual; the paranormal conclusions remain scientifically disputed.

### 10. Religious conversion — disputed narrative

Formal Catholic conversion before death is not established. His strong attraction to Catholicism is established. His decision to remain publicly/unbaptizedly Jewish under antisemitism is also established through family testimony and testamentary material.

### 11. Vichy exemption and registration — documented core, embellished details

Refusal of exceptional treatment and Jewish registration are well attested. Specific dramatic details — exact registration wording, return of every honor, causal link between a cold queue and his death — vary in evidentiary quality.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: Bergson came from comfortable bourgeois wealth

**Story:** He was the cultivated son of a prosperous cosmopolitan musician, naturally progressing into elite Parisian education.  
**Where it appears:** older summary biographies and encyclopedia compression.  
**Evidence:** Greenberg's archival research shows Michel's Geneva career collapsing, rent difficulties, repeated migration, and the young Henri being supported through scholarships and separated from his family.  
**Effect:** the wealthier version makes Bergson's career look more socially predetermined and hides childhood precarity.

### Myth 2: “You could have been a mathematician; you will be a mere philosopher”

**Story:** a mathematics teacher lamented Bergson's choice of philosophy.  
**Appearance:** transmitted through major later biographies and the SEP.  
**Evidence:** plausible and deeply embedded in biographical tradition; not an independently stenographed contemporary record in the sources consulted.  
**Effect:** dramatizes the later Einstein episode by making Bergson the mathematician who “chose” philosophy.

### Myth 3: Bergson caused Broadway's first traffic jam

**Story:** crowds going to his Columbia lecture in 1913 produced the first traffic jam in Broadway history.  
**Appearance:** twentieth- and twenty-first-century biographies, SEP, 1914–1918 Online, popular essays.  
**Evidence:** very large crowds are credible and well attested; the exact “first ever” superlative is much harder to verify.  
**Effect:** it became the perfect emblem of the otherwise hard-to-imagine period when a metaphysician was a mass celebrity.

### Myth 4: *élan vital* is literally a supernatural fluid or occult force

**Story:** Bergson explained evolution by positing a mysterious invisible energy.  
**Appearance:** hostile simplifications and textbook caricature.  
**Evidence:** Bergson unquestionably used vitalist language and rejected mechanistic sufficiency, but his argument was aimed at the temporal creativity and branching novelty of life, not the laboratory discovery of a new physical fluid.  
**Effect:** helped identify Bergsonism with anti-scientific mysticism.

### Myth 5: Henri's philosophy came from his Golden Dawn sister

**Story:** because Moina Mathers was a major occultist, Bergson's intuition, mysticism, or psychical research derived from Golden Dawn esotericism.  
**Appearance:** occult-oriented speculation and modern associative retellings.  
**Evidence:** kinship is certain; Moina's occult career is certain; Henri's psychical research is certain. Direct Golden Dawn doctrinal transmission to Henri has not been demonstrated.  
**Effect:** creates an appealing occult genealogy but exceeds the documentary record.

### Myth 6: Einstein “destroyed” Bergson in one debate

**Story:** Bergson publicly challenged relativity, Einstein refuted him, and philosophy lost authority over time.  
**Appearance:** histories of modern science/philosophy and later polemic.  
**Evidence:** the 6 April 1922 exchange occurred, Bergson published *Durée et simultanéité*, and physicists rejected important claims. But Bergson accepted relativity's physical achievement and the dispute concerned the scope of physical versus lived time.  
**Effect:** became a foundational story about the twentieth-century separation of science and philosophy.

### Myth 7: Bergson prevented Einstein from receiving a Nobel Prize for relativity

**Story:** controversy generated by Bergson's critique helped force the Nobel committee to honor Einstein for the photoelectric effect instead of relativity.  
**Appearance:** modern popular histories surrounding the Einstein–Bergson debate.  
**Evidence:** Einstein's Nobel citation did emphasize the photoelectric effect and theoretical physics more generally; the Nobel deliberations over relativity were complex and included skepticism from multiple scientists. A direct causal claim that Bergson himself prevented a relativity Nobel is not established by the sources consulted.  
**Effect:** magnifies the Paris dispute into institutional scientific history.

### Myth 8: Bergson converted secretly to Catholicism

**Story:** he became Catholic at the end of life, perhaps secretly.  
**Appearance:** Catholic commemorative literature, oral traditions, later biographies.  
**Evidence:** his testament unmistakably expresses deep movement toward Catholicism; Louise Bergson's testimony says he deliberately resolved **not** to be baptized while Jews were persecuted. No baptismal document establishing formal conversion was located.  
**Effect:** he became a symbolic figure in Catholic-Jewish debates about conversion, fulfillment, and solidarity.

### Myth 9: Bergson refused to wear the yellow star

**Story:** Vichy/Nazi authorities offered him exemption from the yellow star and he refused.  
**Appearance:** modern encyclopedia and memorial retellings.  
**Evidence against:** he died 4 January 1941; the compulsory yellow-star order in occupied France took effect in June 1942.  
**Underlying true story:** he refused privileged exemption from anti-Jewish measures and registered as Jewish in 1940.  
**Effect:** later memory replaced an administrative act of solidarity with the Holocaust's most recognizable visual symbol.

### Myth 10: registering as a Jew killed him

**Story:** he stood for hours in winter cold to register, contracted pneumonia, and died.  
**Appearance:** postwar biographies and Jewish/Catholic memorial narratives; SEP explicitly calls it rumor.  
**Evidence:** registration, frailty, and subsequent respiratory death are real; medical causation is unproven.  
**Effect:** converts his last political gesture into martyr-like narrative form.

### Myth 11: Louise burned every paper, including a half-written masterpiece

**Story:** obeying Bergson's will, Louise threw his papers into the fireplace, including a major unfinished manuscript.  
**Appearance:** longstanding Bergson scholarship; SEP labels the unfinished-manuscript component rumor.  
**Evidence:** Bergson did direct destruction of private papers, and the archival record is strikingly thin compared with contemporaries. Yet not literally everything disappeared: correspondence, manuscripts of printed works, lecture notes/stenographic records, family documents, and later acquisitions survive in multiple repositories. The “half-written masterpiece” is not securely documented.  
**Effect:** contributed to the aura of a philosopher who controlled his posthumous image and left scholars reconstructing a deliberately thinned archive.

## Films, Documentaries, Interviews, Recordings, and Archival Material

Bergson died before the age of television documentary culture, and his insistence on controlling publication left less audiovisual material than his fame might suggest. The most important research resources are archival and audio/lecture based rather than feature-film biographies.

### Archives

- **Bibliothèque littéraire Jacques Doucet (BLJD), Paris:** important Bergson holdings, including personal library, surviving papers, correspondence, and later acquisitions. The traditional claim that the archive contains “only his library” is now too strong because additional letters and material have been acquired.
- **Bibliothèque nationale de France (BnF), Richelieu:** the Société des amis de Bergson reports that manuscripts of Bergson's printed works were bequeathed to the BnF.
- **Archives nationales / Paris civil archives:** birth and educational records cited by modern historians.
- **Collège de France:** institutional biography, course history, exhibitions, and records of his chairs.
- **Académie française:** election records, Bergson's 1918 reception discourse, and Paul Valéry's 1941 memorial address.
- **League of Nations / United Nations Geneva archives:** records of the International Committee on Intellectual Cooperation.
- **Society for Psychical Research:** records relating to his presidency and 1913 address.
- **Columbia University Libraries:** early Bergson bibliography and material surrounding his 1913 visit.

### Audio / voice

A short recording circulated as **the only surviving recording of Bergson's voice**, containing remarks about art. Provenance should be checked against institutional sound archives before treating every online repost as authoritative. The rarity of the recording makes it an important object for further archival verification.

### Contemporary and later audiovisual discussions

- Institut de France podcasts, including programs on **Bergson and Jaurès**, Bergson's religious conversion, and portraits assembled from Academy speeches.
- Modern long-form interviews with biographer **Emily Herring** on Bergson's life and philosophy.
- Gifford Lectures archive materials on the 1914 “Problem of Personality” series.
- Numerous modern lecture series and podcasts exist, but most are interpretive secondary material rather than documentary sources about his life.

No major theatrically released biographical film devoted exclusively to Bergson emerged from the sources consulted. His afterlife is much stronger in philosophical documentary, radio, lecture, and archival culture than in cinema biography.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate memorial reputation

Paul Valéry's 1941 Académie française memorial represented Bergson as one of France's great intellectual figures at a moment when France itself was occupied. His late Jewish registration and Catholic sympathy allowed divergent communities to memorialize him in different symbolic registers.

### Mid-century eclipse

Bergson's influence declined sharply after the Second World War. Several forces contributed:

- the rise of phenomenology and existentialism;
- the prestige of Husserl and Heidegger among French philosophers;
- later structuralism and the linguistic turn;
- the scientific reputation of relativity and the memory of the Einstein dispute;
- analytic-philosophical hostility inherited from Russell;
- the religious and mystical tone of *Les Deux Sources*;
- the relative thinness of the archival estate.

“Sartre replaced Bergson” is too simple: Sartre himself acknowledged the formative impact of reading Bergson, and Merleau-Ponty later returned to him.

### Deleuzian revival

**Gilles Deleuze's *Le Bergsonisme* (1966)** was decisive in reopening Bergson as a philosopher of multiplicity, difference, virtuality, memory, and becoming rather than a superseded spiritualist. Deleuze later built his cinema books partly through Bergsonian distinctions between movement and time.

### Influence on twentieth-century philosophy

Bergson's influence or serious reception can be traced through:

- William James and pragmatism;
- Alfred North Whitehead and process-oriented metaphysics;
- phenomenology and its critics;
- Emmanuel Levinas;
- Maurice Merleau-Ponty;
- Gilles Deleuze;
- debates in philosophy of biology;
- philosophy of mind and memory;
- political concepts of open/closed sociality;
- aesthetics, literature, and film theory.

### Literature and modernism

Bergson's concepts became part of the intellectual climate in which modernist experiments with memory and time developed. Comparisons with **Proust** are unavoidable because of family connection and thematic proximity but direct doctrinal dependence remains debated. Bergsonian readings have also been developed for **T. S. Eliot, Wallace Stevens, Willa Cather, Virginia Woolf**, and other modernists. In Willa Cather's *O Pioneers!*, the protagonist's surname **Bergson** is often discussed in this context, though literary naming is not itself proof of philosophical program.

### Political legacy of “open society”

Bergson's 1932 contrast between closed and open moralities/societies preceded Karl Popper's much more explicitly political *The Open Society and Its Enemies*. The shared vocabulary encouraged later association, but the two projects differ substantially.

### Science and time

The Einstein controversy long damaged Bergson's standing among scientifically oriented philosophers. Recent scholarship has made the episode more historically complicated. Even scholars who reject Bergson's physics often distinguish that rejection from his phenomenological question: whether measured physical time exhausts the ways temporal reality is experienced or conceptualized.

### Contemporary scholarship

The **Société des amis de Bergson**, founded in the twenty-first century, the journal **Bergsoniana**, the **Global Bergson Research Project**, new critical editions, and continuing publication of Collège de France courses and correspondence have produced a substantial revival. Current work emphasizes global reception, biology, neuroscience, ecology, colonial contexts, race, gender, religion, and the politics of international intellectual cooperation.

## Chronology

- **1856** — Michel Bergson marries Katherine Levison in Paris.
- **1858** — Elder sister Juliette born (approximate; archival marriage record implies this year).
- **18 Oct 1859** — Henri-Louis Bergson born in Paris, rue Lamartine area, 9th arrondissement.
- **1863** — Family moves to Geneva after Michel obtains Conservatory employment.
- **1865** — Sister Mina/Minna (later Moina Mathers) born in Geneva.
- **1867** — Michel's Geneva appointment collapses; family returns toward Paris; sister Rachel Elizabeth born in Paris in December.
- **c. 1868–69** — Henri, aided by scholarship, remains in Paris for schooling while most of family eventually relocates to London.
- **1870–71** — Franco-Prussian War and Paris Commune during his school years.
- **1875** — Concours général first prize in rhetoric.
- **1876** — Concours général first prize in philosophy.
- **1877** — Concours général first prize in mathematics.
- **1878** — Mathematical solution published; enters École normale supérieure; becomes French citizen.
- **1881** — Completes ENS; places second in philosophy agrégation; begins teaching at Angers.
- **1882** — Also teaches at École supérieure de jeunes filles d'Angers.
- **1883** — Moves to Clermont-Ferrand; teaches lycée/university courses.
- **1884** — Lucretius educational/critical work.
- **1886** — Publishes hypnosis study “De la simulation inconsciente dans l'état d'hypnotisme.”
- **1888** — Submits doctoral theses.
- **1889** — *Essai sur les données immédiates de la conscience* and *Quid Aristoteles de loco senserit* published; doctorate completed.
- **1889–90** — Returns to Paris; Collège Rollin, then Lycée Henri-IV.
- **7 Jan 1892** — Marries Louise Neuburger; Marcel Proust serves as wedding witness/best man.
- **16 Mar 1893** — Daughter Jeanne Adèle Bergson born in Paris (preferred date over widely repeated 1896).
- **1896** — *Matière et mémoire* published.
- **1898** — Begins teaching at École normale supérieure.
- **1898–99** — Dreyfus Affair reaches peak; Bergson does not enter public campaign.
- **1900** — *Le Rire* published; joins Collège de France in ancient/Greek-Latin philosophy chair.
- **1901** — Elected Académie des sciences morales et politiques.
- **1903** — “Introduction à la métaphysique.”
- **1904** — Moves to Collège de France chair of modern philosophy; Geneva congress paper on psycho-physiological paralogism.
- **1907** — *L'Évolution créatrice*; Bergson's international celebrity rapidly expands; Officer of Légion d'honneur.
- **1911** — Oxford, Birmingham, London lectures; honorary Oxford doctorate.
- **1913** — Major U.S. tour/Columbia lectures; Society for Psychical Research presidency and address.
- **1914** — Elected Académie française; Gifford Lectures in Edinburgh; Bergson works placed on Catholic Index 1 June; outbreak of First World War.
- **Aug–Dec 1914** — Public wartime addresses; “La force qui s'use...” and “La signification de la guerre.”
- **1915** — *The Meaning of the War* circulated in English; further wartime intellectual mobilization.
- **1916** — French cultural-diplomatic mission to Spain; encounter with Alfonso XIII; Madrid activities.
- **Jan 1917** — Confidential French mission to United States; meetings around Wilson administration and case for U.S. entry into war.
- **Apr 1917** — United States declares war on Germany; Bergson is one participant among many in preceding Allied diplomatic pressure.
- **24 Jan 1918** — Formal reception at Académie française.
- **May–Sep 1918** — Further American mission/activity.
- **1919** — *L'Énergie spirituelle*; Commander of Légion d'honneur; withdrawal from regular teaching begins.
- **1920** — Cambridge honorary degree; reduced Collège de France teaching obligations.
- **1921** — End/retirement from active Collège chair duties conventionally dated around this year.
- **1922** — First president/chair of League International Committee on Intellectual Cooperation; 6 April Einstein meeting in Paris; *Durée et simultanéité* published.
- **1923** — Grand Officer of Légion d'honneur.
- **1924** — Letter emphasizes that relativity is a major advance despite disputes over interpretations of time.
- **1925** — Leaves presidency of ICIC; severe rheumatoid arthritis increasingly dominates life.
- **1927** — Awarded Nobel Prize in Literature for 1927.
- **1928** — Nobel ceremonies/recognition associated with delayed 1927 award; too ill to travel; sister Moina dies.
- **1930** — Grand Cross of Légion d'honneur.
- **1932** — *Les Deux Sources de la morale et de la religion*.
- **1934** — *La Pensée et le mouvant*.
- **7 Feb 1937** — Testamentary statement records movement toward Catholicism and decision not to convert amid antisemitism.
- **1940** — German occupation of Paris; Vichy anti-Jewish legislation and German registration measures; Bergson refuses privileged exemption and registers as Jewish.
- **4 Jan 1941** — Dies at 47 boulevard de Beauséjour, Paris; official memorial gives pulmonary congestion as cause.
- **9 Jan 1941** — Paul Valéry delivers Académie française memorial address.
- **1957–59** — *Écrits et paroles* posthumously published.
- **1959** — Centenary *Œuvres* edition.
- **1966** — Gilles Deleuze publishes *Le Bergsonisme*, pivotal in Bergson revival.
- **1968** — *Durée et simultanéité* restored in a posthumous seventh edition.
- **1972** — *Mélanges*.
- **1990–2000** — Four-volume posthumous *Cours* series.
- **2002** — Major *Correspondances* edition; Soulez/Worms scholarly biography edition.
- **2006 onward** — Société des amis de Bergson and renewed international scholarship.
- **2016–present** — Additional Collège de France courses and critical archival materials published.
- **2024** — Emily Herring's major English biography and *Correspondances II* expand biographical and epistolary record.

## Atlas Connections

### Aristotle

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact.** Bergson wrote his Latin supplementary doctoral thesis, *Quid Aristoteles de loco senserit* (1889), specifically on Aristotle’s theory of place. [Bibliographic record](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/1927/bergson/bibliography/)

### Alfred Jarry

- **[T3]** **Documented fact.** Jarry attended Bergson’s philosophy courses at Lycée Henri-IV during the academic years 1891–92 and 1892–93. Manuscript-catalog records identify Jarry’s extensive transcriptions of those lectures. [Catalogue collectif de France/BnF record](https://ccfr.bnf.fr/portailccfr/jsp/index_view_direct_anonymous.jsp?record=eadcgm%3AEADC%3Ab1506445)

### Charles Darwin

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact.** Bergson discussed Darwin and Darwinian evolutionary theory at length in *L’Évolution créatrice* (1907), distinguishing Darwinian variation and selection from his own account of evolutionary creativity. [Digitized French edition](https://archive.org/download/lvolutioncra00berg/lvolutioncra00berg.pdf)

### Gilles Deleuze

- **[T1+]** **Documented fact.** Deleuze made Bergson the subject of *Le Bergsonisme* (1966), reconstructing his concepts of duration, memory, multiplicity, and the virtual; the book became a principal vehicle of Bergson’s postwar philosophical revival. [Duke University Press overview](https://www.dukeupress.edu/Henri-Bergson)

### Georges Sorel

- **[T3]** **Documented fact.** Sorel engaged Bergson’s philosophy in writing and appropriated Bergsonian accounts of intuition, creativity, and action for his own theory of revolutionary myth. This was a one-sided intellectual use, not evidence that Bergson endorsed Sorel’s syndicalism or theory of violence. [Cambridge study of Sorel’s reception](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/modern-intellectual-history/article/regeneration-of-society-thomas-ernest-hulme-and-the-early-british-reception-of-georges-sorel/F1B4BDE04384E4E5C406AB94635F2D2F)

### Albert Einstein

- **[T5]** **Documented fact.** Bergson and Einstein met at the Société française de philosophie in Paris on 6 April 1922. Bergson spoke about relativity, time, and simultaneity, and Einstein replied during the recorded discussion. [Société française de philosophie archive](https://www.sofrphilo.fr/activites-scientifiques-de-la-sfp/conferences/grandes-conferences-en-telechargement/)
- **[T3]** **Documented fact.** Bergson’s *Durée et simultanéité* (1922) was a sustained written engagement with Einstein’s relativity theory. It accepted the theory’s physical importance while disputing philosophical interpretations that Bergson believed reduced time entirely to clock measurements and coordinate systems. [Digitized edition](https://archive.org/details/dureetsimulta00berg)
- **[T4]** **Documented fact.** Bergson and Einstein were founding members of the League of Nations’ International Committee on Intellectual Cooperation and attended its inaugural Geneva session on 1 August 1922; Bergson chaired the committee. [United Nations–associated centenary record](https://intellectualcooperation.org/files/IntellectualCooperation_2022.pdf)

### John Dewey

- **[T3]** **Documented fact.** Dewey wrote the introduction to Columbia University’s *A Contribution to a Bibliography of Henri Bergson* (1913), prepared in connection with Bergson’s American visit. [John Dewey chronology](https://deweycenter.siu.edu/common/documents/chrono.pdf)
- **[T5]** **Documented fact.** Dewey’s scholarly chronology records that he met Bergson during Bergson’s Columbia visit in New York, between approximately 6 and 18 February 1913. [John Dewey chronology](https://deweycenter.siu.edu/common/documents/chrono.pdf)

### Marcel Proust

- **[T5]** **Documented fact.** Proust, a relative of Louise Neuburger, acted as a witness at Louise and Bergson’s Paris wedding on 7 January 1892. The connection made Bergson and Proust cousins by marriage; it does not establish that Proust was Bergson’s disciple. [Document and relationship description](https://www.lemanuscritfrancais.com/fr/manuscrit/proust-marcel-1871-1922-40/)
- **[T3]** **Documented fact.** Proust heard Bergson lecture, including attending his opening Collège de France course in 1900, and addressed Bergson’s ideas in his own critical statements—sometimes expressly denying that his treatment of memory derived from Bergson. [Yale anthology discussion](https://dokumen.pub/french-italian-and-spanish-criticism-1900-1950-volume-8-9780300161588.html)

### Marie Curie

- **[T4]** **Documented fact.** Curie and Bergson attended the International Committee on Intellectual Cooperation’s Geneva session in August 1922; a League of Nations group photograph and its archival description identify both participants. Bergson served as chairman, while Curie was a committee member. [Musée Curie archival record](https://curiotheque.musee.curie.fr/s/fr/item/17128)

### Rabindranath Tagore

- **[T5]** **Documented fact.** Bergson visited Tagore while the poet was staying at Albert Kahn’s property in Boulogne-sur-Seine; Tagore’s chronology dates their meeting to 24 August 1920. [BnF, “Tagore en France”](https://heritage.bnf.fr/france-southasia/tagore-en-france)

### William James

- **[T3]** **Documented fact.** Bergson and James maintained a substantive correspondence from 1902 until James’s death in 1910, exchanging more than twenty letters and postcards about perception, intellectualism, pragmatism, mysticism, and their respective books. Bergson later wrote a preface for a 1924 French selection from James’s correspondence. [Study of the correspondence](https://repozytorium.ukw.edu.pl/bitstream/handle/item/6695/Historia%20korespondencji%20Wiliama%20Jamesa%20i%20Henriego%20Bergsona.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1)
- **[T5]** **Documented fact.** The philosophers met personally in Paris on 28 May 1905 and met again in London in 1908. Their surviving correspondence and James’s reactions document a genuine personal friendship as well as philosophical affinity. [Historical psychology source](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/TaylorWoz.htm)
