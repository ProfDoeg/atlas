**Prepared:** 19 August 2026  
**Scope:** biographical, political, legal, religious, literary, familial, financial, mythographic, and reception history

## Research conventions and evidentiary labels

This dossier reconstructs Thomas More's life chronologically and separates kinds of evidence that are often blended in popular biographies.

**DOCUMENTED** means supported by contemporary records, More's own surviving writings, contemporary correspondence, official records, or several converging early witnesses.

**CONTEMPORARY CLAIM** means reported by a contemporary or near-contemporary but not independently demonstrable.

**LATER TRADITION** means a story first securely visible in later biography, martyrology, confessional polemic, folklore, drama, or family tradition.

**DISPUTED** means substantial historians or sources disagree about the fact, interpretation, chronology, or evidentiary weight.

**ALLEGATION** means an accusation made against More or another actor for which the accusation itself is historically documented but the underlying conduct is not established.

**MYTH / RECEPTION NARRATIVE** means a story, simplification, symbolic construction, or ideological appropriation that has had an independent historical life whether or not it is literally true.

The most important primary or near-primary witnesses used here are More's own writings and letters; Erasmus's 1519 portrait of More; William Roper's near-contemporary family biography; the anonymous Paris newsletter reporting More's 1535 trial and execution; parliamentary and statutory material; and early Protestant and Catholic biographical traditions. Roper was More's son-in-law and wrote sympathetically. Erasmus was an intimate friend and admirer. More's *Apology* is self-defense. John Foxe's later Protestant martyrology is hostile. None should be read as a neutral transcript of reality. Modern scholarship is used to compare and test them. [S1–S12]

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Thomas More.

**Latin name:** Thomas Morus; in humanist Latin correspondence he is regularly *Morus*.

**Title and style:** Sir Thomas More after knighthood in 1521. In office he was Lord Chancellor of England, 1529–1532. He was also a lawyer, judge, royal councillor, diplomat, parliamentary speaker, humanist scholar, polemicist, translator, poet, historian, and author.

**Catholic designation after canonization:** Saint Thomas More.

**Born:** 7 February 1478, London. The modern year 1478 is conventional; English documents of the period could reckon the civil year from 25 March.

**Exact birthplace:** **DISPUTED / TRADITIONAL.** The familiar claim that More was born in Milk Street in the City of London is old and commemorated locally, but recent archival work by Joanne Paul has emphasized that the exact Milk Street birthplace is not securely documented. The secure statement is London. The Milk Street location enters the biographical tradition through later family accounts and cannot presently be treated with the same certainty as the date and city. [S2][S13]

**Died:** 6 July 1535, Tower Hill, London.

**Age at death:** 57.

**Cause of death:** judicial execution by beheading after conviction for high treason.

**Burial:** his body was buried in the Chapel Royal of St Peter ad Vincula inside the Tower of London. His head was displayed on London Bridge. A strong family and ecclesiastical tradition holds that his daughter Margaret Roper recovered it and that it ultimately came to rest in the Roper vault at St Dunstan's Church, Canterbury. The exact chain of custody is partly traditional rather than continuously documented. [S9][S29]

**Major residences and institutional locations:**
- London in childhood.
- St Anthony's School, London.
- Household of Archbishop John Morton, probably centered on Lambeth Palace and Morton's other residences.
- Oxford, c.1491–1493.
- New Inn and Lincoln's Inn, London, during legal training.
- London household after marriage, associated in the sources with Bucklersbury / the Old Barge area.
- Property interest in Crosby Place/Crosby Hall, City of London, in the 1520s; ownership or lease should not automatically be equated with continuous personal residence.
- A substantial riverside house and estate at Chelsea from the mid-1520s until his imprisonment.
- Tower of London, 17 April 1534–6 July 1535.
- Temporary diplomatic stays in Bruges, Antwerp, Calais, France, and Cambrai. [S2]

**No exile** is known. His last fifteen months were imprisonment, not exile.

**Principal modern identities attached to him:** Renaissance humanist; author of *Utopia*; lawyer and Tudor statesman; Catholic controversialist; opponent of the royal supremacy; convicted traitor under Henry VIII; Catholic martyr and saint; later symbol of conscience and rule of law; also a figure attacked in Protestant memory as a persecutor of reformers.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal background

Thomas More was not born into hereditary aristocracy. His family's rise belonged to the increasingly important world of London law, civic office, education, and royal administration.

His father, **John More** (c.1451–1530), became a successful lawyer and judge. He served in senior judicial office and was knighted. The paternal line is associated with London's citizen trades before the family's rise through law; More's grandfather William More is described in genealogical material as a London citizen and baker. The trajectory from city household to legal profession to royal office helps explain Thomas More's own social location: elite, prosperous, and upwardly mobile, but not the heir of an old magnate house. [S14]

His mother was **Agnes Graunger (or Granger)**. She died while Thomas was young. John More remarried, and Erasmus later portrayed Thomas as unusually considerate toward his stepmothers, a detail that belongs to Erasmus's admiring moral portrait rather than an independently documented household record. [S4]

### Siblings

Family reconstructions generally identify Thomas as one of six children of John More and Agnes Graunger. Names commonly given are **Joan/Joanna, Thomas, Agatha, John, Edward, and Elizabeth**. Some of the siblings died young and details vary in later genealogical reconstructions. His sister **Elizabeth More**, born in 1482, is securely important because she married **John Rastell**, lawyer, printer, dramatist, and later publisher; the Rastell connection became central to the preservation and publication of More's writings. [S15]

### First marriage: Jane Colt

In **1505** More married **Jane (Joanna) Colt**, daughter of John Colt of Essex. Early biographical tradition says More was initially attracted to a younger Colt sister but chose Jane, the eldest, because he feared humiliating her if she were passed over. The anecdote appears in sympathetic family biography and should be treated as **LATER/NEAR-CONTEMPORARY TRADITION**, not a verifiable account of his private motives.

Jane and Thomas had four children:
- **Margaret More** (1505–1544), later Margaret Roper.
- **Elizabeth More** (1506–1564), later Elizabeth Dauncey.
- **Cecily More** (1507–?), later Cecily Heron.
- **John More** (1509–1547), his only surviving son. [S2][S4][S5]

Jane died in **1511**, probably still in her twenties. The exact cause is not securely established in the sources used here.

### Second marriage: Alice Middleton

More remarried very quickly after Jane's death, to **Alice Middleton**, a wealthy widow older than More. The rapid remarriage is usually explained in the early biographies as a practical measure for the household and children. There were **no children of Thomas and Alice together**. Alice had a daughter from her earlier marriage, **Alice**, who was incorporated into the household.

The marriage was long-lasting and is represented in anecdotes as affectionate but comic and argumentative. These stories are often shaped by More's own comic persona and by later hagiographic narrative. They should not be used as psychiatric evidence about either spouse. Erasmus depicts More as having encouraged his second wife, despite her age, to learn music. [S4][S5]

### Wards, fostered children, and the extended household

More's household included young people beyond his four biological children.

**Margaret Giggs**, later Margaret Clement, was raised in the household and was treated much like a daughter. **Anne Cresacre**, a ward, later married More's son John in 1529. The family group associated with Hans Holbein includes several of these extended-household members. [S16]

The household also included tutors, clerks, servants, and, famously, the household fool **Henry Patenson**. More kept animals and curiosities; Erasmus mentions his interest in animals as part of the portrait of an observant, playful domestic intellectual. [S4]

### Children's marriages and descendants

- **Margaret More** married **William Roper** in 1521. She became the best-known of More's children, a highly educated Latinist and translator. She preserved correspondence and became central to the martyr-memory of her father.
- **Elizabeth More** married **William Dauncey** in 1525.
- **Cecily More** married **Giles Heron** in 1525. Heron was executed for treason in 1540, five years after More.
- **John More** married **Anne Cresacre** in 1529. [S2]

More's descendants and collateral relatives participated materially in preserving his reputation. William Roper wrote the earliest surviving full family life. More's nephew **William Rastell**, son of John Rastell and Elizabeth More, edited the major 1557 English collection of More's works. A later great-grandson, **Cresacre More**, became associated with another major Catholic family biography.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1478–1491

More's childhood was urban rather than manorial. He grew up inside London's legal, mercantile, clerical, and civic world.

From approximately **1484 to 1489** he attended **St Anthony's School** in London, one of the city's prominent grammar schools. The curriculum centered on Latin grammar and rhetorical formation.

At about twelve he entered the household of **John Morton**, Archbishop of Canterbury and Lord Chancellor. Morton had served Edward IV, survived the Richard III period, and became one of Henry VII's most important servants. More's service as a page gave him an early view of the intersection of church, monarchy, law, patronage, and household politics.

Roper later presents Morton as recognizing More's precocious ability and predicting that the boy would become remarkable. That prediction is a classic retrospective-biographical motif: possible, but impossible to verify. More's later unfinished *History of King Richard III* has sometimes been linked to information or political attitudes inherited from Morton's circle. That is plausible but cannot be reduced to proof that Morton dictated More's interpretation of Richard. [S5][S23]

## Education and Formation

### Oxford, c.1491–1493

More entered **Oxford** at about thirteen or fourteen. The exact college affiliation is not absolutely secure in every reconstruction; Canterbury Hall and St Mary Hall appear in older accounts. What matters more securely is his immersion in the new classical learning.

Among teachers and formative humanists connected to his education were:
- **Thomas Linacre**, physician, classicist, and Greek scholar.
- **William Grocyn**, one of the leading English Hellenists.
- later **William Lily**, with whom More collaborated intellectually.
- the broader English humanist circle that would include **John Colet** and **Erasmus**. [S1][S2]

His father redirected him toward law after roughly two years. Erasmus later says John More limited his son's money because he feared that literary studies would distract him from the law. As with much of Erasmus's 1519 account, the detail is credible as intimate testimony but rhetorically shaped to show the triumph of More's intellectual appetite over practical constraint. [S4]

### New Inn and Lincoln's Inn

From about **1493 to 1495** More studied at **New Inn**, a preliminary legal institution, then entered **Lincoln's Inn** in **1496**. He was called to the bar around **1501**.

The Inns of Court were not merely vocational schools. They were elite social and intellectual institutions combining legal training, rhetoric, performance, patronage, and political networking. More later served Lincoln's Inn as reader, governor, and treasurer.

### Greek, classical studies, and Augustine

Around **1501** More began an especially intensive period of Greek study. His teachers and collaborators included Grocyn, Linacre, and Lily. He became a sufficiently accomplished Hellenist to translate Lucian into Latin and to participate in the northern-European humanist project of returning to Greek and Latin sources.

In 1501, while still young, he lectured publicly on **Augustine's *City of God***. This is important for More's later intellectual development: Augustine furnished a framework for thinking about the imperfect relation between earthly political order and transcendent religious claims.

### Carthusian period and possible religious vocation

From about **1501 to 1504**, More frequented the **London Charterhouse**, living close to or participating in the devotional life of the Carthusian monks without formally joining the order.

Erasmus and Roper describe fasting, prayer, penitential practices, and serious consideration of a clerical or monastic vocation. Later Catholic tradition emphasizes More's use of a hair shirt and self-discipline.

**DOCUMENTED CORE:** More seriously tested a religious vocation and maintained a disciplined devotional life.

**DETAILS OF EXTREME ASCETIC PRACTICES:** some are early and credible, but the accumulated devotional legends should be treated as hagiographically amplified. The surviving “hair shirt of Thomas More” has a relic history, but provenance of devotional relics is a separate question from the historical evidence that he practiced bodily penance.

The eventual decision to marry was not a rejection of religion. More developed a distinctive model of learned lay piety in which marriage, household education, public office, classical scholarship, and intense devotional practice coexisted. [S4][S5]

### John Colet and Christian humanism

**John Colet**, dean of St Paul's, became an important friend and religious-intellectual influence. More's letter to Colet around 1504 survives. Colet's program combined scriptural study, moral reform, and humanist scholarship.

### Erasmus

More met **Desiderius Erasmus** in **1499**. Their friendship became one of the central relationships of northern humanism.

Erasmus later described More as exceptionally suited to friendship and wrote a seven-page verbal portrait of his appearance, habits, humor, family life, education, political instincts, and character in a letter to Ulrich von Hutten dated 23 July 1519. It is invaluable but openly admiring. Erasmus describes More as of medium height, fair-complexioned, with dark-auburn hair and blue-gray eyes, physically healthy but not robust, with one shoulder carried slightly higher than the other. He depicts him as modest in food and drink, careless about dress, witty, socially adaptable, and resistant to tyranny. [S4]

Erasmus wrote *Moriae Encomium* (*The Praise of Folly*) in association with More and dedicated it to him; the title plays on the resemblance between Greek *moria* (“folly”) and More's Latin name *Morus*. More's house was one of the social centers of the humanist network.

## Early Career, 1501–1518

### Legal practice

After his call to the bar, More developed a successful legal practice. Erasmus says he became one of the most sought-after advocates of his generation and portrays him as comparatively indifferent to money. That latter point is praise from a friend, not an audited financial statement. The broader fact of substantial professional success is secure.

He became **reader at Furnivall's Inn** c.1503–1506 and held increasing responsibilities at **Lincoln's Inn**:
- financial secretary, 1507;
- reader again, 1511;
- governor and treasurer, 1512. [S2]

### Parliament of 1504 and the Henry VII subsidy story

More entered Parliament in **1504**. His exact constituency is not established with complete certainty in the sources used here.

Roper and later biographies tell a dramatic story: the young More opposed a large financial demand by Henry VII, defeated it in the Commons, and caused the enraged king to punish More's father, John, with imprisonment and a £100 fine because the son was beyond direct reach.

**STATUS: LATER/NEAR-CONTEMPORARY FAMILY TRADITION, HISTORICALLY DISPUTED.**

The story became important because it foreshadows the later martyr resisting Henry VIII. Modern historians have questioned whether the parliamentary episode and the retaliatory punishment occurred as narrated. There is insufficient reason to treat the polished Roper version as an official parliamentary transcript.

### Marriage, household, and scholarly production

Between 1505 and 1511, while his children were being born, More combined:
- legal practice;
- Inn of Court responsibilities;
- classical translation;
- correspondence;
- early poetry;
- parliamentary work;
- religious study.

His **translations of Lucian**, made in collaboration and friendly competition with Erasmus, were published in **1506**. Lucian's skeptical dialogue, irony, and fictional worlds left a durable mark on More's literary technique.

Around **1510** More prepared an English adaptation/translation of the life and writings of **Giovanni Pico della Mirandola**, based substantially on the biography by Pico's nephew Gianfrancesco. Pico represented the ideal of a learned lay intellectual oriented toward religious conversion and moral seriousness.

### Under-sheriff of London, 1510–1518

In **1510** More became **Under-Sheriff of London**, an important legal and civic office. He held it until entering full royal service in 1518. The work brought him into direct contact with commercial disputes, criminal matters, city government, foreign merchants, and everyday litigation.

His reputation for accessibility and incorruptibility comes strongly from friendly biographical sources, especially Erasmus and Roper. It is reasonable to say he became respected as a London lawyer; claims that he was universally beloved or uniquely free of corruption belong to encomiastic memory.

### Second marriage, 1511

Jane's death and More's quick remarriage to Alice Middleton reorganized the domestic household. More continued a program of education for daughters as well as his son, one of the most discussed features of his family life.

### Education of women in the More household

More insisted that his daughters receive rigorous humanist education in Latin and, to varying degrees, Greek. Tutors included **William Gonell** and **John Clement**. More's letters to tutors and children survive.

Margaret became especially accomplished. Erasmus and other humanists praised her Latin. In 1524 she published an English translation of Erasmus's *Precatio Dominica* / commentary on the Lord's Prayer, becoming an important example of learned female participation in English humanism.

More's educational practice should not be retrojected straightforwardly into modern feminist politics. It is nonetheless documentary evidence that he rejected the notion that intellectual formation was useless to women and invested heavily in daughters' education. [S1][S6]

### c.1508 continental academic travel

A standard chronology records visits around **1508** to the universities at **Paris and Louvain**. Exact itinerary and duration are less well documented than his later diplomatic journeys. [S2]

## Humanist Writing Before Royal Service

### English and Latin verse

More wrote English poems from youth and Latin verse over decades. His Latin epigrams range from jokes and inscriptions to political reflections on kingship, equality, law, and tyranny.

His **“Coronation Ode”** of 1509 welcomed the accession of Henry VIII and Catherine of Aragon. Like later court humanists, More could praise a young monarch while also producing texts skeptical of princely power.

### *History of King Richard III*

More worked on an English and Latin history of Richard III, usually dated **c.1513–1518**. It remained unfinished and unpublished in his lifetime.

It is one of the founding works of English Renaissance historical prose. It portrays Richard as a brilliant political manipulator and usurper and contains memorable character sketches and scenes later transmitted through Edward Hall and Raphael Holinshed into Shakespearean historical culture.

**Important qualification:** More was born in 1478 and was a child during Richard III's reign. The work is not eyewitness history. He had access to older participants, political memory, and possibly traditions from John Morton's circle. Its literary construction is sophisticated enough that it cannot safely be mined as if every speech or scene were a verbatim record.

The later claim that More simply “invented the Tudor propaganda Richard III” is too simple. His work decisively shaped the black legend, but the exact mix of inherited report, Tudor political memory, moral historiography, and literary construction remains debated. [S23]

## 1515–1516: Flanders, Peter Giles, and *Utopia*

### Commercial embassy

In **1515** More joined an English mission to the Low Countries for commercial negotiations, especially involving Anglo-Burgundian trade. He spent time in **Bruges and Antwerp**.

During an interruption in negotiations he developed the material that became Book II of *Utopia*. Erasmus later reported that the island-description was composed before the framing first book.

At Antwerp More's important associate was **Pieter Gillis / Peter Giles**, city secretary and humanist. The fictional frame of *Utopia* presents a conversation involving More, Giles, and the invented traveler **Raphael Hythlodaeus/Hythloday**.

### Publication of *Utopia*

The first Latin edition appeared at **Louvain in 1516**, printed by **Dirk Martens**. Its expanded Latin title begins *Libellus vere aureus... de optimo rei publicae statu deque nova insula Utopia*—a “golden little book” on the best state of a commonwealth and the new island Utopia.

Further authorized/revised editions appeared at Paris and Basel in 1517–1518. The Basel editions associated with Johann Froben helped establish the work's European humanist readership. Ralph Robinson produced the first English translation in **1551**, sixteen years after More's death. [S6][S32]

### What *Utopia* contains

Book I presents a conversation about whether a philosopher should serve rulers, while attacking or questioning:
- aggressive dynastic warfare;
- enclosure and displacement;
- economic arrangements that generate theft and then punish thieves harshly;
- royal counselors who flatter princes;
- the concentration of wealth;
- capital punishment for property crime.

Book II describes an invented island with:
- common rather than private property;
- regulated work;
- communal institutions;
- broad access to basic material goods;
- elective and layered political offices;
- religious pluralism within defined limits;
- slavery as a social institution;
- a militarily cautious but not pacifist foreign policy;
- restrictions and disciplines that would be coercive by modern standards.

The text is not a simple policy manifesto. “Utopia” participates in Greek wordplay between “no-place” and the suggestion of a “good place”; Hythloday's name is itself comic. More appears as a character who does not simply endorse every Utopian institution. The dialogue form deliberately obstructs a one-to-one equation between More and the island.

### Later political appropriations

*Utopia* became:
- a foundational text in the utopian literary genre;
- a resource for socialist and communist history;
- a Christian-humanist critique of political corruption;
- a liberal text about reasoned social design;
- a warning about technocratic or totalizing social arrangements;
- a literary puzzle about irony, counsel, and the limits of ideal theory.

Each appropriation selects real features of the text; none exhausts it.

## 1517: Evil May Day

On **1 May 1517**, London experienced a violent anti-foreigner riot known as **Evil May Day**. More, as an Under-Sheriff, took part in official efforts to control the crowd.

This episode later acquired a remarkable literary afterlife. The collaborative Elizabethan play *Sir Thomas More* dramatizes More addressing the rioters and asking them to imagine themselves as displaced “strangers.” A revised manuscript passage, the famous “Hand D,” is widely regarded by Shakespeare scholars as likely written by **William Shakespeare**.

**HISTORICAL FACT:** More was involved as a London officer in suppressing the riot.

**DRAMATIC MYTH / LITERARY RECONSTRUCTION:** the eloquent “Strangers' Case” speech in the play is not a transcript of More's words in 1517. It is a later dramatic construction, however important to More's posthumous political symbolism. [S25]

## Royal Service, 1518–1529

### Entry into Henry VIII's service

In **1518** More entered regular royal service, despite earlier reluctance. He became a royal councillor and **Master of Requests**, bringing legal petitions to the king.

Erasmus's 1519 letter makes More's move to court part of a tension between his alleged dislike of courts and the crown's desire to employ him. This provides a useful lens but is still Erasmus's interpretation.

### Royal intimacy and the limits of the legend

Roper later describes Henry VIII visiting More at Chelsea, walking with him in the garden with an arm around More's neck, and enjoying his company. Roper also records More warning his son-in-law not to mistake royal favor for security: if More's head could win the king a castle in France, the head would not necessarily be safe.

The stories reflect a real political closeness between Henry and More in the 1520s. Their exact dialogue cannot be independently verified. Later readers often turn the relationship into a dramatic friendship/betrayal narrative, especially under the influence of Robert Bolt.

### Field of Cloth of Gold, 1520

More accompanied the English court to the **Field of Cloth of Gold** in 1520, the spectacular meeting between Henry VIII and Francis I of France near Calais. It illustrates the ceremonial, diplomatic, and financial scale of the Tudor monarchy More now served.

### Knighthood and senior office, 1521

In **1521** More was **knighted** and became **Under-Treasurer of the Exchequer**. He undertook diplomatic work in Bruges and Calais.

That same year Henry VIII published the **Assertio Septem Sacramentorum** (*Defence of the Seven Sacraments*) against Martin Luther and received the papal title *Fidei Defensor* (“Defender of the Faith”).

More was associated with the royal theological enterprise, probably reviewing or assisting aspects of the work. **The exact degree of authorship is uncertain.** It is unsafe to list the *Assertio* as a work co-authored by More. More later recalled warning Henry not to make unnecessarily expansive claims for papal secular authority, an important sign that More's later rejection of royal supremacy did not mean he had always been an uncritical papalist in every jurisdictional question. [S2][S6]

### *Responsio ad Lutherum*, 1523

After Luther's violent answer to Henry, More was asked to respond. His Latin ** *Responsio ad Lutherum* ** appeared in **1523**, associated with the pseudonym **Gulielmus Rosseus / William Ross**.

This is one of More's most aggressive works. It matches Luther's insults with deliberately obscene, scatological rhetoric. It matters because the genial humanist of *Utopia* and Erasmus's friendship circle is sometimes sanitized by later admirers. More was capable of extraordinarily abusive confessional polemic.

The style also belongs to a sixteenth-century polemical culture in which Luther and his opponents intentionally used shocking invective. Context explains the genre but does not erase the words.

### Parliament and Speaker, 1523

More was chosen **Speaker of the House of Commons** in **1523**. His formal petition to Henry VIII for freedom of speech survives. He asked the king not to punish members for imperfectly expressed counsel and to interpret their words favorably.

This is an important documentary moment in parliamentary history, but it should not be anachronistically equated with a modern constitutional right to unrestricted political speech. It was a Speaker's petition made inside a hierarchical monarchical system. [S17]

A celebrated Roper story has Cardinal Wolsey enter the Commons expecting support for royal taxation and find More refusing to give him a substantive answer because the House had resolved not to debate before him. The anecdote has become part of More's parliamentary independence legend. Its source is family biography, not a verbatim Commons journal.

### Crosby Hall / Crosby Place

In the 1520s More acquired a lease or interest in **Crosby Place**, one of the most impressive houses in the City of London, formerly associated with Richard III. The property is important to his wealth and status, but documentary property interest and daily residence are not identical. His major household later shifted to Chelsea.

### Chelsea, c.1524 onward

Around **1524**, More moved to a substantial house at **Chelsea**, then west of dense London. It became:
- a family residence;
- a humanist household;
- a site of education;
- a place where Holbein drew the family;
- a place where religious suspects were sometimes detained or examined during More's anti-heresy activity;
- later a central location in both Catholic domestic hagiography and Protestant torture legends.

The original house no longer survives. Chelsea Old Church retains More-related monuments and traditions, but More's body is not securely buried there. [S26][S29]

### High stewardships and Duchy of Lancaster

More was made **High Steward of Oxford** in 1524 and **High Steward of Cambridge** in 1525. In **1525** he became **Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster**, a major administrative post.

### Hans Holbein the Younger, 1526–1527

The German-Swiss painter **Hans Holbein the Younger**, who entered the English humanist circle with connections to Erasmus, produced one of the definitive visual images of More.

Holbein's famous individual portrait of More dates to **1527**. He also designed a group portrait of the More household. The original family painting is lost, but preparatory drawings and later versions/copies preserve its arrangement. A surviving family version at Nostell identifies More, Alice, children, wards, relatives, fool, and clerk. [S16]

The visual More known to posterity—a grave, richly dressed Tudor official in fur and velvet—thus dates from the period of his highest political ascent.

## Henry VIII's “Great Matter,” 1527–1529

### The king seeks an annulment

By **1527** Henry VIII was actively seeking to end his marriage to **Catherine of Aragon** so that he could marry Anne Boleyn and obtain the possibility of a male heir.

Henry consulted More. More investigated scriptural, canonical, and patristic material and concluded that he could not in conscience support the proposed grounds for ending the marriage.

More did not immediately become a public leader of Catherine's party. He attempted for years to keep his personal judgment separate from his service to the king.

### Wolsey and France

In 1527 More accompanied Cardinal **Thomas Wolsey** on diplomatic business in France. Wolsey remained the central minister until his failure to secure Henry's desired annulment.

### More and Catherine of Aragon

Later Catholic memory sometimes turns More into Catherine's public champion. The documentary picture is more restrained. He respected Catherine and privately rejected the king's case, but he did not become her public lawyer or lead a public opposition campaign while in office.

### Political problem created by conscience

The central contradiction of More's last decade was already visible:
- he believed in monarchy, public order, and service to the crown;
- he also believed the universal church possessed a spiritual authority that an English Parliament and king could not unilaterally replace;
- he believed conscience had to be properly formed by church, law, scripture, tradition, and reason, not simply asserted as private preference.

This was not modern individualism, though later liberal reception would often translate it into that language.

## Lord Chancellor, 1529–1532

### Appointment

After Wolsey's fall, More became **Lord Chancellor** in **October 1529**.

He was now the highest judicial and political officer in the kingdom after the king, while remaining opposed to the central dynastic-religious policy Henry increasingly required.

### Government, courts, and administration

As Chancellor, More:
- presided in Chancery;
- sat in Star Chamber and the royal council;
- handled petitions and equity jurisdiction;
- participated in Parliament and government;
- continued diplomatic and administrative work;
- became one of the most visible lay defenders of traditional Catholic doctrine against evangelical/Protestant reform.

His legal reputation is strongly positive in friendly sources, but the surviving record also places him inside the coercive machinery of a Tudor state.

### Anti-heresy campaign

This is the most contested part of More's public life and requires several distinctions.

#### What is documented

More:
- believed Lutheran and evangelical teaching threatened souls, sacramental religion, ecclesiastical unity, and political order;
- wrote large, increasingly urgent works against Luther, William Tyndale, Simon Fish, John Frith, George Joye, and other reformers;
- supported legal suppression of prohibited religious books;
- participated in searches, arrests, examinations, and detention of suspected heretics;
- held some suspects in custody at or through his Chelsea household;
- worked with bishops, especially **Cuthbert Tunstall** and **John Stokesley**;
- approved in print of the execution by burning of obstinate or relapsed heretics;
- spoke with extreme hostility about particular executed reformers. [S18][S19][S20]

#### The six executions conventionally linked to More's chancellorship

Modern More scholarship commonly speaks of **six men burned for heresy during More's tenure as Chancellor**:
1. **Thomas Hitton** — 1530.
2. **Thomas Bilney** — 1531.
3. **Richard Bayfield** — 1531.
4. **John Tewkesbury** — 1531.
5. **Thomas Dusgate** (often discussed in the wider martyrological record in relation to regional executions; identification and nomenclature vary in summaries).
6. **James Bainham** — 1532. [S18]

The figure “six” should not be misunderstood to mean there were exactly six Protestant/Lollard executions in all of England in those years. Regional martyrological lists include additional deaths depending on date, jurisdiction, and classification. The point in More scholarship is narrower: “half a dozen” is the conventional number directly assigned to the period of his chancellorship when debating claims that More personally caused “hundreds” of executions. Richard Rex explicitly rejects the inflated “few hundred” figure. [S18]

#### More's legal role in death sentences

More was a **lay Chancellor**, not an ecclesiastical judge pronouncing formal heresy sentences. Heresy determinations were made in ecclesiastical proceedings, and secular authority enforced the statutory penalty.

That jurisdictional fact does **not** make More uninvolved. He used secular office in investigation and enforcement and publicly defended burning as lawful punishment for persistent heresy. Historians have therefore disagreed less about his enthusiasm for suppression than about how to describe his exact causal and procedural responsibility for particular deaths.

#### London cases

More took a direct interest in cases including **Bayfield, Tewkesbury, and Bainham**. Bainham, a lawyer, was arrested on More's authority and detained. Tewkesbury's final ecclesiastical sentence was pronounced at More's house. More's published comments approve their executions.

### Allegations of personal torture

John Foxe's *Acts and Monuments* and related Protestant tradition made More personally responsible for brutal torture. Stories include:
- suspects tied to a tree in More's Chelsea garden called the **“Tree of Truth”** or “Jesus' Tree”;
- whipping at More's house;
- cords tightened around the head;
- More witnessing or arranging racking in the Tower.

The best-known names in these stories are **John Tewkesbury** and **James Bainham**.

#### More's contemporary denial

The allegations were already circulating while More was alive. In his **1533 *Apology*** he emphatically denied striking or torturing people who came into his custody for heresy. He did acknowledge two episodes of corporal punishment:
- a young person in his household whom he had caned in connection with Eucharistic irreverence/heretical talk;
- a disturbed man who behaved indecently toward women during Mass and whom More said was whipped as correction and to prevent worse public violence.

More therefore did **not** claim that no coercion or corporal punishment ever occurred in his household. He denied the specific torture narrative concerning heresy prisoners. [S20][S21]

#### Modern assessment

Modern historians across different interpretive camps generally distinguish:
- **secure evidence for zealous persecution, detention, interrogation, censorship, and approval of executions**, from
- **much weaker evidence for More personally torturing heresy suspects**.

Richard Rex emphasizes that Foxe's tradition mixed truth and falsehood and that later stories have been repeatedly embellished. John Guy and Diarmaid MacCulloch have likewise been cited as finding no independent proof that More personally tortured heretics. Richard Marius, despite a much more critical biography of More, regarded the Bainham racking story as doubtful.

**Finding:** the statement “More persecuted heretics” is historically defensible if carefully defined. The statement “More personally tortured heretics at the Tree of Truth” is an **allegation, not established fact**. [S18][S21]

### Censorship and the English Bible

More opposed William Tyndale's unauthorized English New Testament not because he believed all vernacular scripture was intrinsically forbidden, but because he considered Tyndale's translation and annotations doctrinally corrupt and part of an organized evangelical movement.

This distinction matters but does not make More a proponent of unrestricted vernacular Bible publication. He defended ecclesiastical licensing and suppression of texts judged heretical.

### *Dialogue Concerning Heresies*, 1529

At Bishop Cuthbert Tunstall's request, More wrote an English ** *Dialogue* ** defending Catholic doctrine and church practice. First printed in 1529 and revised in 1531, it addresses scripture, images, saints, miracles, pilgrimages, the church's authority, and heresy.

### *Supplication of Souls*, 1529

More wrote the *Supplication of Souls* against **Simon Fish's** anticlerical *Supplication for the Beggars*. Fish depicted clergy as economically parasitic and attacked purgatory-related practices. More answered through the imagined voices of souls in purgatory.

The exchange shows that More's anti-Reformation writing was simultaneously theological, political, economic, and propagandistic.

### *Confutation of Tyndale's Answer*, 1532–1533

This massive unfinished work answered Tyndale's reply to More and became More's longest polemical undertaking. It is repetitive by design, attempting to isolate vocabulary, translation choices, institutional claims, and doctrinal propositions. Eamon Duffy has analyzed both its historical importance and its literary problems. [S19]

## Intellectual and Religious Development

More's life is often divided into two incompatible characters: the tolerant humanist who wrote *Utopia* and the persecuting Chancellor. The sources show both change and continuity.

### Continuing elements

Across his career More retained:
- classical and patristic learning;
- a distrust of court flattery and princely absolutism;
- a preference for legal and institutional reasoning;
- a belief that political life is morally dangerous but unavoidable;
- deep attachment to sacramental Christianity;
- Augustinian suspicion of purely earthly perfection;
- humor, dialogue, fiction, paradox, and role-playing as intellectual methods;
- a strong conception of properly formed conscience.

### Change after the Lutheran rupture

The Reformation sharpened his sense that intellectual dispute could destroy a shared sacramental and political order. From the 1520s his writings become:
- longer;
- more vernacular;
- more doctrinally defensive;
- more personally aggressive;
- increasingly concerned with authority, unity, and the dangers of unauthorized interpretation.

This development is visible in the move from Lucianic humanist play and *Utopia* to the *Responsio*, *Dialogue*, *Confutation*, *Apology*, and anti-evangelical works.

### Religious toleration in *Utopia* versus More's practice

The Utopians tolerate several religions but place limits on aggressive proselytizing and socially destabilizing belief; atheists are not simply treated as citizens equal in honor. The fictional tolerance is therefore not a modern declaration of unlimited freedom of religion.

Still, the contrast between fictional Utopian pluralism and More's later real-life anti-heresy enforcement is substantial and has driven centuries of interpretation. It should not be solved by declaring either the novel or the Chancellor “the real More.”

---

## The King's Supremacy and More's Resignation, 1530–1532

### Growing conflict

By 1530–1531 Henry's government was using pressure on the English clergy and the law of *praemunire* to force recognition of royal authority.

In **1531** the clergy acknowledged Henry as “Supreme Head” of the Church of England with a qualifying formula—often rendered “so far as the law of Christ allows.” More's precise reaction unfolded within a still-changing constitutional situation.

### Christopher St German

The lawyer **Christopher St German** became an important intellectual opponent. He argued for stronger temporal jurisdiction over clergy and criticized ecclesiastical legal practices. More answered him at length after leaving office.

### Submission of the Clergy

On **15 May 1532**, the English clergy submitted to royal restrictions on their independent legislative authority.

On **16 May 1532**, More resigned as Chancellor.

More officially cited ill health. Ill health was real enough to appear in contemporary descriptions, but the timing makes the constitutional-religious conflict central. He could no longer comfortably occupy the highest lay office while the crown was restructuring ecclesiastical authority in ways he believed impermissible. [S2]

### Financial consequences

Resignation materially reduced More's income. The household had to contract. Friendly biographers emphasize the change from high office to reduced means.

It is difficult to translate More's finances into a reliable modern net worth. He had:
- substantial legal earnings;
- royal salaries and fees;
- valuable urban/property interests;
- the Chelsea estate;
- the expenses of a large educated household;
- obligations to children, wards, servants, and dependents.

He was prosperous and elite but not a great landed magnate on the scale of the richest noble houses.

---

## 1532–1533: Private Man, Public Polemicist

Resignation did not produce silence. More continued a rapid series of polemical publications.

### *Apology*, 1533

The ** *Apology of Sir Thomas More, Knight* ** defended his conduct, his anti-heresy activity, and the clergy against criticism. It is a major source because it responds directly to accusations circulating against him, including torture stories.

Its evidentiary value is double:
- it documents what More admitted and denied;
- as self-defense, it cannot by itself prove innocence.

### *Debellation of Salem and Bizance*, 1533

This continued the controversy with arguments associated with Christopher St German over ecclesiastical law, jurisdiction, and clerical power.

### *Answer to a Poisoned Book*, 1533

More's last book published in his lifetime answered **George Joye** on the Eucharist and related doctrine.

### Anne Boleyn's coronation

More did not attend **Anne Boleyn's coronation on 1 June 1533**. His absence was politically visible.

It should not be exaggerated into proof that he was organizing resistance. He had adopted a strategy of avoiding public statements that directly condemned the king's settlement while refusing personal assent to propositions his conscience rejected.

---

## Elizabeth Barton, Bribery Allegations, and Pressure from the Crown

### The “Holy Maid of Kent”

**Elizabeth Barton**, a religious visionary known as the “Holy Maid of Kent,” prophesied against Henry's marriage policy and became entangled in political opposition.

More met or communicated with Barton but warned against political prophecy and later distanced himself from her claims.

When the government moved against Barton and her associates, More was scrutinized. Henry sought to have More included in the legislative attainder associated with the affair. More defended his conduct and his name was removed.

**Documented:** contact existed; the government investigated whether More belonged to a seditious network.

**Not established:** that More joined a conspiracy to overthrow Henry or coordinated Barton's prophecies.

Barton was executed in 1534. Surviving “confessions” and official narratives were produced under Cromwell's regime; historians must therefore distinguish what she demonstrably said and did from the government's construction of a treasonous conspiracy. [S2]

### Gifts and corruption accusations

The crown also collected accusations that More had improperly taken gifts while in judicial office.

Early friendly accounts preserve stories in which More refused, returned, or reciprocated litigants' gifts. The charges did not mature into a successful corruption prosecution before the supremacy crisis overtook them.

There is no documentary basis in the sources used here for describing More as engaged in systemic bribery, fraud, embezzlement, or anything resembling modern money laundering.

**Caution:** the absence of a corruption conviction is not proof that every gift story in sympathetic biography is literally true. Tudor office-holding operated through gift, fee, patronage, and household economies unlike modern civil-service rules.

---

## The Legal Break with Rome, 1533–1534

### Act in Restraint of Appeals

The **Act in Restraint of Appeals (1533)** asserted that England was an empire and sharply restricted appeals to Rome. It was foundational to the legal construction of independent royal ecclesiastical authority.

### Annulment and remarriage

Thomas Cranmer's court declared Henry's marriage to Catherine invalid. Henry's marriage to Anne Boleyn was recognized domestically, and Anne was crowned.

More did not mount public rebellion. His strategy was silence.

### Act of Succession and the oath

In **April 1534**, More was summoned to **Lambeth Palace** and required to swear an oath connected to the Act of Succession.

The popular summary “More refused to recognize Anne Boleyn's children” is incomplete. More indicated that he could accept the succession itself as a matter Parliament could regulate. His objection was to the full oath and the religious/ecclesiological propositions bound into it, including implications about papal authority and the validity of the marriage settlement.

Because he would not take the prescribed oath, he was committed to the Tower on **17 April 1534**.

### Act of Supremacy

The formal **Act of Supremacy** followed later in **1534**, declaring the king “Supreme Head on earth of the Church of England.” The Treasons Act created the criminal framework under which malicious verbal denial of the king's titles could become treason. [S22]

This chronology matters. More was first imprisoned over the succession oath; his later capital prosecution depended on the newer supremacy/treason framework and alleged spoken words.

---

## Imprisonment in the Tower, 17 April 1534–6 July 1535

More spent roughly fifteen months imprisoned in the Tower.

### Conditions

His custody was initially less restrictive than it later became. Family visits and writing materials were at times permitted. The regime tightened:
- books and writing materials were increasingly restricted;
- interrogations intensified;
- Richard Rich removed writing materials in June 1535;
- his health deteriorated.

The contemporary Paris account described him at trial as greatly weakened by imprisonment. [S10]

### Family correspondence

More's Tower letters, especially to **Margaret Roper**, are among the central documents of his last year. They show:
- fear rather than theatrical fearlessness;
- careful legal silence;
- affection;
- theological reasoning about conscience;
- preparation for death;
- attempts not to implicate family members.

The historical More did not simply seek martyrdom. He repeatedly tried to avoid committing a verbal act that could lawfully be construed as treason while also avoiding an oath he believed imperiled his soul.

### *Dialogue of Comfort Against Tribulation*

Written in the Tower in **1534**, the *Dialogue of Comfort Against Tribulation* is set as a conversation in Hungary under threat of Ottoman invasion. It uses geographical displacement to discuss fear, coercion, imprisonment, conscience, and martyrdom.

It was first printed posthumously in **1553**, then included in the 1557 *Workes*.

### *Treatise upon the Passion* and devotional texts

More also worked on:
- *A Treatise upon the Passion*;
- *A Treatise to Receive the Blessed Body*;
- instructions and prayers;
- meditations on temptation, enemies, detachment, and death.

### *De tristitia Christi* / *The Sadness of Christ*

His final extended work, ** *De tristitia Christi* **, examines Christ's agony in Gethsemane. The subject allowed More to think about fear, abandonment, bodily weakness, courage, sleep, betrayal, and impending violent death.

An autograph manuscript survives in Valencia. It became one of the most important physical survivals of More's prison writing. [S6][S7]

---

## John Fisher and the Carthusians

More was not the only prominent opponent of the royal supremacy.

### John Fisher

**John Fisher**, Bishop of Rochester, rejected the supremacy and was imprisoned. More and Fisher corresponded, but More took pains to insist that each man formed his own conscience rather than conspiring.

Fisher was executed on **22 June 1535**, two weeks before More.

### Carthusian monks

Carthusian priors and monks who refused the supremacy were executed in 1535. More's earlier personal association with the Charterhouse made their fate especially resonant in later Catholic biography.

A famous account has Margaret visiting More as condemned Carthusians were led to execution. The broad event is well embedded in the More tradition, though details are filtered through family remembrance.

---

## The Trial, 1 July 1535

### Court and indictment

More was tried at **Westminster Hall** on **1 July 1535** before a special commission of major nobles, officials, and judges.

The prosecution's theory included:
- his allegedly malicious opposition to Henry's second marriage;
- his refusal to answer regarding the royal supremacy;
- alleged coordination with Fisher;
- and, crucially, an alleged conversation with **Richard Rich** in which More supposedly denied Parliament's power to make the king head of the church.

### More's silence defense

The Paris newsletter, derived from a report written very soon after the trial, preserves More's argument that silence could not constitute the required malicious verbal denial. He invoked the legal maxim that silence is ordinarily taken as consent rather than contradiction. [S10]

His strategy for months had depended on this distinction.

### Richard Rich's testimony

**Richard Rich**, then Solicitor General, testified about a conversation in the Tower after More's books and writing materials were being taken away. Rich claimed More used a hypothetical legal discussion to state that Parliament could no more make the king head of the church than it could legislate a theological impossibility.

More vehemently denied Rich's account and attacked his credibility.

Two men present, **Richard Southwell** and **Palmer**, did not provide independent confirmation of the decisive words in the form Rich alleged.

### Was Richard Rich a perjurer?

**HISTORICAL FACT:** Rich gave the decisive damaging testimony. More accused him of lying.

**TRADITION:** Catholic martyr narratives and Robert Bolt's drama make Rich the archetypal perjurer who sells his soul for office.

**EVIDENTIARY STATUS:** the exact Tower conversation cannot now be reconstructed with certainty. The prosecution evidence was highly problematic and depended heavily on Rich, but “Rich committed perjury” should be identified as a historical accusation and later settled reputation rather than a fact demonstrated by a surviving recording or independent transcript.

### Verdict

The jury withdrew and returned a guilty verdict after about **a quarter of an hour**, according to the contemporary Paris account. [S10]

### More speaks after conviction

Once convicted, More abandoned strategic silence and stated openly that he did not believe a temporal ruler could assume supreme spiritual headship over the church.

The Paris account gives him a historical/ecclesiological argument: he opposed the English statute to the authority of saints, councils, and the wider Christian world. It also reports him praying that he and his judges might ultimately be united in charity, invoking Paul and Stephen. [S10]

### Sentence

The statutory punishment for treason was hanging, drawing, and quartering. Henry commuted More's punishment to **beheading**.

The popular joke attributed to More when told of the commutation—thanking the king and hoping his friends were spared similar favors—is part of the anecdotal martyr tradition and not necessary to establish the legal fact.

---

## Execution, 6 July 1535

More was taken to **Tower Hill** on Tuesday, **6 July 1535**.

The Paris newsletter, dated 4 August 1535, reports that shortly before death he asked those present to pray for him, promised to pray for them, asked prayers that the king receive good counsel, and said in substance that he died the king's faithful servant but God's first. [S10]

This is the historical basis for the famous sentence later condensed into:

> “The King's good servant, but God's first.”

The exact English wording varies because early reports survive through transmission and translation. Robert Bolt did not invent the basic sentiment, though his play made the phrase globally famous.

### Scaffold jokes

Later biographies report More joking about the unstable scaffold and asking that his beard be moved out of the executioner's way because the beard “had committed no treason.”

**STATUS:** early/later hagiographic anecdote. These stories fit More's well-documented comic personality, but their exact wording and occurrence are less secure than the contemporary report of his final declaration.

### Decapitation

More was beheaded. His body was buried at St Peter ad Vincula. His head was placed on London Bridge.

## The Head, Burial, and Relic Tradition

### Margaret Roper and the head

A longstanding tradition states that **Margaret Roper** recovered her father's head after it had been displayed on London Bridge and preserved it.

The dramatic details—bribing a bridge keeper, catching the head as it was about to be thrown into the Thames, preserving it in spices—vary and grow in later telling.

After Margaret's death, the relic tradition connects the head with the Roper family and **St Dunstan's Church, Canterbury**. The church today treats the Roper vault as containing More's surviving cranial remains. [S27][S29]

### Current physical condition

When the vault was opened in **1997**, reports described a damaged lead casing and fragmentary remains rather than an intact display skull.

In **2025**, St Dunstan's parochial church council announced an intention, subject to ecclesiastical permission and conservation planning, to exhume and stabilize the remains in advance of the **500th anniversary of More's execution in 2035**. Contemporary reporting described possible conservation, a reliquary or shrine, and a projected fundraising need around £50,000. As of the sources consulted for this dossier, this was a proposal requiring permissions, not a completed exhumation. [S30][S31]

### Chelsea burial legend

Chelsea Old Church has strong More associations and a family monument, but the claim that More's **body** lies at Chelsea is unsupported. The body is traditionally and historically placed at St Peter ad Vincula in the Tower. [S29]

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

This category must be translated carefully into a sixteenth-century context.

### Institutions

More was associated with:
- St Anthony's School.
- Archbishop Morton's household.
- Oxford University.
- New Inn.
- Lincoln's Inn.
- Furnivall's Inn.
- Doctors' Commons.
- City of London government.
- Parliament.
- royal household and Privy Council.
- Exchequer administration.
- Duchy of Lancaster.
- Chancery.
- Star Chamber.
- diplomatic embassies.
- intellectual/printing networks in London, Paris, Louvain, Basel, Antwerp, and elsewhere.

### Printing and publishing networks

More was not a printing-company owner in the modern sense, but print technology was central to his career.

His works moved through:
- Dirk Martens at Louvain;
- Johann Froben at Basel;
- English printers including **William Rastell** and other printers of the 1520s–1530s;
- a transnational humanist correspondence network;
- posthumous Catholic publishing networks on the Continent.

His brother-in-law John Rastell and nephew William Rastell were printers/publishers and legal-humanist figures.

### Property

More held:
- London property interests;
- a lease/interest in Crosby Place;
- a substantial Chelsea estate;
- household goods, books, plate, and the normal assets of a senior Tudor official.

After his conviction and attainder, his goods and property were subject to confiscation.

### Wealth

No reliable single “net worth” figure exists.

His financial life combined:
- professional legal fees;
- civic and royal office income;
- diplomatic expenses and allowances;
- property;
- family and wardship obligations.

Erasmus emphasizes that More did not pursue money greedily. That is character testimony, not financial accounting.

### Business structures

There is no evidence that More founded or operated a “company” in the later corporate sense. He lived before the modern joint-stock and patent systems that would make the user's categories directly applicable.

### Technologies and inventions

No technological invention or patent is associated with More. His relevant “technological” environment was the expansion of **print**, which transformed both his humanist scholarship and the religious propaganda war he later fought.

## Political Development and Positions

### Monarchy

More was not a republican revolutionary. He served Henry VIII for fourteen years and accepted hierarchy, monarchy, and social rank.

Yet his epigrams, *Utopia*, parliamentary conduct, and letters contain recurrent skepticism about:
- flatterers;
- conquest;
- arbitrary princely will;
- the equation of a ruler's desire with law;
- inherited social privilege detached from virtue.

### Parliament

More used Parliament as a working institution, served as Speaker, and made the 1523 free-speech petition. He did not theorize parliamentary sovereignty in the later English constitutional sense.

### Church and state

More accepted significant royal jurisdiction over temporal affairs of the church. His ultimate boundary was the claim that the king and Parliament could constitute the English monarch as supreme spiritual head of the church independent of the larger Catholic communion.

### War and diplomacy

More repeatedly expressed skepticism about war and conquest while serving a monarch who fought expensive wars. His career therefore included the classic humanist problem of counsel: whether an intellectual compromises himself by serving power in hopes of moderating it.

### Poverty and economic criticism

*Utopia* contains some of the sixteenth century's most famous attacks on enclosure, dispossession, criminalization of poverty, concentrated wealth, and predatory state policy. These critiques later made More attractive to socialist readers.

The actual More remained a property-owning elite lawyer and royal officer. His fictional critique did not turn into a program to abolish private property in England.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### John More — father

Lawyer, judge, disciplinarian in Erasmus's account, and More's route into the legal profession. The father's career provided both professional model and social capital.

### John Morton — patron and early political mentor

Archbishop and Lord Chancellor. More's youthful service exposed him to high politics. Morton's memories may have influenced the *Richard III* project.

### John Colet — humanist and religious friend

A major representative of English Christian humanism. Colet's scriptural and moral reformism mattered before “Reformation” divided humanists into hostile confessional camps.

### William Grocyn, Thomas Linacre, William Lily — teachers and collaborators

They helped form More's Greek scholarship and connected him to the English classical revival.

### Erasmus — closest international intellectual friend

The relationship involved:
- correspondence;
- mutual literary promotion;
- joking wordplay;
- shared friends and patrons;
- More's defense of Erasmus in controversies;
- Erasmus's decisive role in constructing the first international image of More.

Erasmus outlived More by one year, dying in 1536.

### Pieter Gillis / Peter Giles — Antwerp humanist

Friend, civic official, and character in *Utopia*. More's Low Countries intellectual network mattered directly to the book's creation.

### Guillaume Budé — French humanist

Part of the European correspondence and reception network around *Utopia*.

### Antonio Bonvisi — merchant-banker and intimate friend

Bonvisi, an Italian merchant resident in London, was a longstanding friend. More's correspondence and tradition connect Bonvisi to practical support during More's imprisonment. This relationship shows how humanist, diplomatic, mercantile, and financial networks overlapped.

### Cuthbert Tunstall — bishop, diplomat, collaborator

Worked with More in diplomacy and in opposition to evangelical books. Tunstall asked More to produce vernacular defenses of traditional religion.

### Thomas Wolsey — superior and political counterpart

More worked under Wolsey, accompanied him diplomatically, and succeeded him as Chancellor. Later drama tends to create a cleaner moral contrast than the working political relationship warrants.

### Henry VIII — king, patron, collaborator, prosecutor

This was the central political relationship of More's life.

Phases:
1. More as humanist admirer of the young king.
2. reluctant recruitment to royal service.
3. increasing access, honors, diplomacy, theological collaboration.
4. disagreement over the annulment.
5. More's service as Chancellor despite disagreement.
6. resignation.
7. escalating pressure.
8. imprisonment and prosecution.
9. execution ordered under the king's authority.

### Catherine of Aragon

More rejected the theological/canonical case for ending Henry's marriage but did not become Catherine's public partisan while in office.

### Anne Boleyn

Anne symbolized the marriage settlement More could not endorse. Claims of a personal hatred should be treated cautiously; the conflict was political, dynastic, ecclesiological, and religious.

### Thomas Cromwell

Cromwell became chief architect of the Henrician supremacy settlement and was deeply involved in interrogating and prosecuting opponents.

More and Cromwell are often turned into opposite archetypes—conscience versus power in Bolt, or reactionary zealot versus pragmatic modernizer in some later narratives. Both constructions simplify complicated Tudor officials.

### Christopher St German

Legal and pamphlet opponent over ecclesiastical jurisdiction.

### Martin Luther

A distant but intense polemical enemy. More never met Luther. Their “relationship” was textual and confessional.

### William Tyndale

More's most important English Protestant intellectual opponent. Their published exchange addressed:
- translation;
- justification;
- church;
- sacraments;
- clergy;
- authority;
- interpretation of scripture.

Tyndale was captured near Antwerp in 1535 and executed in Brabant in 1536, after More's death.

### John Fisher

Fellow opponent of the supremacy, fellow prisoner, and later fellow canonized martyr. The prosecution tried to turn similarity of their positions into evidence of coordination.

### Richard Rich

The decisive prosecution witness at More's trial and, in later drama, one of the most famous embodiments of betrayal in English historical memory.

### Margaret Roper

Daughter, intellectual partner, correspondent, visitor in imprisonment, transmitter of family memory, and central figure in the relic tradition.

### William Roper

Son-in-law. His biography became foundational to the Catholic and later general More tradition.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### 1. Heresy persecution

**DOCUMENTED:** More actively participated in the suppression of Protestant/evangelical heresy, sought prohibited books, detained and interrogated suspects, defended the legal punishment of persistent heresy, and praised some burnings.

**LEGAL QUALIFICATION:** formal heresy convictions were ecclesiastical; More was not himself an ecclesiastical judge issuing those sentences.

**HISTORICAL DEBATE:** how much personal causal responsibility should attach to the Chancellor for particular executions, and how his role compares with bishops, local officers, and the king.

### 2. Personal torture

**ALLEGATION:** More personally whipped or tortured heretics at Chelsea and attended racking.

**SOURCES FOR ALLEGATION:** Protestant testimony and especially Foxe's later *Acts and Monuments*.

**MORE'S RESPONSE:** explicit denial in his 1533 *Apology*, alongside admission of two other corporal punishments.

**MODERN ASSESSMENT:** no secure independent evidence establishes the most famous personal-torture stories. They should not be stated as fact. [S18][S20][S21]

### 3. Illegal imprisonment

Critics accused More of holding religious suspects improperly in his own house. More acknowledged “safe keeping” of suspects but denied abusive treatment.

Domestic detention by a powerful officeholder illustrates Tudor governmental practice that does not map neatly onto modern police/court procedure. Whether individual confinements exceeded lawful authority has been debated.

### 4. Censorship

**DOCUMENTED:** More actively supported suppression of prohibited evangelical books and worked inside a state-church censorship regime.

### 5. Bribery / gifts

**ALLEGATIONS:** after More's resignation, hostile evidence was gathered alleging improper gifts in office.

**OUTCOME:** no corruption conviction resulted. Friendly accounts say gifts were refused or returned. The evidentiary record is insufficient for a claim of systemic bribery.

### 6. Elizabeth Barton conspiracy

**INVESTIGATION / POLITICAL ACCUSATION:** More's contacts with Barton were investigated as possible participation in opposition to the king.

**OUTCOME:** More was not included in the final attainder after defending the limited nature of his contact.

**UNPROVEN:** an organized More-Barton conspiracy to overthrow Henry.

### 7. Treason conviction

**FORMAL COURT FINDING:** More was convicted of high treason on 1 July 1535.

**EVIDENTIARY CONTROVERSY:** the conviction depended critically on Richard Rich's disputed testimony about More's spoken denial of parliamentary competence over spiritual supremacy.

**MORE'S POSITION:** he denied Rich's account and maintained that his prior silence was not treason.

**HISTORICAL LEGAL ASSESSMENT:** modern lawyers and historians continue to debate procedural details, statutory interpretation, indictment structure, and whether the case satisfied even Tudor law. It is safe to call the prosecution politically driven; it is not necessary to pretend Tudor treason procedure was a modern criminal trial. [S24]

### 8. “Money laundering,” organized crime, or intelligence service

No evidence found supports modern allegations of:
- money laundering;
- organized criminal enterprise;
- clandestine intelligence-service membership in the modern sense;
- private assassination networks.

More participated in diplomacy, royal information networks, searches for prohibited books, interrogation, and surveillance of religious dissent—activities that could include informants and cross-border agents—but calling this a modern intelligence agency would be anachronistic.

### 9. Tyndale capture conspiracy

Brian Moynahan advanced a thesis that More helped finance or mastermind the betrayal and capture of **William Tyndale** in Antwerp in May 1535, even while More was imprisoned.

Richard Rex has called the proposition bizarre and later published a focused study of the claim.

**What is secure:** More had spent years opposing Tyndale and wanted his influence suppressed.

**What is not secure:** that More in the Tower directed the operation that captured him.

Tyndale's execution in October 1536 occurred sixteen months after More's own death and under the jurisdiction of the Habsburg Netherlands. The specific More-mastermind theory should therefore be classified **DISPUTED / UNPROVEN**, not fact. [S18][S33]

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: “Born at Milk Street” as an unquestioned fact

**Story:** More was born in a house on Milk Street in the City of London.

**Appearance/circulation:** later family biography and local commemoration; repeated by biographies and plaques.

**Evidence:** London and the 7 February 1478 date are secure. Recent archival work argues that the Milk Street address cannot be securely demonstrated for the family at the date of More's birth.

**Effect:** gave More a precise urban birthplace and strengthened his identity as a City of London figure.

**Classification:** **TRADITIONAL / DISPUTED exact location.** [S13]

### Myth 2: The 1504 “beardless boy” defeats Henry VII

**Story:** young More blocked Henry VII's financial demand in Parliament; the king retaliated by imprisoning/finding father John More.

**Appearance:** Roper/family biography and subsequent lives.

**Evidence:** More was in Parliament, but documentary confirmation for the polished retaliation narrative is weak.

**Effect:** retroactively makes More's entire career a straight line toward resistance to royal tyranny.

**Classification:** **EARLY BIOGRAPHICAL TRADITION; disputed in detail.**

### Myth 3: Morton predicts More's greatness

**Story:** Archbishop Morton watched the page perform and predicted he would become extraordinary.

**Source:** early sympathetic biography.

**Evidence:** no independent verification.

**Function:** hagiographic “child marked for greatness” motif, while reflecting More's genuine presence in Morton's household.

### Myth 4: More nearly became a Carthusian monk

**Story:** More was on the verge of joining the Carthusians but chose marriage.

**Evidence:** serious religious experimentation and close Carthusian association are well supported by Erasmus and Roper. The exact psychological threshold—“nearly became a monk”—cannot be measured.

**Classification:** **DOCUMENTED CORE, biographically dramatized edges.**

### Myth 5: Hair shirt and extreme self-mortification

**Story:** beneath statesman's robes More constantly wore a hair shirt and secretly scourged himself.

**Evidence:** ascetic practice is embedded in early testimony and family relic tradition. Exact frequency and every later devotional detail are not independently verifiable.

**Effect:** central to Catholic saintly iconography and Bolt's domestic More.

### Myth 6: The “Strangers' Case” speech on Evil May Day

**Story:** More delivered the famous humanitarian speech asking London rioters to imagine being refugees.

**Origin:** the Elizabethan collaborative play *Sir Thomas More*, written decades after the event; the famous manuscript revision is widely attributed to Shakespeare.

**Evidence:** More participated in controlling Evil May Day. The literary speech is not a historical transcript.

**Effect:** More has become a symbolic spokesman for migrants and refugees in modern performances. [S25]

### Myth 7: “Tree of Truth” torturer

**Story:** More tied Protestant prisoners to a tree in his Chelsea garden, whipped them, tightened cords around their heads, and supervised the rack.

**Circulation:** Protestant martyrology, especially Foxe; repeated by later anti-Catholic history and modern polemic.

**Evidence for:** named stories and confessional memory; allegations circulated during or soon after More's lifetime.

**Evidence against:** More's explicit contemporary denial; chronology problems in some versions; lack of independent corroboration; skepticism by modern historians across confessional/ideological lines.

**Effect:** creates the anti-saint image of More as inquisitorial sadist and is a central counter-image to *A Man for All Seasons*.

**Classification:** **ALLEGATION / DISPUTED; personal torture not established.** This does not erase documented persecution. [S18][S20][S21]

### Myth 8: Hundreds of Protestants killed by More

**Story:** More personally sent hundreds to death.

**Evidence:** unsupported. Richard Rex notes the inflation from the conventional half-dozen executions during the chancellorship to “a few hundred.”

**Effect:** Internet and polemical simplification.

**Classification:** **FALSE/UNSUPPORTED numerical inflation**, while real persecution remains documented. [S18]

### Myth 9: More captured William Tyndale from the Tower

**Story:** More financed or masterminded Tyndale's betrayal in Antwerp while imprisoned.

**Circulation:** prominently associated with Brian Moynahan's Tyndale narrative.

**Evidence:** More had motive in the broad sense of hostility to Tyndale, but the operational chain is not securely demonstrated. More had been in prison since April 1534.

**Effect:** extends More's anti-heresy campaign beyond his loss of office and across borders.

**Classification:** **DISPUTED / UNPROVEN.** [S18][S33]

### Myth 10: More wrote *Utopia* as a literal communist constitution

**Story:** *Utopia* is More's straightforward blueprint for an ideal communist state.

**Evidence:** common property is central to the island, but the work is a dialogic fiction full of irony, Greek puns, contradictions, and distancing devices. The More-character does not simply endorse Hythloday.

**Circulation:** socialist, communist, Catholic, liberal, and anti-utopian traditions all produced simplified versions.

**Effect:** More was incorporated into socialist genealogy, including Soviet commemorative culture.

**Classification:** **RECEPTION NARRATIVE, not adequate as literal authorial intention.**

### Myth 11: “Lenin erected a statue of Thomas More”

**Story:** Lenin placed a statue of More in Moscow as a communist ancestor.

**Underlying fact:** More's name appeared on the **1918 Obelisk of Revolutionary Thinkers** in Moscow's Alexander Garden, an imperial Romanov monument repurposed after the Revolution with a list of socialist and revolutionary predecessors.

**Effect:** became evidence for More's status in Soviet proto-socialist genealogy.

**Classification:** **REAL COMMEMORATION, frequently misdescribed as a statue.** [S34]

### Myth 12: Richard Rich's perjury as proven courtroom fact

**Story:** Rich deliberately lied and was rewarded for it.

**Evidence:** More accused him; the prosecution relied on him; other present witnesses did not corroborate the decisive content. Rich later had a successful political career.

**Limit:** no independent transcript proves exactly what was said in the Tower.

**Effect:** Rich became the villain of More's martyr story and especially of Bolt's play.

**Classification:** **STRONG HISTORICAL ACCUSATION / UNRESOLVED FACTUAL CORE.**

### Myth 13: Exact final words

**Story:** More said, “I die the King's good servant, but God's first.”

**Evidence:** a near-contemporary Paris report gives substantially this declaration.

**Caveat:** wording varies through French/English transmission.

**Effect:** perhaps the most famous sentence attached to More; foundational for political-conscience reception.

**Classification:** **WELL-ATTESTED SENTIMENT; exact wording varies.** [S10]

### Myth 14: Beard and scaffold jokes

**Story:** he joked about the weak scaffold and moved his beard because it had committed no treason.

**Evidence:** biographical tradition, not the strongest contemporary documentary layer.

**Effect:** demonstrates the saint who keeps humanist wit at the instant of death.

**Classification:** **HAGIOGRAPHIC/ANECDOTAL TRADITION, plausible but unverified in exact form.**

### Myth 15: Margaret catches or secretly steals the head

**Story:** Margaret Roper retrieved the severed head from London Bridge and preserved it.

**Evidence:** strong longstanding family tradition; later physical association of cranial remains with the Roper vault.

**Uncertain details:** the exact method, timing, bridgekeeper transaction, and preservation process.

**Effect:** one of the central daughter-father legends of English Catholic memory.

### Myth 16: More's body is buried at Chelsea Old Church

**Story:** More lies at Chelsea.

**Evidence:** the body was buried at St Peter ad Vincula. Chelsea's More monument and family ties encouraged a rival burial tradition.

**Classification:** **UNSUBSTANTIATED local tradition.** [S29]

### Myth 17: “A Man for All Seasons” was invented by Robert Bolt

**Story:** Bolt coined the phrase.

**Evidence:** **Robert Whittington**, a contemporary schoolmaster and grammarian, described More in 1520 with language later translated as “a man for all seasons.” Bolt reused it as his title.

**Effect:** Bolt's play so thoroughly dominates modern memory that the phrase is often assumed to be twentieth century. [S35]

### Myth 18: One stable “Thomas More”

The central mythographic problem is the creation of mutually exclusive total Mores:
- Catholic saint without coercive politics.
- sadistic torturer without humanist complexity.
- modern liberal individualist.
- socialist revolutionary.
- reactionary medieval clericalist.
- Tudor propagandist.
- defender of parliamentary liberty.
- enemy of free thought.

Each image is built from real material but becomes myth when it suppresses contrary evidence.

## Works by Thomas More

The following list distinguishes original composition, translation, lifetime publication, posthumous publication, collections, and disputed association. Dates are approximate when composition and printing differ. The standard modern scholarly corpus is the 15-volume **Yale Edition of the Complete Works of St. Thomas More** (1963–1997). The 2020 *Essential Works of Thomas More* offers a large one-volume selection. [S6][S7][S36]

### Early English poems, c.1496–1504

Includes poems later collected under titles such as:
- *A Pageant of Life* / pageant verses;
- *A Merry Jest*;
- *A Rueful Lamentation*;
- verses on Fortune;
- other short English poems.

**Status:** authentic core, with exact dating and textual history varying by poem. Many survive through later printing rather than original manuscripts.

### Latin verses to William Holt's *Lac Puerorum*, c.1500

Early humanist Latin verse.

### Letter to John Colet, c.1504

Important for More's early religious-humanist network.

### Translations of Lucian, published 1506

More translated several works of **Lucian of Samosata** from Greek into Latin. Erasmus also translated Lucian, and their versions circulated in shared humanist publishing contexts.

**Status:** genuine translations, not original Lucianic works.

### *The Life of John Picus* / *The Life of Pico della Mirandola*, c.1510

English adaptation/translation derived from **Gianfrancesco Pico's** life of his uncle Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, with translated devotional writings.

**Status:** translation/adaptation, not an original biography from independent research.

### *Coronation Ode*, 1509

Latin celebratory verse for Henry VIII's accession/coronation era.

### Latin *Epigrammata* / Epigrams, chiefly c.1500–1520; printed 1518 and 1520

Hundreds of Latin epigrams on:
- friendship;
- classical themes;
- political power;
- kings;
- tyranny;
- marriage;
- wit;
- humanist rivalry.

Some appeared with *Utopia* and Erasmus's work in Froben editions.

### *History of King Richard III* / *Historia Richardi Tertii*, c.1513–1518

English and Latin versions, unfinished.

**Lifetime status:** not published by More.

**Posthumous transmission:** English text entered print in the 1540s and 1550s; the Latin text was published later. It strongly influenced Hall, Holinshed, and Shakespeare. [S23]

### Letter to Martin Dorp, 1515

Latin humanist defense of Erasmus and humanist theological method.

### *Utopia*, 1516

Original Latin prose dialogue/fiction.

**First edition:** Louvain, 1516.

**Revised early editions:** Paris and Basel, 1517–1518.

**First English translation:** Ralph Robinson, 1551.

### Letters to children and tutor, 1517–1522

Part of More's family-humanist correspondence and evidence for his educational program.

### Letter to Oxford, 1518

Defense of Greek learning and humanist education.

### Letter to a Monk, 1519

Part of More's humanist-religious controversial correspondence.

### Letter to Germain de Brie / Brixius, 1520

Connected to a bitter humanist literary controversy over Latin poetry and reputation.

### *Quattuor Novissima* / *The Four Last Things*, c.1522

Meditation on death, judgment, heaven, and hell.

**Status:** unfinished; published posthumously.

### *Responsio ad Lutherum*, 1523

Latin answer to Martin Luther, associated with the pseudonym **Gulielmus Rosseus**.

**Status:** genuine More work; pseudonymous presentation.

### Letter to Bugenhagen, 1526

A Latin anti-Reformation letter/polemic, later printed posthumously.

### *A Dialogue Concerning Heresies*, June 1529; revised edition 1531

English dialogue defending traditional doctrine and attacking evangelical reform.

### *The Supplication of Souls*, September 1529

Reply to Simon Fish's *Supplication for the Beggars*.

### *The Confutation of Tyndale's Answer*, 1532–1533

Massive English response to William Tyndale.

Published in parts; unfinished as a larger projected enterprise.

### *Letter against Frith*, 1532/1533

Against John Frith's Eucharistic positions.

### *The Apology of Sir Thomas More*, April 1533

Defense of More's conduct, ecclesiastical authority, and anti-heresy program; crucial contemporary source for the torture controversy.

### *The Debellation of Salem and Bizance*, 1533

Continuation of controversy over law and ecclesiastical jurisdiction, associated especially with Christopher St German.

### *The Answer to a Poisoned Book*, December 1533

Against George Joye; More's last book printed while he was alive.

### *A Treatise upon the Passion*, 1534

Tower-era devotional work; unfinished/posthumous.

### *A Treatise to Receive the Blessed Body*, 1534–1535

Devotional Eucharistic writing.

### *A Dialogue of Comfort Against Tribulation*, 1534

Tower prison dialogue; first printed 1553.

### “A Dialogue on Conscience,” 1534

A title used for prison-era conscience material / reconstructed dialogue tradition; textual status differs from the major formal works and should not be confused with a separately published lifetime book.

### “Imploring Divine Help against Temptation,” 1534–1535

Prayer/devotional text.

### “A Godly Instruction” and “A Godly Meditation,” 1534–1535

Prison devotional pieces, including meditation on detachment.

### *De tristitia Christi* / *The Sadness of Christ*, 1535

Latin meditation on Christ's agony in Gethsemane.

**Status:** final major work; autograph manuscript survives.

### “A Devout Prayer,” July 1535

Prayer associated with More's final days.

### Correspondence, 1499–1535

Approximately 280 letters are known in the broader correspondence tradition, with major modern editions. Letters survive in Latin and English to:
- Erasmus;
- Colet;
- children;
- tutors;
- bishops;
- European humanists;
- royal officials;
- friends;
- Antonio Bonvisi;
- Margaret Roper;
- Alice More;
- others.

The correspondence is indispensable for reconstructing More because the public polemical persona, humanist persona, domestic persona, and prison conscience can be compared directly.

### Epitaph, 1532

More composed a Latin epitaph for the family tomb at Chelsea, summarizing public offices, diplomatic work, family relationships, and opposition to heresy. It is self-fashioning as well as biographical evidence.

### Association with Henry VIII's *Assertio Septem Sacramentorum*, 1521

**Status: associated/advisory, not securely co-authored.** More participated in the king's theological milieu and may have reviewed material. The exact division of authorship cannot support listing the royal book simply as “by Henry VIII and Thomas More.”

### Spurious or doubtful material

The canonical work of the Yale editors and manuscript cataloguers rejects or questions several texts traditionally attached to More.

Examples include:
- the prayer beginning “Helpe me dere father,” attributed in manuscript tradition but identified with John Fisher;
- an uncertain Latin epigram on a moneylender/usurer;
- minor epitaphic or short texts with weak attribution.

For attribution work, the **Catalogue of English Literary Manuscripts (CELM)** and the Yale edition should take priority over devotional anthologies that attach attractive anonymous prayers to More. [S7]

Large modern one-volume anthology combining literary, political, religious, epistolary, and prison writings. It is particularly useful for seeing the whole corpus rather than reducing More to *Utopia*. [S6]

## Films, Documentaries, Drama, Television, Podcasts, and Archival Material

### *Sir Thomas More* — Elizabethan/Jacobean collaborative play

Written and revised by several dramatists, traditionally including **Anthony Munday, Henry Chettle, Thomas Dekker, Thomas Heywood**, and, in the famous “Hand D” revision, probably **William Shakespeare**.

The play dramatizes More's rise, Evil May Day, chancellorship, fall, and execution. Its refugee/stranger speech has become a modern human-rights text.

**Historical use:** valuable for More's Elizabethan cultural afterlife, not as a transcript of his life. [S25]

### Robert Bolt, *A Man for All Seasons*

Development:
- earlier BBC radio treatment in the 1950s;
- television adaptation;
- stage play first performed in London in **1960**.

Bolt explicitly constructs More as the individual who protects an inviolable self through law and conscience.

This More is historically grounded but strongly twentieth-century: legalistic, existential, and liberal in emphasis. Bolt's work became so influential that many later people remember its dialogue as if it were Tudor documentation.

### *A Man for All Seasons* (1966)

**Director:** Fred Zinnemann.  
**Screenplay:** Robert Bolt.  
**Thomas More:** Paul Scofield.  
**Henry VIII:** Robert Shaw.  
**Richard Rich:** John Hurt.  
**Cardinal Wolsey:** Orson Welles.

The film won major Academy Awards and became the dominant visual More of the twentieth century. [S40]

### *A Man for All Seasons* (1988 television film)

Directed by and starring **Charlton Heston** as More. A later screen interpretation of Bolt's play.

### *The Six Wives of Henry VIII* (BBC, 1970)

More appears within the larger television reconstruction of Henry's court.

### *The Tudors* (television series, 2007–2010)

**Jeremy Northam** played More. The series presents the humanist, courtier, family man, religious conservative, and victim of royal policy through prestige television conventions rather than strict documentary reconstruction.

### Hilary Mantel, *Wolf Hall* (2009) and adaptations

Mantel's Cromwell-centered novel gives More a sharply hostile fictional treatment. More's treatment of heretics and combative Catholicism are emphasized while the narrative denies him the saintly center he occupies in Bolt.

Richard Rex specifically notes that the novel has its More acknowledge torture allegations that the historical More denied in print; therefore Mantel cannot be used as evidence for the underlying allegation. [S18]

## Physical Appearance, Health, Personality, and Habits

### Appearance

The richest written description is Erasmus's 1519 letter to Hutten:
- medium height;
- proportionate body;
- fair rather than ruddy complexion;
- dark-auburn hair;
- thin beard;
- blue-gray eyes;
- one shoulder carried slightly higher;
- hands somewhat rough compared with his otherwise refined appearance.

Holbein's 1527 portrait provides the best-known visual evidence. [S4]

### Health

Erasmus described More's health in 1519 as adequate but not especially robust.

More cited illness when resigning the chancellorship in 1532. There is no secure diagnosis in the sources consulted.

By his July 1535 trial, after prolonged imprisonment, the Paris newsletter described severe infirmity and the court provided a chair. [S10]

### Diet and drink

Erasmus says More was unfastidious about food and had preferred water in youth, later drinking small amounts socially. No evidence supports alcoholism or another substance addiction.

### Humor

Humor is one of the most consistently attested traits across friend, enemy, writing, and anecdote:
- puns;
- epigrams;
- Lucianic irony;
- practical joking;
- self-deprecation;
- scaffold-wit tradition.

It was not merely temperament. More used joking as a rhetorical strategy capable of intimacy, aggression, ambiguity, and deflection.

### Psychological state

No responsible modern diagnosis can be made from the evidence.

What can be documented in the prison writings is:
- fear of physical suffering;
- anxiety about betrayal of conscience;
- periods of consolation;
- disciplined preparation for death;
- continuing humor;
- attachment to family.

His writings do not support a simplistic picture either of suicidal martyr-seeking or of emotionless heroic certainty.

## Death / Later Life / Present Status

Thomas More died by beheading on 6 July 1535. There is no “later life” beyond imprisonment and execution.

His death was:
- a legal execution under an act of Henry VIII's government;
- interpreted by the government as punishment for treason;
- interpreted by Catholic tradition as martyrdom for refusal to abandon the universal church;
- interpreted by Protestant martyrology in the context of More's own role as a persecutor;
- later interpreted by constitutional and liberal traditions as a conflict between conscience and the state.

The reason for his death cannot be reduced to “he refused Henry's divorce.” The final legal issue was **royal ecclesiastical supremacy and treason**, although the marriage crisis generated the political break that made the supremacy issue unavoidable.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate Catholic family memory

Roper, Harpsfield, Margaret Roper's memory, the Rastell publishing project, and continental Catholic networks transformed More rapidly into a martyr figure.

The 1557 *Workes* during Mary I's reign preserved the English corpus at a moment when Catholic memory briefly had political protection in England.

### Protestant national memory

John Foxe's *Acts and Monuments* supplied a counter-More:
- persecutor;
- enemy of the English gospel;
- participant in imprisonment and suffering of reformers.

This hostile memory became part of English Protestant identity.

### Humanist and literary More

Independent of confessional sainthood, More survived as:
- friend of Erasmus;
- master of Latin and English prose;
- author of *Utopia*;
- historian of Richard III;
- epigrammatist;
- patron of female humanist education.

### Socialist More

From the nineteenth century onward, socialist writers treated *Utopia* as an ancestor of communist/socialist social criticism.

**Karl Kautsky**, in *Thomas More and His Utopia* (1888), produced one of the best-known Marxist appropriations.

The Soviet revolutionary canon placed More's name on the 1918 Moscow Obelisk of Revolutionary Thinkers. This was a selective reception of *Utopia*, not evidence that More himself was a modern Marxist. [S34]

### Catholic saint

Canonization in 1935 consolidated the martyr interpretation in institutional Catholic memory. More became especially associated with:
- lawyers;
- public officials;
- conscience;
- political ethics.

John Paul II's 2000 proclamation as patron of statesmen and politicians globalized that political saint cult. [S28]

### Robert Bolt's liberal-conscience More

*A Man for All Seasons* transformed More into a modern icon of:
- individual conscience;
- due process;
- legal forms as protection against arbitrary government;
- refusal to instrumentalize the self for political convenience.

Bolt's More is not fiction without historical roots. But it suppresses much of the anti-heresy magistrate and gives the Tudor conscience problem a twentieth-century political vocabulary.

### Revisionist More

From the later twentieth century, historians such as G. R. Elton, Richard Marius, John Guy, Alistair Fox, Eamon Duffy, Richard Rex, and others reopened:
- More's political competence;
- his relation to Cromwell;
- religious persecution;
- psychological interpretation;
- humanist continuity;
- trial procedure;
- the difference between evidence and confessional memory.

There is no longer a serious scholarly reason to choose between “saint” and “monster” as total explanation.

### Hilary Mantel's More

Mantel's *Wolf Hall* reverses the dramatic camera position: Cromwell becomes the complex central consciousness and More often appears from outside as brilliant, cruel, rigid, and socially privileged.

This had enormous cultural effect. It also generated renewed argument over the personal-torture allegations and how far historical fiction may compress disputed claims into character.

### Shakespeare and refugee politics

The *Sir Thomas More* manuscript's “Strangers' Case” speech has been repeatedly performed in twenty-first-century debates over migration. The historical More thereby became a symbolic voice for refugees through words almost certainly written decades after his death by a dramatist, probably Shakespeare.

This is an exemplary case of myth becoming culturally real without becoming biographical fact.

### Law and conscience

More remains a recurring figure in legal education and political ethics because his final strategy involved:
- statutory construction;
- silence;
- oath;
- jurisdiction;
- parliamentary competence;
- evidentiary sufficiency;
- conscience.

His own persecution of heresy makes this legacy structurally difficult rather than simple: a man remembered for resisting compelled belief had previously participated in a system that coerced religious dissent.

That tension is a historical fact, not a required moral conclusion.

## Chronology

### 1478
**7 February:** Thomas More born in London to John More and Agnes Graunger. Exact Milk Street birthplace is traditional but not securely established.

### c.1484–1489
Attends St Anthony's School, London.

### c.1489–1491
Serves as a page in the household of Archbishop John Morton, Lord Chancellor.

### c.1491–1493
Studies at Oxford; formation in Latin and emerging Greek humanism.

### c.1493–1495
Studies at New Inn in preparation for legal career.

### 1496–c.1501
Studies at Lincoln's Inn; called to the bar around 1501.

### 1499
Meets Erasmus.

### c.1501
Lectures on Augustine's *City of God*; intensifies Greek study.

### c.1501–1504
Frequents the London Charterhouse and seriously considers religious vocation.

### c.1503–1506
Reader at Furnivall's Inn.

### 1504
Serves in Parliament; later tradition places his clash with Henry VII's financial demands here.

### 1505
Marries Jane Colt. Daughter Margaret born.

### 1506
Lucian translations published. Daughter Elizabeth born.

### 1507
Financial secretary at Lincoln's Inn. Daughter Cecily born.

### c.1508
Chronologies record visits to Paris and Louvain.

### 1509
Son John born. Henry VIII accedes; More writes coronation verse.

### 1510
Parliament. More becomes Under-Sheriff of London. *Life of Pico* belongs around this period.

### 1511
Jane Colt More dies. More rapidly marries widow Alice Middleton. Reader at Lincoln's Inn.

### 1512
Governor and treasurer of Lincoln's Inn.

### c.1513–1518
Works on English and Latin *History of King Richard III*.

### 1514
Public/legal appointments including Doctors' Commons and commission work.

### 1515
Commercial embassy to Bruges and Antwerp. Develops Book II of *Utopia*. Writes to Martin Dorp.

### 1516
*Utopia* published in Latin at Louvain.

### 1517
Further *Utopia* publication. Calais mission. More participates as London officer in suppressing Evil May Day riot.

### 1518
Enters full royal service. Master of Requests. *Utopia* and Latin poems appear in important Basel editions.

### 1519
**23 July:** Erasmus sends Ulrich von Hutten his famous portrait of More.

### 1520
Attends Field of Cloth of Gold. Letter/controversy with Germain de Brie.

### 1521
Knighted. Under-Treasurer. Diplomatic mission. Margaret marries William Roper. Henry VIII's *Assertio Septem Sacramentorum* appears with More involved in the wider royal theological effort.

### c.1522
Writes *Four Last Things*. Serves Henry as secretary/councillor and advises on war.

### 1523
Speaker of Commons; formal petition for freedom of speech. *Responsio ad Lutherum* published. Acquires/leases Crosby Place.

### 1524
High Steward of Oxford. Moves household toward Chelsea.

### 1525
High Steward of Cambridge. Chancellor of Duchy of Lancaster. Elizabeth and Cecily marry William Dauncey and Giles Heron.

### 1526
Royal council work. Tyndale's New Testament circulates in England.

### 1527
Diplomatic travel with Wolsey to France. Henry consults More about ending marriage to Catherine. Holbein produces More family imagery.

### 1528
Bishop Cuthbert Tunstall asks More to defend Catholic teaching in English.

### 1529
Peace of Cambrai diplomatic work. *Dialogue Concerning Heresies*. *Supplication of Souls*.  
**October:** More becomes Lord Chancellor. Son John marries Anne Cresacre.

### 1530
Thomas Hitton burned for heresy during More's chancellorship. Conflict over king's marriage intensifies.

### 1531
Royal supremacy crisis deepens. Thomas Bilney, Richard Bayfield, John Tewkesbury and others suffer heresy prosecution/execution during the period.

### 1532
James Bainham executed. More publishes opening books of *Confutation of Tyndale's Answer*.  
**15 May:** Submission of Clergy.  
**16 May:** More resigns as Lord Chancellor.

### 1533
Further *Confutation*. *Apology*. *Debellation of Salem and Bizance*. *Answer to a Poisoned Book*.  
More does not attend Anne Boleyn's coronation. Government investigates his links to Elizabeth Barton and corruption allegations; neither produces the final case that kills him.

### 1534
More summoned to Lambeth over succession oath.  
**13 April:** examination at Lambeth.  
**17 April:** imprisoned in Tower after refusing prescribed oath.  
Writes *Dialogue of Comfort*, Passion and devotional material.  
Later 1534 legislation formally establishes royal supremacy and expands treason law.

### 1535
Writes *De tristitia Christi* and final prayers/letters.  
**4 May:** family tradition places Margaret's visit around execution of Carthusian priors.  
**22 June:** John Fisher executed.  
**1 July:** More tried at Westminster Hall and convicted of high treason after Richard Rich's disputed testimony.  
**6 July:** More beheaded on Tower Hill. Body buried at St Peter ad Vincula; head displayed on London Bridge.

### c.1556
William Roper composes early biography.

### 1557
William Rastell publishes the large English *Workes*.

### 1563
First edition of Foxe's *Acts and Monuments*; Protestant hostile memory of More develops.

### 1565–1566
Latin *Opera* published at Louvain.

### 1588
Thomas Stapleton publishes *Tres Thomae*, embedding More in Counter-Reformation hagiography.

### early seventeenth century
Cresacre More's family biography written and subsequently printed on the Continent.

### 1886
More and Fisher beatified among English martyrs.

### 1888
Karl Kautsky publishes *Thomas More and His Utopia*, a major Marxist appropriation.

### 1918
More's name appears on Moscow's Obelisk of Revolutionary Thinkers.

### 1935
More and Fisher canonized by Pope Pius XI. R. W. Chambers publishes major biography.

### 1960
Robert Bolt's stage *A Man for All Seasons* opens in London.

### 1963–1997
Yale Edition of the Complete Works published in fifteen volumes.

### 1966
Fred Zinnemann's film *A Man for All Seasons*, starring Paul Scofield, released.

### 1984
Richard Marius publishes major revisionist biography.

### 1998
Peter Ackroyd publishes *The Life of Thomas More*.

### 2000
John Paul II proclaims Thomas More patron saint of statesmen and politicians.

### 2009
Hilary Mantel publishes *Wolf Hall*, generating a new hostile/anti-hagiographic popular More.

### 2011
*The Cambridge Companion to Thomas More* and *Thomas More's Trial by Jury* consolidate major modern scholarly debates.

### 2015
BBC *Wolf Hall* broadcasts with Anton Lesser as More.

### 2023
Richard Rex publishes focused research on the allegation that More arranged William Tyndale's capture.

### 2024
Joanne Paul publishes archival research challenging certainty about Milk Street as More's birthplace.

### 2025
Joanne Paul's major new biography published. St Dunstan's Church publicizes a proposal to conserve the cranial remains traditionally identified as More's head ahead of the 2035 quincentenary.

### 2026
More remains simultaneously a Catholic saint, canonical humanist author, foundational utopian writer, contested Tudor magistrate, and recurrent symbol in debates over conscience, religious coercion, law, and political obedience.
