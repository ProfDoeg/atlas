# Eadweard Muybridge: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs Muybridge’s life from archival descriptions, institutional collections, digitized publications, patents, specialist scholarship, and contemporary trial reporting preserved or transcribed by later repositories.

Labels used:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a primary document, patent, institutional record, contemporary report, or several convergent sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a credible secondary source but not independently confirmed here from the underlying record.
- **Allegation or theory:** asserted by a participant or scholar but not conclusively established.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulating story whose documentary basis is weak, absent, or contradicted.

Dates and spellings vary substantially in the literature. Particularly unstable are Muybridge’s successive names, the date of his emigration, the chronology of his first horse experiments, and the biological paternity of Florado Helios Muybridge.

Muybridge deliberately reconstructed his public name. “Eadweard” evoked an archaic spelling of Edward; “Muybridge” was apparently intended to look Anglo-Saxon. The Kingston coronation stone, whose inscription included the name Eadweard, is frequently identified as an influence. His changing signatures and spellings were part of an unusually sustained act of self-fashioning.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Documented/reported fact:** Muybridge was the second of four sons of:

- **John Muggeridge** (1797–1843), a corn chandler or grain-and-coal merchant in Kingston.
- **Susannah Muggeridge**, née Smith (1807/08–1874), from a Hampton Wick family involved in barge ownership.

The family occupied premises at or near **30 High Street, Kingston**, close to the Thames. Commercial space was on the lower floor and domestic rooms above. Following John Muggeridge’s death in 1843, Susannah continued the business.

### Siblings and extended family

The Science Museum Group identifies four sons but the sources consulted here do not consistently supply all their names. A brother named **Thomas Muggeridge** appears in accounts of Edward’s commercial affairs around 1860.

Reported extended-family connections include:

- **Norman Selfe** (1839–1911), a younger cousin who became an engineer in Australia.
- **Maybanke Anderson**, née Selfe (1845–1927), a cousin associated with Australian education and women’s suffrage.
- **Catherine Smith**, the cousin at whose Kingston home Muybridge reportedly died.

His paternal family included stationers and corn merchants. A grandfather, John Muggeridge, is reported to have worked as a stationer, a trade relevant to Edward’s later bookselling career. Some details in online genealogical summaries remain insufficiently sourced and should not be treated as a complete ancestry.

### Marriage

**Documented fact:** Muybridge married **Flora Shallcross Stone**, née Downs, on **20 May 1871**. She was approximately twenty years younger and had previously been married.

The marriage deteriorated amid Muybridge’s long absences and Flora’s relationship with **Harry Larkyns**, an English-born journalist, drama critic, adventurer, and sometime photographer. Flora filed for divorce in December 1874 while Muybridge was awaiting trial for killing Larkyns. The initial petition, based on extreme cruelty, was dismissed in March 1875; another proceeding followed.

Flora died on **18 July 1875**, reportedly at St Mary’s Hospital in San Francisco.

### Florado Helios Muybridge

Flora gave birth on **14 April 1874** to a son named **Florado Helios Muybridge**.

The child’s paternity is unresolved:

- Muybridge initially treated him as his son.
- Testimony and reporting surrounding the murder case indicated that Flora and Larkyns had a sexual relationship.
- A photograph of the infant allegedly sent or intended for Larkyns was reportedly marked “Little Harry.”
- Later writers commonly state that Larkyns was the biological father, but the sources consulted disclose no genetic evidence or legally conclusive paternity finding.

Accordingly, Larkyns’s paternity is best classified as a **strong contemporary allegation or probability, not a documented biological fact**.

After Flora’s death, Florado was placed in an orphanage or children’s home. Muybridge reportedly paid some expenses but did not raise him. Florado later worked as a ranch hand and gardener and died in California in 1944, after being struck by an automobile, according to later biographical accounts.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1830–1850

Muybridge grew up in a commercially active riverside town rather than in an artistic or scientific household. His father died when Edward was thirteen. Little reliable evidence survives about his schooling, teachers, or formal qualifications.

No source consulted establishes:

- attendance at a university or academy;
- formal training in physics, engineering, anatomy, or art;
- apprenticeship to a professional photographer during his childhood.

His family’s stationing, grain, transport, and retail connections plausibly gave him experience with bookkeeping, merchandise, printed matter, and commercial networks, but this is contextual inference rather than documented vocational instruction.

By about 1850 he had adopted at least one altered spelling of his surname and left England for the United States. Some institutional biographies date his migration to **1850**, while the University of Pennsylvania gives **1852**. The earlier date is better represented in modern specialist accounts, but the discrepancy should be preserved.

## Education and Formation

Muybridge’s education was primarily practical and self-directed.

### Bookselling and publishing

In New York he worked in importing or selling English books. This placed him within transatlantic print distribution and exposed him to publishing, publicity, illustrated books, and copyright questions.

### Silas T. Selleck

**Documented/reported fact:** The photographer and daguerreotypist **Silas T. Selleck** was an important early associate. Muybridge knew him in New York and later worked alongside or in association with him in San Francisco. Selleck is the most plausible identifiable formative photographic connection, although surviving evidence does not establish a formal master-apprentice relationship.

### Photographic training after the 1860 accident

After returning to England following his stagecoach injury, Muybridge spent several years away from California. Biographers generally infer or report that during this period he learned wet-plate photography and developed the technical competence visible after his 1867 return. No securely identified teacher has been established.

### Intellectual formation

His later work drew upon:

- commercial stereography;
- picturesque and sublime landscape traditions;
- geological and government surveys;
- mechanics of animal gait;
- optical toys such as the phenakistiscope and zoetrope;
- magic-lantern projection;
- electrophotographic timing;
- contemporary disputes over photography as scientific evidence.

Muybridge was not a conventional academic scientist. He developed knowledge through experiments, commissions, engineering assistance, collaboration, demonstrations, and commercial publication.

## Early Career: Bookseller and Entrepreneur, c. 1850–1860

### New York and migration west

Muybridge initially represented or sold books in New York. By **1855 or 1856** he was established in San Francisco, where he operated a bookshop at **113 Montgomery Street**.

He participated in the city’s literary-commercial world and served on the board of the **Mercantile Library Association**. He sold books, prints, and photographs and worked in an increasingly competitive publishing market.

### Commercial partners

Sources associate him with:

- **Silas Selleck**, photographer;
- **W. H. Oakes**, in bookselling or publishing;
- the London printing and publishing trade;
- local San Francisco libraries and photographic sellers.

The evidence depicts Muybridge as an active businessman before he became a professional photographer. He was not an impoverished outsider suddenly discovered by Stanford.

### 1860 stagecoach accident

In 1860 Muybridge began traveling east, apparently intending to continue to England. Near the Texas–Arkansas frontier, a runaway stagecoach struck an obstruction or overturned.

**Documented core:** There was a serious coach accident; Muybridge suffered a head injury and prolonged neurological symptoms.

Symptoms later attributed to him included:

- unconsciousness or a period for which he retained no memory;
- headache;
- double vision;
- impaired hearing;
- loss or alteration of smell and taste;
- confusion;
- emotional instability.

Some accounts say he awoke at Fort Smith, Arkansas, with no memory of approximately nine days. Details depend heavily on his later testimony and recollection.

### Personality-change theory

At his 1875 murder trial, acquaintances testified that his personality had changed after the accident—from genial and even-tempered to eccentric, irritable, emotionally volatile, or obsessive.

Psychologist **Arthur P. Shimamura** later proposed that Muybridge may have sustained orbitofrontal and anterior temporal injury.

This is an **expert retrospective theory**, not a clinical diagnosis. No neurological imaging or contemporary medical chart survives to establish the location of the injury. It is relevant because the accident preceded Muybridge’s photographic career and formed part of his insanity defense.

### English interlude, 1860–1866

Muybridge recovered partly in England. He appears to have pursued technical and commercial ventures and may have participated in patent or invention-related activity beyond photography. He returned to the United States as a technically accomplished photographer by 1866–67.

Claims that brain injury “unlocked” his creativity are speculative. The chronology permits the theory but does not prove causation.

## Chronological Life History

## 1866–1871: Helios and the Western landscape

Back in San Francisco, Muybridge worked with or near Selleck’s **Cosmopolitan Gallery of Photographic Art** and adopted the commercial identity **Helios**.

“Helios, the Flying Studio” referred to the mobile wagon used to carry cameras, glass plates, chemicals, tents, water, and darkroom equipment.

### Yosemite, 1867 onward

In 1867 Muybridge undertook his first major photographic expedition to Yosemite Valley. Working with wet-collodion negatives required coating, exposing, and developing a glass plate while it remained wet. In wilderness conditions this demanded a portable darkroom and considerable logistical support.

His Yosemite photographs emphasized:

- extreme viewpoints;
- steep cliffs and drops;
- waterfalls;
- foreground trees and rocks;
- dramatic contrasts;
- human figures used to signal scale;
- the visual vocabulary of the sublime.

His work entered a field already defined by **Carleton Watkins** and **Charles Leander Weed**. Muybridge borrowed locations and conventions from predecessors but frequently sought more perilous or theatrical vantage points. Contemporary observers noted that he took equipment where packers were reluctant to follow.

### Construction, railroads, and urban development

Muybridge photographed:

- San Francisco buildings and streets;
- estates and industrial sites;
- railroad routes and construction;
- coastal scenery;
- government installations.

His photography was both artistic and infrastructural. Railroads, landowners, government departments, and publishers used photographs to document and promote western expansion.

### Alaska, 1868

Following the United States’ purchase of Alaska from Russia, Muybridge traveled there in 1868, apparently in connection with federal or military activity. His subjects included Sitka, Tlingit people, forts, harbors, and landscapes.

The photographs functioned simultaneously as geographic records, commercial images, and visual instruments of colonial incorporation. Captions and staged compositions should not automatically be read as transparent ethnographic evidence.

### Railroad survey, 1869

He photographed routes connected with the Central Pacific and Union Pacific railroads in California, Nevada, and Utah. The assignment placed him within the network of capital, transportation, and territorial transformation dominated in California by the Central Pacific’s owners.

### Lighthouses, 1871

The United States Lighthouse Board engaged Muybridge to photograph Pacific Coast lighthouses. Between approximately March and July 1871, he traveled aboard the lighthouse tender **Shubrick**.

This was a documented federal commission and helped establish his standing as a technically reliable government photographer.

---

## 1871–1874: Marriage, Stanford, Yosemite, and war photography

### Marriage and domestic life

Muybridge and Flora lived in San Francisco, although his work repeatedly took him away. The marriage’s surviving record is dominated by later courtroom evidence, making ordinary domestic life difficult to reconstruct.

### Leland Stanford commission

Former California governor and Central Pacific president **Leland Stanford** commissioned Muybridge to investigate horse locomotion.

A persistent story says Stanford wagered **$25,000** that a horse at speed had all four hooves off the ground. No reliable primary evidence for that amount was found. The wager is treated below as myth.

The actual question concerned the phases of a horse’s trot or gallop, with practical implications for breeding, training, anatomy, and artistic representation.

### First experiments, 1872–1873

The University of Pennsylvania’s institutional history gives 1872 as the beginning of the commission. Early exposures were technically inadequate or inconclusive. By 1873 Muybridge reportedly obtained a recognizable instantaneous image of Stanford’s horse **Occident**, although the better-known sequence came in 1878.

The work was interrupted rather than proceeding continuously for six years.

### Yosemite mammoth plates, 1872

Muybridge returned to Yosemite with large-format equipment and produced fifty-one mammoth-plate views. These photographs strengthened his reputation and were sold through **Bradley & Rulofson**.

He accompanied or crossed paths with painter **Albert Bierstadt** and geologist **Clarence King**, who was associated with the Fortieth Parallel Survey. The 1872 photographs received favorable reviews and an award at the 1873 Vienna exhibition is reported in Stanford’s institutional account.

### Modoc War, 1873

Muybridge photographed the Modoc War in northern California and southern Oregon, a conflict involving the United States Army and a Modoc group led by Kintpuash, commonly called Captain Jack.

Some images attributed to the campaign were staged or reconstructed rather than made during combat. “A Modoc Warrior on the War Path,” for example, participates in a theatrical visual vocabulary and should not be treated as spontaneous battlefield reportage.

This body of work is significant both as early war photography in the American West and as imagery produced within a settler-colonial military campaign.

### Public use of “Muybridge”

The British Museum notes that prints associated with the Modoc work marked the point when he dropped the anonymous “Helios” and publicly used Muybridge in connection with his photography.

---

## 1874–1875: Larkyns killing, jail, trial, and family collapse

### Discovery of the affair

In October 1874, Muybridge obtained information from a maternity nurse or family acquaintance, often identified as **Mrs Smith**, about Flora’s relationship with **Harry Larkyns**.

Letters and the infant photograph marked “Little Harry” were presented in contemporary accounts as proof of intimacy and possible paternity.

### Killing of Harry Larkyns

**Documented fact:** On **17 October 1874**, Muybridge traveled to the vicinity of Calistoga, California, located Larkyns, called him to the door, and shot him at close range. Larkyns died.

Muybridge reportedly said words to the effect that he had a message from his wife and then fired. He surrendered without attempting escape.

The killing was deliberate and planned. Muybridge arranged affairs beforehand, traveled armed, sought Larkyns out, and afterward explained his motive. These circumstances were established through witness testimony as reported during the trial.

### Detention

Muybridge was confined in the **Napa County jail** pending proceedings. Flora filed for divorce while he was imprisoned.

### Trial: People of the State of California v. Edward J. Muybridge

The trial began in early February 1875 in California’s Seventh District Court. Judge **F. W. Wallace** presided.

Counsel included:

- prosecution: District Attorney **D. Spencer**, with Judge **Thomas P. Stoney**;
- defense: **W. W. Pendegast/Pendergast**, assisted by **Cameron H. King** and **E. S. Gottschalk**.

The defense admitted that Muybridge killed Larkyns. It presented insanity evidence connected with the 1860 brain injury. Witnesses described changed personality, odd behavior, and emotional instability.

Muybridge weakened that argument by describing calculated actions and asserting that he intended to kill Larkyns. The judge instructed the jury that adultery did not legally justify private execution.

### Verdict

On **6 February 1875**, the all-male jury acquitted Muybridge.

The jurors did not simply accept a medical insanity defense. Contemporary reporting indicates that they treated the homicide as justified according to what they called the “law of human nature,” despite the court’s statement that it was not legally justified.

Therefore:

- **Documented fact:** Muybridge intentionally killed Larkyns.
- **Documented fact:** He was charged and tried for murder.
- **Documented fact:** He was acquitted.
- **Not a documented fact:** The killing was lawful self-defense.
- **Historically accurate characterization:** The verdict was an instance of jury nullification or extralegal acceptance of retaliatory homicide.

Reports describe Muybridge collapsing into intense sobbing after the verdict.

### Divorce and deaths

Flora’s first divorce petition was dismissed on 27 March 1875. She died on 18 July. Muybridge’s mother Susannah had died in 1874, making this a period of clustered personal losses.

No evidence consulted indicates that Muybridge resumed parental domestic life with Florado.

---

## 1875–1877: Central America and return to California

Soon after acquittal, Muybridge sailed south on a **Pacific Mail Steamship Company** vessel. The journey has often been called “working exile,” although no legal sentence exiled him.

The Library of Congress documents stops in:

- Panama;
- Costa Rica;
- Guatemala;
- El Salvador;
- Honduras.

He photographed:

- ports and shipping;
- streets, churches, and civic architecture;
- coffee plantations;
- workers;
- landscapes;
- rail and commercial infrastructure.

He sometimes used the identity **“Eduardo Santiago Muybridge”**, a Hispanicized version of Edward James, in connection with Central American work.

The photographs supported tourism, investment, and corporate representation. Their depiction of plantation labor and “development” belongs to the visual economy of foreign capital in the region.

### San Francisco panoramas

After returning, Muybridge produced ambitious panoramas of San Francisco. A five-part 1876 panorama and a more extensive 1877 panorama documented a rapidly expanding city.

The Getty notes that some component prints do not align perfectly, demonstrating that these works were assembled views rather than a single optically continuous exposure.

## 1877–1882: Successful motion experiments and international recognition

### Palo Alto camera array

At Stanford’s Palo Alto stock farm, Muybridge constructed a bank of cameras placed along a measured track. In the best-known 1878 arrangement:

- cameras stood in a line;
- each held a sensitized plate;
- thin wires or electrical circuits triggered exposures in succession;
- a marked background allowed phases and distances to be compared;
- rapid shutters reduced blur.

### Sallie Gardner, June 1878

On **19 June 1878**—some popular accounts give 15 June—Muybridge photographed Stanford’s mare **Sallie Gardner** running at speed.

The sequence demonstrated that a galloping horse becomes completely airborne when its legs are gathered beneath the body, not when stretched forward and backward as artists often depicted it.

Witnesses and newspaper representatives were invited to inspect the negatives, in part to answer suspicions that the images had been manipulated.

### Authorship and technical collaboration

Muybridge’s public image emphasized solitary invention, but the Palo Alto project depended on:

- Stanford’s money, horses, track, and employees;
- engineers and mechanics;
- chemical suppliers;
- trainers and riders;
- printers and publishers;
- witnesses who authenticated the plates.

Later disputes concerned Stanford’s appropriation of the work and the precise contribution of engineers. The surviving record supports Muybridge as the photographic director and named patentee, not as the project’s only contributor.

### The Horse in Motion

In 1878 Muybridge issued sets of cabinet cards titled **The Horse in Motion**, bearing sequential “automatic electro-photographs.”

Stanford later sponsored **The Horse in Motion as Shown by Instantaneous Photography** (1882), written by physician **J. D. B. Stillman**. Muybridge supplied the photographic basis but was not the book’s principal author.

The 1882 volume used tracings and analysis derived from his images while giving Stanford’s project prominence. Muybridge believed the publication inadequately credited him. The dispute damaged his relationship with Stanford and complicated European reception.

### Zoopraxiscope

Around 1879 Muybridge developed the **zoopraxiscope**, a projection apparatus in which images arranged around a rotating glass disc were projected to create an illusion of movement.

Important qualification: most disc images were painted or traced from photographs; the device did not project the original sequential negatives as modern film. It belongs to the history of projected animation and motion pictures but was not a flexible-film cinema camera-projector.

### Patent 212,865

Muybridge applied on 27 June 1878 for an improvement in photographing objects in motion. United States Patent **No. 212,865** was granted on **4 March 1879**.

The specification covers methods and apparatus for instantaneous sequential photography.

### Lectures and Europe

Muybridge projected motion sequences in lectures in the United States and Europe. He demonstrated in Paris and London and addressed scientific, photographic, and artistic audiences.

### Étienne-Jules Marey

French physiologist **Étienne-Jules Marey** was already studying locomotion through graphical instruments. Muybridge’s sequences gave Marey a photographic means of testing movement and helped stimulate Marey’s development of chronophotography, including his photographic gun and single-plate sequential images.

Their relationship was one of exchange and influence, but also of differing methods:

- Muybridge commonly used multiple cameras to isolate successive phases.
- Marey sought to record phases from a single viewpoint, often on one plate, to analyze trajectories.

Marey entertained or met Muybridge in Paris in 1881, according to the established historical literature. Marey’s scientific prestige helped validate the motion photographs, while Marey moved beyond Muybridge’s method in analytical economy.

## 1883–1887: Patent and University of Pennsylvania project

### Patent 279,878

United States Patent **No. 279,878**, “Method of and Apparatus for Photographing Changing or Moving Objects,” was granted on **19 June 1883**. The application had originally been filed on 31 August 1881 and renewed on 19 April 1883.

It described systems for recording successive phases of moving bodies and identified the inventor as Edward J. Muybridge of San Francisco.

### Philadelphia network

Muybridge corresponded with:

- artist and anatomy teacher **Thomas Eakins**;
- engineer, horseman, and former Penn professor **Fairman Rogers**.

Rogers invited him to lecture at the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts and the Academy of Music in 1883. The lectures led to a meeting in Provost **William Pepper’s** office.

Philadelphia supporters provided:

- space on the University of Pennsylvania grounds;
- an initial **$5,000 advance**;
- institutional oversight;
- access to animals and human models;
- a subscription and publication network.

Publisher **J. B. Lippincott** was among the influential supporters.

### University commission

The oversight commission included:

- Harrison Allen;
- George F. Barker;
- Lewis M. Haupt;
- William D. Marks;
- Edward A. Coates;
- Thomas Eakins.

Other Penn-associated participants included physiologist **Edward Reichert** and veterinarian **Rush Shippen Huidekoper**.

### Studio and methods

In early 1884 Penn constructed an outdoor studio near **36th and Pine Streets**, with indoor space in Biological Hall.

Muybridge used three camera batteries to record subjects:

- laterally;
- from front or rear;
- at approximately 45 degrees.

Electromagnets released shutters at regular intervals. A ruled background allowed spatial comparison. Dry plates replaced earlier wet-collodion procedures and simplified repeated exposures.

### Models

Subjects came from:

- University of Pennsylvania faculty and students;
- Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts circles;
- Philadelphia Zoo;
- the neighboring almshouse;
- professional athletes and workers;
- people with disabilities.

Named Penn-associated models included George Brinton, Robert Edward Glendinning, Frank Bird Gummey, Thomas Love Latta, Morris Hacker Jr., and Rush Huidekoper.

Men frequently appeared nude; women were more often shown nude or partly draped while performing activities coded as domestic. The selection and presentation reveal Victorian assumptions about gender, athleticism, disability, race, labor, and scientific normality.

### Thomas Eakins dispute

Eakins valued photography as an anatomical teaching tool and experimented with motion images. Relations between Eakins and Muybridge were cooperative but competitive. Muybridge guarded control of his methods, negatives, and credit. Eakins developed his own approach to recording successive movement on a single plate.

Evidence supports collaboration and rivalry, not the reduction of either man to the other’s assistant.

### Animal Locomotion

The result was **Animal Locomotion: An Electro-Photographic Investigation of Consecutive Phases of Animal Movements** (1887), comprising **781 published collotype plates**. Penn’s archive holds a nearly complete but not absolutely complete set.

The plates were sold by subscription to libraries, universities, scientists, artists, and wealthy collectors. Production required substantial capital and was not a conventional mass-market publication.

Muybridge retained strong control over the imagery and later repackaged selections in more affordable books.

## 1888–1893: Edison, lecture circuits, and Chicago

### Meeting with Thomas Edison

On **25 February 1888**, Muybridge lectured in Orange or West Orange, New Jersey. On **27 February**, he consulted **Thomas Alva Edison** about combining the zoopraxiscope with Edison’s phonograph.

The meeting is documented by Muybridge’s later published account and histories of Edison’s motion-picture work.

Muybridge proposed coordinated projected images and recorded sound. Edison did not enter a partnership. Soon afterward Edison filed a caveat concerning a device that would do for the eye what the phonograph did for the ear. Edison’s laboratory, especially **William Kennedy Laurie Dickson**, subsequently developed the Kinetograph and Kinetoscope.

It is reasonable to say the meeting formed part of Edison’s motion-picture context. It is not established that Edison simply copied the Kinetoscope from Muybridge; their mechanisms and media differed materially.

### Lecture business

Muybridge’s lectures combined:

- scientific explanation;
- projected still images;
- zoopraxiscope animation;
- demonstrations for artists, physicians, athletes, and general audiences.

They were a source of income and a means of asserting priority.

### World’s Columbian Exposition, 1893

At Chicago’s World’s Columbian Exposition, Muybridge operated **Zoopraxographical Hall** on the Midway Plaisance. He delivered lectures under the auspices of the United States Bureau of Education and projected moving sequences.

The hall has been called the first purpose-built commercial motion-picture theater. That claim depends on definitions: it was a dedicated public building for projected motion displays, but the displays used rotating glass discs, not cinematographic film.

He published **Descriptive Zoopraxography** in connection with the exposition.

---

## 1894–1904: Return to England, final publications, and death

Muybridge returned to England in 1894 and continued lecturing. He revisited the United States in **1896–97** to settle financial and property matters connected with Pennsylvania and his negatives.

### Final books

He republished selected motion sequences for a broader audience:

- **Animals in Motion** (1899);
- **The Human Figure in Motion** (1901).

These books extended the commercial life of the Penn negatives and became durable reference works for artists, anatomists, animators, and designers.

### Later residence

He lived in Kingston upon Thames, eventually at the home of his cousin Catherine Smith at **2 Liverpool Road**.

A frequently repeated story says that he constructed or excavated a miniature representation of the American Great Lakes in the garden. The claim appears in biographical tradition, but its exact date, form, and surviving evidence are uncertain.

### Death and estate

Muybridge died of prostate cancer on **8 May 1904**, aged seventy-four. His body was cremated and the ashes interred at Woking.

He left Kingston a major collection including:

- the zoopraxiscope;
- most surviving glass discs;
- projection lanterns;
- slides;
- photograph albums;
- a San Francisco panorama;
- a scrapbook;
- money.

Kingston Museum opened in 1904 and became the principal institutional center for his material legacy.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Bookselling enterprises

Muybridge’s first American businesses involved imported books, publishing, and photographic merchandise in New York and San Francisco. Exact partnership structures changed and are incompletely reconstructed in the consulted institutional sources.

### Helios / Flying Studio

This was Muybridge’s mobile photographic business and brand, not merely a nickname. It enabled commercial landscape, architectural, stereographic, and commissioned work.

### Government clients

Documented or strongly reported clients included:

- United States Lighthouse Board;
- United States Army or military authorities during the Modoc War;
- survey and railroad interests;
- government-connected Alaskan expeditions.

### Railroad relationships

His work intersected directly with the Central Pacific network through Leland Stanford and with western rail surveys. The photographs promoted landscapes being transformed into transport corridors and real-estate opportunities.

### Pacific Mail Steamship Company

The company facilitated or commissioned aspects of his 1875 Central American journey. Its interest lay in ports, commercial routes, tourism, and investment.

### University of Pennsylvania project

The Penn project was financed institutionally and through wealthy Philadelphia subscribers. The initial advance was $5,000. Its infrastructure included university land, constructed studios, staff, animals, models, equipment, and publication arrangements.

### Patents

1. **US 212,865**, granted 4 March 1879: improvement in methods and apparatus for photographing objects in motion.
2. **US 279,878**, granted 19 June 1883: method and apparatus for photographing changing or moving objects.

Muybridge’s system used existing optical and electrical principles in a novel operational arrangement. Patent ownership documents legal claims; it does not show that every component originated with him.

### Zoopraxiscope

The zoopraxiscope combined antecedents from:

- magic lanterns;
- phenakistiscopes;
- zoetropes;
- rotating-disc projection;
- images derived from sequential photographs.

It was technologically important as a public projection system, although discontinuous with later flexible-film cinema in several crucial respects.

### Property and finances

Muybridge earned income through:

- retail bookselling;
- print and stereograph sales;
- private and government commissions;
- patents;
- lecture admissions and sponsorship;
- subscriptions to plate portfolios;
- later book publication.

The evidence consulted does not support a reliable lifetime net-worth estimate. Stanford and Penn supplied capital unavailable to most photographers, but patronage repeatedly created disputes over ownership, authorship, and priority.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Politics

No sustained party affiliation, elected office, political campaign, or ideological manifesto was found.

His work nevertheless participated in political processes:

- federal incorporation of Alaska;
- railroad expansion;
- military representation of the Modoc War;
- promotion of plantation and transport development in Central America;
- scientific classification of bodies;
- institutional construction of “normal” and “abnormal” movement.

These are functions and contexts of his photographs, not proof of a systematic political creed.

### Religion

No clear evidence of active religious affiliation, conversion, doctrinal writing, or organized religious role was found. His use of “Helios” was a classical-commercial identity rather than evidence of pagan religious belief.

### Intellectual development

Muybridge moved from picturesque landscape representation toward analytical decomposition of time. His central intellectual contribution was not a single theory but a method: actions too fast or continuous for unaided perception could be divided into measurable photographic intervals.

### Artistic position

He claimed scientific truth for the motion studies while making numerous artistic decisions:

- choosing models and actions;
- staging backgrounds;
- selecting frames;
- retouching and printing;
- translating photographs into painted disc silhouettes;
- assembling panoramas;
- circulating particular sequences.

Consequently, “objective record” and “constructed image” coexist in his work.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Leland Stanford — patron, employer, later adversarial claimant

Stanford financed the horse studies and supplied facilities, animals, and social access. Muybridge delivered the images that made both men internationally famous. Their relationship deteriorated over credit, particularly after Stillman’s 1882 book foregrounded Stanford’s role.

### Flora Shallcross Stone Muybridge — wife

Their marriage linked Muybridge’s public career to the Larkyns case. The record is filtered through adversarial court proceedings and newspaper sensationalism. Flora’s own surviving perspective is comparatively limited.

### Harry Larkyns — acquaintance, rival, victim

Larkyns was close to Flora and was probably her lover. Muybridge shot and killed him. The allegation that Larkyns fathered Florado remains unresolved.

### Silas T. Selleck — early photographic associate

Selleck connected Muybridge’s bookselling and photographic worlds and may have been an informal mentor.

### Thomas Eakins — collaborator and rival

Eakins helped bring Muybridge to Philadelphia and served on the Penn commission. Both used photography to study anatomy and movement, but disputes over technique and credit strained the relationship.

### Fairman Rogers — intermediary and institutional sponsor

Rogers’s interests in horses, engineering, art, and Penn made him a crucial bridge between Muybridge and Philadelphia’s patrons.

### William Pepper — institutional patron

As Penn provost, Pepper helped organize financing, facilities, and oversight for *Animal Locomotion*.

### Étienne-Jules Marey — scientific colleague and methodological rival

Muybridge’s sequences influenced Marey, while Marey’s physiological research helped place motion photography within experimental science. Their methods diverged.

### Thomas Edison — inventor consulted about image and sound

Muybridge met Edison in 1888 and proposed joining the zoopraxiscope and phonograph. No partnership resulted.

### Carleton Watkins — commercial and artistic rival

Watkins’s Yosemite photographs preceded and influenced Muybridge’s western landscape practice. Muybridge competed through scale, viewpoint, and publicity.

### Albert Bierstadt and Clarence King

Muybridge traveled with or worked in proximity to Bierstadt and King in 1872. Their combined artistic and geological interests helped construct Yosemite as both sublime landscape and scientifically surveyed territory.

## Controversies, Crime, Litigation, and Disputed Questions

### Killing of Harry Larkyns

Classification:

- Killing: **documented fact**.
- Premeditation: **documented by Muybridge’s conduct and reported statements**.
- Murder charge: **documented fact**.
- Insanity defense: **documented fact**.
- Acquittal: **documented fact**.
- Legal justification: **not established**; the judge rejected adultery as legal justification.
- Jury nullification: **strong historical interpretation supported by the jurors’ reported explanation**.

### Brain injury as explanation

The accident and changed behavior are documented through testimony. The claim that neurological damage caused the homicide or his photographic creativity is a retrospective theory, not a proven medical conclusion.

### Florado’s paternity

Larkyns’s paternity is probable in many biographies but remains unproven. It must not be converted from allegation to fact merely through repetition.

### Stanford credit dispute

Muybridge believed Stanford and Stillman appropriated or minimized his contribution in *The Horse in Motion*. Stanford had financed the research, but Muybridge created the photographic method and images. The controversy concerns allocation of credit more than simple theft.

### Image manipulation

Muybridge’s landscapes and motion work were not free from manipulation:

- clouds could be printed from separate negatives;
- images were retouched;
- panoramas were assembled;
- sequences could be selected or reordered;
- zoopraxiscope discs used traced or painted forms;
- war scenes were staged.

This does not make the entire motion record fraudulent. It does limit claims that every published image is an untouched, mechanically neutral transcription.

### Colonial and institutional subjects

His photographs of Indigenous people, Central American workers, disabled people, women, and almshouse residents were produced within unequal power relations. The surviving record does not always establish whether subjects consented freely or were compensated.

### No evidence found

No reliable evidence was found of:

- corruption or money laundering;
- espionage or intelligence service;
- organized political violence beyond the Larkyns homicide;
- fraud conviction;
- substance addiction;
- psychiatric hospitalization;
- imprisonment other than pretrial detention.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “Stanford’s $25,000 bet”

**Story:** Stanford wagered $25,000 that all four hooves of a galloping horse left the ground.

**Appearance and circulation:** Repeated in popular histories, television treatments, museum publicity, and internet summaries.

**Evidence:** Stanford unquestionably commissioned the experiments, but no strong primary record for a $25,000 wager was identified. The amount is extraordinarily large for the period.

**Assessment:** **Rumor or myth built around a documented commission.**

**Effect:** It converts a prolonged program of equine research into a dramatic one-question wager.

### “Muybridge invented the motion picture”

**Story:** The 1878 horse sequence was the first film and Muybridge single-handedly invented cinema.

**Evidence for:** He made influential sequential photographs and publicly projected animated images through the zoopraxiscope before commercial film projection.

**Evidence against:** His sequences were separate still plates; the zoopraxiscope generally projected painted glass-disc images; optical-motion devices predated him; flexible film, intermittent transport, camera mechanisms, and projection systems were developed by others.

**Assessment:** **Honorific simplification, not a literal technical fact.**

### “The first movie theater”

**Story:** Zoopraxographical Hall in Chicago was the first movie theater.

**Evidence for:** It was a dedicated venue presenting projected moving-image entertainment to a paying exposition audience.

**Qualification:** It did not show flexible-film cinema in the later Lumière/Edison sense.

**Assessment:** **Defensible only under a broad definition of projected motion pictures.**

### “The horse experiment settled whether horses ever leave the ground”

The photographs documented an aerial phase. However, horsemen and theorists had debated or observed aspects of the gait earlier. Muybridge’s decisive contribution was durable photographic demonstration of the leg position during suspension.

### “His hair turned gray in three days”

This is associated with the 1860 accident. Sudden apparent whitening is a familiar trauma legend and lacks strong contemporary medical verification here.

**Assessment:** **Rumor or biographical embellishment.**

### “Brain damage made him a genius or murderer”

The accident may have changed his personality. Direct claims that it caused either creativity or homicide exceed the evidence.

**Assessment:** **Retrospective neurological theory.**

### “Working exile”

Muybridge’s Central American trip followed his acquittal and removed him from scandal. No court banished him.

**Assessment:** **Interpretive metaphor, not legal exile.**

### “Little Harry proves paternity”

The inscription and affair are significant evidence, but they do not conclusively prove biological paternity.

**Assessment:** **Strong circumstantial evidence, unresolved claim.**

### Great Lakes garden model

Later accounts say Muybridge was excavating or building a scale model of the Great Lakes at his cousin’s home.

**Assessment:** **Reported anecdote; exact physical and documentary evidence not established in the sources consulted.**

### Misspelled grave

The “Maybridge” inscription is real or consistently institutionally reported. It has become a posthumous irony attached to a man who repeatedly changed the spelling of his own name.

## Works by Eadweard Muybridge

The boundary between “works” and commercial photographic products is porous. Muybridge produced thousands of negatives, prints, stereographs, albums, portfolios, projection discs, lectures, and derived publications.

### Major photographic series and portfolios

- **Yosemite Valley photographs** (beginning 1867): stereographs and larger-format landscape views, frequently branded Helios.
- **Alaska photographs** (1868): Sitka, forts, Indigenous subjects, settlements, and landscapes.
- **Railroad and western survey photographs** (c. 1869).
- **Pacific Coast lighthouse series** (1871).
- **Yosemite mammoth-plate series** (1872), approximately fifty-one major views.
- **Modoc War photographs** (1873), including staged and documentary images.
- **Central America and Isthmus of Panama series** (1875–76).
- **Panoramas of San Francisco** (1876–78), including the five-part 1876 and extensive 1877 panoramas.
- **The Horse in Motion** (1878), cabinet-card sequences of Stanford’s horses.
- **Attitudes of Animals in Motion** (1881), an album or portfolio associated with the Stanford experiments.
- **Animal Locomotion: An Electro-Photographic Investigation of Consecutive Phases of Animal Movements** (Philadelphia, 1887), 781 collotype plates.
- **Zoopraxiscope discs** (c. 1879–93), painted or traced motion cycles derived from photographs.

### Books and authored publications

1. **The Pacific Coast of Central America and Mexico; The Isthmus of Panama; Guatemala; and the Cultivation and Shipment of Coffee** (c. 1876). Photographic and promotional work; titles and issue forms vary.
2. **Attitudes of Animals in Motion** (1881). Muybridge’s photographic publication; surviving copies vary.
3. **Animal Locomotion: The Muybridge Work at the University of Pennsylvania: The Method and the Result** (1888). Institutional and explanatory publication associated with the project.
4. **Descriptive Zoopraxography; or, The Science of Animal Locomotion Made Popular** (Philadelphia, 1893). Genuine authored exposition prepared in connection with his Chicago lectures.
5. **Animals in Motion: An Electro-Photographic Investigation of Consecutive Phases of Animal Progressive Movements** (London, 1899). Genuine selection and synthesis drawn substantially from earlier photographs.
6. **The Human Figure in Motion: An Electro-Photographic Investigation of Consecutive Phases of Muscular Actions** (London, 1901). Genuine Muybridge publication based on earlier Penn work.

### Patents

- **US212865A**, *Improvement in the Method and Apparatus for Photographing Objects in Motion*, granted 4 March 1879.
- **US279878A**, *Method of and Apparatus for Photographing Changing or Moving Objects*, granted 19 June 1883.

### Lectures and demonstrations

Muybridge delivered numerous lectures under changing titles, commonly concerning:

- animal locomotion;
- human locomotion;
- instantaneous photography;
- zoopraxography;
- the relationship of art, anatomy, and photography.

Many survive only through programs, summaries, advertisements, or his later synthesis in *Descriptive Zoopraxography*.

### Works not authored solely by Muybridge

- **J. D. B. Stillman, *The Horse in Motion as Shown by Instantaneous Photography* (1882):** written by Stillman under Stanford’s sponsorship, using Muybridge-derived photographs and tracings. It is not a Muybridge-authored book.
- **Muybridge’s Complete Human and Animal Locomotion** (1979 and later editions): posthumous compilation of the 781 plates, commonly edited or introduced by later scholars.
- Modern Dover selections and reprints: posthumous editorial packages, not newly authored works.

### Correspondence and archives

Substantial correspondence and related manuscripts survive in:

- Kingston Museum and Kingston Borough Archive;
- University of Pennsylvania Archives;
- Penn’s Kislak Center;
- William Pepper papers;
- Smithsonian Archives of American Art;
- Stanford-related collections;
- patent records and contemporary newspapers.

No complete scholarly edition of all Muybridge correspondence was identified.

### Hostile, critical, or revisionist strands

No single standard “hostile biography” dominates the literature. Critical work instead concentrates on:

- the premeditated Larkyns homicide;
- staged war photography;
- manipulation and retouching;
- Stanford’s and Muybridge’s competing priority claims;
- gendered and racialized presentation of bodies;
- institutional use of disabled and impoverished models;
- colonial visual culture in Alaska, the Modoc territory, and Central America.

The exhibition catalogue *Helios* and the documentary *Exposing Muybridge* are particularly important for revising the image of Muybridge as a purely objective recorder.

## Death and Posthumous Reputation

Muybridge died just as flexible-film cinema was becoming a mass medium. By then Edison’s and the Lumières’ systems had displaced rotating-disc projection.

His reputation subsequently moved through several phases:

1. **Scientific and artistic reference source:** Artists, anatomists, and physical educators continued using the plates.
2. **Cinema-origin figure:** Film historians presented him as a direct father of motion pictures.
3. **Modernist precursor:** Seriality, fragmentation, repetition, and mechanical vision attracted twentieth-century artists and theorists.
4. **Biographical sensationalism:** The Larkyns homicide became inseparable from popular accounts.
5. **Revisionist photographer of empire and institutions:** Recent scholarship situates Alaska, railroads, the Modoc War, Central America, gender, disability, and labor alongside the motion studies.
6. **Digital-era icon:** His sequences became ubiquitous online, often converted into GIFs and animations without contextual captions.
7. **Biotechnological afterlife:** In 2017 researchers encoded frames of the mare Annie G. into bacterial DNA using CRISPR, making his sequence a demonstration object in biological data storage.

Influence appears in:

- cinema and animation;
- scientific cinematography;
- biomechanics and sports analysis;
- medical gait studies;
- dance notation and movement study;
- painting and sculpture;
- photographic serialism;
- visual-effects techniques resembling multi-camera “bullet time.”

Later artists and theorists frequently influenced by or engaged with Muybridge include Francis Bacon, Marcel Duchamp, Sol LeWitt, Hollis Frampton, and contemporary video and installation artists. These are histories of reception; they do not necessarily imply personal contact.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted record are included. Mere contemporaneity or shared geography has been excluded.

### Thomas Alva Edison — documented fact

- **Nature:** Meeting and technological discussion.
- **Date and place:** Muybridge lectured in Orange/West Orange, New Jersey, on 25 February 1888 and consulted Edison at his laboratory on 27 February.
- **Substance:** Muybridge proposed coupling the zoopraxiscope with Edison’s phonograph to unite projected movement and recorded sound. Edison did not form a partnership; his laboratory subsequently pursued the Kinetograph and Kinetoscope.
- **Qualification:** Influence is plausible and historically significant, but the encounter does not establish that Edison copied Muybridge’s apparatus.

### Étienne-Jules Marey — documented fact

- **Nature:** Scientific exchange, acknowledged influence, and methodological rivalry.
- **Dates and places:** Their work intersected particularly from 1881 onward; Muybridge demonstrated motion images in Paris and met Marey there.
- **Substance:** Marey used Muybridge’s results as evidence relevant to animal gait, while Muybridge benefited from Marey’s scientific recognition. Marey then developed single-camera chronophotographic methods distinct from Muybridge’s multi-camera arrays.

### William James — reported fact / institutional-intellectual connection

- **Nature:** Reception of motion studies within American psychology and the analysis of perception.
- **Evidence:** Muybridge’s images circulated among late nineteenth-century scientists concerned with perception and movement, including intellectual environments in which James worked.
- **Qualification:** The consulted sources did not establish a personal meeting or correspondence between James and Muybridge. It is therefore not entered as a documented personal connection.

### Charles Robert Darwin — documented influence, no personal meeting established

- **Nature:** Muybridge’s animal-motion research operated within scientific debates shaped by Darwinian evolutionary thought, and later editions discussed comparative animal movement.
- **Qualification:** No documentary evidence consulted establishes that Darwin and Muybridge met or corresponded. Darwin died in 1882, before the Penn plates. This is an intellectual-context connection, not a personal relationship.

### Isadora Duncan, Loïe Fuller, Martha Graham, Rudolf Laban, and other movement figures on the roster — no documented personal crossing found

Muybridge’s sequences later influenced the visual and analytical culture of dance, but the sources consulted did not establish a direct meeting, correspondence, or explicitly acknowledged individual influence involving these roster members.

### Francis Bacon — documented posthumous influence

- **Nature:** Artistic use of Muybridge’s photographic sequences as source material.
- **Dates and place:** Principally Bacon’s postwar work in Britain.
- **Substance:** Bacon repeatedly used reproductions of Muybridge’s human and animal figures when constructing painted bodies.
- **Qualification:** Posthumous influence; the men never met.

### Thomas Eakins, Leland Stanford, Fairman Rogers, William Pepper, Carleton Watkins, Silas Selleck, Albert Bierstadt, Clarence King, and J. D. B. Stillman

These are major documented connections but none appears in the supplied atlas roster and therefore they are not atlas cross-links.

### Result of roster audit

Among the 896 listed lives, the clearest supported intersections are **Thomas Alva Edison** and **Étienne-Jules Marey**, with **Francis Bacon** as a substantial posthumous influence. No other personal connection should be inferred merely from shared institutions, cities, periods, photographic interests, or later use of motion analysis.
