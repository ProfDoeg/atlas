# Carlos María della Paolera: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Carlos María della Paolera Buschiazzo (1890–1960)  
**Principal occupations:** civil engineer, urbanist, teacher, municipal planner  
**Places chiefly associated with him:** Buenos Aires, Paris, Rosario, Junín, Mar del Plata, Mendoza, La Plata  
**Prepared:** 20 August 2026  

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Limits

This dossier reconstructs Della Paolera's life from contemporary publications, university and municipal histories, surviving bibliographies, archival catalogues, plans, specialist scholarship, and later family testimony. The record is uneven. His professional writings and public work are substantially documented; his private life, finances, health, and day-to-day activities are not. The surviving personal archive was still described as incompletely classified when Alicia Novick and Margarita Piccioni conducted their foundational study. Some family details below therefore come from user-contributed genealogies and are identified as provisional.

Several familiar claims require correction or qualification. Most importantly, Della Paolera was **not an identified author of the 1925 municipal plan for Buenos Aires**. That document was prepared while he was studying in France by a commission associated with Mayor Carlos M. Noel and chaired by Martín Noel. Della Paolera's own major Buenos Aires study was his 1927 thesis, defended in Paris in 1928. He later criticized the 1925 work, directed Buenos Aires's municipal planning office, and gave the Avenida 9 de Julio its influential parkway form. These distinct episodes are often telescoped into a single authorship legend.

Dates and titles are normalized where the documentary record permits. Typographical errors in later bibliographies—such as “1741” for 1941—are silently corrected only when the intended date is unambiguous; otherwise the conflict is stated.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and architectural family background

Della Paolera was born into an Italian-Argentine building family. His father was **Cayetano della Paolera Carusso** (born about 1850), described by Della Paolera's son Claude as a constructor or builder. His mother was **Ángela Buschiazzo** (born about 1859–60). Through her he was connected to **Juan Antonio Buschiazzo** (1845–1917), the Italian-born architect who became one of late nineteenth-century Buenos Aires's most important municipal architects. Juan Antonio's work on public buildings, parks, boulevards, sanitation, and the modernization of Buenos Aires supplied an immediate family precedent for Carlos's later profession.

Later architectural scholarship also identifies Carlos as a close relative—usually a first cousin—of architect and historian **Mario J. Buschiazzo**. The exact relationship is sometimes compressed in secondary accounts, which alternate between “uncle” and “cousin” when discussing the Buschiazzo connection. The secure proposition is that Juan Antonio Buschiazzo belonged to his mother's immediate family and that Carlos grew up within a kin network tied to construction and architecture.

One public, user-edited genealogy gives his paternal grandparents as **José Della Paolera** and **Teresa Carusso**, and his maternal grandparents as **Carlos Buschiazzo** and **Margarita Bresciani**. It also lists a younger brother, **Félix Ismael Della Paolera Buschiazzo** (13 March 1898–30 April 1972). These data have not been checked against civil or parish certificates and should be treated as provisional rather than archival findings.

### Marriage and children

He married **Marie Thérèse Rouault**, apparently a Frenchwoman met during or connected with his years in France. No marriage certificate, date, or detailed biography of her was located.

Private genealogical compilations attribute two sons to the couple:

- **Carlos María José della Paolera Rouault**, reportedly born in Paris on 25 March 1926 and died in Buenos Aires on 4 September 2009.
- **Claude Francisco C. M. della Paolera Rouault**, reportedly born on 4 July 1931 and died in Buenos Aires on 2 September 2013. Claude became an architect and participated in commemorations of his father's urbanist work.

These dates come from user-contributed genealogy rather than official civil records. Claude's existence, profession, and public role are independently corroborated by press interviews. In one interview he recalled that his grandfather Cayetano was a builder and his great-uncle Juan Buschiazzo an architect, and he continued promoting World Town Planning Day, the observance founded by his father.

A grandson, **Gerardo della Paolera**, an economic historian and academic, helped initiate the 2021 CEDODAL book on Carlos. Gerardo stated that he had never known his grandfather and had grown up with a family oral tradition about him; he promised his own father that he would help assemble a documentary book. This testimony is valuable for the family's custodial role but is not independent proof of the stories it reports.

### Property and family finances

No reliable inventory of Della Paolera's estate, real property, investments, salary, inheritance, or household wealth was found. His background was professionally established rather than proletarian: the family had construction and architectural connections, and he was able to undertake prolonged postgraduate study in Paris. The source base does not establish whether that study was privately financed, supported by employment, or subsidized by an institution.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1890–1912

Carlos María della Paolera was born in Buenos Aires during the rapid demographic and physical expansion that followed federalization of the capital. He grew up as the city absorbed mass immigration, extended sanitation and tram networks, opened avenues, and recast its public architecture. His maternal architectural connections made this transformation a family as well as a civic environment.

He attended the **Colegio Nacional de Buenos Aires**, the elite secondary school attached to the University of Buenos Aires. No school register or evidence about his specific teachers and classmates was located. He then entered the **Facultad de Ciencias Exactas, Físicas y Naturales of the Universidad de Buenos Aires (UBA)** to study civil engineering.

He graduated as a civil engineer in **1912**. His thesis, dated 19 November 1912, was titled **“Proyecto de provisión de agua para una ciudad de 100.000 habitantes”** (“Water-supply project for a city of 100,000 inhabitants”). The subject belongs to the sanitary-engineering origins from which urban planning emerged in Argentina: water, drainage, circulation, statistics, and municipal public works. It also shows that his initial professional identity was technical and infrastructural rather than architectural.

## Education and Formation

### Early professional formation in Argentina, 1912–1921

The period immediately after graduation is poorly documented. His earliest known publications date from about **1916**, although a complete run and secure titles have not been recovered. By **1920** he had entered the professional debate about cities. The **Centro Argentino de Ingenieros** delegated him to the Congreso de la Habitación organized by the Museo Social Argentino. A bibliographical notice also credits him with a 1920 study of the Paris plan.

This early work placed him in networks joining engineers, housing reformers, hygienists, municipal officials, and social scientists. In Argentina, “urbanismo” was not yet an autonomous profession: city questions were divided among public works, sanitation, architecture, surveying, transport, landscape design, and social reform. His subsequent strategy was to define urbanism as a distinct, scientific, coordinating discipline.

### Paris and the Institut d'Urbanisme, approximately 1921–1928

Sources disagree on the exact beginning of his residence in France. Some institutional biographies say that he went to Paris in **1921** and remained through 1928; others place the decisive journey in **1924**. The difference may reflect an initial affiliation or preliminary stay followed by formal enrollment. He was associated in 1921 with the **Association Amicale des élèves et anciens élèves de l'École des Hautes Études Urbaines**, the institution that became the **Institut d'Urbanisme de l'Université de Paris (IUUP)**.

In July **1924** he attended the international city-planning congress at Amsterdam as a representative of the Centro Argentino de Ingenieros. This gave him access to the transnational municipal and planning network then linking Paris, Brussels, Amsterdam, London, and the garden-city movement.

At the Institut he studied with, or absorbed the work of:

- **Marcel Poëte**, urban historian, librarian, and advocate of studying the city as a living organism; Poëte supervised Della Paolera's thesis and presided over his jury.
- **Pierre Lavedan**, historian of urban form.
- **Léon Jaussely**, architect and planner.
- **Donat-Alfred Agache**, whose planning practice joined surveys, social diagnosis, and formal design.
- **Augustin Rey**, associated with hygienist architecture and sunlight.
- **Edmond Joyant**, municipal and technical planning specialist.

His wider intellectual sources included **Patrick Geddes** and the civic survey; **Ebenezer Howard** and the garden city; **Camillo Sitte** and **Raymond Unwin** on urban composition; the human geography of **Paul Vidal de la Blache**; **Henri Pirenne's** economic history of cities; and historical approaches associated with **Fustel de Coulanges**, **Jules Michelet**, and **Henri Bergson's** concept of duration. These influences do not form a single doctrine. Della Paolera combined historical continuity, organic metaphors, statistics, hygiene, regional geography, and monumental civic composition.

While in France, he wrote reports for the Buenos Aires newspaper **La Razón**. Articles from 1925–27 discussed urban “lungs,” monumental ensembles, the grid, city evolution, and a regulatory plan for the Buenos Aires agglomeration. Journalism served both as public education and as a way of importing the new professional vocabulary to Argentina.

### Paris thesis and diploma, 1927–1928

His thesis was **“Contribution à l'étude d'un plan d'aménagement, d'embellissement et d'extension de Buenos Aires. Étude sur l'évolution de la ville”**. Bibliographies date its submission to November **1927**. A study of IUUP diploma records gives the formal defense as **31 March 1928**. The apparent discrepancy between “1927 graduate” and “1928 graduate” in later accounts can therefore be explained by submission versus defense/diploma dates.

The thesis reconstructed Buenos Aires historically before prescribing intervention. This was a defining methodological choice: a plan should grow from the city's formation, geography, economy, and population rather than impose an abstract figure. In the available IUUP student sequence, Della Paolera appears to have been the **first Latin American to complete the urbanism diploma**. The often-repeated formula “first Latin American urbanist” is defensible only in this specific institutional sense; Latin Americans had planned cities long before the diploma existed.

In 1928 he became a founding member of the **Société des Urbanistes de Paris** according to Argentine biographical accounts, and represented South America in the **Union Internationale des Villes** in Brussels. Exact membership records should be checked in the relevant French and Belgian archives before treating every title as continuous rather than event-specific.

## Early Career and Return to Argentina, 1928–1931

Della Paolera returned to Argentina in **1928 or early 1929**. His first visible base was Rosario, where architect **Ángel Guido** and the local professional association invited him to lecture. His 1928 pamphlet **“Dos conferencias sobre urbanismo. Rosario y sus problemas urbanos”** publicized the historical-diagnostic method. He also delivered a series of ten UBA lectures, **“La evolución de Buenos Aires y bosquejo de un Plan Regulador,”** from May to September 1928.

On **31 December 1929**, the Universidad Nacional del Litoral created a chair or course in urbanism in its Rosario architecture program and appointed him. Specialist histories describe this as Argentina's first university chair devoted to urbanism. A separate date, **25 May 1933**, belongs to the establishment of his urbanism teaching at UBA. Popular accounts sometimes call the 1933 appointment “the first chair,” omitting the Rosario precedent.

### Rosario regulatory and extension plan

In 1929 Rosario commissioned a **Plan Regulador y de Extensión**. The team comprised Della Paolera, civil engineer **Adolfo P. Farengo**, and architect **Ángel Guido**. Their division of expertise represented Della Paolera's ideal of coordinated urbanism: regional diagnosis and planning, engineering, and civic architecture.

The commission was not politically uncontested. The three professionals and their supporters promoted a direct appointment; the **Centro Argentino de Ingenieros** backed the arrangement. Opponents in Rosario's professional and civic spheres objected that no competition had been held. The episode is documented as a procurement and professional-jurisdiction controversy, not as bribery or a criminal offense. The plan was completed and published in **1935**. It proposed railway reorganization, a port and circulation structure, parks and open spaces, zoning, and extension controls. It won first prize at the First Argentine Congress of Urbanism in 1935, but implementation was partial and fragmented.

### Werner Hegemann and international networks

German planner and critic **Werner Hegemann** visited Argentina in **1931**. Della Paolera chaired or played a leading part in the informative committee organized by the **Asociación Amigos de la Ciudad** and accompanied Hegemann through Buenos Aires, Rosario, and Mar del Plata. He supplied the prologue to the Spanish publication **“Problemas urbanos de Rosario.”** Hegemann's mixture of civic art, housing reform, and practical criticism reinforced Della Paolera's opposition to isolated monumental gestures unsupported by urban diagnosis.

In Mar del Plata, Della Paolera lectured in 1931–32 on the resort's evolution, garden-city principles, and open space. He envisaged a coastal city whose landscape, leisure economy, and seasonal expansion required planning. No evidence was found that the municipality adopted a complete plan by him.

## Chronological Life History

### 1932–1933: Creation and weakening of the Buenos Aires planning office

On **5 February 1932**, by municipal ordinance (commonly cited as no. 4,576), Buenos Aires approved the **Oficina Técnica del Plan de Urbanización**, also called the **Dirección Técnica del Plan de Urbanización**, with Della Paolera as director. It was the first sustained municipal office in Buenos Aires organized explicitly around a permanent planning process.

The office inherited a difficult institutional field. The city had a public-works bureaucracy, national ministries controlled major infrastructure, railways were largely private and foreign-owned, and the 1925 commission had already produced a monumental plan. Della Paolera sought autonomy for a small multidisciplinary unit that would collect data, coordinate agencies, and transform planning from a one-time drawing into a continuing municipal function.

Its first program was an **“expediente urbano”**: a civic case file or clinical history. Staff studied the city's evolution, geology and geography, population, land uses, transport, activity distribution, housing, public services, and open space. Della Paolera's recurrent metaphor treated the city as a living organism and the urbanist as a physician who must diagnose before prescribing.

In **April 1933**, the office's budget was reportedly reduced from approximately **350,000 pesos to 40,000 pesos**, and it lost much of its initial autonomy. The cut coincided with political changes and conflict among municipal and national technical bodies. Scholarship offers plausible explanations—loss of political patrons, professional rivalry, and disputes over jurisdiction—but no single surviving document proves one cause. The office nevertheless mounted an urbanism exhibition in 1933 and continued under Della Paolera.

On **25 May 1933**, he began or formally established urbanism teaching in UBA's School of Architecture. His two university platforms—Rosario and Buenos Aires—trained architects and engineers to treat urbanism as a specialized discipline.

### 1933–1937: Avenida 9 de Julio and the center of Buenos Aires

The north–south avenue later called **Avenida 9 de Julio** had a long prehistory. Mayor Francisco Seeber and architect Juan Antonio Buschiazzo discussed a central avenue in the 1880s–90s; National Law 8,855 of **1912** authorized expropriations for a 33-meter artery; and the 1925 municipal plan included it. Della Paolera was therefore neither the first inventor of the alignment nor the author of the expropriation law.

His decisive contribution was to reject the narrow street model and reconceive the corridor as a monumental **avenida-parque**. His office developed variants roughly 100–140 meters wide by clearing nearly entire blocks between Cerrito–Lima and Carlos Pellegrini–Bernardo de Irigoyen. A 1933 version proposed a high-speed underground road; a 1934 version linked the axis to a government center; a 1937 surface version accommodated conflicts with subway construction and national public works. The new form joined movement, sunlight, vegetation, civic display, and a visual axis.

The project provoked technical, architectural, and financial criticism. **Fermín Bereterbide, Wladimiro Acosta, and Ernesto Vautier**, among others, questioned the treatment of traffic, architecture, pedestrian space, and grade separation. Critics argued that full-block clearance destroyed saleable central land and turned expensive property into landscape without a fully engineered metropolitan transport solution. The project also required extensive expropriation and demolition, including the Church of San Nicolás de Bari and theatres and buildings in the corridor. These were legal public-works actions, but their social and heritage costs belong to the record.

Della Paolera served on a commission concerned with locating national public buildings, then withdrew amid jurisdictional conflict. His office and the national Ministry of Public Works disputed control of the avenue and related civic projects. Sources describe a prolonged administrative and professional contest, not a documented personal lawsuit.

The first five blocks opened on **12 October 1937**. Later expansion turned the avenue into the emblematic “widest avenue” associated with his name. The historically accurate formulation is that he was the principal ideologue and municipal planner of the wide parkway version, not the avenue's sole creator.

### 1934–1935: Symbol of Urbanism and professional organization

In **1934**, Della Paolera designed and promoted the **Symbol of Urbanism**: a golden sun, green ground or vegetation, and a deep blue field representing the essentials of urban life—sun, greenery/earth, and air. His manifesto included the phrase: **“La ciudad, como el árbol, no puede desligarse de la tierra que la sustenta”** (“The city, like the tree, cannot be separated from the earth that sustains it”).

An international congress at Besançon in **1935** adopted or recognized the symbol. That year Argentina held its First Congress of Urbanism; the Rosario plan received first prize. These events consolidated Della Paolera as the discipline's chief organizer in Argentina.

Later commemorations often say he “created World Urbanism Day in 1935.” This collapses two episodes. The symbol dates to 1934 and its international adoption to 1935; **World Town Planning Day was initiated in 1949**, with 8 November as the annual date. International planning organizations still credit Della Paolera with founding it.

### 1936–1939: Metropolitan scale, exhibitions, and provincial projects

Della Paolera increasingly used **“Gran Buenos Aires”** for the metropolitan organism extending beyond the federal capital. Population growth, rail corridors, industrial decentralization, bridges, airports, parks, and water systems ignored municipal borders; planning therefore needed regional coordination. He studied links at the **Puente Uriburu/Alsina** and **Puente Avellaneda**, proposed metropolitan open-space systems, and called for national–municipal coordination.

His office's projects included zoning, building-height controls, public-building locations, pedestrian and automobile circulation, underground parking, the use of the subsurface, housing studies, the Bajo Flores/Bañado de Flores area, and proposals for Plaza de Mayo. A projected new cathedral on the former penitentiary lands and a government/civic center remained proposals. The office's second major exhibition in **1939** displayed this transition from survey to concrete projects.

In **Junín**, Della Paolera and **Rodolfo Henry** prepared the 1939 Avenida San Martín project on railway lands. The proposal converted a divisive rail corridor into a landscaped avenue with ramblas and stitched the urban grid together. It is one of the clearest provincial applications of his principle that infrastructure should become civic landscape.

He was involved in the foundation of the **Instituto Argentino de Urbanismo** in **1939**, an independent professional and educational body. With **Miguel Roca**, he later published an institutional account, *Buenos Aires. Una obra de progreso al servicio de la nación* (1942).

### 1930s political setting

Della Paolera's municipal career unfolded after the military coup of 6 September 1930, during the conservative order later called the **Década Infame**, marked by electoral fraud, political repression, and close relations between government and economic elites. He accepted technical office within that state and worked under appointed municipal authorities. This institutional fact should not be converted into evidence of a personal role in the coup, fraud, repression, or a political party: none was found. Conversely, describing him only as a neutral expert would obscure that large planning projects depended on an undemocratic governmental structure capable of rapid expropriation and centralized intervention.

### 1940–1946: Diminished municipal authority and broader commissions

Novick and Piccioni conclude that Della Paolera's public career lost force during the 1940s. Former allies in the Asociación Amigos de la Ciudad criticized the planning office. Martín Noel's 1939 proposal for national planning legislation gave little recognition to Della Paolera's work. Professional competition, institutional fragmentation, political turnover, and the gap between ambitious regional planning and municipal capacity all contributed; the record does not sustain a single personal or political explanation.

The strongest academic chronology has him directing the Buenos Aires planning office until **1943**. Some popular biographies give **1939** as the end of his leadership, probably using the second exhibition or an administrative reorganization as the cutoff. Without a personnel file, 1932–43 is the better-supported tenure, with marked decline in autonomy after 1933.

His work in this period included:

- building-height and zoning studies;
- the municipal cadastral survey and building code, associated in later accounts with 1942–44 reforms;
- a 1941 planning proposal or competition entry concerning Mendoza, followed by the 1943 **“Reorganización del sistema ferroviario de Mendoza”** with Adolfo Farengo;
- a **Gran Centro Cívico de La Plata** proposal in 1944;
- a 1945 lecture or study on Rosario's present and future green space;
- the **Gran Parque del Sur / Bañado de Flores** proposal in 1946;
- reported projects for a summer settlement at San Javier, Tucumán (1942), and La Quebradita (1943), known chiefly from the 2021 retrospective book and requiring archival confirmation.

No evidence indicates that these commissions created a private planning company. They appear as municipal, academic, professional, or competition work undertaken personally or with named collaborators.

### 1946–1956: University institute and international observance

An advanced urbanism course preceded the formal **Instituto Superior de Urbanismo (ISU)** at UBA. Secondary accounts variously date the precursor to 1943 or 1946. UBA's institutional history gives the decisive acts: the university created the ISU on **11 March 1948**, appointed Della Paolera director on **24 April 1948**, and later named the institute after him.

The institute sought postgraduate specialization and an interdisciplinary approach embracing architecture, engineering, geography, law, economics, sanitation, transport, and municipal administration. It gave durable institutional form to the educational campaign he had pursued since Rosario in 1929.

In **1949**, Della Paolera initiated **Día Mundial del Urbanismo / World Town Planning Day**, observed on **8 November**. The international event used the symbol and message of urban planning to link professionals across national borders. Spanish planner **César Cort** and other members of Della Paolera's international network supported its dissemination. The current international planning bodies ISOCARP, the Global Planning Education Associations Network's International Planning History Society-related World Town Planning Day platform, and the Royal Town Planning Institute continue to recognize the Argentine founder.

In **1950** he published or presented **“L'Aéroport National du Grand Buenos Aires,”** extending his metropolitan thought to aviation. The document reflects his continuing focus on gateways and regional infrastructure even as his direct municipal power had ebbed.

On **28 September 1956**, **Sergio Fernández Pico** succeeded him as director of the UBA institute. The available official chronology does not describe this as a punitive dismissal. It was a succession at the end of Della Paolera's active institutional leadership; the precise circumstances are not documented in the sources consulted.

### Final years and death, 1956–1960

Little is published about his last four years. He remained a reference point for professional urbanism but no major new built project or book has been identified after 1950. He died in Buenos Aires on **15 September 1960**, eight days after his seventieth birthday.

No consulted source records a terminal illness, accident, addiction, psychiatric crisis, or specific cause of death. No evidence was found of exile, imprisonment, forced migration, or political proscription. His only documented extended migration was the educational residence in France.

### Technologies, inventions, and patents

Della Paolera did not invent a proprietary technology in the patent sense, and no patent was found under his name. His innovations were methodological and institutional:

- the **expediente urbano**, a continuously updated diagnostic record;
- metropolitan mapping that treated the capital and suburbs as one functional region;
- coordination of census, cadastral, land-use, transport, geology, and open-space data;
- the wide urban parkway as a combined traffic, landscape, and hygienic device;
- the Symbol of Urbanism as an international professional emblem.

### Businesses, investments, and financial structures

No company directorship, partnership, securities holding, land-development business, concession, patent income, or identifiable private investment has been found. His career was based on public office, university teaching, professional commissions, lectures, and publication. Claims about personal enrichment would be unsupported by the evidence located.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### The city as organism and historical process

Della Paolera argued that a city could not be designed as if history and geography did not exist. From Poëte he took the idea of urban evolution and civic “duration.” His plans begin with origin, topography, circulation, demographic growth, economy, and inherited form. The organic metaphor was not ornamental: it justified a diagnostic sequence and continuous administration. The planner was a physician; the plan was not a final picture but a course of treatment that must change as the organism changed.

### “Scientific urbanism”

He promoted urbanism as simultaneously scientific, technical, social, and artistic. Statistics and cadastral surveys were necessary but insufficient; the planner also needed historical judgment and civic composition. This position distinguished him from both beautification alone and narrow traffic engineering. It also supported his campaign for specialized institutes and a municipal office above individual public-works departments.

### Hygiene, nature, and open space

Sun, air, soil, and vegetation recur throughout his work. He called parks the city's lungs, criticized Buenos Aires's shortage of open space, and proposed metropolitan park systems and planted avenues. Later writers sometimes call him an early “ecologist.” The record supports an environmental concern, but its period vocabulary was mainly hygienist, organicist, garden-city, and regional-planning discourse, not late twentieth-century ecology.

### Modernism and historical continuity

He was not an anti-modern traditionalist: he supported motor circulation, underground routes and parking, airports, cadastral modernization, zoning, and major demolition. But he opposed the erasure of urban history in abstract schemes. He criticized the anti-historical implications he saw in **Le Corbusier's** reconstruction proposals, especially the idea of beginning again from a cleared ground. This placed him at odds with doctrinaire functionalism while leaving him open to monumental intervention on the 9 de Julio.

The apparent contradiction is real: he defended historical diagnosis yet endorsed an extremely destructive central avenue. His own resolution was that the broad parkway answered the evolved metropolis's needs and created a new civic landscape. Critics did not accept that this reconciled the social, architectural, and traffic costs.

### Organicism, eugenic language, and later interpretation

Recent scholarship has noted that interwar organic and hygienic urbanism sometimes shared metaphors with eugenics—health, degeneration, circulation, surgery, and purification—and, in Europe, with nationalist ideas about people and soil. Della Paolera used medical and organic language. That documentary fact permits analysis of the intellectual field; it does **not** establish that he belonged to a eugenics organization, advocated coercive reproductive policy, or supported fascism or Nazism. No such organizational or political evidence was located.

### Politics and administration

His explicit public language emphasized professional competence, municipal coordination, and the common civic interest rather than a party program. He depended on political patrons and administrative appointments, lobbied for institutions, and competed for jurisdiction. His “apolitical” scientific posture was itself a claim to authority in a political field, not proof that planning decisions were outside politics.

## Successes, Failures, and Unresolved Outcomes

### Significant successes

- Completed the Paris urbanism diploma and produced an early modern study of Greater Buenos Aires.
- Established the first documented Argentine university chair devoted to urbanism at Rosario in 1929 and a second teaching center at UBA in 1933.
- Secured creation of Buenos Aires's first continuing technical planning office in 1932.
- Recast the Avenida 9 de Julio as a broad parkway and saw its first section open in 1937.
- Coauthored the major Rosario regulatory plan and the built Junín avenue intervention.
- Helped institutionalize the profession through congresses, the Instituto Argentino de Urbanismo, and UBA's Instituto Superior de Urbanismo.
- Created the Symbol of Urbanism and founded World Town Planning Day.

### Failures or incomplete objectives

- His 1927 Buenos Aires thesis did not become an adopted comprehensive metropolitan plan.
- The municipal office was financially and administratively weakened after only a year.
- Buenos Aires never acquired the unified metropolitan government or permanent cross-jurisdictional planning authority he advocated.
- The Rosario plan and several provincial/civic-center projects were only partially implemented or not built.
- His 9 de Julio solution remained contested and did not by itself solve metropolitan traffic.
- By the 1940s he had lost much of the central administrative influence he sought, although his university position endured.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, and Financial Questions

### Documented controversies

1. **Rosario commission without a competition (1929).** Della Paolera, Guido, and Farengo obtained a direct commission supported by the Centro Argentino de Ingenieros. Local opponents protested the absence of an open competition. This is evidence of professional lobbying and a disputed procurement method. No source consulted alleges or proves a bribe, kickback, fraud, or criminal charge.

2. **Avenida 9 de Julio expropriation, demolition, and cost.** The broad parkway required clearing nearly full blocks, increased public acquisition costs, erased buildings and street fabric, and sacrificed land that critics thought could be resold. Architects and engineers contested its traffic logic and civic design. These are documented policy and design disputes; no finding of personal financial misconduct was located.

3. **Conflict of jurisdiction.** The municipal planning office clashed with the national Ministry of Public Works and other commissions over the 9 de Julio, civic buildings, transport, and authority. Della Paolera withdrew from one commission. The sources support bureaucratic conflict, not a verified court case or criminal investigation.

4. **Service during the Década Infame.** He held office in a political system born from a coup and sustained by fraud and repression. His technical work benefited from that state's intervention powers. No evidence found connects him personally to electoral fraud, torture, political violence, or an intelligence service.

5. **Professional marginalization.** The 1933 budget cut and later loss of authority may have reflected political shifts, rival professional groups, or dissatisfaction with results. These are scholarly explanations, not proven allegations of incompetence or political punishment.

### Matters for which no evidence was found

No consulted source documents an arrest, indictment, conviction, civil fraud judgment, corruption investigation, money laundering, tax case, bankruptcy, secret-police role, political violence, abuse allegation, addiction, plagiarism proceeding, or falsified credential. Absence from the consulted record does not prove that no private dispute ever existed; it means none should be included as fact or allegation without evidence.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. “Della Paolera authored Buenos Aires's 1925 city plan”

**Story.** Modern summaries sometimes identify him as the author or designer of the 1925 *Proyecto orgánico para la urbanización del municipio*.

**Appearance and circulation.** The claim circulates in compressed web biographies and derivative descriptions that combine all early Buenos Aires planning into one career narrative.

**Evidence.** The scanned 1925 plan and planning histories identify the commission around Mayor **Carlos M. Noel**, chaired by **Martín Noel**, with **René Karman, Carlos Morra, Sebastián Ghigliazza/Gagliazza**, and, in fuller accounts, **Víctor Spotta**, with French landscape planner **Jean-Claude Nicolas Forestier** as adviser and **Ernesto Vautier** contributing drawings. Della Paolera was in France, publishing his own Buenos Aires studies, and later described the 1925 document critically as a static *plano* rather than the continuing scientific *plan* he advocated.

**Assessment.** **Contradicted by chronology and authorship records.** His relevant work is the 1927–28 Paris thesis and, from 1932, the municipal office—not authorship of the 1925 plan.

**Reputational effect.** The error enlarges his role by merging predecessor, critic, and successor into one “founding father.”

### 2. “He created the Avenida 9 de Julio”

**Story.** Della Paolera single-handedly imagined and designed the avenue.

**Appearance and circulation.** Family remembrance, journalism, commemorative articles, and the 2021 book promotion call him its “creator.”

**Evidence.** The alignment and expropriation project predated him by decades, and national legislation dates to 1912. The 1925 plan also included the avenue. Della Paolera's office did make the decisive transformation from a conventional artery to the exceptionally wide avenida-parque and guided the design during first construction.

**Assessment.** **Partly true if “creator” means creator of the wide parkway form; false if it means originator of the avenue itself.**

**Reputational effect.** It provides a visible monument for a career otherwise dominated by institutions, surveys, and unbuilt plans.

### 3. “Father of Argentine urbanism”

**Story.** Della Paolera founded urbanism in Argentina.

**Appearance and circulation.** University histories, newspapers, professional commemorations, family narratives, and World Town Planning Day material.

**Evidence.** He was exceptionally important in creating chairs, a municipal planning office, institutes, terminology, international networks, and the annual observance. But Argentina had earlier urban interventions and theorists: hygienists and engineers, landscape architects such as **Carlos Thays**, planners and visitors such as **Joseph Bouvard** and Forestier, municipal architects including Buschiazzo, and the Noel commission. Rosario also had its own reform networks.

**Assessment.** **Honorific shorthand, not exclusive factual paternity.** “Principal institutionalizer of scientific urbanism in Argentina” is more precise.

**Reputational effect.** The title rescued him from later professional “amnesia” but can erase predecessors and collaborators.

### 4. “The first Latin American urbanist”

**Story.** He was Latin America's first urban planner.

**Appearance and circulation.** Institutional biographies and commemorative press.

**Evidence.** IUUP diploma research places him first in the recorded series of Latin American students completing that specialized Paris qualification in 1928. Latin American city planning and professional city experts plainly existed earlier.

**Assessment.** **Supported only when specified as the first known Latin American graduate of the Paris institute's urbanism diploma.**

**Reputational effect.** It lends a clear origin date to a profession whose formation was actually gradual.

### 5. “He founded World Urbanism Day in 1935”

**Story.** The international day began when his symbol was adopted in 1935.

**Appearance and circulation.** Repeated in municipal and professional commemorations.

**Evidence.** He designed the symbol in 1934; it was adopted at Besançon in 1935. International professional bodies date the actual annual observance to **1949**.

**Assessment.** **Conflation of related but separate events.** The founder attribution is supported; the 1935 date for the day is not.

**Reputational effect.** It ties his 1930s peak directly to a global annual institution and suppresses the fourteen-year development between emblem and observance.

### 6. “An ecologist before ecology”

**Story.** His blue-green-gold symbol and park proposals made him a modern environmentalist ahead of his era.

**Appearance and circulation.** Family testimony, planning-day commemoration, and contemporary ecological reinterpretations.

**Evidence.** He consistently demanded sunlight, air, vegetation, park systems, and respect for regional geography. These were genuine commitments. Their historical sources were hygienism, organicism, civic art, and the garden-city/regional-planning movement; they did not encompass the full scientific or political content of contemporary environmentalism.

**Assessment.** **Retrospective analogy with a documentary basis, but anachronistic if taken literally.**

**Reputational effect.** It has renewed interest in his regional green-infrastructure proposals.

### 7. “His home received all the world's great urbanists”

**Story.** A family account describes a household through which the major international planners passed.

**Appearance and circulation.** Gerardo della Paolera repeated inherited family memories around the 2021 book.

**Evidence.** Della Paolera demonstrably maintained correspondence with Marcel Poëte and other European planners and hosted Werner Hegemann in Argentina. Surviving archival catalogues support an international network. The universal wording cannot be verified, and some contacts may have been institutional rather than domestic.

**Assessment.** **Plausible family memory in its core, unverified in its expansive form.**

**Reputational effect.** It portrays him as Argentina's personal bridge to a global professional elite.

## Death and Final Status

Della Paolera died in Buenos Aires on **15 September 1960**. There is no reliable evidence in the consulted record about the immediate cause, place of death within the city, hospital, funeral, burial site, will, or estate. His wife and at least two sons survived into the later twentieth or early twenty-first century according to genealogical and press records.

Because cause-of-death and health information are absent, later statements that he died from a particular disease should be treated as unverified unless supported by a civil death certificate, obituary, or family medical record.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Institutional legacy

UBA's institute became the **Instituto Superior de Urbanismo, Territorio y Ambiente “Carlos María della Paolera”**, preserving his name in postgraduate urban studies. The Rosario chair, Buenos Aires planning office, Instituto Argentino de Urbanismo, and ISU form a sequence through which a loose field became a teachable profession and municipal specialty.

### World Town Planning Day

The most geographically extensive legacy is the observance of **8 November**. International professional institutions continue to identify Della Paolera as its founder. The annual event has outlived the specific interwar doctrine in which it began, and is now used for contemporary questions of climate, equity, housing, and sustainable urbanization.

### Built and spatial legacy

The Avenida 9 de Julio remains the public object most strongly attached to him. Its symbolic success also distorts memory by obscuring the long prehistory, collective authorship, and demolition controversy. Junín's Avenida San Martín offers a smaller but more integrated example of the rail-to-civic-landscape approach. Rosario's plan survives more as a planning reference and source of partial interventions than as a fully executed city.

### Historiographical cycles

After his death, Della Paolera's reputation diminished. Modernist planning, later metropolitan agencies, and changing professional institutions did not always claim his historical-organic method. The 1977 Randle anthology preserved a selection of writings. Novick and Piccioni's work from 1990 onward explicitly diagnosed an **“amnesia del urbanismo argentino.”** Subsequent studies reincorporated him into histories of transatlantic expertise, regional planning, university education, and Buenos Aires infrastructure. The 2021 CEDODAL monograph, supported by family initiative, produced the fullest illustrated commemoration.

Current interpretation is divided less between praise and denunciation than among scales of attribution. A narrow heroic account calls him the father and creator; a contextual account sees him as one powerful institutionalizer among engineers, architects, politicians, international teachers, and municipal staffs. The second is better supported without eliminating the exceptional density of his institutional work.
