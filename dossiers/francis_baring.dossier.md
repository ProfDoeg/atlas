# Francis Baring: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier concerns **Sir Francis Baring, 1st Baronet (1740–1810)**, the Exeter-born London merchant, financier, East India Company director, government adviser, and founder-manager of the house that became Baring Brothers & Co. It distinguishes Francis from his son **Alexander Baring (1774–1848)**, later 1st Baron Ashburton, who was the principal Baring negotiator in Paris and the United States for the Louisiana Purchase financing.

The principal modern biographical reconstruction is John Orbell’s *Oxford Dictionary of National Biography* entry, which draws heavily on the Baring Archive’s Northbrook papers and correspondence. Ralph W. Hidy’s *The House of Baring in American Trade and Finance* supplies a detailed institutional and American-finance narrative, although it was published in 1949 and should be read alongside newer work on slavery, empire, and the Louisiana transaction. The Baring Archive’s digitized papers are the central primary-document collection. For the slavery question, this dossier relies especially on Historic England’s archival study *The Slavery Connections of Northington Grange*, which tests rather than merely repeats the claim that Baring began by trading enslaved people.

Where contemporary or later sources conflict, the disagreement is stated. Monetary figures are historical nominal amounts unless otherwise indicated; conversion into modern money would vary radically depending on whether one measures consumer prices, income, economic share, or capital value.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Francis Baring.
- **Later style:** **Sir Francis Baring, Baronet**; more fully, **Sir Francis Baring, 1st Baronet**, after 29 May 1793.
- **Business styles associated with him:** John & Francis Baring & Co.; Francis Baring & Co.; Sir Francis Baring & Co.; ultimately Baring Brothers & Co.
- **Honorific and institutional descriptions:** merchant; merchant banker; government loan contractor; underwriter; Member of Parliament; director and chairman of the East India Company; chairman of Lloyd’s Patriotic Fund; president of the London Institution.
- **Contemporary sobriquets:** Lord Shelburne reportedly called him the **“Prince of Merchants.”** The *Times* paired him with Abraham Goldsmid as one of the **“Pillars of the City.”** Lord Erskine’s obituary called him the foremost merchant in Europe in knowledge, character, and wealth. These were contemporary reputational formulas, not formal titles.
- **Birth:** **18 April 1740**, at Larkbeare (also spelled Larkbear), just outside Exeter, Devon, England.
- **Death:** Most modern biographical and family sources give **11 September 1810**, at Lee, Kent. The historic-Hansard person index gives **12 September**. The 11 September date is followed here because it is used by the ODNB-derived account, the older *Dictionary of National Biography*, and Hidy’s archival history.
- **Burial:** **20 September 1810**, family vault, church of St Mary the Virgin, Micheldever, Hampshire.
- **Nationality and legal status:** British subject; English-born son of a naturalized Bremen immigrant.
- **Religion:** His paternal ancestry was Lutheran—his grandfather was a Bremen Lutheran pastor—but Francis lived within Anglican England, married in the Church of England, was buried in an Anglican church, and had clerical descendants. No consulted source establishes an intense personal theology or conversion narrative.
- **Physical condition:** Incurably deaf or severely hard of hearing from youth. He used an ear trumpet. Contemporary description: middle-sized and very thin; Thomas Lawrence’s portrait signals the deafness through his hand placed near his ear.
- **Principal residences and business addresses:** Larkbeare near Exeter; London during schooling and apprenticeship; early countinghouse in Mincing Lane; family home at 6 Mincing Lane after marriage; 11 Devonshire Square from the early 1790s; Camden House/Beddington property in Surrey; Manor House at Lee near Blackheath, Kent; Hill Street near Berkeley Square after 1802; Stratton Park, Hampshire.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line: Bremen to Exeter

Francis’s paternal grandfather, **Franz Baring (1657–1697)**, was a Lutheran theologian and pastor in Bremen. Franz died shortly before the birth of his son **Johann Baring (1697–1748)**. Johann was raised partly in the mercantile Vogds family, his mother Rebecca Vogds belonging to a Bremen wool-merchant family.

Johann came to Exeter in **1717**, aged about twenty, to learn the wool trade. He completed his apprenticeship, became a naturalized British subject in **1723**, anglicized his name to **John Baring**, and remained in Devon. He developed a textile-manufacturing and cloth-exporting business during Exeter’s importance in the woollen-serge trade. In **1728/29** he married **Elizabeth Vowler (1702–1766)**, daughter and heiress of the prosperous Exeter merchant John Vowler. The frequently repeated figure of a **£20,000 dowry** is plausible in the family literature but extraordinarily large and should be understood as an inherited-family-wealth estimate rather than an independently verified marriage-settlement figure in the sources consulted here.

John acquired Larkbeare House and land in the 1730s and Lindridge/Linbridge shortly before his death. A Carnegie Museum of Art provenance timeline, synthesizing family and archival histories, reports that he died on **30 October 1748**, possibly of consumption, leaving about **£40,000**: £17,000 to Elizabeth, £15,000 to his eldest son John, and £2,000 to each other child. Francis was eight.

### Mother: Elizabeth Vowler Baring

**Elizabeth Vowler Baring** continued or supervised the family’s commercial affairs after her husband’s death. Later accounts emphasize her financial discipline and state that she nearly doubled the family’s worth. At her death in **1766**, the Carnegie timeline values the family property at about **£70,000**, divided principally among her surviving children. Her role was formative: Francis entered adult commerce with family capital, established commercial connections, and a model of active maternal business management, not from poverty or complete self-creation.

### Siblings

The children of John/Johann and Elizabeth are generally listed as:

1. **John Baring (1730–1816)** — Exeter merchant, landowner, MP for Exeter from 1776, and nominally senior/silent partner in the London house until 1800.
2. **Thomas Vowler Baring (1733–1758)** — died young, in the year of his marriage.
3. **Francis Baring (1740–1810)**.
4. **Charles Baring (1742–1829)** — managed the Exeter house; his speculative management repeatedly endangered it and produced a severe fraternal business conflict.
5. **Elizabeth Baring (usually dated 1744 or 1748–1809)** — married the barrister and politician John Dunning, later 1st Baron Ashburton. Sources differ on her birth year.

### Marriage: Harriet Herring

On **12 May 1767**, at Croydon, Francis married **Harriet Herring (1750–1804)**, daughter of the merchant William Herring. A Herring relative was Thomas Herring, Archbishop of Canterbury. Harriet’s sister Mary married the London private banker **Richard Stone** of Martin, Stone & Co., creating another family-bank connection.

The ODNB-derived account estimates that Harriet ultimately contributed about **£20,000** to the family capital through paternal and family inheritances. For much of the marriage she ran a household physically attached to the business. Later accounts describe her both as frugal in household management and as a prominent social hostess who helped widen Francis’s contacts. A family chronicler’s hostile phrase—“vain, worldly fine woman”—is evidence of one family judgment, not a neutral characterization. Francis, after her death at Bath on **4 December 1804**, wrote of her “superior” mind and affectionate heart.

The Baring Archive states that Francis and Harriet had **twelve children**, eleven born at Mincing Lane; ten reached adulthood. Published lists vary in detail because one or more infant deaths are omitted. The reconstructable list is:

1. **Harriet Baring (1768–1838)** — married **Charles Wall (1756–1815)**, a partner in the house; known for religious controversial writing. Their son was MP Charles Baring Wall.
2. **Maria Baring (1769–1835)** — married Richard Stainforth.
3. **Dorothy Elizabeth Baring (1771–1859)** — married **Pierre César Labouchère**, a leading Hope & Co. partner and central intermediary between Hopes and Barings.
4. **Sir Thomas Baring, 2nd Baronet (1772–1848)** — eldest surviving son; entered the house, succeeded to the baronetcy and principal estates, later left active banking.
5. **Alexander Baring (1774–1848)** — merchant banker, Louisiana financier, politician, President of the Board of Trade, and 1st Baron Ashburton.
6. **Catherine Baring (born 1775)** — poorly documented in the consulted sources; probably one of the children who died young.
7. **Henry Baring (1777–1848)** — merchant banker and MP; married Maria Matilda Bingham, sister of Alexander’s wife.
8. **William Baring (born 1778)** — died in infancy or childhood.
9. **William Baring (1779–1820)** — married Frances Poulett-Thomson.
10. **George Baring (1781–1854)** — traded in Canton before entering the Church of England; his partnership contributed to the lineage of Dent & Co.
11. **Frances Baring (1783–1825)** — married the MP and property developer Thomas Read Kemp.
12. **Lydia Baring (1786–1854)** — married the clergyman Philip Laycock Story.

The family became a durable financial-political dynasty through the baronetcy and the later peerages of Ashburton, Northbrook, Revelstoke, Cromer, and Howick. That later reach should not be projected backward as if it already existed in Francis’s childhood; he converted an affluent provincial merchant inheritance into the institutional base for it.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1740–1755

Francis grew up first at Larkbeare in the commercially active Exeter household. His father’s death in 1748 made Elizabeth the central parental influence. The family remained wealthy by provincial standards.

In the early 1750s Francis was sent to London. He attended **Mr Fargue’s French school at Hoxton**, then **Mr Fuller’s academy in Lothbury**. This was practical merchant formation rather than university education: languages, accounts, correspondence, commercial arithmetic, and metropolitan connections mattered more to his future occupation than a classical degree. No evidence has been found that he attended Oxford or Cambridge.

His early deafness was permanent. A later obituary called it an “incurable” obstruction. Rather than preventing commerce or Parliament, it affected how he conducted conversation and public life. He habitually used an ear trumpet; portraitists and contemporaries noticed the condition.

## Education and Formation: Apprenticeship to Samuel Touchet, 1755–1762

In **1755**, aged fifteen, Francis was apprenticed for seven years to **Samuel Touchet**, a leading London-based Manchester cotton manufacturer, importer, West India merchant, and MP. Elizabeth reportedly paid the substantial premium of **£800**.

Touchet’s firm connected Lancashire manufacturing to the Levant, West Indies, West Africa, and Atlantic shipping. Historic England’s study establishes the following context:

- Touchet claimed an interest in twenty West India ships in 1751.
- He part-owned Dukinfield Hall plantation in Jamaica.
- His family imported slave-produced Caribbean cotton and invested in transatlantic slaving voyages.
- During Francis’s clerkship, Touchet helped finance the 1758 British expedition against French Senegal, seeking gum, ivory, gold, and access to the region’s trade in enslaved Africans.
- Touchet invested in the slaving ship *Lydia*, which left Liverpool in 1756, embarked 340 captive Africans at Anomabu, and delivered about 290 survivors to Jamaica.

This environment trained Baring in commodity arbitrage, bills, insurance, shipping, colonial markets, and political-commercial contracting. It also placed him inside a business directly tied to plantation slavery and the trafficking of Africans.

The often-repeated assertion that Francis personally “made his first money dealing in slaves at sixteen” is not established by these facts. Historic England found his name absent from the ownership records of slaving vessels in the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database and judged direct investment by a sixteen-year-old apprenticed clerk in the *Lydia* “extremely unlikely.” The claim and its afterlife are treated separately below.

Touchet’s business failed in **1763**, shortly after Francis completed his term. That collapse supplied a negative lesson in concentration, leverage, and speculative overreach. It did not prevent Baring from pursuing large market operations of his own.

## Early Career and Foundation of the House, 1762–1777

### Partnership structure

Francis completed his apprenticeship on **Christmas Day 1762**. On that day the surviving brothers signed articles creating two linked houses:

- **John & Francis Baring & Co.**, London.
- **John & Charles Baring & Co.**, Exeter.

The London house began business on **1 January 1763**. John held a major or nominally senior interest in both; Francis directed London; Charles directed Exeter. A retiring family friend, **Nathaniel Paice**, transferred accounts and goodwill. The firm began as London agent for the Exeter textile business and other West Country merchants: selling goods, purchasing imports, collecting and remitting funds, negotiating bills, arranging storage and shipping, and taking joint-account commodity positions.

### Addresses and domestic-commercial integration

The first office was in or near Mincing Lane. After marrying, Francis and Harriet lived at **6 Mincing Lane** for about twenty-three years, above or closely joined to the countinghouse. The household was therefore part of the business infrastructure: kin, apprentices, clerks, visitors, and correspondence circulated through the same space.

### Early losses

The later image of unbroken prudence is misleading. The firm reportedly lost money in **eight of its first fourteen years**. Francis began with about **£10,000** available but calculated his net worth at only **£2,500 in 1777**. Another set of private accounts places his assets near £5,000 in 1763 and £13,000 by the time of his Royal Exchange appointment; varying accounting bases explain some apparent contradiction.

### Royal Exchange Assurance and Lloyd’s

In **1771**, the Royal Exchange Assurance appointed him to its court; he served until 1780 and held about **£820** in its stock. The position publicly marked him as a recognized City merchant. He also underwrote marine risks at Lloyd’s, an activity inseparable from wartime and colonial shipping.

### Break with the Exeter house

Charles Baring engaged in speculative operations Francis described as “wild, strange, incoherent” and “almost monstrous.” Francis feared that failure in Exeter would destroy the family name and London credit. In **1777** the interlocking partnerships were dissolved, separating capital and management. Francis continued to assist or rescue Charles privately, but London ceased to be structurally liable for Exeter’s operations. John remained a sleeping partner in the London house until 1800.

The 1777 separation was decisive: Francis could enforce centralized bookkeeping, select correspondents, limit exposures, and build a house whose primary asset was its acceptance credit rather than a provincial stock of textiles.

## Chronological Life History

### 1777–1782: independent London management

After separation, Francis managed the London house with increasing autonomy. Partnership capital was about **£20,000 in 1777**. His model combined:

- commission trade;
- trading on the firm’s own or joint account;
- acceptance of bills of exchange for selected correspondents;
- advances against goods or reliable names;
- currency and securities operations;
- political and commercial intelligence gathered through a wide correspondence.

His method depended on choosing correspondents for capital, competence, and character and on refusing indiscriminate credit. This became a house tradition, though later generations did not always maintain it.

In **1781**, the clerk **James/J. F. Mesturas** and **Charles Wall** entered as nominal or junior partners and later became full partners. Wall eventually married Francis’s eldest daughter.

### 1782–1790: Shelburne’s adviser and entry into Parliament

Francis’s sister Elizabeth married **John Dunning**, a lawyer, MP, and ally of **William Petty, 2nd Earl of Shelburne** (later Marquess of Lansdowne). When Shelburne became prime minister in 1782, Baring became a confidential commercial adviser—William Cobbett’s later hostile description was Shelburne’s “handy City man.”

Baring advised on the commercial settlement following the American War of Independence, relations with the new United States, trade with Turkey, Gibraltar, public borrowing, and related questions. He knew American and European markets in a way most ministers did not. He corresponded or associated with Isaac Barré, Edmund Burke, Henry Dundas, William Pitt the Younger, Jeremy Bentham, Sir Samuel Romilly, Lord Camden, Lord Erskine, and others in the Shelburne/Bowood network.

The relationship mixed advice, patronage, and finance. From about **1783 to 1789**, Baring lent Lansdowne **£5,000** on security. After Lansdowne’s death in 1805, Baring became a trustee charged with addressing estate debts of about **£90,000**.

In **1784**, Baring entered the Commons for **Grampound**, a Cornwall borough, at a reported cost of **£3,000**. The mechanism was eighteenth-century patronal/bought borough politics, not a mass democratic mandate. Pitt also appointed him a commissioner to investigate fees and gratuities in public offices.

Baring lost the Grampound seat in 1790 and then unsuccessfully contested Ilchester. He later represented Lansdowne-controlled seats:

- **Chipping Wycombe**, 1794–1796.
- **Calne**, 1796–1802.
- **Chipping Wycombe**, 1802–1806.

His hearing and voice made set-piece oratory difficult. Contemporary accounts nevertheless described his financial speeches as clear, compact, and evidence-based.

### East India Company, 1779–1810

Baring became a director of the **East India Company in 1779**. By 1783 he led an important City interest on the Court of Directors; by 1786 contemporaries called him one of its most capable members. He devoted Wednesdays and sometimes Fridays to Company business.

His position combined ideas that later summaries often flatten into “free trade.” He advocated freer commerce in many Atlantic and domestic contexts but defended the East India Company’s corporate monopoly and institutional autonomy. He resisted the sweeping India proposals associated with Lord North and Charles James Fox, opposed direct government absorption of Company rule, and reportedly declared: **“India is not a colony and God forbid that it ever should be.”** The statement meant opposition to treating the Company’s Indian dominions as an ordinary Crown colony; it did not reject British domination or Company sovereignty.

He worked with fellow director and old associate **Richard Atkinson** to modify and secure the implementation of **Pitt’s India Act of 1784**, which created greater ministerial oversight while preserving Company forms. He was elected Company chairman for **1792–93** and handled the difficult charter-renewal negotiations of 1793.

Chairmanship strained his health and finances. In 1792 he traveled for health reasons and failed to monitor a fall in British government securities that reportedly cut a private £20,000 position in half. He complained to Lansdowne that the directors were generally “knaves or fools.” Pitt’s government created him a baronet on **29 May 1793**, conventionally described as recognition of his Company service and charter work.

He remained a director until death, although less dominant after the later 1790s. From 1807 he argued within the Company for Americans’ ability to trade with India through indirect voyages, again demonstrating that his free-trade position varied with the institutional and geopolitical question.

### 1786: tea taxation and the Commutation Act

The **Commutation Act 1784** sharply reduced tea duties to suppress smuggling and enlarged the East India Company’s legal tea market. In **1786**, Baring published *The Principle of the Commutation-Act, Established by Facts*. He defended the tax reduction through observed revenue and trade effects. The pamphlet expressed his preference for reducing prohibitions and rates that made illicit trade more profitable than legal trade.

### Hope & Co. and the failed cochineal corner, 1787–1788

Baring’s correspondence with Amsterdam’s **Hope & Co.** began by 1766 and developed into a strategic alliance. In **1787**, the two houses attempted to buy enough of Europe’s cochineal supply to influence the market. Agents from Cádiz to St Petersburg spent about **£450,000**; Barings carried roughly one-quarter and Hopes the balance. Prices did not rise as intended. By 1788 the expected loss was serious enough that Baring partners renounced their year’s profit share.

This was a deliberate attempted market corner, not passive hedging. It is the clearest counterexample to the later myth that Francis never engaged in aggressive speculation. The loss did not destroy the house, and the alliance with Hopes survived.

### 1790s: war, Amsterdam’s displacement, government finance

The Revolutionary and Napoleonic wars changed European finance. French occupation displaced Amsterdam houses; Hope principals and capital moved to London between 1795 and 1803. London gained from naval power, state borrowing, and interrupted continental channels. Baring and Hope collaborated in commodities, foreign exchange, securities, subsidies, and state loans.

Partnership capital rose from about **£70,000 in 1790** to **£400,000 in 1804**. Annual profit reached roughly **£40,000** in the 1790s and an exceptional **£200,000-plus in 1802**. Francis supplied 40 percent of capital in 1790 and about 54 percent in 1804; his profit entitlement rose from roughly one-half after 1777 to three-quarters by 1801.

The house participated in British public loans and contracts. Hidy identifies Francis Baring & Co. among the agencies marketing Pitt-era loans, including a share of the **£20.5 million loan of 1800** with Robarts & Co. and Giles. After 1799 the house’s name appeared repeatedly among public-debt contractors. The ODNB-derived account calculates that the house headed or joined public loan contracting in twelve of the next fifteen years, with aggregate attributed profit near **£190,000**. Some of that activity continued after Francis’s formal retirement because his name reassured subscribers.

Baring opposed Pitt on major political questions and objected to seemingly endless war expenditure, but his firm financed the British state and its allies during those wars. Political disagreement and financial participation coexisted.

### West Indian, slavery, and imperial-war connections

The firm’s Atlantic business included sugar, cotton, coffee, rum, dyewoods, bills and consignments generated in plantation economies. The evidence is stronger for finance and commodity connection than for Francis’s direct ownership of a slave ship.

Historic England documents that the Baring house shared in government contracts for the British expedition to the Caribbean in **1793**. A Baring Archive account described funds for the colonial corps as including the purchase of “provisions, stores, negroes, horses.” Enslaved men were purchased by British forces as soldiers and auxiliaries during the attempt to conquer Saint-Domingue and suppress Black revolutionary resistance. The record establishes a firm/state-finance connection; it does not by itself show Francis personally selected or purchased individuals.

The same study reports a **1799 plan** by Francis for a large West Indian speculation, particularly in Dutch Surinam commodities depressed by wartime conditions. Other local research has associated him with credit or ownership interests in Jamaica’s Bogue estate, but the consulted archival study did not provide enough direct documentation to assign him a precise personal ownership fraction here. Later Baring Brothers plantation ownership, mortgages, and slave-compensation claims in the 1820s–1840s occurred after Francis’s death and must not be retroactively described as his personal acts.

### North American networks, 1774–1802

The house opened its first North American account in **1774**. Francis built relationships with Philadelphia and Boston merchants, including **Thomas Willing**, **William Bingham**, **Robert Morris**, the Codmans, Willing & Francis, Robert Gilmor, and later Stephen Girard. Correspondence and marriage made the network unusually dense.

For the United States government:

- In **1795**, the Barings marketed up to **$800,000 in U.S. six-percent stock** to fund negotiations with Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli. They advanced funds before all stock had been sold, sometimes beyond strict instructions. Hope & Co. shared later risk.
- In **1798–1801**, they bought about **11,000 muskets and 330 cannon** for the United States during the undeclared naval conflict with France, charging commission.
- In **1802**, Alexander helped purchase the federal government’s remaining Bank of the United States shares, enabling debt liquidation and remittance to Europe.
- The house became London banker or intermediary for the Bank of the United States, whose president Thomas Willing was a longstanding client and the father-in-law of William Bingham.

### Maine lands and dynastic American marriages, 1795–1802

In 1795 Francis sent Alexander to the United States to investigate **William Bingham’s Maine lands**. Alexander arrived in Boston at Christmas, traveled through New York to Philadelphia, and in early 1796 purchased more than a million acres for Barings and Hopes at roughly **£90,500–£107,000**, depending on which tracts and costs a source includes. The parties also financed improvements. The land investment did not produce the rapid fortune envisioned, but it embedded Barings in U.S. finance and politics.

Alexander married **Anne Louisa Bingham** in 1798. Henry Baring married her sister **Maria Matilda Bingham** in 1802. Bingham’s fortune included privateering, Caribbean commodity trade, and proceeds connected to enslaved captives; the marriages therefore brought both U.S. political-financial influence and slave-derived wealth into the next Baring generation.

### 1797–1801: paper money and the Boyd controversy

The Bank of England suspended cash payments in 1797 under wartime pressure. Francis defended the restriction and a managed paper circulation in:

- *Observations on the Establishment of the Bank of England, and on the Paper Circulation of the Country* (1797).
- *Further Observations…* (1797).
- *Observations on the Publication of Walter Boyd, Esq., M.P.* (1801).

He rejected the claim that paper issue alone explained high prices, stressed confidence and the external drain of specie, and imagined the Bank as a “citadel or rock” from which circulation could be reconstructed after panic. He proposed constraints on country-bank notes payable on demand. His position was not identical to later unrestricted central-bank discretion; it combined defense of suspension with limits, information, and institutional hierarchy.

Walter Boyd answered him, and contemporary pamphlet literature included anonymous “twelve-penny answers.” Bibliographies sometimes assign those replies to “F. Baring,” but authorship is not sufficiently secure to include them as unquestionably genuine works.

### Property, art, and entry into the landed elite

From about 1790 Francis acquired property at **Beddington, Surrey**, associated with Camden House. In **1796** he purchased **Manor House, Lee**, from or through his friend Joseph Paice as trustee for Thomas Lucas. Sources give approximately **£20,000 to £27,000**. He bought additional Buckinghamshire land for about £16,000.

Around **1800–1802** he purchased **Stratton Park**, Hampshire, from the Duke of Bedford. Sources disagree on the exact year; **£150,000** is the standard price. The estate eventually comprised thousands of acres; later accounts give 12,000 acres and substantial rental income. Architect **George Dance the Younger** remodeled the house, at costs variously estimated from £25,000 to £40,000.

Baring collected Dutch and Flemish Old Masters, including works attributed to Rembrandt, Rubens, Van Dyck, and Rachel Ruysch, as well as contemporary British art. His collecting expenditures began by the mid-1790s; by 1808 he valued the pictures at about **£15,000**. He commissioned:

- **Sir Joshua Reynolds**, *The Marquess of Lansdowne, Colonel Barré and Lord Ashburton*—a visual record of his political network.
- **Sir Thomas Lawrence**, the 1806 group portrait of Francis, John Baring, and Charles Wall—an institutional memorial marking transition from founder to successors.

The phrase **“Kingdom of Stratton”** captured both scale and the conversion of merchant profit into land, art, hereditary title, and county status. A contemporary joke said he might soon enclose Hampshire with his park paling.

### Reorganization and withdrawal, 1800–1806

On **1 April 1800**, John Baring retired and John & Francis Baring & Co. became **Francis Baring & Co.** The partners were Francis, Charles Wall, and Thomas Baring. Alexander and Henry entered in 1803/04.

Francis began surrendering his profit share in **1803** and retired as an active partner in **1804**, though he left approximately **£70,000** in the firm—about 17 percent of its resources at death—and his name continued to be used in public-loan contracts. A new partnership styled **Baring Brothers & Co.** dates to **1 February 1806** in Hidy; the Baring Archive’s director list places the sons’ effective succession in 1804 and the physical nameplate change in 1807. These are stages of a transition, not necessarily contradictions.

His intended super-house combining Barings and Hopes never formed. Alexander refused permanent subordination within Hope & Co.; Francis told Lansdowne he had to abandon the “colossal plan” of one foot in England and one in Holland.

## The Louisiana Purchase Financing, 1803–1804

### What Francis did—and what Alexander did

The shorthand “Francis Baring financed the Louisiana Purchase” is institutionally true but biographically incomplete.

- **Francis** was the senior founder, capital provider, and approving authority in London. He had built the U.S. network, accepted the exposure, negotiated with British officials, and authorized Alexander’s role.
- **Alexander Baring**, assisted by **Pierre César Labouchère**, was the active negotiator in Paris and then the representative who traveled to the United States, waited for ratification and bond issuance, and received a tranche of securities.
- **Hope & Co.** supplied capital, continental expertise, and distribution capacity.
- The sovereign negotiators were Thomas Jefferson’s ministers **Robert R. Livingston** and **James Monroe**, French treasury minister **François Barbé-Marbois**, and U.S. Treasury secretary **Albert Gallatin**.

Thus the transaction belongs to Francis’s career because it occurred under Francis Baring & Co., depended on his approval and credit, and affected him personally; it should not erase Alexander’s operational leadership.

### Structure

The treaty and two conventions were agreed in Paris at the end of April 1803 and signed in early May. The total headline price was **80 million francs / $15 million**:

- **60 million francs / $11.25 million** in U.S. six-percent stock, redeemable between 1819 and 1822.
- Up to **20 million francs / $3.75 million** for American citizens’ claims against France.

France wanted usable money, not long-dated American paper. French bankers and the Bank of France would not intermediate on acceptable terms. Baring and Hope contracted to buy the $11.25 million stock from France at a discount, commonly stated as **52 million francs** for securities with a face value of 60 million—about a **13.3 percent discount**. They would advance money to France and distribute the securities in European markets.

Alexander and Labouchère obtained powers of attorney in early May. A contract with France was signed **6 June 1803**. Alexander went to the United States in July. He received one-third of the bonds on **16 January 1804** and soon sailed for Europe; the other two-thirds were transmitted to the American minister in Paris. Barings and Hopes advanced funds before final resale and arranged payments in Paris and Baltic ports even after Britain and France resumed war.

### Risk and profit

The operation was enormous relative to the house. Francis wrote that his nerves were equal to it while admitting that “we all tremble” at the American exposure; he later described being overpowered by the strain. Gallatin estimated potential financier profit around **$3 million** if securities sold at prevailing London prices. Hidy reports this estimate but not a verified final net. Historic England suggests the two houses probably earned more than $1 million in commissions/profit; precise allocation remains unresolved.

The firm also obtained the long-running account for paying interest on much of the Louisiana stock—more than **£80,000 annually** passed through it according to Hidy.

### Bribery question

Larry Neal’s 2024 reconstruction identifies a **30 January 1803 letter from Labouchère to Alexander Baring** proposing that payments to key individuals would “grease the wheels.” Neal characterizes these as bribes and notes that approval from Francis Baring and Henry Hope was required before Alexander could proceed. This is documentary evidence that corrupt inducements were contemplated in the financiers’ planning.

The publicly accessible abstract does **not** by itself establish: the identity of every proposed recipient; the amount actually paid; that every proposed payment occurred; or a surviving instruction in Francis’s own hand expressly approving a particular bribe. The responsible classification is therefore: **documented bribery proposal within a plan requiring Francis’s approval; execution and Francis’s specific authorization require examination of the underlying correspondence and Neal’s full archival apparatus.** No criminal investigation, indictment, or court finding against Francis resulted.

### British “trading with the enemy” issue

Britain and France returned to war while the financing was being completed. British ministers initially permitted the operation, preferring Louisiana in American rather than French hands and valuing the commercial advantages. Francis dealt with British officials over passports and payment permissions. Hidy notes that enemy trading was less precisely codified than in later wars. This was a politically licensed cross-border operation, not evidence of a prosecution for treason or unlawful enemy trade.

### Slavery and Indigenous sovereignty

The treaty transferred France’s claimed sovereignty or pre-emption rights over a vast territory without consent from the Indigenous nations that occupied and governed it. The transaction later enabled U.S. settler expansion, dispossession, and the geographic growth of slavery. Historic England notes that Louisiana became an intensive sugar and slave economy and estimates that about 280,000 enslaved people were moved into the region through the U.S. domestic trade between 1810 and 1860.

These are documented consequences of the transaction. Evidence does not show that Francis designed the deal principally to expand slavery or personally determined subsequent U.S. territorial policy. Institutional financing, intended purpose, and later consequence should remain distinct.

## Final Years, 1804–1810

Harriet died at Bath in December 1804. Francis increasingly divided his time among Lee, Hill Street, and Stratton, art and estate management, public institutions, and residual financial-political duties.

He served as **chairman of Lloyd’s Patriotic Fund from 1803 to 1810**, administering awards, annuities, and relief for wounded service members and dependants of those killed in the wars. He became **president of the London Institution in 1805** and held the office until death.

He was frequently selected as an arbiter or trustee in commercial failures. His most important insolvency role concerned **Boyd, Benfield & Co.**, which failed in 1799 after major Austrian-loan and government-finance operations. His 1801 pamphlet defended his analysis of paper circulation against Walter Boyd’s attack. This was not a prosecution of Baring; it was a public monetary controversy complicated by his trustee role.

In **1805–08**, he assisted the Baring-Hope-Ouvrard-Parish operation moving Mexican silver and goods through neutral and British channels to satisfy Spanish obligations to Napoleonic France. Francis persuaded Pitt’s government that the operation could benefit British commerce and provide silver valuable to the East India Company. It involved blank Spanish trading licenses, blockade exceptions, large insurance risks, and commissions. Hidy reports that more than **£862,000** was eventually divided among Hopes, Barings, David Parish, and two agents, but the Baring share is unknown. The operation demonstrates how state permission, blockade, private finance, and enemy payments could overlap.

In 1808 Francis’s toast to the U.S. president at a dinner for Spanish patriots reportedly drew hisses—evidence that his American sympathies or commercial associations were publicly visible during strained Anglo-American relations.

In **1810**, he testified before the Bullion Committee on the high price of gold and became involved through Labouchère in Joseph Fouché’s abortive unofficial peace feelers between France and Britain. This was elite back-channel diplomacy, not a documented intelligence-service appointment. Calling it an “intelligence connection” would overstate the evidence; it was a merchant-diplomatic channel based on family and international banking networks.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### John & Francis Baring & Co. / Francis Baring & Co. / Baring Brothers & Co.

The firm was a merchant house before “bank” became its common retrospective label. Its main technologies were legal-accounting instruments and information systems:

- bills of exchange and acceptances;
- correspondent accounts;
- advances secured on goods, land, stock, or reputable signatures;
- marine insurance underwriting;
- foreign exchange;
- government bonds and subscription syndicates;
- dispersed letters, price reports, and credit intelligence;
- joint-account commodity trading;
- remittance networks capable of routing payments around war and blockade.

It was not a deposit bank in the later branch-banking sense. Its power came from the acceptability of its signature and the geographic reach of its correspondents.

### Royal Exchange Assurance

Court member/director, 1771–1780; shareholder. The appointment strengthened his City standing and connected him to insurance capital.

### Lloyd’s

Marine underwriter; chairman of the Patriotic Fund, 1803–1810. The fund converted wartime commercial organization into quasi-public welfare administration.

### East India Company

Director, 1779–1810; chairman, 1792–93. He defended Company autonomy and monopoly, negotiated charter renewal, advised on imperial trade and security, and participated in governance of a territorial-commercial corporation ruling millions in South Asia.

### U.S. government and Bank of the United States

The house marketed securities, purchased arms, remitted diplomatic funds, acted as London fiscal agent after 1803, handled Bank of the United States transactions, financed the Louisiana stock, and paid interest to European holders.

### Hope & Co.

Strategic alliance beginning in the 1760s: commodity positions, Maine lands, Portuguese and other state loans, Louisiana, wartime remittances, Mexican silver, and family marriage through Labouchère. Francis tried but failed to turn the alliance into a single cross-Channel family-controlled house.

### Government public loans and subsidies

Contractor and distributor for British state borrowing and transfers to continental allies. His name itself became a credit enhancement after his active retirement.

### Land and art

Properties at Beddington/Camden House, Lee, Buckinghamshire, and Stratton Park. Art collection centered on Dutch/Flemish masters and commissioned portraits. These assets represented both investment and social conversion from merchant to hereditary landed elite.

### No inventions or patents

No patents, mechanical inventions, or proprietary technical devices are documented. His innovation was organizational: integrating commodity commerce, acceptance credit, state debt, political access, and transatlantic correspondent banking.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Whig connection without simple party consistency

Baring’s formative political allegiance was to Shelburne/Lansdowne’s Whig network. He did not become a reliable Pitt partisan even though Pitt used his expertise and awarded the baronetcy. After Pitt’s death Baring emphasized that they had disagreed on major political questions for more than twenty years.

### Free trade and monopoly

His general maxim was that regulation was restriction and commerce had “a thousand relations,” so a rule beneficial in one point might damage the whole. He favored lower duties, fewer trade barriers, and greater access to American commerce. At the same time he defended the East India Company monopoly and institutional autonomy. His position was therefore not doctrinal laissez-faire; it was a merchant’s differentiated political economy in which some monopolies and corporate jurisdictions were treated as necessary strategic structures.

### Monetary thought

Baring belongs to the practical prehistory of British central-banking theory. He defended suspension of specie payments under emergency conditions, separated confidence and credit from a purely metallic view of money, warned against fragile country-bank note issue, and treated the Bank of England as a system-stabilizing center. His thought was expressed in intervention pamphlets rather than an abstract treatise.

### Empire

He defended the East India Company against absorption by the Crown, supported strategic seizure proposals concerning Mauritius, Bourbon/Réunion, and the Cape in wartime, helped finance Caribbean campaigns, and profited from imperial trade. His statement that India was “not a colony” should not be misread as anti-imperialism; it asserted a distinct corporate-imperial constitutional form.

### Religion

No substantial theological writings, devotional diary, sectarian affiliation, or crisis of faith by Francis was found. The paternal Lutheran and marital Anglican settings are documented; a precise private creed is not.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Financial Questions, and Abuses

### 1. Slave-trading origin claim

**Classification:** widely repeated allegation/biographical legend; direct personal investment unverified and judged unlikely by the strongest consulted archival study.

Francis unquestionably apprenticed in a firm invested in slaving voyages and plantation commerce. The *Lydia* voyage occurred while he was there. No consulted record names him as shipowner or voyage investor. The claim that he personally made his first money from slave trading at sixteen appears in Peter Fryer’s 1984 *Staying Power* and has been repeated by museums and biographies; Historic England argues that it probably conflates an apprentice’s presence with his master’s ownership.

### 2. Broader slavery-derived business

**Classification:** documented institutional and economic connection; precise personal profit often not separately quantifiable.

The house traded and financed goods produced by enslaved labor; participated in Caribbean military-supply finance that included purchase of enslaved people; planned West Indian speculation; joined networks whose fortunes included plantation and privateering proceeds; and financed a territorial transfer that enabled the later enlargement of U.S. slavery. Later Baring Brothers plantation and mortgage interests should be attributed to later partners, not automatically to Francis.

### 3. Attempted cochineal market corner

**Classification:** documented speculative market-manipulation attempt; no identified criminal charge.

Barings and Hopes secretly accumulated cochineal in 1787 to influence price. The market did not respond; the venture produced expected loss. Eighteenth-century commodity corners were not policed by modern securities or competition law.

### 4. Parliamentary patronage and purchased boroughs

**Classification:** documented feature of the unreformed parliamentary system; not a contemporary conviction for bribery.

Baring reportedly spent £3,000 to enter for Grampound and later sat for Lansdowne’s controlled boroughs. His political access benefited the house. This was patronal politics and a conflict-of-interest environment by modern standards, though legal categories and electoral norms differed.

### 5. Public office and private finance

**Classification:** documented structural conflict-of-interest risk; no proved corruption case.

He advised ministers, served in Parliament, directed the East India Company, contracted for public loans, underwrote war risks, and transacted for foreign states. Information and reputation obtained in one sphere benefited the others. The record shows patronage and profit but no prosecution or court finding that he stole public funds.

### 6. Louisiana bribery planning

**Classification:** documented proposal in correspondence as described by modern archival scholarship; execution and Francis’s individual authorization not fully established in the accessible evidence.

Labouchère’s January 1803 plan contemplated inducements to officials and required approval from Francis and Henry Hope. Further primary-document review is needed before naming Francis as payer of an executed bribe.

### 7. Enemy trade and blockade circumvention

**Classification:** documented, generally government-permitted or tolerated operations; no conviction.

Louisiana payments and the later Mexican-silver scheme moved value across belligerent lines. Francis sought and received British governmental permission or exceptions. The legal-political ambiguity was part of wartime finance.

### 8. Financing war and imperial coercion

**Classification:** documented business and state contracting.

The house marketed British debt, transferred subsidies, purchased U.S. arms, financed Caribbean expeditionary supply, and insured shipping. Its financial technologies supported both trade and state violence. No evidence suggests Francis was a battlefield commander or organizer of political assassinations.

### 9. East India Company rule

**Classification:** documented office and policy advocacy.

As director and chairman, Baring participated in the governance and defense of a monopoly corporation exercising fiscal, military, and territorial power in India. He resisted direct Crown control while helping implement Pitt’s oversight settlement. The abuses of Company rule form the institutional context; individual responsibility for a particular atrocity requires event-specific evidence not found in the sources consulted.

### 10. Wealth estimates

**Classification:** conflicting contemporary and modern accounting estimates.

Published death estimates range from **£606,000** in a reconstructed estate breakdown to **£700,000–£1.1 million** in contemporary or later accounts. The lower figure itemizes roughly £70,000 left in the firm, £175,000 distributed among children other than Thomas, about £400,000 in Hampshire/Lee estates, and nearly £30,000 in art, jewels, and furniture, but categories may overlap. Claims that he “earned £7 million” or left exactly £1 million should not be treated as settled.

### 11. Criminality, fraud, money laundering, lawsuits

No consulted source records Francis Baring’s indictment or conviction for fraud, theft, money laundering, or another crime. “Money laundering” is an anachronistic legal label for his period. His role in insolvency settlements concerned other firms. His business involved secrecy, influence payments proposed by associates, blockade permissions, market corners, and complex remittance schemes, all of which merit documentary scrutiny without converting them automatically into modern criminal findings.

### 12. Intelligence connections

He supplied commercial intelligence to ministers and participated in unofficial diplomatic communications, including the 1810 Fouché-Labouchère peace feeler. No evidence consulted identifies him as an officer or paid agent of a formal intelligence service. **Commercial-political intelligence network** is supported; **professional spy** is not.

## Health, Personality, and Domestic Life

- **Deafness:** permanent from youth; ear trumpet; no known cause in the consulted record.
- **1792 health travel:** he wrote of being obliged to travel for health while East India Company duties and market losses pressed on him. Diagnosis not recorded.
- **Louisiana strain:** his letters describe severe nervous strain, but there is no basis for a retrospective psychiatric diagnosis.
- **Appearance:** middle height, thin body and face.
- **Temperament:** contemporary and family evidence describes method, dexterity, attention to detail, family involvement, generosity to selected friends, and impatience with contradiction.
- **Domestic involvement:** a family source says he ordered children’s shoes and clothes, attended to nursery details, and arranged schooling.
- **Addictions:** none documented.
- **Major accident or injury:** none documented.
- **Cause of death:** not established in the consulted sources. He died at seventy after continuing public and Company work.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: “He made his first fortune trading slaves at sixteen”

1. **Story:** Francis supposedly invested in or profited from a slave voyage while a teenage apprentice and used it as the foundation of Barings.
2. **Appearance and circulation:** The formulation appears at least in Peter Fryer’s *Staying Power* (1984), then in museum text, web biographies, and modern discussions of banking and slavery.
3. **Circulators:** anti-slavery historical writing, museum provenance projects, popular biographies, and social-media summaries.
4. **Evidence:** Touchet, his master, invested in the *Lydia* and other slavery-connected ventures. Francis was sixteen and worked in the firm. Against direct investment: no Francis Baring ownership entry in the slave-voyage data located by Historic England; an apprentice’s capital and legal position make personal investment unlikely; no primary account of his personal profit has been produced in the consulted material.
5. **Reputational effect:** The story provides a simple origin point for a much broader and real institutional entanglement. Its weakness has sometimes been used to dismiss the wider slavery connections; conversely, its repetition has obscured which links are actually documented.

### Myth 2: “Francis personally went to Paris and financed the Louisiana Purchase”

1. **Story:** The elderly founder directly negotiated the purchase with Napoleon and supplied the $15 million.
2. **Appearance:** institutional shorthand and popular histories of Barings.
3. **Circulators:** bank histories, general web summaries, heritage accounts.
4. **Evidence:** Francis approved and bore the London-house risk; Alexander and Labouchère negotiated and traveled; Barings and Hopes bought and marketed $11.25 million in bonds, while $3.75 million concerned American claims. The United States remained the debtor.
5. **Effect:** It magnifies the founder and erases Alexander, Hope & Co., Labouchère, Gallatin, and the treaty’s two-part payment structure.

### Myth 3: the solitary self-made banker

1. **Story:** Deaf provincial youth arrives without advantages and creates a bank solely through personal genius.
2. **Appearance:** Victorian merchant-prince biography and corporate commemoration.
3. **Circulators:** family histories, obituaries, institutional publicity.
4. **Evidence:** His ability and management were central, but he inherited family capital and connections; his mother enlarged the estate; brothers and Paice supplied business; Harriet brought money and networks; Shelburne supplied political access; Hopes supplied capital and continental reach.
5. **Effect:** The myth converts a family, marital, political, and imperial network into an individual morality tale.

### Myth 4: unvarying conservatism and risk avoidance

1. **Story:** Barings’ founder never speculated and succeeded only through cautious lending.
2. **Appearance:** reputation-centered banking history, especially when later Barings crises are contrasted with the founder.
3. **Evidence against:** early losses, the cochineal corner, large securities positions, Maine land investment, Louisiana exposure, and wartime remittance schemes. Evidence for: diversification, close bookkeeping, selective correspondents, and survival of failed ventures.
4. **Effect:** The story became part of the institutional morality invoked after the 1890 and 1995 Barings crises.

### Myth 5: the immaculate “first merchant in Europe”

1. **Story:** commercial greatness and personal integrity were complete and universally recognized.
2. **Appearance:** Lord Erskine’s 1810 obituary; *Times* praise; later family and bank histories.
3. **Circulators:** friends, obituarists, descendants, institutional historians.
4. **Evidence:** strong contemporary credit reputation and repeated public trust are real. The eulogy omits patronal boroughs, market-corner attempts, slavery-connected commerce, imperial coercion, structural conflicts of interest, and contemplated influence payments.
5. **Effect:** It established the founder as the ethical standard against which descendants and employees were judged.

### Myth 6: the 1995 collapse as Francis’s personal legacy

Popular accounts sometimes append Nick Leeson’s derivatives fraud and Barings’ 1995 failure directly to Francis’s biography. The institutional line is historically relevant, but the collapse occurred **185 years after his death** under radically different ownership, regulation, technology, and management. It cannot be treated as evidence about his conduct.

## Death, Estate, and Succession

Francis died at Lee, Kent, on **11 September 1810** (12 September in one parliamentary index). He was buried nine days later at Micheldever. No reliable cause of death was located.

His eldest son **Thomas** became 2nd Baronet and inherited the principal estates. Other children received about **£175,000** in one reconstructed distribution. Around **£70,000** remained invested in Baring Brothers. The Hampshire and Lee estates were later valued around **£400,000**, with art, jewelry, and furnishings near **£30,000**. A consolidated estate figure of **£606,000** competes with contemporary estimates of £700,000 to £1.1 million.

Operational leadership passed to Charles Wall and Francis’s sons, above all Alexander and Henry. The hereditary, landed, political, and collecting projects continued through Thomas and later Northbrooks; the international merchant bank continued through Alexander and later partners.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Merchant banking

Francis helped transform a commission house into a merchant bank whose accepted bills functioned internationally. The firm’s combination of correspondent selection, credit underwriting, state finance, bond distribution, and political intelligence became a model of nineteenth-century merchant banking.

### London’s rise over Amsterdam

His alliance with Hopes belongs to the broader shift of the European payments and sovereign-debt center from Amsterdam to London during the revolutionary wars. Barings did not cause that structural change alone, but it used naval power, British public credit, displaced Dutch expertise, and Atlantic connections unusually effectively.

### Anglo-American finance

The house became a principal European intermediary for the United States, the Bank of the United States, merchants, land ventures, and public debt. The Louisiana operation internationalized U.S. sovereign securities and established an enduring Baring role in American finance.

### Family dynasty

His sons and descendants entered banking, Parliament, the cabinet, diplomacy, colonial administration, the Church, and the peerage. The names Ashburton, Northbrook, Revelstoke, and Cromer became parts of the political geography of the British Empire.

### Monetary thought

His 1797 and 1801 pamphlets remain primary texts in the Bank Restriction and bullion controversies. Historians treat him as a practical merchant theorist of credit and paper circulation rather than a systematic economist.

### Art and provenance

The Northbrook collection grew from his purchases and passed through descendants before major dispersals. Current provenance projects read the collection together with Atlantic slavery, East India Company wealth, state finance, and family inheritance.

### Slavery reinterpretation

Older institutional histories foregrounded integrity, prudence, and American finance. Recent research reconstructs plantation commodities, Touchet’s voyages, Caribbean military finance, Bingham wealth, Louisiana’s expansionary consequences, and later Baring plantation interests. The strongest current interpretation neither accepts the teenage slave-trader story uncritically nor treats its weakness as exoneration from broader slavery-derived finance.

### Barings crises and symbolic founder

The house survived the 1890 Argentine crisis through a Bank of England-led rescue but failed in 1995 after unauthorized derivatives trading by Nick Leeson. In both episodes, commentators invoked the founder’s reputation for control, information, and credit judgment. This posthumous use turned Francis into a symbol of “old” partnership discipline, sometimes more idealized than his actual record warrants.

## Atlas Connections

### Alexander Baring

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Alexander Baring (1774–1848) was Sir Francis Baring’s son, business partner, and principal overseas agent. Francis sent him to the United States in 1795 for the Baring–Hope Maine land investment and later authorized him to negotiate and execute the Louisiana Purchase financing in Paris and Washington during 1803–04. [Baring Archive](https://baringarchive.org.uk/exhibition/the-louisiana-purchase/)

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Surviving correspondence records their sustained father-son and principal-agent engagement. Alexander’s letter from Washington dated 21 October 1803 reported directly to Francis on congressional approval of the Louisiana treaty, negotiations with the U.S. Treasury, and Spain’s protest; the archive also preserves correspondence between them concerning the Maine lands from 1795 onward. [Baring Archive Louisiana papers](https://baringarchive.org.uk/exhibition/the-louisiana-purchase/), [Baring Archive catalogue](https://baringarchive.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/northbrook_np1.pdf)

### Napoleon Bonaparte

- **[T3] Documented fact:** Francis and Napoleon entered an indirect but concrete written–legal engagement through the Louisiana financing in 1803. Francis Baring & Co. empowered Alexander Baring to contract with French treasury minister Barbé-Marbois for the acquisition of the $11.25 million U.S. stock owed to France; Napoleon subsequently ratified the Louisiana agreement, and contemporary Treasury correspondence discussed whether he had also ratified the financiers’ contract. No evidence located establishes a personal meeting or direct correspondence between Francis and Napoleon, so this connection is limited to engagement through authorized agents and government instruments. [U.S. National Archives power of attorney](https://docsteach.org/document/power-of-attorney-alexander-baring/), [Gallatin to Jefferson, 31 August 1803](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/01-41-02-0231), [Baring Archive](https://baringarchive.org.uk/exhibition/the-louisiana-purchase/)

No other roster member was found to have a sufficiently documented direct crossing with Sir Francis Baring under the specified connection types; indirect institutional involvement, mere contemporaneity, and later association with Barings have been excluded.
