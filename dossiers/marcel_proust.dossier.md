# Marcel Proust: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs Marcel Proust’s life from documentary collections, correspondence-based scholarship, institutional chronologies, contemporary publication records, and later historical research.

Labels used for contested material:

- **Documented fact**: supported by primary records, manuscripts, correspondence, official registers, or multiple strong sources.
- **Reported fact**: stated by a credible witness or biographer but not independently established here.
- **Interpretation**: a scholarly argument about evidence or texts.
- **Allegation**: an accusation made at the time or afterward.
- **Rumor or myth**: a recurrent story lacking adequate documentary proof.
- **Unresolved**: evidence permits more than one plausible reconstruction.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full baptismal name:** Valentin Louis Georges Eugène Marcel Proust.
- **Usual name and authorial name:** Marcel Proust.
- **Birth:** 10 July 1871, at 96 rue La Fontaine, Auteuil, then a western suburb and now Paris’s 16th arrondissement.
- **Baptism:** 5 August 1871 at Saint-Louis-d’Antin, Paris.
- **Death:** 18 November 1922, 44 rue Hamelin, Paris, aged fifty-one.
- **Burial:** Père-Lachaise Cemetery, Paris, in the Proust family tomb.
- **Nationality:** French.
- **Religion and descent:** baptized and raised Roman Catholic; his father was Catholic and his mother came from a cultivated, assimilated Jewish family. Proust did not convert from Judaism because, under the patrilineal civil and Catholic framework of his household, he had been baptized in infancy.
- **Occupations:** novelist, essayist, critic, journalist, translator and editor of his own continually revised texts. He briefly held an unpaid library appointment but never maintained a conventional profession.
- **Principal achievement:** *À la recherche du temps perdu*, published in seven conventionally enumerated parts between 1913 and 1927, the last three posthumously.
- **Pseudonyms:** no stable literary pseudonym comparable to a separate public identity. Some early pieces were unsigned or appeared under initials; the mature books appeared as Marcel Proust.
- **Spouse or children:** none documented.
- **Sexuality:** his surviving letters, intimate relationships and contemporary evidence establish predominant romantic and sexual attachment to men. Public disclosure could expose men to scandal and legal or professional consequences, and Proust did not publicly identify himself through a modern sexual-identity category.

A detailed institutional chronology is supplied by the Musée Carnavalet’s [Proust exhibition dossier](https://www.carnavalet.paris.fr/sites/default/files/2023-09/dp_marcel_proust_-_musee_carnavalet_-_uk.pdf); manuscript history is documented by the [Bibliothèque nationale de France](https://gallica.bnf.fr/selections/fr/html/litteratures/la-recherche-du-temps-perdu).

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal family

Proust’s father, **Adrien Achille Proust** (1834–1903), was born at Illiers in Eure-et-Loir, the son of a grocer. Through education and professional advancement he became a physician, professor of hygiene, hospital doctor, epidemiologist and international authority on cholera and quarantine. His work took him to conferences and sanitary missions abroad. He represented the confident medical and civic establishment of the Third Republic.

Illiers and the nearby family country would become the geographical substrate for the fictional Combray. The town officially adopted the name **Illiers-Combray** in 1971, Proust’s centenary year.

### Maternal family

His mother, **Jeanne Clémence Weil** (1849–1905), came from a prosperous, assimilated Parisian Jewish family. Her father, **Nathé Weil**, was a stockbroker; her mother was **Adèle Berncastel Weil**. Jeanne was highly literate, humorous and proficient in English. She supplied literal drafts and linguistic assistance for Marcel’s translations of John Ruskin, which he then extensively rewrote and annotated.

Proust’s maternal great-grandfather **Baruch Weil** and other relatives were buried in the Jewish section of Père-Lachaise. A late letter by Proust remembered the family custom of placing stones on those graves. Recent scholarship has used this evidence to reopen the question of his relationship to Jewish memory; see Antoine Compagnon’s [*Proust, a Jewish Way*](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/proust-a-jewish-way/9780231211352/).

### Immediate family

Marcel’s younger brother, **Robert Émile Sigismond Léon Proust** (1873–1935), became a surgeon and urologist. Their relationship included ordinary fraternal differences—Robert developed a conventional medical career, married and had a daughter—yet Robert became essential to Marcel’s literary estate.

After Marcel’s death, Robert and Jacques Rivière prepared *La Prisonnière*, *Albertine disparue* and *Le Temps retrouvé* for publication from manuscripts that Proust had not brought to a final uniform state. Robert’s daughter **Suzy Mante-Proust** later safeguarded and transferred much of the archive. In 1962 she placed the central manuscript collection at the BnF. The [BnF exhibition history](https://www.bnf.fr/sites/default/files/2022-09/dp_Proust_la_fabrique_de_l_oeuvre.pdf) describes this provenance.

Proust never married and had no documented children.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1871–1882

Proust was born shortly after the fighting and upheaval of the Franco-Prussian War and Paris Commune. The family’s principal Paris residence during his childhood was at **9 boulevard Malesherbes**. They also visited Auteuil and the paternal relatives at Illiers.

Family holidays at Illiers, particularly those involving his father’s sister **Élisabeth Amiot** and her husband **Jules Amiot**, supplied people, houses, gardens, walks and sensory memories later transformed into Combray. The transformation was artistic rather than stenographic: no single relative, house, town or episode maps perfectly onto the novel.

At approximately nine, Proust suffered his first severe recorded asthma attack after a walk in the Bois de Boulogne. Asthma, allergies and recurrent respiratory infections thereafter structured his movements, sleep, social engagements and work. A 2025 historico-medical review concludes that he experienced severe recurrent asthma from childhood, later obstructive pulmonary disease, and finally fatal infectious pneumonia: [*The Lancet Respiratory Medicine* review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S221326002500013X).

His attachment to Jeanne Proust was intense. The famous fictional drama of the child waiting for his mother’s goodnight kiss draws on the emotional structure of that attachment, but it is not a transcript whose every detail can safely be treated as biography.

## Education and Formation, 1882–1895

### Lycée Condorcet

Proust entered the Lycée Condorcet on 2 October 1882 after attending the Cours Pape-Carpentier. Illness caused absences, but he developed a distinguished circle including:

- Jacques Bizet, son of Georges Bizet and Geneviève Halévy;
- Daniel Halévy;
- Robert Dreyfus;
- Fernand Gregh;
- Abel Desjardins;
- Léon Brunschvicg.

His philosophy teacher **Alphonse Darlu** was a major formative influence. Darlu emphasized intellectual honesty and moral examination. Newly publicized school papers complicate the retrospective image of effortless precocity: at least one exercise received a sharply negative assessment for uncertain development, vague abstraction and heavy prose. The document belongs to the large family archive described in the BnF’s [2025 acquisition announcement](https://www.bnf.fr/sites/default/files/2025-09/CP%20BnF-Souscription-Fonds-in%C3%A9dit-Marcel-Proust.pdf).

In 1886 he completed one version of the fashionable confession album now commonly called the **Proust Questionnaire**. Proust did not devise the questionnaire; he merely answered a pre-existing parlor-game form. Its later naming after him is a posthumous cultural association.

Early literary work appeared in school reviews, including *La Revue verte* and *La Revue lilas*. Reading Augustin Thierry in 1886 was remembered by Proust as a decisive stimulus to becoming a writer.

### Military service

In November 1889 Proust volunteered for one year’s military service with the **76th Infantry Regiment at Orléans**. Contrary to the image of an entirely secluded invalid, he remembered portions of military life with pleasure and formed friendships there. His poor health and personal means helped prevent a prolonged military career.

### Higher education

After service he attended the **École libre des sciences politiques**, studying among others with Albert Sorel and Anatole Leroy-Beaulieu. He did not complete the diplomatic section as a professional qualification.

He also pursued studies at the Paris Faculty of Law and the Sorbonne, obtaining a law degree and, in 1895, a licence ès lettres in philosophy. He attended lectures and read widely in aesthetics, philosophy, history and psychology.

### Henri Bergson

The philosopher **Henri Bergson** married Proust’s relative Louise Neuburger; Proust served as a groomsman at their January 1892 wedding. He later attended Bergson’s lectures and annotated *Matière et mémoire*. The relationship and textual contact are documented, but the claim that Proust’s novel simply fictionalizes Bergsonian duration is disputed. Proust resisted reductive claims of Bergson’s direct influence. The evidence is reviewed in this [Oxford study](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A5672fce1-6bac-4ac6-bc9d-7690a6ed81f8/files/m0ad1875ae20412426cbb3501479c51b2).

## Early Career and Social Formation, 1891–1904

### Salons, friendship networks and observation

Proust moved among interconnected bourgeois, artistic and aristocratic salons:

- Geneviève Straus, widow of Georges Bizet;
- Madeleine Lemaire;
- Madame Arman de Caillavet;
- Princesse Mathilde;
- Comtesse Greffulhe;
- Comte Robert de Montesquiou;
- the Faubourg Saint-Germain aristocracy;
- publishers, critics, musicians and diplomats.

These circles were not merely leisure. They supplied an observational archive of speech, status behavior, exclusion, fashion, patronage, artistic judgment and political fracture. Proust’s social climbing attracted mockery, but the salons were also his field of inquiry.

**Anatole France**, introduced through Madame Arman de Caillavet, wrote the preface to *Les Plaisirs et les Jours*. **Robert de Montesquiou** acted as an arbiter and intermediary into elite society and became one among several—not the sole—sources later associated with Baron de Charlus. The BnF cautions against one-to-one identifications while documenting Proust’s use of many social “models” in its [manuscript-collections account](https://www.bnf.fr/fr/les-modeles-de-marcel-proust-dans-les-collections-du-departement-des-manuscrits).

### Employment

Proust briefly obtained an unpaid attaché position at the **Bibliothèque Mazarine** in 1895. Extended medical leave followed, and he resigned in 1900. He never depended on salaried employment.

### *Les Plaisirs et les Jours*

Published in 1896 by Calmann-Lévy, *Les Plaisirs et les Jours* collected stories, prose poems and portraits, with a preface by Anatole France, illustrations by Madeleine Lemaire and musical contributions by Reynaldo Hahn. Its luxurious production and youthful social milieu encouraged charges that it was precious, decadent and insubstantial.

### Reynaldo Hahn and Lucien Daudet

Proust met the Venezuelan-born French composer **Reynaldo Hahn** in Madeleine Lemaire’s salon in 1894. Surviving affectionate letters document an emotionally intimate relationship generally regarded as sexual and romantic, followed by lifelong friendship. Absolute reconstruction of its private boundaries is impossible.

He subsequently became deeply attached to **Lucien Daudet**, the adolescent son of Alphonse Daudet. A photograph of Proust seated before Lucien Daudet and Robert de Flers became the subject of a story that Jeanne Proust was scandalized by Lucien’s intimate gesture. The photograph is real; the exact maternal reaction is a **reported fact**, not independently verifiable.

### Jean Lorrain duel, 1897

On 3 February 1897 critic and novelist **Jean Lorrain** reviewed *Les Plaisirs et les Jours* in *Le Journal*, ridiculing Proust’s style and insinuating an improper relationship with Lucien Daudet. Proust challenged him.

The pistol duel occurred on 6 February 1897 at Meudon. Both men fired and neither was injured. The encounter is **documented fact**. It illustrates the period’s honor code and the danger of public homosexual insinuation; it does not show that the underlying intimacy was invented.

### *Jean Santeuil*

Between approximately 1895 and 1899 Proust worked on the unfinished novel now called *Jean Santeuil*. He abandoned it without publication. The manuscript already explored memory, art, social ambition and the Dreyfus Affair. Bernard de Fallois assembled a posthumous edition in 1952; its imposed arrangement is editorial, not a final text authorized by Proust.

## Political Activity: The Dreyfus Affair

Proust’s principal sustained political intervention concerned **Captain Alfred Dreyfus**, the Jewish army officer wrongfully convicted of treason.

He signed the January 1898 petition demanding revision of the case and helped obtain Anatole France’s signature. In a 1919 letter Proust described himself as perhaps “the first Dreyfusard” because he had solicited France’s participation. This was retrospective self-description, not a demonstrable chronological priority over every other defender. The letter is reproduced in a [correspondence-based source](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/dreyfus-affair/proust/letter-strauss.htm).

Proust attended portions of Émile Zola’s trial and followed the Affair closely. His position divided him from anti-Dreyfusard acquaintances and parts of aristocratic society. He nevertheless maintained relationships across the divide, sometimes trying to reconcile hostile camps. That social flexibility has been interpreted variously as generosity, curiosity, conflict avoidance or political inconsistency.

The Affair became structural material in *Jean Santeuil* and the *Recherche*: loyalties shift with fashion; antisemitism, class, institutions and personal advantage intersect; no character’s speech can automatically be equated with the author’s position.

Proust otherwise avoided a stable party affiliation. During the First World War he followed military events intensely, worried about friends at the front and incorporated the wartime transformation of Paris into his novel. His health prevented service.

## Ruskin, Art Criticism and Intellectual Development, 1899–1906

Proust’s intensive study of **John Ruskin** redirected his work from the abandoned *Jean Santeuil* toward translation, criticism and a theory of perception.

Because Proust’s English was inadequate for independent literal translation, the work was collaborative:

1. Jeanne Proust made literal drafts.
2. Marie Nordlinger and others provided linguistic help.
3. Proust reworked the French prose and added extensive introductions and notes.

This should be described as an acknowledged collaborative translation process, not as fraudulent authorship. The interpretive apparatus is substantially Proust’s.

He traveled to Ruskinian sites, including **Amiens**, **Rouen**, **Venice**, **Padua** and other Italian locations. He also visited Belgium, the Netherlands, Normandy and Brittany at different periods, while health increasingly limited travel.

Works from this phase included:

- *La Bible d’Amiens*, Ruskin translation, 1904;
- “La Mort des cathédrales,” *Le Figaro*, 1904;
- *Sésame et les lys*, Ruskin translation, 1906;
- the preface “Sur la lecture,” later published separately.

Proust eventually moved beyond Ruskin’s doctrine that truth is disclosed through the privileged interpreter. He increasingly argued that the artist must descend into an individual interior experience irreducible to another person’s prescriptions.

## Parental Deaths and Personal Crisis, 1903–1907

Adrien Proust died in November 1903. Jeanne Proust died in September 1905 following serious illness. Her death was the central personal catastrophe of Marcel’s adult life. He spent several weeks at Dr. Paul Sollier’s sanatorium in Boulogne-Billancourt late in 1905.

Descriptions of this period as a “nervous breakdown” are common but medically imprecise. **Documented facts** include grief, insomnia, increased seclusion, somatic illness and sanatorium treatment. Psychoanalytic diagnoses made at a distance remain interpretations.

The family inheritance gave Proust financial independence. He was affluent, employed servants, hired automobiles and drivers, maintained costly hotel and restaurant habits, and could later subsidize his publication. He was not a business proprietor and created no company, patent or invention.

## Creation of *À la recherche du temps perdu*, 1907–1913

In 1907–08 Proust published essays and celebrated literary pastiches in *Le Figaro*, mimicking Balzac, Flaubert, Sainte-Beuve, the Goncourts and others while treating the contemporary Lemoine diamond-fraud affair. The fraud was Henri Lemoine’s; Proust’s role was literary parody, not participation.

Around 1908–09 he worked on the hybrid critical-narrative project later called *Contre Sainte-Beuve*. He rejected Sainte-Beuve’s method of explaining literature primarily through an author’s social and biographical self. Proust argued that the creating self differs from the everyday social person.

The project expanded into *À la recherche du temps perdu*. Seventy-five principal notebooks, thousands of manuscript pages, typescripts, proofs and pasted additions or **paperoles** show continual expansion and recombination. The BnF describes approximately eight thousand autograph pages in the notebooks and notes that only a portion has received full diplomatic publication: [BnF Cahier 71 description](https://editions.bnf.fr/marcel-proust-cahiers-1-75-de-la-bibliotheque-nationale-de-france-cahier-71).

### Residences and working environment

Important Paris addresses included:

- 9 boulevard Malesherbes: family residence;
- 45 rue de Courcelles: family apartment after 1900;
- 102 boulevard Haussmann: Proust’s principal residence from late 1906 to 1919;
- 8 bis rue Laurent-Pichat: temporary residence in 1919;
- 44 rue Hamelin: final home, 1919–1922.

At boulevard Haussmann, Proust installed cork lining to reduce noise and dust. The “cork-lined room” is factual, but popular legend exaggerates it into complete hermetic isolation. He often worked in bed by night and slept by day, yet continued to receive visitors, dine at the Ritz, telephone, hire cars and make nocturnal expeditions.

### Rejection and self-financed publication

The manuscript was rejected or declined by several publishers. André Gide, then influential at the Nouvelle Revue Française, participated in the NRF rejection and later called it a grave error.

Through René Blum, Proust arranged publication with Bernard Grasset **at the author’s expense**. *Du côté de chez Swann* appeared on 14 November 1913. Proust also arranged or paid for promotional notices. This was a recognized but ethically ambiguous publishing practice of the period. There is no evidence that favorable notices constituted a criminal scheme; they are relevant to the management of his reputation.

The original Grasset contract and extensive publishing correspondence survive in the family archive discussed by the [BnF](https://www.bnf.fr/fr/mediatheque/gilles-pecout-president-de-la-bnf-presente-un-exceptionnel-fonds-marcel-proust).

## Alfred Agostinelli and the Albertine Cycle, 1907–1914

Proust first encountered **Alfred Agostinelli**, a young chauffeur, around 1907. Agostinelli later entered his household as secretary and companion, sometimes using the name Marcel Swann in aviation training.

Proust’s attachment became possessive and financially entangled. He supported Agostinelli, gave gifts and tried to retain or recover him after he left. Whether every later claim about surveillance, coercion or sexual arrangements is accurate cannot be settled, but Proust’s letters document jealousy and efforts to control access and movement.

Agostinelli died on 30 May 1914 when the airplane he was piloting crashed into the Mediterranean near Antibes. Proust’s grief and jealousy became major sources for the Albertine sequence. The statement “Albertine was Agostinelli” is too simple: Albertine combines transformed elements from multiple people, experiences and inventions.

## War, Publication and Recognition, 1914–1920

The First World War interrupted publication. Proust’s brother Robert served as a military surgeon. Friends including **Bertrand de Fénelon** died in combat. Parisian air raids, blackouts, military communiqués, shifting alliances and wartime social behavior entered *Le Temps retrouvé*.

Proust used the delay to enlarge the novel radically. What had been planned on a smaller scale grew into a multi-volume structure.

Gide apologized, and Proust transferred publication to the **Nouvelle Revue Française/Gallimard**. A new Gallimard edition of *Swann* and *À l’ombre des jeunes filles en fleurs* appeared in 1919.

On 10 December 1919 *À l’ombre des jeunes filles en fleurs* won the **Prix Goncourt**, defeating Roland Dorgelès’s war novel *Les Croix de bois* by six votes to four. Opposition focused on Proust’s age—forty-eight, though the prize was popularly associated with youth—his wealth, society background, alleged lobbying and the preference for his book over a combat veteran’s war narrative. Supporters defended the award on literary grounds.

The controversy is documented; claims that the result was simply “bought” remain **allegation**, not a proven bribery finding. Gallimard’s correspondence shows the immediate commercial consequences and emergency reprinting: [Sotheby’s description of the Gallimard archive](https://www.sothebys.com/en/auctions/ecatalogue/2018/livres-et-manuscrits-pf1803/lot.183.html).

Proust was appointed a **Chevalier of the Legion of Honour** in 1920.

## Sexual Networks and the 1918 Police Raid

Proust knew **Albert Le Cuziat**, a former servant in aristocratic households who operated the Hôtel Marigny, a clandestine male brothel at 11 rue de l’Arcade. Proust supplied Le Cuziat with money connected to the establishment and used his knowledge of servants, masters and sexual subcultures as social material.

On the night of 11 January 1918 police raided the establishment. A surviving police record identifies “Proust, Marcel, 46 years old, man of letters” among those present. This is **documented fact**, though compressed retellings sometimes exaggerate it into an arrest, prosecution or conviction. No evidence consulted here shows that Proust was convicted of an offense arising from the raid.

His financial support of Le Cuziat is material because it places Proust not merely as an observer but within a commercial sexual network. It does not establish that Proust owned or managed the brothel. Later accounts sometimes confuse loans, patronage and ownership.

## Final Years, 1920–1922

Proust continued expanding and correcting the novel under deteriorating health. *Le Côté de Guermantes* appeared in stages in 1920–21; *Sodome et Gomorrhe* began publication in 1921 and continued in 1922.

His working routine combined confinement with bursts of social activity. **Céleste Albaret**, his housekeeper and confidante, managed callers, messages, meals, medicines and manuscripts. Her much later memoir preserves indispensable detail but must be read as retrospective testimony shaped by loyalty and decades of recollection.

Proust used fumigations and numerous medications, including caffeine-containing stimulants and sedatives, within a medically hazardous regimen. Claims that he was an “addict” in a precise modern diagnostic sense exceed the documentary evidence. Severe asthma, insomnia, self-medication and irregular treatment are established.

### The Majestic dinner

On 18–19 May 1922 Sydney and Violet Schiff hosted a supper at the Hôtel Majestic after the premiere of Stravinsky’s *Renard*. Guests included Proust, **James Joyce**, **Pablo Picasso**, **Igor Stravinsky**, **Sergei Diaghilev** and Erik Satie.

Proust and Joyce met there for the first and apparently only time. Conflicting recollections describe a disappointing exchange—mutual statements that each had not read the other, discussion of headaches, truffles or household details. The meeting is documented; its supposedly perfect dialogue is **reported anecdote** preserved in varying versions. The event is examined in a [Princeton bibliographical account](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/odd-encounters-from-marcel-prousts-sodome-et-gomorrhe-to-albert-c/).

## Death

In autumn 1922 Proust developed a respiratory infection that progressed to pneumonia. He distrusted or resisted parts of the recommended medical treatment and continued working. His brother Robert and other doctors attended him.

Marcel Proust died at 44 rue Hamelin on 18 November 1922. Contemporary and modern medical reconstruction identifies bronchopneumonia or infectious pneumonia superimposed on chronic severe respiratory disease as the cause. Stories that he died solely because he refused treatment oversimplify a patient already affected by severe asthma and obstructive lung disease.

He was buried at Père-Lachaise on 22 November. Robert Proust became executor of the literary manuscripts.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects and Financial Interests

Proust did not found companies, develop technology, claim patents or hold major corporate office.

His material and institutional relationships included:

- inherited Weil and Proust family wealth;
- securities and investment income, with losses and anxieties but no fully reconstructable modern portfolio;
- an unpaid Bibliothèque Mazarine appointment;
- self-financing of *Du côté de chez Swann* through Grasset;
- contracts with Grasset and later Gallimard/NRF;
- payments connected to publicity;
- financial assistance, gifts or loans to friends, servants, chauffeurs and Albert Le Cuziat;
- expensive housing, taxis and automobiles, restaurant and hotel expenditure;
- the posthumous literary estate administered first by Robert Proust and later descendants.

Published anecdotes often portray him as simultaneously extravagant, confused about money and capable of minute bargaining with publishers. Correspondence supports all three tendencies, but exact lifetime net worth cannot be established from the sources consulted.

The modern value of Proust manuscripts is unrelated to his personal finances. In 2025 the BnF valued a newly surfaced family archive of more than 900 pieces at €7.7 million. The separate **Aristophil manuscript-investment fraud** involved investment products containing manuscripts by Proust and other famous figures; it occurred many decades after his death and does not implicate him. It is relevant only to the commercial afterlife of his name.

## Religious, Philosophical, Intellectual and Artistic Development

### Religion and Jewish identity

Proust was sacramentally Catholic but had a Jewish mother, Jewish relatives and direct family knowledge of Jewish burial customs. He did not become an observant Jewish religious writer. Nor did baptism erase his maternal ancestry in an antisemitic society that classified people genealogically.

Scholarship divides over his treatment of Jews:

- **Documented fact:** he defended Dreyfus and confronted antisemitism as a political and social force.
- **Documented fact:** the novel reproduces antisemitic stereotypes and slurs in character speech and sometimes in narratorial description.
- **Interpretation:** some critics identify distance from or discomfort with his Jewish inheritance, including “latent antisemitism.”
- **Competing interpretation:** other scholars argue that the polyphonic novel anatomizes rather than endorses antisemitic discourse and that Jewish memory remains structurally important.

The controversy began well before current identity debates. Siegfried van Praag criticized Proust as a witness to “dejudaized Judaism” in 1937; Hannah Arendt later used his society portrait in *The Origins of Totalitarianism*. Antoine Compagnon’s recent work challenges a simple “self-hating Jew” thesis. A useful account of the debate appears in the [2025 *New York Review of Books* review](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2025/06/26/prousts-jewish-question-maurice-samuels/).

### Philosophy and psychology

Principal intellectual concerns included:

- voluntary versus involuntary memory;
- time, habit and discontinuity of the self;
- jealousy as an engine of interpretation;
- the instability of social knowledge;
- art as recovery and transformation of experience;
- the distinction between social personality and creating self;
- perception mediated by expectation and naming.

Influences and interlocutors included Darlu, Bergson, Ruskin, Schopenhauer, French moralists, Saint-Simon, Racine, Baudelaire and contemporary psychology. His novel is not a systematic philosophical treatise and cannot be reduced to one thinker.

### Literary and artistic formation

Proust read and absorbed Homer, Racine, Madame de Sévigné, Saint-Simon, Balzac, Nerval, Baudelaire, Flaubert, George Eliot, Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Ruskin, Emerson, Stevenson and many others.

Music—especially Wagner, Beethoven, Fauré, Saint-Saëns, Franck, Debussy and Hahn—provided structural analogies. The fictional Vinteuil is composite, not a disguised single composer.

Painting and architecture enter through Vermeer, Carpaccio, Giotto, Botticelli, Whistler, Monet and Gothic cathedrals. The fictional Elstir likewise has multiple sources.

## Important Relationships and Networks

- **Jeanne Proust:** mother, linguistic collaborator, emotional center and household authority.
- **Adrien Proust:** father, physician; pressured Marcel toward a profession while financing his education and early adulthood.
- **Robert Proust:** brother, surgeon and posthumous editor.
- **Céleste Albaret:** housekeeper, gatekeeper, amanuensis-like assistant and later memoirist.
- **Reynaldo Hahn:** lover or romantic partner in the mid-1890s; lifelong friend and musical interlocutor.
- **Lucien Daudet:** intimate youthful attachment; subject of the Lorrain insinuation.
- **Alfred Agostinelli:** chauffeur, secretary and object of intense love and jealousy.
- **Bertrand de Fénelon:** close friend; his death in the First World War deeply affected Proust.
- **Robert de Montesquiou:** society guide, poet and partial source for fictional traits.
- **Comtesse Greffulhe:** aristocratic acquaintance and one source among several for aspects of the Guermantes world.
- **Geneviève Straus:** salon host, friend and correspondent.
- **Madame Arman de Caillavet and Gaston de Caillavet:** early literary and social network.
- **Anatole France:** early patron and Dreyfusard associate.
- **André Gide:** initially associated with rejection, later advocate and apologetic correspondent.
- **Gaston Gallimard:** publisher of the mature sequence.
- **Bernard Grasset:** first publisher of *Swann* under a subsidized arrangement.
- **Jacques Rivière:** NRF editor and collaborator with Robert Proust on the posthumous texts.
- **Albert Le Cuziat:** informant, borrower or financial beneficiary, and operator of the Hôtel Marigny.
- **Marie Nordlinger:** linguistic collaborator on Ruskin.
- **Louis de Robert, Robert de Billy, Antoine Bibesco and Emmanuel Bibesco:** friends, correspondents and intermediaries in literary and diplomatic society.
- **Jean Cocteau:** younger writer and admirer, sometimes subjected to Proust’s tactical mixture of praise and criticism.
- **Jean Lorrain:** hostile critic and duel opponent.

## Controversies, Allegations and Negative or Contested Material

### Paid publicity

**Documented fact:** Proust helped arrange and sometimes paid for favorable promotional material around *Swann*.  
**Unresolved interpretation:** whether this should be classified as ordinary Belle Époque publicity, covert advertising, or manipulation.  
**No finding located:** criminal fraud.

### Goncourt lobbying

**Documented fact:** Proust and his network cultivated reviewers, jurors and publishers; the 1919 award generated accusations of privilege and lobbying.  
**Allegation:** that the award was purchased or corruptly fixed.  
**Finding:** no court or documentary finding of bribery was located. Voting is recorded as six to four.

### Treatment of servants and dependents

Correspondence and memoirs show generosity through gifts and payments, but also nocturnal demands, elaborate instructions, emotional manipulation and class hierarchy. Céleste Albaret’s protective memoir should not erase other testimony about an exacting employer.

### Agostinelli and possessive conduct

**Documented fact:** Proust’s letters reveal surveillance-like inquiry, jealousy, payments and attempts to influence Agostinelli’s movement.  
**Interpretation:** biographers differ on whether this constitutes ordinary possessive jealousy, coercive control, or a relationship distorted by economic inequality.  
**No formal legal case located.**

### Hôtel Marigny

**Documented fact:** association with Le Cuziat, financial assistance, and presence during the 1918 raid.  
**Unsupported embellishment:** that Proust was convicted or publicly exposed through a major prosecution.  
**Unresolved:** the precise extent and terms of his financial stake.

### Sexual secrecy and fictional gender transformation

Proust publicly resisted insinuations about his intimate life while writing extensively about male and female homosexuality. Critics have argued that Albertine transforms male beloveds into a woman to conceal autobiography.

**Documented fact:** male biographical sources contributed to the Albertine material.  
**Interpretation, not fact:** that every female beloved is merely a cryptographic man or that the entire novel can be mechanically “decoded.”

### Antisemitism

The novel contains derogatory language and physiognomic stereotypes. It also stages the destructiveness and social opportunism of antisemitism.

**Documented fact:** Proust was an active Dreyfusard.  
**Documented fact:** objectionable stereotypes occur in the work.  
**Disputed scholarly question:** whether some belong to Proust’s own unexamined assumptions rather than solely to satirized characters.

### Colonial and racial representation

Readers have criticized the novel’s few representations of non-European people, including racialized exhibition imagery. These passages are documentary evidence of Belle Époque imperial culture and have attracted modern criticism. No evidence indicates sustained anticolonial political activity by Proust.

### Medicine and self-treatment

He frequently overrode medical advice and used complex medication combinations. Claims of hypochondria must be balanced against documented severe respiratory disease. His illness was neither wholly invented nor necessarily treated rationally.

### Dedicated 2024–2026 adversarial pass

Searches in English and French combined Proust’s name with “controversy,” “scandal,” “allegation,” “lawsuit,” “fraud,” “accusation,” “conspiracy,” “criticism,” *scandale*, *procès*, *fraude* and *complot*.

The principal recent findings were:

1. Renewed debate over Jewish identity, assimilation and alleged latent antisemitism, prompted particularly by Antoine Compagnon’s work.
2. The 2025 surfacing of a major family archive, followed by institutional authentication, counter-expertise and a BnF campaign to acquire it.
3. Proust manuscripts appearing in reporting on the Aristophil investment-fraud trial; this concerns later dealers and investors, not Proust.
4. Continuing disputes over sexuality, biographical gender substitution, translations and editorial states of posthumous volumes.
5. No credible new allegation from 2024–26 that Proust committed fraud, corruption, political violence, espionage or another serious crime.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors and Disputed Narratives

### “Proust invented the Proust Questionnaire”

- **Story:** he created the personality questionnaire bearing his name.
- **Evidence:** he answered at least two existing English-style confession albums.
- **Finding:** myth. His celebrity later attached his name to the format.
- **Effect:** turned Proust into a symbol of psychological self-revelation.

### “The madeleine reproduced one exact childhood episode”

- **Story:** a madeleine dipped in tea literally unlocked the entire novel.
- **Evidence:** manuscripts show earlier variants involving toast, stale bread or biscuit.
- **Finding:** the published scene is an artistically developed motif, not a secure autobiographical transcript.
- **Effect:** “Proustian madeleine” became shorthand for involuntary memory.

### “He never left his cork-lined room”

- **Story:** after his mother’s death he became a total hermit.
- **Evidence:** extensive correspondence, Ritz visits, automobile excursions, dinners and the 1922 Majestic supper.
- **Finding:** exaggeration built around real seclusion and illness.
- **Effect:** established the archetype of the bedridden nocturnal genius.

### “He wrote the whole novel in bed at the end of his life”

- **Story:** the work emerged in one terminal burst.
- **Evidence:** decades of preparation, *Jean Santeuil*, Ruskin, *Contre Sainte-Beuve*, notebooks and continual revision from approximately 1908.
- **Finding:** mythologized compression.

### “Every character has one real-life key”

- **Story:** Charlus equals Montesquiou, Albertine equals Agostinelli, Oriane equals Greffulhe, and so forth.
- **Evidence:** manuscripts and correspondence show compositing, redistribution and invention.
- **Finding:** misleading. Real traits were dispersed among multiple characters.
- **Effect:** fueled gossip, model-hunting and biographical tourism.

### “Proust was ashamed of being Jewish” / “Proust was simply a Jewish writer”

Both are retrospective totalizations. He was baptized Catholic, descended maternally from Jews, remembered Jewish family practices, supported Dreyfus and wrote amid pervasive antisemitism. The precise inward meaning of this inheritance remains partly inaccessible.

### “Proust and Joyce exchanged a perfect modernist dialogue”

Accounts differ over what was said at the Majestic and afterward in a taxi. The encounter occurred, but polished exchanges circulated in memoirs should be treated as anecdote.

### The deathbed proof-correction legend

Céleste Albaret and later narrators present Proust correcting the death of Bergotte while dying himself. He did work on proofs during his last illness. Exact dramatic staging varies and belongs partly to literary hagiography.

## Works by Marcel Proust

### Lifetime books and translations

1. **1896 — *Les Plaisirs et les Jours***  
   Stories, prose poems and portraits; preface by Anatole France, illustrations by Madeleine Lemaire, music by Reynaldo Hahn. Genuine authorial collection.

2. **1904 — John Ruskin, *La Bible d’Amiens***  
   French translation, introduction and notes by Proust, with acknowledged practical assistance from Jeanne Proust and others.

3. **1906 — John Ruskin, *Sésame et les lys***  
   French translation with extensive preface and notes. “Sur la lecture” derives from the preface.

4. **1913 — *Du côté de chez Swann***  
   First part of *À la recherche du temps perdu*, Grasset; reissued by Gallimard in 1919.

5. **1919 — *Pastiches et mélanges***  
   Proust-authorized collection of pastiches, essays and criticism.

6. **1919 — *À l’ombre des jeunes filles en fleurs***  
   Second part of the *Recherche*; Prix Goncourt.

7. **1920–1921 — *Le Côté de Guermantes***  
   Published in separate volumes.

8. **1921–1922 — *Sodome et Gomorrhe***  
   Publication began with material once labeled *Sodome et Gomorrhe I* and continued in volumes during Proust’s lifetime.

### Posthumous principal works

9. **1923 — *La Prisonnière (Sodome et Gomorrhe III)***  
   Posthumously edited from manuscripts by Robert Proust and Jacques Rivière. Genuine Proust writing; final form not wholly author-authorized.

10. **1925 — *Albertine disparue* / *La Fugitive***  
    Posthumous and textually unstable. A later-discovered typescript with extensive deletions reopened the question of intended form.

11. **1927 — *Le Temps retrouvé***  
    Posthumously edited. Broad architecture is Proust’s; local inconsistencies reflect unfinished revision.

12. **1927 — *Chroniques***  
    Posthumous collection of journalism and essays.

13. **1930–1936 — early correspondence collections**  
    Genuine letters, selectively edited.

14. **1952 — *Jean Santeuil***  
    Unfinished 1890s novel, assembled posthumously by Bernard de Fallois. Not a final authorial edition.

15. **1954 — *Contre Sainte-Beuve, suivi de Nouveaux mélanges***  
    Bernard de Fallois’s posthumous construction from manuscript materials. Later genetic scholarship has challenged treating it as one finished book.

16. **1971–1993 — *Correspondance de Marcel Proust*, 21 volumes, edited by Philip Kolb**  
    Monumental scholarly chronological edition. Genuine letters, editorially dated and annotated.

17. **1978 — *Essais et articles*, edited by Pierre Clarac and Yves Sandre**  
    Posthumous scholarly compilation.

18. **2019 — *Le Mystérieux Correspondant et autres nouvelles inédites*, edited by Luc Fraisse**  
    Early stories omitted from *Les Plaisirs et les Jours*. Genuine manuscripts, posthumously selected and edited.

19. **2021 — *Les Soixante-Quinze Feuillets et autres manuscrits inédits*, edited by Nathalie Mauriac Dyer**  
    Early *Recherche* materials; genuine manuscripts, not a lifetime book.

20. **2026 — *Correspondance retrouvée: cent lettres inédites*, edited through the BnF**  
    Newly recovered letters and received correspondence, including material involving Lucien Daudet, Alfred Vallette, servants and Céleste Albaret; posthumous scholarly collection. The BnF describes it at [*Marcel Proust—Cent lettres inédites*](https://editions.bnf.fr/index.php/marcel-proust-cent-lettres-inedites?page=1).

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Robert Proust and Jacques Rivière’s publication of the final volumes completed public access to the cycle but also created enduring editorial questions. Early posthumous editions normalized punctuation, repetitions and contradictions. Later Pléiade and genetic editions restored or re-evaluated manuscript states.

Proust’s international reputation expanded through translation. Charles Kenneth Scott Moncrieff’s English title, *Remembrance of Things Past*, adapted Shakespeare rather than literally translating *À la recherche du temps perdu*. Later editions increasingly use *In Search of Lost Time*. Translation of Proust’s long syntactic structures remains controversial.

Influence is documented in or strongly acknowledged by writers and thinkers including:

- Virginia Woolf;
- Samuel Beckett;
- Vladimir Nabokov;
- Walter Benjamin;
- Jorge Luis Borges;
- Claude Lévi-Strauss;
- Roland Barthes;
- Gilles Deleuze;
- W. G. Sebald;
- filmmakers including Luchino Visconti, Raúl Ruiz and Chantal Akerman.

The work became central to modernist studies, memory studies, narratology, queer studies, Jewish studies, theories of jealousy and habit, and the sociology of elites.

The popular cult condenses Proust into a madeleine, cork room and long sentence. Specialist scholarship has moved in the opposite direction: reconstruction of manuscripts, publishing strategy, sexuality, household labor, Jewish ancestry, media networks and historical politics.

Illiers became Illiers-Combray; the aunt’s house became the Maison de Tante Léonie museum. Bedrooms, furniture and cork panels became relic-like objects. This heritage industry is part archival preservation, part literary pilgrimage and part reconstruction: visitors do not encounter an untouched total environment exactly as Proust left it.

The 2020 acquisition of the *Soixante-Quinze Feuillets* and emergence of another large archive in 2024–25 demonstrate that the documentary record is not closed. The BnF reports that manuscripts and correspondence continue to alter genetic and biographical understanding.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by consulted sources are included. Mere shared subject matter or residence in Paris is excluded.

- **Captain Alfred Dreyfus — documented fact.** Proust signed the January 1898 revision petition, solicited Anatole France’s signature, attended Zola’s trial and made the Affair a major element of his fiction. No close personal friendship with Dreyfus was established here.

- **Henri-Louis Bergson — documented fact.** Bergson married Proust’s relative Louise Neuburger in January 1892; Proust served as groomsman. They corresponded intermittently, shared salons, and Proust attended lectures and annotated Bergson’s work. Direct philosophical dependence remains disputed.

- **James Augustine Aloysius Joyce — documented fact.** Proust and Joyce met once at the Hôtel Majestic supper, Paris, 18–19 May 1922. The detailed dialogue is reported in conflicting anecdotes.

- **Pablo Ruiz Picasso — documented fact.** Present with Proust at the Majestic supper of 18–19 May 1922. No deeper collaboration established.

- **Igor Fyodorovich Stravinsky — documented fact.** Proust attended the supper following the premiere of *Renard* on 18 May 1922; Stravinsky was a principal guest.

- **Sergei Pavlovich Diaghilev — documented fact.** Present at the Majestic event connected to his Ballets Russes and Stravinsky’s *Renard*. Proust had also followed Ballets Russes productions.

- **Erik Satie — documented fact.** Both attended the Majestic supper. No substantial personal relationship established.

- **Achille-Claude Debussy — documented artistic connection; personal relationship not established.** Proust admired *Pelléas et Mélisande* and repeatedly engaged Debussy’s music. The consulted evidence does not establish a close friendship.

- **Gustave Flaubert — documented influence.** Proust was an intensive reader, critic and pasticheur of Flaubert. They could not meet: Flaubert died in 1880, when Proust was eight.

- **Honoré de Balzac — documented influence.** Proust read and pastiched Balzac and developed his own multi-volume social world partly in dialogue with *La Comédie humaine*. No meeting was possible.

- **John Ruskin — documented influence and translation relationship, but no meeting.** Proust translated and annotated Ruskin and traveled to Ruskinian sites. Ruskin died in 1900; no personal encounter is established.

- **Oscar Fingal O’Flahertie Wills Wilde — reported social connection.** Both moved in overlapping Parisian and aristocratic circles, and Wilde has been proposed as one among several sources for Charlus. The sources consulted do not establish a significant sustained relationship.

- **Sarah Bernhardt — reported fact.** Proust encountered her through salon and theatrical networks and admired her art. No close collaboration established.

- **Vaslav Nijinsky — documented artistic connection, not a personal bond.** Proust attended Ballets Russes performances associated with Nijinsky’s fame; no supported intimate relationship was found.

- **Friedrich Wilhelm Richard Wagner — documented artistic influence.** Wagner’s leitmotif, operatic structures and music recur throughout the *Recherche*. No meeting was possible.

- **Friedrich Nietzsche — possible intellectual parallel, not a demonstrated personal connection.** No explicit, sufficiently documented direct relationship was found in the consulted sources; excluded as an Atlas connection beyond this caution.

- **Walter Benjamin, Roland Barthes, Gilles Deleuze, W. G. Sebald and Jorge Luis Borges — documented posthumous reception.** Each engaged Proust’s work after his death through criticism, philosophy or literature. These are influence/reception connections, not life encounters.

No documentary crossing was found with the overwhelming majority of the atlas roster. Their absence is a finding; contemporaneity, residence in the same country or general thematic resemblance was not treated as evidence.
