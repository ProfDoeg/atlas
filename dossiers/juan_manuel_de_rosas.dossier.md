# Juan Manuel José Domingo Ortiz de Rozas y López de Osornio: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier concerns Juan Manuel de Rosas (1793–1877), landowner, militia commander, governor of Buenos Aires, and principal political authority of the Argentine Confederation between 1835 and 1852.

Evidence is classified as follows:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a primary document, official record, contemporary testimony, or convergent modern scholarship.
- **Reported fact:** widely accepted in serious biographies but not independently verified here in a surviving primary record.
- **Allegation:** asserted by an identifiable contemporary or later author without sufficient evidence to treat it as established.
- **Court finding:** judgment entered by a court, regardless of questions concerning the proceeding’s independence or fairness.
- **Disputed interpretation:** historians agree substantially on the evidence but disagree about its meaning.
- **Propaganda, rumor, or myth:** politically or culturally consequential material lacking adequate factual substantiation.

The name “Argentina” is sometimes used below for readability. During most of Rosas’s career there was no consolidated Argentine national state: Buenos Aires and the other provinces constituted a shifting confederation of formally autonomous provinces.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full baptismal name:** Juan Manuel José Domingo Ortiz de Rozas y López de Osornio.

**Common public name:** Juan Manuel de Rosas. He simplified “Rozas” to “Rosas,” the form universally associated with his public life.

**Frequently used forms:**

- Juan Manuel Ortiz de Rosas
- Juan Manuel Ortiz de Rozas
- Don Juan Manuel
- General Rosas
- Brigadier General Juan Manuel de Rosas

**Titles and political designations:**

- Governor and Captain General of the Province of Buenos Aires
- Brigadier general
- “Restaurador de las Leyes” (“Restorer of the Laws”), granted after his first accession to the governorship
- “Encargado de las Relaciones Exteriores de la Confederación Argentina,” the provincial governor entrusted by other provinces with foreign relations
- “Jefe Supremo de la Confederación,” an honorific or descriptive designation used under his regime, not a constitutional national presidency
- “Héroe del Desierto,” a title arising from the 1833–1834 campaign

**Hostile names and epithets:** tyrant, dictator, “Calígula del Plata,” “Nerón argentino,” and “monstruo.” These belong principally to anti-Rosas polemic and émigré propaganda.

**Birth:** 30 March 1793, Buenos Aires, Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata.

**Death:** 14 March 1877, Southampton, Hampshire, England.

**Cause of death:** generally reported as pneumonia following exposure to cold while inspecting or working on his farm.

**Principal residences and operational bases:**

- Family house in Buenos Aires
- Rincón de López, the maternal family estancia
- Los Cerrillos, San Miguel del Monte
- Estancias and saladero properties south of Buenos Aires
- Palermo de San Benito, his governmental-residential complex outside the contemporary city
- Santos Lugares military camp
- Headquarters on the Colorado River during the 1833 expedition
- Rockstone House, Carlton Crescent, Southampton
- Burgess Street Farm, Swaythling, near Southampton

**Burial:** Southampton Old Cemetery, 1877–1989; remains repatriated to Argentina in 1989 and reinterred in the family vault in La Recoleta Cemetery, Buenos Aires.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and ancestry

Rosas was the son of:

- **León Ortiz de Rozas** (1760–1839), a Buenos Aires landowner, livestock and hide merchant, and militia officer.
- **Agustina Teresa López de Osornio** (1769–1845), member of a prominent landowning family.

His paternal grandfather, **Domingo Ortiz de Rozas y Rodillo**, came from Spain and belonged to a family tracing itself to Rozas in the Soba valley of Cantabria. His maternal grandfather, **Clemente López de Osornio**, was killed with a son during an Indigenous attack on the Rincón de López estancia in 1783.

Modern biographers emphasize Agustina’s forceful personality and authority within the household. Accounts of Juan Manuel’s break with his parents commonly describe a dispute over property or money and his decision to abandon the older spelling “Rozas.” Details vary, and hostile or psychologizing biographies have exaggerated the quarrel into evidence of lifelong filial cruelty.

Rosas came from a large family. Sources differ in counting siblings because several children died young. He was the eldest surviving son or among the eldest surviving children. The family belonged to the Buenos Aires rural and mercantile elite, though Rosas later fashioned a public identity combining elite ancestry with the manners, vocabulary, riding culture, and labor discipline of the campaign.

### Marriage

On 16 March 1813 he married **María de la Encarnación Ezcurra y Arguibel** (1795–1838).

A persistent family story says Rosas overcame his mother’s opposition by having Encarnación pretend to be pregnant. It appears repeatedly in biographies but is best classified as a **reported family anecdote**, not a securely documented episode.

Their biological children were:

- **Juan Bautista Pedro Rosas**, born 30 July 1814.
- **María Rosas**, born 26 March 1816 and dead the following day.
- **Manuela Robustiana Rosas**, “Manuelita,” born 24 May 1817.

The couple also raised **Pedro Pablo Rosas y Belgrano**, the extramarital son of Encarnación’s sister María Josefa Ezcurra and Manuel Belgrano. Rosas reportedly informed him of his parentage when he reached adulthood. Pedro retained the Rosas surname and added Belgrano.

### Encarnación Ezcurra’s political role

Encarnación was not merely a governor’s spouse. During Rosas’s absence on the 1833 campaign she organized correspondence, political pressure, street mobilization, and contact with military and popular supporters. Her activity was central to the **Revolution of the Restorers**, which displaced Governor Juan Ramón Balcarce.

She helped organize or animate the **Sociedad Popular Restauradora**. The society and its coercive offshoot or associated network, the **Mazorca**, subsequently became instruments of intimidation and violence. Historians dispute the exact institutional relationship among Rosas, Encarnación, the Sociedad, the police, and the Mazorca, but not Encarnación’s importance to the restoration of her husband’s ascendancy.

She died in Buenos Aires on 20 October 1838. Her funeral became a major display of federal political mourning.

### Manuela “Manuelita” Rosas

After Encarnación’s death, Manuelita became her father’s principal hostess and informal intermediary. Foreign diplomats, petitioners, opponents seeking clemency, and members of the federal elite approached her at Palermo. She possessed social influence but held no statutory office.

In 1852 she married **Máximo Terrero**, son of Rosas’s former business partner Juan Nepomuceno Terrero. She lived in Britain and maintained the family archive. She died in London in 1898.

### Eugenia Castro and children outside marriage

After Encarnación’s death, **Eugenia Castro**, a young woman attached to the Rosas household, became Rosas’s intimate partner or mistress. Biographical and genealogical sources attribute five children to them:

- Ángela
- Nicanora
- Joaquín
- Adrián
- Justina

The relationship was neither publicly formalized nor treated as equivalent to his marriage. Rosas did not publicly acknowledge the children in the manner expected of legitimate heirs. Later accounts range from presenting Castro as a consensual companion to emphasizing the extreme inequality of age, status, and household power. No surviving evidence reviewed here permits a confident reconstruction of her subjective freedom within the relationship.

### Incest and sexual-abuse accusations

José Rivera Indarte’s anti-Rosas literature alleged incestuous or sexual relations between Rosas and Manuelita. Later sensationalist writing has repeated or transformed the accusation.

**Evidentiary status:** **propaganda/allegation unsupported by credible evidence.** The charge appeared in an intensely polemical campaign that also used invented dialogue, sexual degradation, animal imagery, and atrocity catalogues to encourage foreign intervention and Rosas’s assassination. No reliable contemporary documentary evidence establishes incest.

This finding does not affect the separately documented relationship with Eugenia Castro.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1793–1813

Rosas grew up between Buenos Aires and the family’s rural properties. His formal education was limited. He attended a private school in Buenos Aires, commonly identified with Francisco Javier Argerich, but his formative education occurred mainly on estancias.

He learned:

- riding and livestock handling;
- branding, slaughter, salting, and hide production;
- organization of mounted labor;
- management of peons, enslaved workers, and dependents;
- frontier geography;
- negotiation and warfare involving Indigenous communities;
- the patron-client practices of the rural political world.

During the British invasions of Buenos Aires, the thirteen-year-old Rosas served in a youth or militia capacity. The precise combat role attributed to him was later enlarged by family and patriotic memory. His participation is broadly accepted; heroic accounts of decisive individual action are not substantiated.

A defining early environment was the violent southern frontier. His maternal grandfather’s death in 1783 remained part of family memory. Rosas nonetheless developed policies that alternated military destruction with trade, gifts, diplomacy, employment, and alliances with Indigenous leaders. His later policy cannot accurately be reduced either to simple extermination or to peaceful coexistence.

## Education, Formation, and Intellectual Influences

Rosas had no university education and did not belong to the lawyer-intellectual class that supplied many Unitarian leaders. His surviving papers nevertheless demonstrate administrative literacy, close attention to information, and an ability to dictate or compose detailed instructions.

Principal formative influences included:

- the hierarchical organization of the estancia;
- Spanish colonial concepts of paternal government;
- Catholic public culture;
- military command;
- the politics of reputation, loyalty, and personal service;
- the post-1810 collapse of central authority;
- recurrent rural and factional violence;
- practical relations with gauchos, militia officers, merchants, enslaved and free Afro-Porteños, and Indigenous leaders.

His governing ideal emphasized order, obedience, visible unanimity, and postponement of national constitutional organization until the provinces were pacified. Opponents regarded this as a pretext for indefinite personal rule. Rosas maintained that premature constitutional construction would renew civil war.

His writings do not reveal a systematic political philosophy comparable to that of Juan Bautista Alberdi or Domingo F. Sarmiento. His thought must be reconstructed from correspondence, official messages, decrees, and the 1834 **Carta de la Hacienda de Figueroa**.

## Early Career: Estancias, Saladeros, and Militia, 1813–1828

After marriage Rosas moved toward economic independence from his parents. He managed rural properties and formed commercial partnerships with **Juan Nepomuceno Terrero**, **Luis Dorrego**, and members of the **Anchorena** family.

### Rosas, Terrero y Compañía

The partnership operated estancias and the **Las Higueritas** saladero near Quilmes. Saladeros converted cattle into exportable products, especially hides, tallow, and salted beef or *tasajo*, much of it destined for slave societies such as Brazil and Cuba.

Rosas became a wealthy rural entrepreneur through:

- livestock ownership;
- estancia management;
- slaughter and salting;
- land acquisition or occupation;
- commercial partnerships;
- connections with Buenos Aires merchants and exporters.

He managed or owned properties including Los Cerrillos, San Martín, San Genaro/Jenaro, and El Rey; property nomenclature and ownership changed across partnerships and inheritances.

His **Instrucciones a los mayordomos de estancias**, drafted in versions from approximately 1819 to 1825, prescribed surveillance, inventories, allocation of labor, animal treatment, fire control, rationing, religious observance, and punishment. The text is important evidence of his managerial method: exhaustive information, strict hierarchy, delegated authority, and personal responsibility at every level.

### Rural militia

In the disorders of 1820 Rosas mobilized his disciplined rural workers and clients as the **Colorados del Monte**. His forces helped Governor Martín Rodríguez restore order in Buenos Aires. He received promotion and became a significant intermediary among government, landowners, rural inhabitants, and frontier groups.

Although later propaganda presented him as an elemental gaucho, Rosas was an elite proprietor who consciously mastered gaucho codes and converted rural dependence into political-military loyalty.

### Frontier diplomacy

He participated in frontier commissions and negotiated with Indigenous chiefs, including leaders associated with Catriel and Cachul. The “negocio pacífico de indios” used rations, livestock, alcohol, trade, military ranks, gifts, intelligence, and the settlement of “friendly Indians.”

The arrangement provided periods of security but also divided Indigenous coalitions and subordinated communities to Buenos Aires’s military economy.

### Political emergence

Rosas initially supported federal provincial autonomy. He opposed the centralizing constitution of 1826 and the presidency of Bernardino Rivadavia. He supported Governor **Manuel Dorrego**, a Federalist, but did not prevent Dorrego’s overthrow and execution by Juan Lavalle in December 1828.

The execution radicalized the civil conflict. Rosas allied with Santa Fe governor Estanislao López, defeated Lavalle at **Puente de Márquez** on 26 April 1829, and negotiated the **Pact of Cañuelas** and **Convention of Barracas**. Juan José Viamonte became interim governor, preparing Rosas’s accession.

## First Government of Buenos Aires, 1829–1832

Rosas became governor on 8 December 1829. The legislature conferred extraordinary faculties and awarded him the title **Restorer of the Laws**.

His first government sought:

- suppression of Unitarian military resistance;
- restoration of fiscal and administrative order;
- reinforcement of rural policing;
- alliance among Federalist provinces;
- control of political printing and public symbolism;
- concentration of foreign relations in Buenos Aires.

The **Federal Pact of 4 January 1831**, initially joining Buenos Aires, Santa Fe, and Entre Ríos, became the principal legal framework of the Confederation. Corrientes joined shortly afterward. The pact promised provincial autonomy and mutual defense but left customs revenue and foreign affairs under Buenos Aires predominance.

Rosas cultivated a political language of unanimity. Federal red, portraits, public rituals, and loyalty formulas increasingly distinguished friends from enemies. Coercion existed during the first term, although the worst urban terror occurred later.

In 1832 the legislature offered reelection without the same extraordinary authority. Rosas refused. Juan Ramón Balcarce succeeded him.

## The 1833–1834 Campaign and Return to Power

### Expedition against Indigenous peoples

Rosas led the eastern column of a multi-provincial campaign beginning in March 1833. It advanced through southern Buenos Aires toward the Colorado and Negro rivers and Choele Choel.

The campaign combined:

- attacks on settlements;
- pursuit and killing of fighting-age men;
- capture of women and children;
- seizure of livestock;
- rescue or recovery of Christian captives;
- mapping and establishment of posts;
- alliances with Indigenous auxiliaries;
- diplomatic incorporation of selected communities.

Rosas reported more than 800 Indigenous dead by September 1833; later official summaries claimed approximately 3,200 killed and about 1,200 prisoners across the campaign. Those larger totals are difficult to audit and may combine columns, later operations, or propagandistic accounting.

Rosas’s own correspondence and the campaign diary document orders or practices of killing captured fighting men while preserving women and children as prisoners. Modern scholarship therefore treats forced displacement, mass killing, family separation, and captivity as documented features.

Whether the campaign should be classified legally or analytically as **genocide** is disputed. “War,” “ethnic cleansing,” “territorial dispossession,” “massacre,” and “genocidal practice” appear in modern scholarship. The evidence establishes organized violence intended to destroy hostile military capacity, remove populations, and open territory to settler control; it does not yield a consensus on the application of the later legal category of genocide.

Rosas simultaneously maintained alliances with Catriel, Cachul, and other “friendly” groups. Describing him simply as either exterminator or protector erases this deliberate combination of alliance and destruction.

### Meeting with Charles Darwin

In August 1833 Charles Darwin visited Rosas’s camp on the Colorado River. They conversed, and Rosas furnished a passport facilitating Darwin’s passage through military posts toward Bahía Blanca and Buenos Aires.

Darwin described Rosas as a man of extraordinary influence, austere habits, horsemanship, and severe discipline. He also recorded enthusiasm around Rosas’s war against peoples described as “barbarians” and later repeated an anecdote in which a killer justified homicide because the victim had spoken disrespectfully of Rosas. The latter was hearsay, not an event Darwin witnessed.

### Revolution of the Restorers

While Rosas campaigned, Encarnación Ezcurra mobilized his supporters against Balcarce. The October 1833 Revolution of the Restorers forced Balcarce from office. Juan José Viamonte and then Manuel Vicente Maza governed amid escalating factional pressure.

The **Sociedad Popular Restauradora** emerged as an organized Rosista political association. The **Mazorca** developed as a coercive network associated with it, the police, and militant Federalists.

## Second Government and the Suma del Poder Público, 1835–1852

### Quiroga’s murder and accession

Facundo Quiroga was assassinated at Barranca Yaco on 16 February 1835. The killing produced a political crisis. No reliable evidence demonstrates that Rosas ordered Quiroga’s murder. The Reinafé brothers and their associates were prosecuted and executed; allegations of Rosas’s secret responsibility remain **unproven theories**.

The Buenos Aires legislature elected Rosas governor for five years with the **suma del poder público**—the concentration of executive, legislative, and substantial judicial authority—subject in formula to defense of Catholicism and the Federal cause.

A plebiscite reportedly produced 9,316 votes for acceptance and four against. The count is conventionally cited from official records. It demonstrates organized public ratification but cannot be treated as a modern free election: dissent operated under factional pressure, limited suffrage conventions, and an increasingly coercive political environment.

Rosas assumed office on 13 April 1835. He was repeatedly reelected and remained governor until February 1852.

### Structure of rule

Rosas was formally governor of one province. His national predominance rested on:

- Buenos Aires’s customs revenue;
- delegated control of foreign affairs;
- military power;
- alliances with provincial governors;
- correspondence and intelligence networks;
- management of appointments, subsidies, and recognition;
- suppression of Unitarian and dissident Federalist opposition.

He did not create a normal national bureaucracy, congress, or constitution. Provincial governors retained significant autonomy, but dependence on Buenos Aires varied.

### Political religion and public ritual

The regime required or strongly pressured inhabitants to display the **divisa punzó**, use Federal slogans, and participate in ceremonies. Official documents opened with formulas such as “¡Viva la Federación!” and “¡Mueran los salvajes unitarios!”

Portraits of Rosas appeared in public buildings, churches, homes, clothing, medals, and festivals. His wife’s death generated quasi-sacral public mourning. Rosas himself cultivated an image of reluctant service: resignation letters or annual offers to retire were followed by petitions for continuation.

Interpretations divide between:

- an early authoritarian popular politics based on plebiscitary mobilization;
- a republican language emptied by coercion;
- a paternalist order modeled partly on the estancia;
- a personal dictatorship sustained by genuine social coalitions.

These interpretations are not mutually exclusive.

## Economic Policy, Property, and Financial Interests

### Customs and provincial supremacy

Buenos Aires controlled the principal customs house and did not distribute its revenue according to a settled federal formula. This financed Rosas’s government and military superiority while producing recurring resentment in littoral and interior provinces.

The **Customs Law of 1835**, effective in 1836, prohibited or raised duties on selected imports. It offered protection to some provincial and Buenos Aires manufactures while retaining the export-oriented livestock economy. Revisionist historiography later portrayed it as a comprehensive nationalist-industrial program; liberal critics treated it as expedient tariff politics. The law was protectionist in important respects but did not displace dependence on customs and pastoral exports.

### Currency and public finance

Rosas converted the former National Bank into the state-controlled **Casa de Moneda**, an antecedent of the Bank of the Province of Buenos Aires. Wartime deficits were financed partly through paper issuance. Inflation, blockades, and military expenditure repeatedly strained the economy.

Payments on the Baring loan were reduced, interrupted, or renegotiated. Rosas eventually resumed partial servicing. Claims that he completely extinguished the foreign debt are inaccurate.

### Private-public boundary

Rosas remained a major proprietor while governing. Palermo became both residence, administrative center, experimental estate, and public symbol. After his fall prosecutors alleged that he had misappropriated public funds for Palermo and other private purposes.

Rosas denied stealing treasury funds and argued that expenditures had public and military functions or legislative authorization. The post-Caseros court ordered restitution and confiscation. Because the proceedings were conducted by victorious enemies, the judgments establish the legal action but do not independently settle every accounting allegation.

### Confiscations under Rosas

In September 1840 the government ordered confiscation of property belonging to persons characterized as “savage Unitarians.” Confiscation punished opponents, financed government, and redistributed or controlled resources. Application was uneven and sometimes mediated by family petitions or Manuelita’s intervention.

This was documented state economic coercion, not merely hostile legend.

## Foreign and Interprovincial Conflicts

### War with the Peru-Bolivian Confederation

Rosas’s Confederation entered war against Andrés de Santa Cruz’s Peru-Bolivian Confederation in 1837. Argentine operations were less decisive than Chilean and Peruvian campaigns. Santa Cruz’s defeat at Yungay in 1839 ended the threat without major Argentine territorial gains.

### French blockade, 1838–1840

France blockaded Buenos Aires after disputes over military-service exemptions, diplomatic privileges, and treatment of French subjects. France supported Rosas’s enemies; Rosas presented resistance as defense of sovereignty.

The blockade aggravated economic and political conflict. It coincided with:

- the southern ranchers’ revolt of 1839;
- conspiracies in Buenos Aires;
- Lavalle’s invasion;
- intensified repression.

The **Mackau-Arana Convention** of 29 October 1840 ended the blockade through mutual concessions.

### Uruguay and the siege of Montevideo

Rosas supported Uruguay’s former president **Manuel Oribe** against **Fructuoso Rivera**. After victory at Arroyo Grande in 1842, Oribe’s forces besieged Montevideo from 1843 to 1851.

Montevideo became the base of Argentine exiles, foreign volunteers, commercial interests, and European naval intervention. Rosas’s opponents described Oribe as his instrument and the siege as attempted annexation. Rosas claimed to support Uruguay’s lawful president and defend regional security.

**Giuseppe Garibaldi**, commanding naval and military forces for Montevideo’s defense, fought forces aligned with Rosas and Oribe. No personal meeting between Rosas and Garibaldi is documented.

### Anglo-French intervention and Vuelta de Obligado

Britain and France blockaded the Río de la Plata and attempted to navigate the Paraná in 1845 without Rosas’s authorization.

On 20 November 1845 Argentine forces under Lucio Norberto Mansilla resisted at **Vuelta de Obligado**, using shore batteries and chains across the river. The allied fleet broke through: tactically, Rosas’s forces lost. The expedition’s commercial and political results were poor, resistance continued, and Britain and France ultimately negotiated withdrawals.

Treaties included:

- **Arana-Southern Convention** with Britain, 1849/1850;
- **Arana-Le Prédour Convention** with France, 1850.

Revisionist memory transformed Obligado into the central symbol of Rosas’s defense of sovereignty. The underlying resistance and eventual diplomatic recognition are documented; claims that the Argentine force militarily defeated the fleet at Obligado are false.

### Paraguay

Rosas long declined formally to recognize Paraguay’s independence, treating its status as unresolved within the former viceroyalty. Recognition came only after his fall. His policy encouraged Paraguayan cooperation with his enemies.

## Repression, Political Violence, and the Mazorca

### Documented mechanisms

Rosas’s political order employed:

- press censorship;
- closure or intimidation of opposition publications;
- compulsory political formulas and emblems;
- surveillance and denunciation;
- dismissal or exclusion from employment;
- confiscation;
- exile;
- imprisonment;
- public execution after summary or politically conditioned proceedings;
- extrajudicial assault and killing by Rosista agents.

The Mazorca’s victims were beaten, stabbed, shot, beheaded, or had their throats cut. Homes were invaded and property destroyed. The greatest urban terror occurred in 1840–1842, particularly after the Lavalle invasion and during military crisis.

### Rosas’s responsibility

Direct written orders from Rosas for every Mazorca killing do not survive. That absence has allowed apologists to portray the violence as unauthorized popular excess.

The contrary evidence includes:

- perpetrators’ links to the police and Sociedad Popular Restauradora;
- the regime’s political benefit from intimidation;
- non-prosecution during the terror;
- Rosas’s centralized information and disciplinary system;
- testimony in the 1853 Mazorca proceedings;
- official and private correspondence demonstrating close monitoring of enemies.

The most supportable formulation is that Rosista state and party structures enabled and protected the terror, while individual killings cannot all be attributed to a specific personal order by Rosas.

### Death totals

José Rivera Indarte’s **Tablas de sangre** attributed more than 20,000 deaths to Rosas, sometimes including battlefield deaths, deaths throughout the civil wars, and persons for whose killing Rosas’s responsibility was doubtful or nonexistent. Rosas’s opponents circulated the figure internationally.

Later researchers produced much lower lists for executions and murders directly attributable to the regime. Exact totals remain unresolved because categories differ:

- judicial executions;
- summary military executions;
- Mazorca murders;
- battlefield deaths;
- Indigenous deaths;
- deaths by allied provincial forces;
- disappearances or undocumented killings.

Accordingly, “20,000 political murders personally ordered by Rosas” is **propagandistic and unsupported as a precise total**. That conclusion does not negate the documented terror, executions, torture, and political murder.

### The Camila O’Gorman case

In 1847–1848 **Camila O’Gorman**, a young woman from an elite Federal family, eloped with Catholic priest **Ladislao Gutiérrez**. They were captured, returned to Buenos Aires, and executed by firing squad at Santos Lugares on 18 August 1848. Camila was pregnant.

Rosas accepted responsibility in later correspondence, saying canon and civil advisers supported the death penalty and that he had ordered it without consulting others. Diplomatic representatives, clergy, Federalists, and opponents had exerted conflicting pressures.

Their execution is a documented government act and became one of the most enduring symbols of the regime’s cruelty. Claims that Camila was killed simply for romance omit the contemporary charges involving abduction, clerical violation, scandal, and false identities; those charges do not alter that the state executed a pregnant civilian for conduct that would not ordinarily be treated as a capital crime today.

## Afro-Argentines, Slavery, and Rosas

Afro-Porteño associations and many Black residents formed an important part of Rosas’s urban coalition. Rosas and Manuelita attended or patronized African “nation” festivities, and federal imagery represented Black supporters.

Reasons for support included:

- access to the governor and petitions;
- recognition of African associations;
- militia participation;
- opportunities for manumission;
- hostility between many elite Unitarians and lower-status sectors;
- incorporation into Federal public culture.

The relationship was not uniformly voluntary or universal. Afro-Argentines also experienced military recruitment, policing, economic dependence, and factional coercion. Some opposed Rosas.

Slavery still existed under Rosas. The 1813 free-womb legislation had not abolished it. Rosas signed an 1840 treaty with Britain suppressing the slave trade, but legal slavery ended nationally with the Constitution of 1853—outside Buenos Aires at first—and in Buenos Aires in 1861.

Later representations of “the Black population” as uniformly devoted to Rosas are mythologized. So are older claims that Afro-Argentines disappeared principally because Rosas used them as cannon fodder. Demographic change involved warfare, disease, poverty, intermarriage, changing census categories, and mass European immigration.

## Fall from Power, 1851–1852

Entre Ríos governor **Justo José de Urquiza**, previously a Rosas ally, issued his pronunciamiento on 1 May 1851. He accepted Rosas’s repeatedly tendered resignation from control of foreign relations and demanded constitutional organization and free river navigation.

Urquiza allied with Corrientes, Brazil, and the government in Montevideo. His **Ejército Grande** defeated Oribe’s position in Uruguay and invaded Buenos Aires.

At the **Battle of Caseros**, 3 February 1852, Rosas’s army was defeated. Rosas reportedly wrote his resignation during or immediately after the battle and sought protection at the British legation.

He embarked aboard the British vessel *Conflict*, travelled via Plymouth, and arrived in England in April 1852. He never returned to the Río de la Plata.

The fall reflected:

- provincial resentment of Buenos Aires’s customs monopoly;
- Rosas’s refusal to call a constitutional congress;
- exhaustion from regional warfare;
- Urquiza’s military and commercial interests;
- Brazilian financial and military power;
- defections within the Rosista coalition.

## Confiscation, Prosecution, and Judicial Condemnation

### Confiscation

Buenos Aires authorities confiscated Rosas’s property after Caseros. Urquiza temporarily annulled the measure in August 1852, enabling Rosas’s agent Juan Nepomuceno Terrero to sell an estancia and remit funds. After the Buenos Aires revolution of September 1852, confiscation was reinstated.

### Legislative prosecution

In 1857 the Buenos Aires legislature declared Rosas a traitor and authorized prosecution. The proceeding was openly political in origin: legislators intended both punishment and an official historical condemnation of the defeated regime.

### Death sentence

Judge **Sixto Villegas** sentenced Rosas to death on 17 April 1861, ordered restitution of property allegedly taken from individuals and the treasury, and contemplated extradition. A higher judgment in 1864 sustained his condemnation.

**Evidentiary label: court finding.** Rosas was legally convicted in absentia by Buenos Aires courts.

**Qualification:** the proceedings occurred under governments formed by his victorious enemies, were intertwined with confiscation and historical denunciation, and lacked the political independence expected of a modern transitional-justice process. This affects assessment of fairness but does not erase the existence of the judgment.

Britain did not extradite him.

---

## Exile in England, 1852–1877

Rosas initially lived at Rockstone House, Carlton Crescent, Southampton. Manuelita and Máximo Terrero later established their own household.

He eventually rented or acquired the tenancy of **Burgess Street Farm** at Swaythling. Local histories describe it as approximately 400 acres, although other accounts give smaller measures, possibly reflecting different tenancies or conversions.

He attempted to reproduce estancia discipline in an English agricultural setting:

- personally supervising labor;
- riding regularly;
- maintaining strict schedules;
- farming and raising livestock;
- corresponding extensively with Argentina and Europe.

His finances deteriorated. Confiscation deprived him of most Argentine income, farming was not consistently profitable, and he relied partly on remittances, gifts, and loans from family and former associates. Stories that he lived in absolute destitution are exaggerated, but his circumstances were markedly reduced.

His 1862 will named Lord Palmerston, or the serving British foreign secretary, as executor, explaining that Buenos Aires had confiscated his and Manuelita’s property and invoking the consideration shown him in Britain. This appointment was unusual and apparently symbolic; Palmerston did not administer the final estate.

Rosas corresponded with former allies and political adversaries, including **Bartolomé Mitre**, and commented on Argentine affairs. He defended his record and hoped for restitution or eventual vindication but organized no credible return to power.

### Religion and habits

Rosas remained publicly Catholic. His testament opened with an invocation to God and the Virgin. Accounts portray him as personally abstemious, physically disciplined, and meticulous into old age. Claims of pathological madness, sadism, or sexual degeneracy come primarily from polemical literature and retrospective amateur diagnosis.

### Death

In March 1877, after going outdoors in severe weather, he became ill with pneumonia. Manuelita attended him. He died on 14 March, sixteen days before his eighty-fourth birthday.

His reported last exchange with Manuelita—she asked how he felt and he answered, “No sé, niña”—has been transmitted as family testimony. Exact wording cannot be independently established.

He was buried in Southampton Old Cemetery. A funeral monument remained after repatriation of his remains.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Encarnación Ezcurra

Wife, closest political organizer, and intermediary. Her mobilization in 1833 was decisive for his return to supremacy.

### Manuelita Rosas

Daughter, hostess, petitioner’s intermediary, companion in exile, and guardian of the family papers.

### Juan Nepomuceno Terrero

Business partner, confidant, and later financial agent. His son Máximo married Manuelita.

### The Anchorena family

Relatives, landowners, merchants, political associates, and financial partners. Tomás Manuel de Anchorena was an important Federal statesman and supporter, though elite allies did not always agree with Rosas.

### Manuel Dorrego

Federal governor and early political associate. Rosas’s inability or unwillingness to save him from Lavalle’s firing squad became a recurrent subject of accusation. No sound evidence shows Rosas desired Dorrego’s execution.

### Estanislao López

Governor of Santa Fe and principal ally in defeating Lavalle and building the Federal Pact. Their relationship mixed cooperation with rivalry over leadership and resources.

### Facundo Quiroga

Powerful provincial Federal leader. Rosas and Quiroga cooperated but differed over constitutional timing. Rosas’s Hacienda de Figueroa letter urged delay. Claims that Rosas ordered Quiroga’s murder remain unproved.

### Manuel Vicente Maza

Legal adviser, political associate, and interim governor. Maza was assassinated in the legislature in June 1839 while his son Ramón prepared to join an anti-Rosas conspiracy. The murder’s immediate authors were Rosista militants. Whether Rosas specifically ordered it remains unproved.

### Manuel Oribe

Uruguayan ally and military commander. Oribe’s forces fought Rosas’s Argentine enemies and besieged Montevideo.

### Justo José de Urquiza

Longtime provincial ally, supplier, and commander who broke with Rosas in 1851 and defeated him at Caseros.

### Lucio Norberto Mansilla

Brother-in-law and military commander; led the defense at Vuelta de Obligado.

### Lord Palmerston and British diplomats

Diplomatic adversaries or interlocutors during the Río de la Plata conflicts; Palmerston later received Rosas’s symbolic confidence as proposed executor.

### José de San Martín

Correspondent and prestigious supporter of Rosas’s resistance to European intervention. San Martín bequeathed his curved saber to Rosas.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

Rosas’s political trajectory was not a simple movement from liberalism to dictatorship. He consistently privileged:

- provincial sovereignty over a centralized constitutional state;
- order before constitutional organization;
- personal obligations over impersonal party institutions;
- Catholic public legitimacy;
- rural-military authority;
- Buenos Aires control of customs and foreign relations.

His Federalism contained a structural contradiction. He opposed doctrinaire Unitarian centralization but used Buenos Aires’s resources to exercise national predominance. Opponents therefore called him a “unitario con divisa federal.”

His support crossed class and racial categories:

- large landowners and merchants;
- rural militia officers;
- smallholders and tenants;
- gauchos and peons;
- urban artisans;
- Afro-Porteño associations;
- allied Indigenous communities.

Support was real, but so were surveillance, dependency, ritual compulsion, and repression. Neither “popular national liberator” nor “mere oligarchic tyrant” adequately describes the coalition.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

### Established or strongly documented

- Concentration of public powers after 1835.
- Censorship and mandatory Federal political symbolism.
- Confiscation of opponents’ property.
- State-supported or state-protected political terror.
- Judicial and summary executions.
- Mazorca killings during the 1840–1842 crises.
- Execution of pregnant Camila O’Gorman and Ladislao Gutiérrez.
- Organized killing, captivity, and forced displacement during the 1833 campaign.
- Refusal to establish a national constitutional government.
- Use of Buenos Aires customs revenue to sustain provincial and confederal predominance.
- Intervention in Uruguay’s civil war.
- Legal conviction and death sentence in absentia after his fall.

### Serious but attribution-specific questions

**Manuel Vicente Maza’s murder:** committed by Rosista militants; a personal order from Rosas is unproved.

**Quiroga’s assassination:** perpetrators linked to Córdoba’s Reinafé leadership were convicted; Rosas’s secret authorship is an enduring but unsubstantiated theory.

**Every Mazorca killing personally ordered by Rosas:** not demonstrable. Institutional responsibility is much better supported than individual written authorization in each case.

**Misappropriation of public funds:** alleged by prosecutors and accepted in the post-Caseros judgment; disputed by Rosas, who invoked legislative authority and the public function of Palermo. A complete neutral audit is unavailable.

### Unsupported or propagandistic claims

- Exact personal responsibility for 20,000 political murders.
- Incest with Manuelita.
- Serving human ears or body parts at banquets.
- Universal Black enthusiasm for Rosas.
- Rosas as sole creator of Argentine national unity.
- A literal Argentine military victory at Vuelta de Obligado.
- Complete elimination of Argentina’s foreign debt.
- A fully peaceful Indigenous policy.
- A campaign aimed indiscriminately at exterminating every Indigenous person.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Tablas de sangre”

**Story:** Rosas’s government murdered more than 20,000 people and committed an enormous catalogue of atrocities.

**Appearance and circulation:** José Rivera Indarte’s writings, especially the 1843 *Rosas y sus opositores* and the material known as *Tablas de sangre*, circulated from Montevideo and internationally among anti-Rosas exiles and supporters of intervention.

**Evidence:** numerous killings were real. The aggregate mixed categories, repeated unverified reports, and assigned Rosas responsibility for deaths extending across complex civil wars.

**Effect:** supplied the classic liberal image of Rosas as a uniquely pathological tyrant and helped internationalize humanitarian arguments for intervention.

### “It is a holy action to kill Rosas”

Rivera Indarte’s literature framed assassination as morally legitimate. The phrase illustrates how atrocity reporting, political theology, and a call to violence merged in anti-Rosas propaganda.

### Incest with Manuelita

**Story:** Rosas maintained a sexual relationship with his daughter.

**Origin:** anti-Rosas sexual libel, particularly Rivera Indarte.

**Evidence:** none considered reliable.

**Effect:** contributed to the Gothic and monstrous image of Rosas and later sensational literature.

### The severed ears banquet

Stories claimed Manuelita or Rosas presented salted human ears to guests.

**Evidence:** no dependable corroboration; characteristic of atrocity folklore.

**Effect:** reinforced the regime’s association with bodily mutilation and cannibalistic symbolism.

### Rosas the gaucho

Federal iconography and later nationalism represented Rosas as the authentic gaucho ruler.

**Evidence:** he was an elite Creole landowner, not a socially subordinate gaucho. He was, however, an exceptional rider and close student of rural speech, work, and warfare.

**Effect:** enabled both popular-nationalist appropriation and elite fears of “barbarism.”

### Rosas and the “national sovereignty” cult

The memory of Vuelta de Obligado recast Rosas as the definitive defender of Argentine sovereignty.

**Evidence:** he did resist unauthorized Anglo-French navigation and secured treaties acknowledging Argentine control. The battle itself was a tactical defeat.

**Effect:** became central to twentieth-century revisionism, Peronist historical symbolism, and the establishment of 20 November as National Sovereignty Day.

### Rosas as protector of Black Argentines

**Story:** Rosas uniquely emancipated, protected, and was universally loved by Afro-Argentines.

**Evidence:** documented patronage, political participation, petitions, manumissions, and visible support; also continued slavery, coercive recruitment, surveillance, and diversity of Black political positions.

**Effect:** used both to celebrate his popular coalition and, by hostile writers, to racialize Rosismo as mob rule.

### Rosas as genocidal precursor to Julio A. Roca

Modern comparisons link the 1833 expedition to the later Conquest of the Desert.

**Evidence:** Rosas’s campaign killed, captured, displaced, and territorially subordinated Indigenous populations and opened frontier land. It also relied on enduring Indigenous alliances and negotiated settlement.

**Interpretation:** continuity with later settler expansion is documented; identity of policy, scale, state capacity, and ideological framework is disputed.

### Rosas met Karl Marx in London

A modern anecdote places Rosas and Karl Marx together in London, sometimes with a detailed conversation.

**Evidence:** no reliable diary, correspondence, visitor record, or contemporary testimony establishes a meeting.

**Status:** rumor or literary invention.

### Darwin as British spy

Some nationalist accounts describe Darwin’s scientific voyage and visit to Rosas as espionage.

**Evidence:** the *Beagle* performed British naval surveying with strategic utility, while Darwin conducted natural-historical research. No evidence reviewed here shows that Darwin personally visited Rosas as an intelligence agent.

**Status:** allegation or nationalist reinterpretation, not documented fact.

## Works by Juan Manuel de Rosas

Rosas was principally an administrator and correspondent, not a conventional book author. Much material issued under his authority was drafted collaboratively by secretaries and ministers.

### Substantially genuine works

1. **Instrucciones para la administración de estancias / Instrucciones a los mayordomos de estancias**  
   Drafted in versions around 1819–1825. First separately published posthumously or after his fall as *Administración de estancias y demás establecimientos pastoriles en la campaña de Buenos Aires* (1856). Genuine managerial text, though editions differ.

2. **Carta de la Hacienda de Figueroa a Facundo Quiroga**  
   20 December 1834. Major statement on provincial organization and the postponement of a national constitution. Genuine letter.

3. **Diario de la expedición al desierto / Diario de marcha**  
   1833–1834. An official expedition record produced under his command. It should not be treated as wholly autograph authorship.

4. **Gramática de la lengua pampa**  
   Manuscript associated with Rosas’s collection and linguistic work; posthumously edited with the dictionary in 1947. Rosas’s precise personal authorship versus compilation from Indigenous informants and assistants requires qualification.

5. **Diccionario de la lengua pampa: pampa-ranquel-araucano**  
   Manuscript bequeathed in his codicil; published with editorial work by Oscar R. Suárez Caviglia in 1947. Genuine association and ownership are clear; the collaborative conditions of knowledge production are incompletely recorded.

6. **Testamento and codicils**  
   Especially the Southampton will of 28 August 1862 and codicil of 22 April 1876. Genuine autobiographical and legal documents.

### Official messages, decrees, and diplomatic texts

These were issued in Rosas’s name but frequently drafted or revised through a governmental secretariat:

- Annual messages to the Buenos Aires legislature.
- Customs Law of 1835.
- Decree confiscating property of Unitarians, 16 September 1840.
- Diplomatic instructions and correspondence concerning France, Britain, Brazil, Paraguay, and Uruguay.
- Proclamations of the 1833 expedition.
- Communications with provincial governors and military commanders.
- Orders and correspondence on Indigenous diplomacy.
- Official statements surrounding the Mackau-Arana, Arana-Southern, and Arana-Le Prédour negotiations.

### Correspondence collections

Important published bodies include:

- *Correspondencia de Juan Manuel de Rosas*.
- *Papeles de Rosas*, edited or used by Adolfo Saldías.
- Correspondence with Encarnación Ezcurra.
- Correspondence with Facundo Quiroga.
- Correspondence with Estanislao López and provincial governors.
- Correspondence with José de San Martín.
- Correspondence with Manuelita Rosas.
- Letters written from Southampton.
- Exchanges with Bartolomé Mitre and other former opponents.

Editions should be checked against archival originals. Selections assembled by Rosista or anti-Rosista editors may omit context.

## Books and Major Works About Rosas

### Early and contemporary hostile works

- José Rivera Indarte, *Rosas y sus opositores* (1843), including the *Tablas de sangre*. Foundational anti-Rosas atrocity compilation and political pamphlet.
- Domingo F. Sarmiento, *Facundo o civilización y barbarie* (1845). Ostensibly a life of Facundo Quiroga; also a sustained attack on Rosas.
- José Mármol, *Amalia* (serialized 1851; book editions thereafter). Political novel set during Rosista terror.
- Florencio Varela, writings on Rosas’s government and the Buenos Aires frontier.
- *Causa criminal seguida contra el ex-gobernador Juan Manuel de Rosas* (1864). Printed judicial record.
- Antonino Reyes and other former officials’ memoirs and testimony.

### Foundational historical works

- Adolfo Saldías, *Historia de la Confederación Argentina* (1881–1888). Based partly on Rosas’s archive; an early major reassessment.
- Ernesto Quesada, studies of the Rosas period and Lavalle.
- José María Ramos Mejía, *Rosas y su tiempo* (1907). Influential psychological and sociological interpretation.
- Carlos Ibarguren, *Juan Manuel de Rosas: su vida, su drama, su tiempo* (1930).
- Julio Irazusta, *Vida política de Juan Manuel de Rosas a través de su correspondencia* (1941).
- Manuel Gálvez, *Vida de don Juan Manuel de Rosas* (1940). Popular sympathetic biography.
- Ernesto Celesia, *Rosas: aportes para su historia*.
- José María Rosa, numerous revisionist histories and essays.

### Modern scholarship

- John Lynch, *Argentine Dictator: Juan Manuel de Rosas, 1829–1852* (1981). Major English-language biography; emphasizes authoritarian rule and social foundations.
- Jorge Myers, *Orden y virtud: el discurso republicano en el régimen rosista* (1995). Important analysis of Rosista republican discourse.
- Ricardo Salvatore, *Wandering Paysanos: State Order and Subaltern Experience in Buenos Aires during the Rosas Era* (2003).
- Jorge Gelman and Raúl O. Fradkin, *Juan Manuel de Rosas: la construcción de un liderazgo político* (2015). Major modern synthesis situating Rosas within rural society, war, and state formation.
- Marcela Ternavasio, studies of nineteenth-century political institutions and Rosismo.
- Gabriel Di Meglio, studies of popular political participation.
- Pilar González Bernaldo de Quirós, work on sociability and political culture.
- Ariel de la Fuente, studies of Federal political culture, violence, and rural mobilization.
- Cristina Teresa González, “Relatos del terror en Buenos Aires, 1833–1842” (2006).
- Alejandro Eujanian, “El rosismo después de Rosas,” on the persistence and political uses of Rosista memory.
- Works by Silvia Ratto, Ingrid de Jong, Martha Bechis, and others on frontier and Indigenous relations.
- George Reid Andrews and later scholars of Afro-Argentine political and social history.

### Sympathetic and hostile traditions

The bibliography divides imperfectly into:

- nineteenth-century liberal denunciation;
- early archival reassessment;
- nationalist revisionism;
- Marxist and dependency-oriented reinterpretation;
- social-history studies of state formation and subaltern agency;
- recent Indigenous, racial, gender, and memory studies.

Modern scholarship generally rejects both the old portrait of an inexplicable monster ruling only by terror and the hagiographic portrait of a democratic nationalist betrayed solely by foreign interests.

## Films, Drama, Documentaries, and Archival Material

### Films and dramatic works

- **Manuelita Rosas** (1925), silent Argentine film directed by Ricardo Villarán. Historical melodrama centered on Rosas’s daughter.
- **Juan Manuel de Rosas** (1972), directed by Manuel Antín, screenplay associated with José María Rosa. A major revisionist screen portrayal.
- **Camila** (1984), directed by María Luisa Bemberg. Dramatizes Camila O’Gorman and Ladislao Gutiérrez; Rosas’s regime forms the political setting.
- **La malasangre**, play by Griselda Gambaro (1981), later staged and adapted in various media. Uses a Rosista setting to explore patriarchal and political violence.
- Numerous Argentine television documentaries and historical programs have treated Rosas, Vuelta de Obligado, Encarnación, Manuelita, the Mazorca, and Caseros. Their evidentiary quality varies substantially.

### Archival repositories

- Archivo General de la Nación, Argentina: government correspondence, police records, private collections, campaign material, and Rosas papers.
- Academia Nacional de la Historia: Rosas documentation and correspondence catalogues.
- Museo Histórico Nacional: portraits, Federal emblems, weapons, Manuela Rosas material, San Martín’s saber, and artifacts of the Confederation.
- Instituto Nacional de Investigaciones Históricas Juan Manuel de Rosas: digitized texts, publications, and documentary reproductions; institutionally associated with Rosas’s commemoration.
- Museo Histórico Sarmiento: anti-Rosas correspondence and documents relating to Sarmiento’s exile.
- Southampton Archives and Southampton Old Cemetery records: local evidence for Rosas’s exile and burial.
- Darwin Online and Cambridge University Library: Darwin’s journal and field documents recording the 1833 encounter.

Rosas carried a substantial documentary archive to Britain. Manuelita later made materials available to Adolfo Saldías; portions eventually entered Argentine collections. The archive’s survival helped make Rosas unusually accessible to later documentary reinterpretation.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate aftermath

After 1852 the victorious coalition officially condemned Rosas. His name was associated with tyranny, and public honors were removed. Trials of alleged Mazorca members and Rosas’s own prosecution sought to isolate criminal responsibility in Rosas and a defined group of agents.

At the same time, rural Federal loyalties survived. Authorities recorded people wearing red badges or shouting “¡Viva Rosas!” years after Caseros.

### Liberal national narrative

Bartolomé Mitre, Sarmiento, Mármol, and related traditions placed Rosas within a struggle between civilization and barbarism. Rosas became the negative figure against which constitutional Argentina defined itself.

This interpretation preserved essential evidence of censorship and terror but frequently racialized or dismissed the popular sectors that supported him and treated all opposition to centralizing liberal state formation as historical backwardness.

### Revisionism

Beginning in the late nineteenth century with Saldías and expanding in the 1930s–1960s, revisionists argued that Rosas:

- defended sovereignty against empire;
- preserved territorial unity;
- represented the Federal provinces and popular classes;
- protected national production;
- was slandered by victors who depended on foreign support.

Revisionism ranged from conservative nationalism to left nationalism and Peronism. It did not form a single doctrine.

### Peronist appropriation

Analogies between Rosas and Juan Domingo Perón emphasized mass loyalty, elite opposition, exile, nationalism, and control of political symbolism. Some Peronists adopted Rosas; others avoided the identification.

Militant Peronists stole San Martín’s saber from the Museo Histórico Nacional in the 1960s, treating the chain San Martín–Rosas–Perón as a symbol of national resistance.

### Repatriation

After decades of campaigning, President Carlos Menem’s government arranged the return of Rosas’s remains in 1989. The repatriation was both state reconciliation and political symbolism. His remains were placed in Recoleta Cemetery.

### Twenty-first-century interpretation

Rosas remains prominent in disputes over:

- federalism and Buenos Aires centralism;
- sovereignty and foreign intervention;
- authoritarian populism;
- Indigenous conquest;
- Afro-Argentine political participation;
- use of state history institutes;
- comparisons between nineteenth-century personalism and present governments.

Recent scholarship treats Rosismo as a durable political culture rather than merely one man’s pathology. It also gives greater attention to Indigenous victims, Black political agency, women’s political labor, public ritual, property relations, and the unstable boundary between popular mobilization and coercion.

## Atlas Connections

Only documented or well-supported connections with persons on the supplied roster are included.

### Charles Robert Darwin — documented fact

Darwin visited Rosas’s headquarters on the Colorado River in August 1833. They conversed for approximately two hours, and Rosas supplied him with a passport for movement through military posts. Darwin described Rosas in his voyage journal and correspondence.

### José de San Martín — documented fact

Rosas and San Martín corresponded during the French and Anglo-French conflicts. San Martín praised the defense of Argentine sovereignty and, in his 1844 will, bequeathed his saber to Rosas. There is no documented face-to-face meeting.

### Domingo Faustino Sarmiento — documented fact

Sarmiento became one of Rosas’s most influential intellectual enemies. *Facundo* attacked the Rosas government from Chile. Rosas twice sought Sarmiento’s extradition; the Chilean government refused. Sarmiento accompanied or publicized Urquiza’s anti-Rosas campaign and entered Buenos Aires after Caseros.

### Manuel Belgrano — documented indirect family connection

Rosas and Encarnación Ezcurra raised Pedro Pablo Rosas y Belgrano, the extramarital son of Manuel Belgrano and María Josefa Ezcurra. Rosas reportedly disclosed Pedro’s parentage when he reached adulthood. No important direct political collaboration between Rosas and Belgrano is established; Belgrano died in 1820, before Rosas’s rise to governorship.

### Juan Martín de Pueyrredón y O’Doggan — reported institutional and political connection

Both belonged to the Buenos Aires elite and participated in the political-military world created by the British invasions and independence. Pueyrredón’s relatives and political circle later intersected with Federal and anti-Federal networks. No decisive personal partnership or meeting has been established from the sources consulted, so the connection should not be enlarged beyond shared documented institutions and elite networks.

### Carlos María de Alvear — documented political-military connection

Alvear served Rosas diplomatically in the United States and represented the Confederation abroad. The relationship was institutional rather than one of close personal friendship.

### Giuseppe Maria Garibaldi — documented adversarial military connection

Garibaldi fought for besieged Montevideo against forces of Manuel Oribe and the Rosas-aligned coalition during the 1840s. No personal meeting with Rosas is documented.

### Jorge Luis Borges — documented posthumous family and literary connection

Borges descended maternally from families opposed to Rosas and repeatedly used the Rosas era, ancestral warfare, and Federal-Unitarian memory in his writing. This is posthumous reception, not a lifetime meeting.

### Juan Domingo Perón — documented posthumous political association

Peronist and nationalist currents constructed a symbolic lineage connecting San Martín, Rosas, and Perón. Perón did not know Rosas; the connection is one of later political memory.

### Cristina Elisabet Fernández de Kirchner — documented posthumous political association

Her government promoted National Sovereignty commemorations and revisionist historical institutions in which Rosas occupied a prominent place. This is a later use of Rosas’s image, not a personal connection.

### Javier Gerardo Milei — reported posthumous comparison

Contemporary historians and journalists have compared Milei’s conflict with legislatures and provinces to Rosas’s political style. The connection is interpretive and posthumous, not documentary evidence of ideological descent.

### Julio Argentino Roca — documented historical-policy comparison, no personal relationship

Roca’s 1870s Conquest of the Desert is frequently compared with Rosas’s 1833 campaign. The two men did not have a meaningful personal relationship: Roca was a child when Rosas fell. The connection concerns continuity and difference in frontier conquest.

### Karl Marx — rumor or myth

Stories circulate that Rosas met Marx in London. No reliable supporting documentation was found. Mere simultaneous residence in England does not establish contact.

No adequately supported direct connection was found with the overwhelming majority of the other rostered persons. Their contemporaneity, shared nationality, or later symbolic comparison alone was not treated as evidence.
