# Daniel Giannone: Research Dossier

## Scope, Identification, and Evidentiary Note

This dossier concerns **Daniel Darío Giannone**, the Argentine painter and textile artist born in Córdoba in 1964 who, since 2003, has worked with Leonardo Facundo “Leo” Chiachio under the collective identity **Chiachio & Giannone**.

The name is not unique. Searches also identify an American jazz drummer and educator named Dan or Daniel Giannone, a New York elections employee, and other professionals. Nothing found connects those individuals with the Argentine artist. The identification used here is confirmed by:

- The artists’ official curriculum vitae.
- Argentine government records concerning the 2019 National Visual Arts Salon.
- A 2024 Argentine trademark notice giving his full name as **Daniel Darío Giannone**.
- Museum, gallery, exhibition, and interview records consistently identifying him as born in Córdoba in 1964 and partnered professionally and personally with Leo Chiachio.

Published information about Giannone’s life before 2003 is limited. After 2003, the artists deliberately submerged individual authorship in a collective identity. Consequently, many later events can be attributed securely to **Chiachio & Giannone**, but not divided reliably between the two collaborators.

**Evidentiary labels used below**

- **Documented fact:** supported by a government record, institutional record, official curriculum vitae, artwork record, or direct recorded statement.
- **Reported fact:** stated by reputable journalism, a curator, gallery, or secondary publication but not independently documented here.
- **Self-report:** stated by Giannone or Chiachio in an interview.
- **Critical interpretation:** an interpretation advanced by a critic, curator, or scholar.
- **Allegation or theory:** a contested claim requiring proof.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story circulating without adequate documentary confirmation.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Self-report:** Giannone has said that both his parents were Italian and that his genetic ancestry was, in his description, “100% centroeuropeo.” No consulted source supplies their names, birthplaces, occupations, immigration dates, or documentary genealogy.

He also stated that both his and Chiachio’s fathers had skilled trades and fine manual dexterity. Watching their parents work with their hands became, in retrospect, part of the artists’ account of their own manual practice.

The evidence therefore supports:

- Italian immigrant or Italian-born parentage.
- Childhood in the Córdoba region that Giannone characterized as the **“pampa gringa,”** the central Argentine agricultural zone extensively shaped by European immigration.
- A household in which skilled handwork was visible.

It does **not** establish:

- The parents’ names.
- Whether both parents were born in Italy or were ethnically Italian descendants born elsewhere.
- Their exact trades.
- The date or route of their migration.

### Siblings and extended family

No reliable published information about Giannone’s siblings, grandparents, or extended relatives was found.

A potentially confusing family story belongs to Leo Chiachio rather than Giannone: the 2023 exhibition title *Comechiffones* referred partly to Chiachio’s reported Comechingón great-grandmother. Giannone explicitly clarified that he—not Chiachio—was born and raised in Córdoba, but that the documented or genetically inferred Comechingón ancestry belonged to Chiachio. It should not be reassigned to Giannone merely because Córdoba is Giannone’s birthplace.

### Partner and household

Giannone met Leo Chiachio in Buenos Aires in 2003. They became partners in life and art and have lived and worked together in Buenos Aires since that year.

Their household became the central subject of their work. Their dachshund **Piolín** appears repeatedly as a son or family member; later works and institutional descriptions refer to additional dogs. The designation “son” is the artists’ affectionate, artistic construction rather than a claim of legal human parenthood.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1964–1977

**Documented fact:** Daniel Giannone was born in Córdoba, Argentina, in 1964.

**Self-report:** His first sustained contact with visual art occurred in primary school. He attended a school run by nuns where both boys and girls were taught embroidery. He remembered embroidery alongside glitter, crêpe paper, glazed paper, tempera, and watercolor as an unusually compelling world of materials.

This experience later acquired biographical importance because embroidery became the medium through which he and Chiachio formed their collective identity. Giannone—not Chiachio—brought remembered school embroidery stitches to their first shared work.

The school’s name and the names of the nuns or teachers have not been published in the sources consulted.

Giannone later described himself as a blond, light-eyed, effeminate child who embroidered and who experienced discrimination in the immigrant society of the Córdoba agricultural region. This is a **self-report**; the precise incidents and dates are not documented independently.

## Education and Formation, 1978–Early 1990s

### Secondary education under dictatorship

Giannone entered secondary school in **1978**, during Argentina’s 1976–1983 military dictatorship.

**Self-report:** He contrasted the material freedom of primary-school art with a secondary curriculum centered on technical drawing. He remembered this as a loss of freedom and looked back nostalgically on the earlier creative environment.

In a 2023 interview he said that, as the older member of the couple, he “came out of the closet in the middle of the dictatorship.” No exact year, place, or surrounding circumstances were supplied. This is significant first-person testimony but cannot be reconstructed more precisely from available records.

No evidence found in this research connects Giannone personally to detention, disappearance, armed organizations, state security services, or political imprisonment during the dictatorship.

### University study

Giannone began a university course in **Economics** in Córdoba. One interview says that he moved to Buenos Aires halfway through the degree for employment reasons. Another profile calls him an **administrator of companies**. It is unclear whether he finished the economics or business-administration degree.

Accordingly:

- Enrollment in economics/business study: **self-reported and repeatedly reported**.
- Completion of a degree: **unresolved**.
- Institution: not named in the strongest sources; some secondary summaries refer generally to workshops at the University of Córdoba, but the precise institution and enrollment record were not established.

### Artistic education

After moving to Buenos Aires, Giannone began workshop-based art training rather than a conventional fine-arts degree.

His official CV names the following teachers:

- **Sergio Bazán**
- **Teresa Lascano**
- **Graciela Canero**
- In the Spanish short CV, **Constanza Martínez**

An earlier bilingual exhibition profile also reports that he attended workshops connected to the University of Córdoba and the Centro Cultural Ricardo Rojas and took art-history courses with **Laura Batkis** and **Hugo Petruchansky**. The dates and formal status of these courses are not given.

Giannone’s formation combined:

- Childhood embroidery.
- Economics/business training.
- Employment in administration or accounting.
- Painting workshops.
- Art-history study.
- The Buenos Aires contemporary-art scene.

He and Chiachio consistently describe themselves as **painters**, even when working in thread, textiles, porcelain, photography, or installation. Their formula is to “paint with needles and threads,” treating the stitch as a brushstroke.

## Migration to Buenos Aires and Early Career

### Move from Córdoba

Giannone moved from Córdoba to Buenos Aires around the midpoint of his economics studies for work. No exact date was found.

A 2017 account records that, on arrival in Buenos Aires, he worked as an **accountant in a company** and did not yet expect to become a professional artist. A voile curtain from his first Buenos Aires home was later incorporated into *Arqueología suave* as an object of personal memory.

This establishes a significant geographical and vocational transition:

1. Childhood and education in Córdoba.
2. Economics or business studies.
3. Employment-motivated move to Buenos Aires.
4. Accounting or company-administration employment.
5. Workshop training and painting.
6. Full artistic partnership after 2003.

No employer, salary, job dates, or corporate affiliation has been publicly identified.

### Pre-2003 artistic work

Both Giannone and Chiachio painted before forming the collective. The artists later said that they relinquished their separate prior artistic careers in favor of a new joint author.

No dependable catalogue of Giannone’s individual paintings has been located. The scarcity is partly intentional: the collective’s retrospective mythology emphasizes the birth of a new shared identity in 2003.

## Meeting Leo Chiachio and Formation of the Collective, 2003

Giannone met **Leo Chiachio** at a friend’s house in Buenos Aires in 2003. The friend has not been publicly identified in the consulted sources.

They became a couple and began living and working together that year. Curator **Ana María Battistozzi** invited them to participate in **Estudio Abierto 2003**, held in the former Harrods department store in Buenos Aires.

Their first joint work was **_Hechizo_**:

- An old quilted or padded mattress.
- Embroidered with full-length images of the two men.
- Produced in a context of post-2001 Argentine economic and institutional crisis.
- Shown at Estudio Abierto for approximately fifteen days.

The artists’ accounts supply two related origin narratives:

- Giannone remembered the embroidery stitches he had learned from nuns in primary school.
- Chiachio had received a bag containing thread and needles during the economic crisis.

They concluded from the response to *Hechizo* that their collaboration worked better than their separate practices. A gallery associated with **Sonoridad Amarilla** reportedly offered them an exhibition after seeing it.

From this point, they began signing work jointly as **Chiachio & Giannone**. They have described this not as two artists collaborating occasionally but as the creation of a third identity: a single artist made by two people.

## Chronological Life History

## 2004–2008: Embroidery, Saint Sebastian, and the Family Image

In 2004 the duo exhibited embroideries based on **Saint Sebastian**, including the series commonly called *Sebastianos*. María Gainza’s contemporaneous article situated the work in the saint’s long history as martyr, erotic male body, and modern gay icon.

Their early practice developed several lasting devices:

- Embroidered self-portraits.
- Reused domestic fabrics.
- The gay male couple represented as a family.
- Camp, kitsch, religious, erotic, indigenous, cinematic, and popular imagery.
- Extremely labor-intensive manual production.
- A refusal of the institutional hierarchy that treated painting as “fine art” and embroidery as feminine domestic craft.

Selected events:

- **2004:** Saint Sebastian embroideries received major press attention.
- **2005:** Received a Fondo Cultura B.A. subsidy; participated in *Sobre el amor y el arte contemporáneo* at Centro Cultural Borges and *Línea amarilla* at Barrio Joven arteBA.
- **2005:** *Chinitos Kids*, an embroidery on a pillowcase, demonstrated their reuse of household fabric.
- **2006–2008:** Produced *Pombero, Pomberito y Yaguareté*.
- **2007:** Major exhibition *Desborde de alegría* at Centro Cultural Recoleta, Buenos Aires.
- **2007:** The “Museo Piolín” framework placed their dog and domestic archive at the center of their mythology.
- **2008:** *Piolín’s World*, Saint-Barthélemy.

During these years the couple’s self-portrait expanded into an intentionally theatrical family portrait. They appeared as saints, children, court figures, indigenous or folkloric characters, and hybrid human-animal beings.

The Aubusson project required translation between the artists’ painted/embroidered conception and tapestry made with French artisans. The large work developed over several years and was completed in a later form around 2019.

### Arqueología suave

This project assembled embroidery, textile mosaics, porcelain, reused curtains, household material, and references to women and textile artists who had been marginalized within conventional art histories.

Figures cited or honored included:

- Anni Albers
- Sonia Delaunay
- Sophie Taeuber-Arp
- Gunta Stölzl
- Otti Berger
- Ida Kerkovius
- Elena and Victoria Izcue
- Sheila Hicks
- Benita Koch-Otte
- Ruth Holló
- Margarete Bittkow-Köhler
- Yente/Eugenia Crenovich

The project did not claim literal collaboration with these mostly earlier artists. It used quotation, appropriation, recreation, and homage to construct what curator Francisco Lemus called a “soft archive.”

In 2017 Chiachio & Giannone were finalists for the **Loewe Craft Prize**.

A structural difficulty also became public: some Argentine art prizes admitted individuals but not pairs or collectives. A 2017 critic reported that this excluded the duo from competitions, although the rule and its implementation varied by institution.

## 2024–Present

### 2024 retrospective

The Fortabat collection presented **_Vivir sus vidas: dos décadas de trayectoria de Chiachio & Giannone_**, curated by Leandro Martínez Depietri. Reporting described approximately 150 works spanning twenty years.

The retrospective organized their work through family, desire, craft, regional identity, Asian imagery, indigeneity, landscape, archives, and the relation between painting and textiles.

A critical review drew attention to unresolved tensions in the work:

- Asian or “Oriental” imagery could simultaneously critique and reproduce exoticizing spectacle.
- The recurring family self-image could be read as an expansion of kinship or as a form of collective narcissism.
- Indigenous imagery could function as counter-colonial fantasy while still being produced through constructed identities and art-historical conventions.

These are **critical interpretations**, not allegations of crime or findings of misconduct.

### Film

In 2024 Giannone and Chiachio co-directed, with filmmaker **Agustina Comedi**, the short film **_Nunca fuimos un desierto_**. The film emerged from their installation and used performers, costumes, and a transformed version of Argentina’s national folk dance, the pericón, to challenge the nineteenth-century “Conquest of the Desert” narrative of an empty, white, binary, civilized nation.

Credits reported by the Valdivia International Film Festival include:

- Directors: Agustina Comedi; Chiachio & Giannone.
- Producer: Sandra Rojas/PAN Contenidos.
- Cinematography: Benjamín Ellenberger.
- Sound: Mercedes Gaviria Jaramillo and Guido Deniro.
- Music: Violeta García.
- Editing: Ezequiel Salinas.
- Performers including David Gudiño, Daniela Díaz, Rebe López, Zezé Fassmor, Jaguar Dorado, and Cristina Coll.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Sexuality and dictatorship

Giannone’s account of coming out as gay during Argentina’s dictatorship is central to his understanding of the later work. The decision by two gay men to use embroidery—a practice socially coded as feminine—was simultaneously personal, formal, and political.

### Family

Their recurring family portrait contests the assumption that the normative family must be heterosexual, reproductive, and exclusively human. The dog-as-child device is both lived household language and deliberate iconography.

### Craft and gender

The artists reject the hierarchy that opposes:

- Fine art to craft.
- Painting to embroidery.
- Masculine production to feminine domestic labor.
- Individual genius to collective authorship.
- Professional art materials to discarded household fabric.

### Religion and sacred imagery

Their work repeatedly uses:

- Saint Sebastian.
- Holy Family imagery.
- Votive and devotional forms.
- Altars, relic-like objects, and popular saints.
- The ekeko and other Andean or folk religious imagery.

No source establishes Giannone’s personal religious affiliation or belief. Sacred forms operate principally as artistic iconography.

### Indigenous and Latin American imagery

Guaraní, Andean, Comechingón, and other indigenous references appear throughout the work, often combined with European or imperial forms. Their stated aim is not archaeological reconstruction but imaginative reconfiguration of Argentine and Latin American identity.

This practice has generated interpretive tension because Giannone identifies his own parents as Italian and his ancestry as European. The Comechingón family claim belongs to Chiachio. Their joint authorship deliberately merges the partners’ identities, but documentary biography must keep them distinct.

### Political statements

The duo has associated its work with:

- LGBTQ visibility and rights.
- Opposition to discrimination.
- Recognition of feminized labor.
- Public art education.
- Defense of public cultural institutions.
- Community participation.
- Critique of colonial narratives.
- Skepticism toward right-wing attacks on acquired social rights.

No evidence found indicates party membership, elected office, political employment, campaign finance, armed activity, or clandestine political organization.

### Artistic provocation involving national heroes

**Self-report/reported controversy:** The artists said that they “generated controversy” by treating the sexuality of national heroes and military figures irreverently. One inspiration was a subway image from the film *El santo de la espada* in which actors portraying **José de San Martín** and **Simón Bolívar** appeared, from a particular angle, to be kissing.

The resulting work played with homoerotic reinterpretation of heroic imagery. Available reporting establishes provocation and discussion, not censorship, prosecution, or a legal finding.

### Institutional exclusion of collectives

A 2017 critic reported that Argentine prize regulations often excluded the duo because only individuals could enter. This was a dispute about authorship rules, not an accusation of misconduct. Their subsequent 2019 national award demonstrates that at least one major competition recognized the collective formally.

### Orientalism

**Critical interpretation:** A 2024 retrospective review argued that their Asian imagery exists between critique and participation in exoticization: an invented Asian aesthetic may resist Western urban norms while becoming an ornamental fantasy seen through Westernized Argentine eyes.

No source found records a formal complaint, institutional sanction, or adjudication. This remains art criticism.

### Narcissism and self-representation

**Critical interpretation:** Because the artists repeatedly depict themselves and their household, critics have discussed narcissism. Some defend the practice as political family representation rather than egotism; a 2024 review suggested that the interspecies family remains structured by a narcissistic image of the couple.

This is an aesthetic debate, not a psychological diagnosis.

### Indigenous identity and appropriation

The work uses Guaraní, Andean, and Comechingón motifs. The artists distinguish between Chiachio’s reported Comechingón ancestry and Giannone’s European ancestry. No primary source found a formal accusation of falsely claiming Giannone’s own indigenous ancestry. Critical questions about appropriation and constructed identity remain possible but were not located as substantiated allegations or institutional findings.

### Commercialization

The 2024 trademark applications formalized commercial and educational uses of the collective name. No evidence indicates deceptive registration, ownership litigation, tax evasion, or improper use of public awards.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The artist with two heads and four hands”

- **Story:** Chiachio & Giannone are one artist rather than two collaborators.
- **Origin/circulation:** Repeated by the artists, galleries, curators, and journalists from at least the 2000s onward.
- **Evidence:** Their works are jointly signed; their mature production is intentionally indivisible.
- **Status:** **Self-created professional mythology**, grounded in an actual collaborative method but not a literal claim.
- **Effect:** It helped solve the artistic problem of authorship while sometimes colliding with prize rules designed for individuals.

### Birth from the post-2001 ruins

- **Story:** In a devastated Argentina, two painters could not afford paint; Chiachio received thread and needles, Giannone recovered childhood stitches, and the new artist was born.
- **Appearance:** Repeated in interviews, retrospective journalism, and curatorial accounts.
- **Evidence:** Consistent first-person testimony supports the broad sequence.
- **Uncertain elements:** The absolute claim that there was “nothing” or that inability to buy paint alone caused the transition is rhetorical and retrospective.
- **Status:** **Documented autobiographical narrative with mythic compression**.
- **Effect:** It makes material scarcity, love, recycling, and national crisis the collective’s creation story.

### The disappearance of their former selves

- **Story:** Giannone and Chiachio abandoned or dissolved their earlier identities entirely in 2003.
- **Evidence:** Their public authorship became collective, but both remained legally distinct people with separate biographies.
- **Status:** **Artistic mythology**, not literal disappearance.
- **Effect:** It has obscured Giannone’s individual pre-2003 work and made conventional single-person biography difficult.

### Piolín as son and museum founder

- **Story:** Their dog Piolín is their child and the center of a “Museo Piolín.”
- **Evidence:** Piolín was a real household animal and recurring model. The kinship and museum language are deliberately expanded artistic conventions.
- **Status:** **Documented family mythology**.
- **Effect:** It made interspecies family one of their best-known contributions to queer domestic representation.

### Comechingón ancestry confusion

- **Story:** Because Giannone is from Córdoba and the duo used *Comechiffones*, Giannone has Comechingón ancestry.
- **Evidence against:** Giannone explicitly said the Comechingón ancestry belonged to Leo Chiachio; he described his own parents as Italian.
- **Status:** **Incorrect inference**, not a supported claim.
- **Effect:** It demonstrates how completely the collective identity can blur the partners’ separate genealogies.

## Reputation, Influence, and Legacy to Date

Giannone’s individual reputation is inseparable from Chiachio & Giannone. Their established historical significance rests on:

- Making a same-sex male household the repeated subject of monumental family portraiture.
- Moving embroidery and textile mosaic into Argentine contemporary-art institutions without abandoning the artists’ self-understanding as painters.
- Joining queer representation with domestic craft, humor, camp, animal kinship, and public participation.
- Recovering or quoting women textile artists excluded from conventional modernist canons.
- Challenging individual authorship through a permanent two-person signature.
- Building international connections among Argentine, European, North American, Chinese, and Latin American institutions.
- Producing one of the most institutionally recognized Argentine textile practices of the early twenty-first century.

The **Konex de Platino**, Loewe finalist status, Aubusson prize, 2019 national acquisition prize, international residencies, museum exhibitions, and 2024 retrospective demonstrate substantial institutional recognition.

Interpretation remains divided around:

- Whether the self-portraits are expansive family politics or narcissistic repetition.
- Whether their Asian and indigenous fantasies undo colonial imagery or sometimes reproduce exoticism.
- Whether institutional acceptance has neutralized the early work’s transgressive force.
- Whether merging two artists into one collective liberates authorship or obscures unequal individual histories.

No settled posthumous reputation exists because Giannone remains alive.
