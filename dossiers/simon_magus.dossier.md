# Simon Magus: Research Dossier

## Scope and Method

Simon Magus is not recoverable as an ordinary ancient biography. No document by a known contemporary gives his birth, family, education, career, or death. The earliest surviving narrative is Acts 8:4–25, a Christian literary account whose date is disputed and which ends while Simon is alive. Beginning in the middle of the second century, apologetic and heresiological writers attach to him a birthplace, a Roman mission, a female companion, a theology, disciples, and a genealogy of heresy. A late-second-century apocryphal novel then gives him a public contest with Peter and a fatal fall while flying over Rome. Fourth-century Pseudo-Clementine novels supply parents, Alexandrian education, a relationship to John the Baptist and Dositheus, extensive journeys, crimes, and debates. These sources cannot simply be combined into a continuous factual life.

The dossier therefore uses four evidentiary labels:

- **Early textual datum:** what the earliest surviving source actually says, without assuming that its narrative is contemporaneous reportage.
- **Patristic report:** a claim made by a Christian apologist or heresiologist, usually decades or centuries after the putative lifetime.
- **Simonian material:** teaching attributed to Simon or to a later Simonian community; personal authorship is not assumed.
- **Legend or literary construction:** apocryphal, hagiographic, polemical, folkloric, or artistic material preserved because of its historical influence, not because it establishes biography.

Dates for the ancient texts are approximate and contested. Jan N. Bremmer dates Acts in this connection to about 100–120 CE or later, Justin and Irenaeus to about 150–180, the *Acts of Peter* to about 190, and the extant Pseudo-Clementine *Homilies* to the mid-fourth century. Other chronologies date Acts earlier and the *Acts of Peter* within the broader late second century. The chronological distance between a claim and Simon's supposed lifetime is material evidence.

The Greek *magos* had a wide semantic field: Persian priest, dream interpreter, astrologer, ritual expert, wise man, wonder-worker, or, in hostile usage, sorcerer and fraud. Translation as “magician” is conventional but can hide that range. The narrative in Acts already places Simon and Philip in a competition of spectacular power; later writers sharpen the accusation until “magic” becomes the defining opposite of apostolic miracle.

## Family and Ancestry

No family member is named in Acts, Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, or the *Acts of Peter*. Nothing reliable is known about Simon's ancestry, parents, siblings, marriage, or children.

The Pseudo-Clementine *Recognitions* 2.7 and *Homilies* 2.22 call his father **Antonius** and his mother **Rachel**, identify him as Samaritan, and place his origin at Gitta/Gettones. These are fourth-century novelistic details, written roughly three centuries after the putative life and unattested in earlier sources. They belong to Simon's literary biography, not to a demonstrable genealogy.

**Helena/Helen** is variously presented as companion, consort, former enslaved prostitute, embodiment of Simon's First Thought (*Ennoia*), Wisdom (*Sophia*), Mother of All, or the true Helen behind the Trojan War. No source establishes a legal marriage or children. Justin's *First Apology* 26 is the earliest extant witness: Helena traveled with Simon, had formerly been a prostitute, and was said to be his “first idea.” Irenaeus expands this into a descent-and-rescue myth. The Pseudo-Clementines turn Helena into a member of John the Baptist's circle and Simon's cosmic “Truth.” Whether a historical woman stood behind these constructions cannot be established.

## Childhood and Early Life

No recoverable evidence describes Simon's childhood.

The only geographically early datum is Acts' placement of his activity in “the city of Samaria,” a phrase whose precise referent is debated. The narrative does not name Sebaste, Neapolis, Gitta, or another settlement. Justin, writing in the mid-second century and himself from Flavia Neapolis in Samaria, names **Gitto** as Simon's native village. Later spellings include Gitta, Gittha, Gittae, and Gettones. The modern location is not securely identified.

The Pseudo-Clementine claim that Simon was the son of Antonius and Rachel and came from a village six *schoenoi* from “the city” is too late to establish childhood residence. It demonstrates that late-antique authors wanted to give the arch-heretic the conventional apparatus of a philosophical biography: parents, birthplace, formal education, master, companions, rivalry, travels, and deathward pursuit.

## Education and Formation

### Historically recoverable minimum

Acts depicts Simon as an established public specialist before Philip arrived. He had practiced magic “for a long time,” amazed the population, described himself as “someone great,” and was acclaimed by people “from the least to the greatest” as “the Power of God that is called Great.” This implies a reputation, clientele or audience, and a repertoire of performances or religious claims. Acts provides no teacher, school, books, language training, or ritual techniques.

### Pseudo-Clementine education

The *Recognitions* calls him “exceedingly well trained in Greek literature”; later summaries place this education in **Alexandria**. The *Homilies* make him a highly capable disputant, familiar with Greek mythology and philosophical argument. In their story he travels to Egypt to cultivate magic, belongs to the circle of John the Baptist, returns after John's death, temporarily accepts second place under Dositheus, and then displaces him. This is a sophisticated fourth-century account shaped by the genres of philosophical biography, religious succession narrative, and anti-heretical novel.

### Intellectual influences attributed by later sources

The anonymous *Refutation of All Heresies* Book 6—traditionally attributed to Hippolytus—preserves or paraphrases a *Great Declaration* (*Apophasis Megalē*) and accuses its author of drawing on Heraclitus. The text's cosmology also uses philosophical and physiological language familiar from Greek traditions: infinite power, fire, roots, male-female pairs, mind, thought, voice, name, reason, reflection, image, generation, and the microcosmic human. Whether Simon personally wrote it is disputed. M. David Litwa's 2024 reconstruction treats the *Declaration of Great Power* as an early-second-century Simonian work produced in Alexandria or Egypt, not as a secure autograph of the Acts figure.

## Early Career

### Public religious specialist in Samaria

Acts 8:9–11 introduces Simon before any Christian affiliation. He practices *mageia*, amazes a Samaritan city, says he is “someone great,” and receives an acclamation connecting him to God's “Great Power.” The text does not describe his income, ritual fees, property, or political office. The existence of a durable audience is explicit; the organizational form of that audience is not.

The acclamation can be read in several ways: as a divine title used by Simon's adherents; as Luke's hostile compression of a local religious claim; as language for an angelic or intermediary power; or as a literary setup for the greater power displayed by Philip. Later sources convert it into an assertion that Simon was himself the highest God.

### Encounter with Philip

In Acts 8:12–13, Philip preaches “the good news about the kingdom of God and the name of Jesus Christ.” Men and women are baptized. Simon “also believed,” is baptized, remains with Philip, and is amazed by Philip's signs and great acts of power. The grammatical statement that he believed is explicit. Later writers frequently reinterpret it as counterfeit belief because of the ensuing conflict, but Acts itself does not use the word “feigned.”

### Encounter with Peter and John

The Jerusalem apostles send Peter and John when they hear that Samaria has accepted the word of God. They pray and lay hands on the baptized, who then receive the Holy Spirit. Simon sees that the Spirit is given through apostolic laying-on of hands and offers money, asking for the same authority.

Peter replies that Simon's silver should perish with him because he thought God's gift could be acquired with money; Simon has “no part or share in this,” his heart is not right, and he is in “the gall of bitterness and the chains of wickedness.” Peter orders repentance and prayer. Simon answers: “Pray for me to the Lord, that nothing of what you have said may happen to me.” The narrative ends without reporting expulsion, apostasy, death, reconciliation, or subsequent travel.

This exchange later generated the word **simony**, but the precise object Simon sought in Acts was not an ecclesiastical office. He sought the transferable power to confer the Spirit by laying on hands. Canon law and medieval reform extended the name to buying and selling ordinations, benefices, sacraments, and other spiritual things.

## Chronological Life History

### Before the Samaritan mission: unknown date

- Simon acquires a long-standing public reputation in a Samaritan city through activities categorized by the author of Acts as magic.
- He presents himself as “someone great”; his audience calls him God's Great Power.
- Nothing establishes his age, household, wealth, education, or relations with Samaritan institutions.

### The Samaritan mission: conventionally the 30s CE, but not independently dated

- Philip arrives after persecution disperses members of the Jerusalem community.
- Simon believes Philip's proclamation, receives baptism, and attaches himself to Philip.
- Peter and John arrive from Jerusalem. Simon witnesses reception of the Spirit through their hands.
- Simon offers money for the same power and receives Peter's condemnation.
- He requests apostolic intercession. Acts ends his story here.

The conventional placement soon after Jesus' death depends on accepting Acts' narrative sequence as chronology. The episode has no independently datable administrative event.

### Claudian Rome: patristic claim, 41–54 CE

Justin Martyr states that Simon, a Samaritan from Gitto, performed mighty magic in Rome “in the reign of Claudius Caesar,” was considered a god, and received a statue on the Tiber island between two bridges inscribed *Simoni Deo Sancto*. Justin adds that almost all Samaritans and some others worshiped Simon as the first God, with Helena as his First Thought. Justin wrote approximately a century after Claudius' accession and provides no travel route, date, Roman patron, prosecution, or eyewitness chain.

The statue claim is undermined by archaeology. A base dedicated to the Sabine deity **Semo Sancus** was found on Tiber Island in 1574; the inscription *Semoni Sanco Deo Fidio* closely resembles Justin's wording. The find makes misidentification a strong explanation. It does not by itself prove that Simon never visited Rome, but it removes Justin's supposed monument as confirmation.

### Helena and the salvation myth: mid- to late second-century reports

Justin first connects Simon to Helena. Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 1.23, supplies a more developed system:

- Simon is the supreme Power who appeared in different forms: as Father among Samaritans, Son among Judeans, and Holy Spirit among other nations.
- His First Thought, Helena, generated angels and powers.
- Those powers created the world, imprisoned her through successive female bodies, and subjected her to humiliation; Helen of Troy was one incarnation.
- Simon descended to rescue her and to bring knowledge of himself.
- Salvation came by Simon's grace rather than righteous works; the world-creating angels had given the prophets and law.
- Followers possessed images of Simon as Zeus and Helena as Athena and practiced rites and magic, according to hostile heresiological description.

The report may preserve Simonian myth, but Irenaeus' theological genealogy also makes Simon the root from which all later heresies descend. Charges of sexual license and magical crime are conventional anti-heretical tropes and lack independent corroboration.

### Menander and succession

Justin identifies **Menander**, another Samaritan from Capparetaea, as Simon's disciple, active in Antioch, teaching that adherents would not die. Irenaeus and Eusebius repeat a succession Simon → Menander and then attach later movements to this heretical family tree. Menander's existence as an independent teacher is plausible, but his personal discipleship under Simon is known only through adversarial sources.

### The *Great Declaration* and Simonian intellectual history: probably early second century or later

The *Refutation* Book 6 preserves extended material from a writing it calls the *Great Declaration*. Its “Boundless Power” is likened to fire and contains six roots in three male-female pairs: Mind and Thought, Voice and Name, Reason and Reflection. The text reads Eden and the human body cosmologically; divine potential exists in each person as an image that must be actualized. It associates the “Standing One” with what has stood, stands, and will stand.

The author of the *Refutation* identifies this material with Simon. Modern conclusions differ:

1. Simon personally authored the work.
2. A disciple wrote it soon after him under his authority.
3. It is a second-century Simonian composition with no recoverable wording from the historical Simon.
4. The *Refutator* has combined sources and polemical interpretation.

Litwa argues that the *Declaration of Great Power* and *The Concept of Our Great Power* (Nag Hammadi Codex VI,4) are early-second-century Simonian works from Alexandria or Egypt. That model separates the first-century Samaritan leader from the later movement's intellectual products.

### Roman contest and legendary death: late-second-century *Acts of Peter*

The *Acts of Peter* relocates Simon into an apocryphal contest for Rome. Paul departs for Spain; Simon arrives or operates from Aricia and destabilizes the Roman church. Marcellus, a wealthy senator and benefactor, transfers loyalty and resources to Simon. Peter travels from Jerusalem to Rome after a revelation. The narrative stages escalating wonder competitions:

- a speaking dog carries Peter's challenge to Simon;
- an infant speaks;
- Simon kills or appears to kill, while Peter restores life;
- miracles expose Simon's deceptions;
- Marcellus repents and says he erected a statue to Simon “the new God”;
- Simon announces a public ascent over Rome.

At the climax Simon rises in the air above the city. Peter prays not simply that he die but that he be disabled and exposed. Simon falls on the **Via Sacra**, breaks a leg in three places, is stoned by the crowd, is carried at night to Aricia, then to Terracina to Castor, and dies while being treated or operated on by two physicians. This is the earliest extant flying-death narrative. It is literary legend, not corroborated execution or medical history.

David L. Eastman argues that this apocryphal Simon is a composite made principally from the Simon of Acts 8 and Elymas/Bar-Jesus of Acts 13. The later figure shares Elymas' characterization as a Jewish magician and false prophet, his access to a Roman ruler, confrontation before that ruler, demonic identification, obstruction of an apostolic mission, deception, and spectacular punishment. This helps explain why the apocryphal Simon differs so markedly from Acts' baptized Samaritan.

### Variant Roman death: later *Acts of Peter and Paul* and Passion traditions

The later *Acts of Peter and Paul* places Peter, Paul, Simon, and Nero together. Simon orders a wooden tower, flies, and is dropped when Peter commands the supporting angels or demons to release him. He falls and is divided into four parts. Nero retains the corpse for three days to test Simon's claim that he will rise. Peter and Paul are then imprisoned and martyred.

Other versions vary the height, place, bodily injuries, apostolic participants, and immediacy of death. Cyril of Jerusalem reports Simon traveling through the air in a demon-drawn chariot and falling as a mangled corpse through the prayer of Peter and Paul. The *Golden Legend* synthesizes late antique traditions and made the contest one of medieval Europe's most familiar images of defeated magic.

### Alternative burial-resurrection death

A different tradition, reported in the *Refutation*, has Simon order his disciples to bury him in a trench or grave after promising to rise on the third day; he never emerges. This is structurally a failed imitation of Jesus' resurrection. It cannot be reconciled with the flying death and has no historical confirmation.

### Pseudo-Clementine itinerary: fourth-century literary biography

The *Homilies* and *Recognitions* create an extensive eastern Mediterranean career. Their precise recensional history is complex: the extant forms are generally dated to the fourth century, draw on an earlier “basic writing,” and survive in Greek, Rufinus' Latin translation, and early Syriac witnesses. Within the novels:

- Simon is born at Gitta to Antonius and Rachel.
- He receives Greek education and becomes a formidable dialectician.
- He associates with John the Baptist's circle of thirty, with Helena among them.
- While Simon is in Egypt studying magic, John dies and Dositheus takes leadership.
- Simon returns, accepts second rank, challenges Dositheus, and takes control.
- He travels with Helena and former associates, including Aquila and Nicetas before their defection.
- Peter pursues and debates him through Caesarea, Tyre, Sidon, Berytus, Tripolis, Laodicea, and Antioch in the narrative geography.
- Simon argues about God, creation, evil, prophecy, law, visions, resurrection, and religious authority.
- He transforms the face of Faustinianus to resemble his own, trying to divert imperial pursuit; Peter reverses or exploits the transformation.

These works preserve important fourth-century debates about revelation, law, philosophical education, and authority. They are not usable as a travel diary for a first-century Simon.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Ancient categories do not map neatly onto companies or technologies. No company, landholding, investment, salary, inheritance, office, or financial account is documented.

### Movement and organization

- **Samaritan following:** Acts attests a broad attentive public but not a formally named sect.
- **Simonians:** Justin, Irenaeus, Origen, Hippolytus/Refutator, Epiphanius, and others report a movement bearing Simon's name. The continuity between the Acts audience and second-century Simonians is plausible but unproved.
- **Disciples:** Menander is the principal named successor in patristic sources. Dositheus is teacher, rival, or predecessor in later sources. Helena occupies a unique mythic and ritual role.
- **Images and cult objects:** Irenaeus reports images of Simon modeled as Zeus and Helena as Athena. The evidence comes from an adversary; no securely identified Simonian cult image survives.
- **Books:** The *Great Declaration* represents a substantial theological project of a Simonian intellectual community if Litwa's attribution is accepted.

### Techniques called magic

Later adversarial catalogs attribute exorcisms, incantations, love magic, dream sending, familiar spirits, animation or creation of a human from air, transformations, invisibility, flight, necromancy, and deceptive resurrections to Simon or his followers. The Pseudo-Clementines accuse him of killing a boy and using the separated soul as a magical assistant. These are literary accusations, not documented crimes or technical records.

### Money

The single early financial act is Simon's offer of money in Acts. No amount or transaction is recorded; Peter refuses it. Later authors make wealth and patronage narratively important through Marcellus, but the senator and his transfer of patronage belong to the *Acts of Peter*. There is no evidence of fraud proceeds, money laundering, property acquisition, or Roman financial structures.

## Political / Religious / Intellectual Development

### From local power to baptized adherent

Acts presents a transition from celebrated Samaritan wonder-worker to baptized follower of Philip. The conflict is not over doctrine but over the acquisition and control of charismatic power. The arrival of Jerusalem apostles also places the episode within a jurisdictional question: Samaritans have accepted the message through Philip, yet reception of the Spirit is narrated through Peter and John.

### From failed purchaser to arch-heretic

Irenaeus converts the unresolved Acts episode into a definitive apostasy. Simon allegedly intensifies his magic, contends with the apostles, teaches a rival salvation, and becomes the origin of heresy. Eusebius later repeats this as ecclesiastical history. This genealogy served an institutional purpose: diversity could be represented as a single corrupt succession beginning immediately after the apostles, while orthodoxy retained apostolic succession.

### Was Simon the “first Gnostic”?

The ancient label and modern category must be separated.

- Irenaeus and later heresiologists treat Simon as progenitor of schools now grouped under “Gnosticism.”
- The Helena-Ennoia myth includes themes often called gnostic: a transcendent divine source, lower creators, a trapped feminine divine thought, hostile cosmic powers, descent of a revealer, salvation through recognition, and rejection or demotion of the law-giving angels.
- Acts itself contains none of this cosmology.
- The *Great Declaration* is philosophically elaborate but cannot securely be assigned to Simon personally.
- “Gnosticism” is a modern umbrella category whose coherence and boundaries remain disputed.

Stephen Haar concluded that no single answer to “first Gnostic?” is possible. Litwa's 2024 work reconstructs a real second- and third-century Simonian Christian movement but treats its own literature separately from the first-century founder. The most defensible formulation is: later Simonians developed teachings that can be compared with other second-century gnostic currents; the historical Simon cannot be proved to have originated “Gnosticism.”

### Simonian theology in the heresiologists

The following cluster is principally from Irenaeus and the *Refutation*:

- an ultimate divine Power beyond the world;
- Simon as manifestation, revealer, or embodiment of that Power;
- Ennoia/Helena as first divine Thought and Mother of All;
- angels and powers generated through Ennoia;
- creation and misrule of the world by lower powers;
- Ennoia's captivity through successive female forms;
- Simon's descent to rescue her and awaken humans;
- salvation by grace or recognition rather than observance of rules produced by lower angels;
- docetic language: Simon appeared in different forms and only seemed to suffer;
- the “Standing One,” the power that has stood, stands, and will stand;
- divine fire and six paired roots in the *Great Declaration*.

The degree to which these elements belonged to one system, different Simonian communities, or heresiological synthesis is unresolved.

### Law, works, and antinomian accusation

Irenaeus alleges that followers regarded ordinary conduct as morally indifferent because salvation came through Simon's grace and not works. He then accuses them of sexual license. Such charges recur against many rival groups and require corroboration that is absent here. They document what Catholic heresiologists said about Simonians, not proven practice.

The Pseudo-Clementines instead use Simon to contest the authority of scripture, prophecy, visions, and Mosaic law. Simon sometimes voices arguments reminiscent of Pauline claims about revelation and law. This produced the modern theory that “Simon” functions partly as a cipher for Paul. That literary function does not establish that the historical Simon and Paul were the same person.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Attempt to buy spiritual authority

**Documented within the earliest narrative:** Acts says Simon offered money for the power to confer the Holy Spirit. It does not say payment changed hands, name an amount, or show a completed fraud. Peter rejects and condemns the proposal. Later canon law turned this into the archetype of trafficking in spiritual goods.

### Fraud and deceptive wonders

**Patristic allegation:** Irenaeus says Simon treated apostolic miracles as a superior magic and competed by cultivating magical arts. **Legend:** the *Acts of Peter* and Pseudo-Clementines depict staged deaths, false resurrections, transformations, demonic flight, and manufactured apparitions. No independent victim statement, judicial proceeding, or material evidence survives.

### Murder of a boy

**Pseudo-Clementine allegation/legend:** former associates say Simon admitted extracting a child's soul through incantations to create a familiar spirit and kept the child's image in his bedroom. This appears centuries late in a hostile religious novel. It is not a documented homicide.

### Sexual exploitation and prostitution

**Patristic report:** Justin says Helena had formerly been a prostitute. Irenaeus locates her in a brothel at Tyre and portrays her as the degraded divine Thought. **Heresiological allegation:** Simonian salvation supposedly licensed sexual conduct. None of this is independently documented. The rescue of a divine woman from a brothel may be both mythic narrative and social polemic.

### Self-deification and imposture

**Acts:** Simon says he is “someone great”; the crowd supplies the acclamation. **Justin and Irenaeus:** Simon claims or receives worship as first God, Father, Son, Spirit, and redeemer. **Apocrypha:** he claims to be Christ and promises ascension or resurrection. The escalation across sources is traceable; Simon's own wording is not.

### Political access

The *Acts of Peter and Paul* makes Simon an intimate of Nero. This is legend. No Roman historian, inscription, rescript, trial record, or contemporary Roman source links Simon to Claudius or Nero.

### The Josephus identification

Josephus, *Jewish Antiquities* 20.142, describes a Jewish Cypriot magician associated with the procurator Felix who persuades Drusilla to leave Azizus and marry Felix. Greek manuscript traditions call this figure Atomus or Atomos; some Latin manuscripts have Simon. Hans Waitz and others proposed identification with Simon Magus.

Evidence against identity is substantial: Josephus' figure is Jewish and Cypriot, while Christian tradition makes Simon Samaritan and from Gitta; he works for Felix in the 50s rather than appearing as an independent religious leader; and the name varies textually. The identification remains an unresolved minority hypothesis, not a fact.

### No surviving investigations or convictions

There is no documented arrest, indictment, civil suit, ecclesiastical trial, conviction, imprisonment, exile, tax record, or confiscation relating to Simon. Apocryphal scenes before Nero are literary trials. Castor's exile “on accusations of sorcery” belongs to the same fictional world.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. Simon's statue on Tiber Island

1. **Story:** Romans honored Simon as a god with a statue inscribed “To Simon the Holy God.”
2. **Appearance:** Justin, *First Apology* 26, mid-second century; repeated by Irenaeus, Tertullian, Eusebius, and others.
3. **Circulation:** Christian apologists and heresiologists used it to contrast Roman tolerance of a magical impostor with persecution of Christians.
4. **Evidence:** a statue base found on Tiber Island in 1574 was dedicated to **Semo Sancus Dius Fidius**, a Sabine deity. The inscription and location strongly suggest Justin misread or misremembered this cult monument. No Simon statue is securely identified.
5. **Effect:** the claim gave Simon a Roman cult, imperial-scale fame, and apparent archaeological reality; it also entered later accounts as proof of pagan Rome's credulity.

### 2. Helena as fallen and rescued Ennoia

1. **Story:** Simon's first Thought created angels, was imprisoned by them in body after body, appeared as Helen of Troy, finally became a prostitute or enslaved woman at Tyre, and was rescued by Simon.
2. **Appearance:** kernel in Justin; developed in Irenaeus 1.23; elaborated by later heresiologists and Pseudo-Clementines.
3. **Circulation:** Simonians may have told a version; all extant descriptions are mediated by opponents except material scholars reconstruct as Simonian.
4. **Evidence:** no independent biography of Helena survives. The myth's internal structure connects Hellenic epic, Wisdom speculation, divine descent, sexual captivity, and salvation.
5. **Effect:** Helena became at once prostitute, goddess, Sophia, Helen of Troy, and proof in hostile writing of Simon's sexual and theological corruption.

### 3. The flight and fall over Rome

1. **Story:** Simon rises over Rome by magic or demons; Peter, sometimes with Paul, prays; the sustaining spirits release him; he falls, is broken, and dies.
2. **Appearance:** earliest full form in the late-second-century *Acts of Peter*; variants in *Acts of Peter and Paul*, Cyril, apostolic passions, medieval sermons, and the *Golden Legend*.
3. **Circulation:** apocryphal storytellers, preachers, hagiographers, artists, and Roman pilgrimage tradition.
4. **Evidence:** absent from Acts, Justin, and Irenaeus; mutually inconsistent death details; no Roman record. It is legend.
5. **Effect:** the falling magician became Simon's dominant visual identity and a proof-image of apostolic miracle defeating demonic magic.

### 4. Failed resurrection by burial

1. **Story:** Simon orders burial and promises to rise after three days but remains dead.
2. **Appearance:** the *Refutation of All Heresies*.
3. **Circulation:** heresiological tradition and modern retellings.
4. **Evidence:** contradicts the flying-death traditions and lacks corroboration.
5. **Effect:** casts Simon as a failed counterfeit Christ.

### 5. Simon as father of all heresy

1. **Story:** every later Christian deviation descends genealogically from Simon through Menander and subsequent teachers.
2. **Appearance:** Irenaeus' heresiology; systematized by later catalogues and Eusebius.
3. **Circulation:** Catholic and Orthodox heresiology, canon law, sermons, histories, and art.
4. **Evidence:** Acts documents no later school or doctrine; second-century sources attest people called Simonians but do not prove that all “gnostic” movements derived from one founder.
5. **Effect:** Simon became the anti-apostle: Peter anchors legitimate succession, Simon illegitimate succession.

### 6. Simon as Paul in disguise

1. **Story:** Pseudo-Clementine attacks on Simon encode an attack on Paul; stronger versions identify the historical Simon with Paul and treat canonical rehabilitation of Paul as a cover-up.
2. **Appearance:** modern critical discussion associated especially with Ferdinand Christian Baur and the Tübingen school, developed in later radical reconstructions.
3. **Circulation:** nineteenth-century scholarship, modern mythicist and revisionist literature, internet debate.
4. **Evidence:** Pseudo-Clementine Simon claims superior visionary revelation over eyewitness knowledge and opposes law-observant Peter—clear Pauline resonances. Against literal identity: Simon also holds numerous doctrines never held by Paul, Acts distinguishes Simon and Saul, the Pseudo-Clementines are late composite novels, and the cipher can be literary rather than biographical.
5. **Effect:** the theory made Simon central to reconstructions of Petrine-Pauline conflict and turned “the first heretic” into a mask for the canonical apostle to the gentiles.

### 7. Simon as Marcion or a composite rival

Claims that Simon is a disguise for Marcion or another second-century teacher exist but lack consensus. A more constrained literary-composite theory has stronger textual support: Eastman argues that the Simon of the apocryphal acts combines Acts' Simon with Elymas/Bar-Jesus. This explains features without requiring secret historical identity.

### 8. Simon the Druid and Mog Ruith

Medieval Irish tradition connects Simon with the druid **Mog Ruith**, sometimes making the druid a student or associate who helped construct Simon's flying apparatus. Latin *magus* could designate a magician or druid, facilitating the transfer. The story localizes the universal Christian arch-magician within Irish learned tradition; it does not document a journey by Simon to Ireland.

### 9. Simon as prototype of Antichrist, Muhammad, and Faust

Late antique and medieval polemic treated Simon as an Antichrist-like false ascender. Some medieval anti-Islamic writers compared Muhammad's ascension traditions to Simon's failed flight. Later scholarship and popular writing have proposed Simon as an ancestor or partial prototype of Faust: scholar-magician, pact-like demonic power, public wonders, pride, and catastrophic fall. Direct literary dependence of the *Faustbuch* on a Simon text is not established.

### 10. Peter's knee-prints and the place of the fall

Santa Francesca Romana in Rome preserves or displayed a stone said to bear the knee impressions of Peter and Paul as they prayed against Simon. The material object belongs to pilgrimage and sacred topography; it does not authenticate the aerial contest. The tradition fixed a mobile apocryphal story to a physical Roman location.

## Works by or Attributed to Simon

### Secure works

None.

### *Apophasis Megalē* — *Great Declaration*, *Great Pronouncement*, or *Declaration of Great Power*

- **Original language:** Greek.
- **Date:** disputed; often assigned to a later Simonian milieu, with a possible early-second-century date.
- **Survival:** fragments or extensive excerpts embedded in *Refutation of All Heresies* 6.9–18.
- **Attribution:** the *Refutator* treats it as Simon's or Simonian. Personal authorship by the first-century Acts figure is unproved.
- **Content:** infinite Great Power; intelligible fire; six roots in male-female pairs; divine image and actuality; cosmological interpretation of Genesis and human anatomy; the “Standing One.”
- **Modern assessment:** central evidence for Simonian theology, but not a safe verbatim record of Simon's teaching.

### *The Concept of Our Great Power* (*NHC* VI,4)

- **Original language:** probably Greek; survives in Coptic in Nag Hammadi Codex VI.
- **Date:** usually second or third century; Litwa argues for an early-second-century Simonian work from Alexandria or Egypt.
- **Attribution:** not transmitted under Simon's personal name. It is included here only as a proposed Simonian communal work, not as Simon's book.

### Other alleged lost titles

Modern reference works sometimes list *The Four Quarters of the World* and *The Sermons of the Refuter*. Their ancient attestation and exact original titles are uncertain in readily verifiable sources. They should not be treated as a secure Simonian bibliography without a critical citation to the underlying patristic text.

### Speeches and sayings

Acts, Irenaeus, the *Refutation*, *Acts of Peter*, *Acts of Peter and Paul*, and Pseudo-Clementines place speeches in Simon's mouth. These are quotations only in the literary sense appropriate to their authors' narratives. None can be certified as a transcript.

## Books and Major Works About Simon

### Primary and near-primary ancient sources

- **Acts of the Apostles 8:4–25** — earliest surviving narrative; baptism, offer of money, Peter's rebuke.
- **Justin Martyr, *First Apology* 26 and 56; *Dialogue with Trypho* 120** — Gitta, Claudian Rome, statue, Helena, worship, Menander.
- **Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 1.23; 4.33** — expanded biography, Helena myth, doctrines, practices, genealogy of heresy.
- **Epistula Apostolorum** 1, 7 — Simon and Cerinthus as false apostles.
- **Anonymous/Pseudo-Hippolytus, *Refutation of All Heresies* 6** — *Great Declaration*, Simonian cosmology, Rome, alternative burial death.
- **Tertullian, *Apology* 13; *On Idolatry* 9; Pseudo-Tertullian, *Against All Heresies* 1** — statue, expulsion, doctrinal epitome.
- **Origen, *Against Celsus* 1.57; 6.11, 62** — Simon as failed divine claimant; very small surviving Simonian/Helenian presence.
- **Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 2.13–14; 3.26** — synthesis of Justin and earlier heresiology; Simon as author of heresy; Menander.
- **Cyril of Jerusalem, *Catechetical Lecture* 6.14–15** — inventor of heresy; demon-carried flight and fall through Peter and Paul's prayer.
- **Epiphanius, *Panarion* 21** — late-antique compilation of Simonian doctrine and practice.
- **Apostolic Constitutions** 6 — developed heresiological narrative.
- **Pseudo-Clementine *Homilies* and *Recognitions*** — extensive late-antique philosophical novel of Peter's pursuit and debates with Simon.
- ***Acts of Peter*** — first full Roman flight-and-fall story.
- ***Acts of Peter and Paul* / Passion of Peter and Paul** — Nero, tower flight, death, apostolic martyrdom.
- **Jacobus de Voragine, *Golden Legend*, feast of Peter and Paul** — influential medieval synthesis.

- Ferdinand Christian Baur, *Die christliche Gnosis, oder die christliche Religions-Philosophie in ihrer geschichtlichen Entwicklung* (1835). Foundational Tübingen-school context for Simon/Paul interpretation.

## Films, Documentaries, Interviews, and Archival Material

There is no known contemporary visual representation, autograph, documentary film based on archival footage, or securely identified archaeological artifact belonging to Simon.

### Textual and manuscript archives

- **Codex Vercellensis 158:** principal Latin witness to the *Actus Petri cum Simone* section of the *Acts of Peter*.
- **British Library Add MS 12150 (dated 411 CE):** early Syriac witness containing Pseudo-Clementine material.
- **Nag Hammadi Codex VI:** contains *The Concept of Our Great Power*, proposed by Litwa as Simonian.
- **Patristic manuscript traditions:** Greek and Latin witnesses of Justin, Irenaeus, the *Refutation*, Eusebius, and the Pseudo-Clementines; none is an autograph.
- **Semo Sancus base:** archaeological inscription from Tiber Island, discovered in 1574, relevant because it probably explains Justin's statue claim.

### Recorded scholarship

- M. David Litwa, “Secrets of Simon Magus,” MythVision interview/video. Useful as a modern scholar's oral presentation; it is secondary commentary rather than evidence.
- Early Christian Texts, “Simon Magus: Villain or Simply Misunderstood?” interview with Benjamin De Vos (2026), focused on Pseudo-Clementine characterization.

### Literary and dramatic afterlives

- Dante Alighieri, *Inferno* 19 (c. 1308–1321): invokes “Simon Magus” and his followers at the entrance to the bolgia of simoniacs.
- Danilo Kiš, “Simon Magus,” in *The Encyclopedia of the Dead* (1983): modern fictional reversal and doubling of Simon's legendary deaths.
- Richard L. Tierney, Simon of Gitta stories, collected in *The Scroll of Thoth* (1997) and expanded as *Sorcery Against Caesar* (2020): sword-and-sorcery/Cthulhu reimagining.
- The Faust tradition is often compared to Simon's legend, but a direct line of textual descent remains unproved.

## Visual and Artistic Reception

The fall supplied a durable iconographic program because it staged an immediately readable vertical opposition: Simon aloft by false power, Peter or Peter and Paul grounded in prayer, then Simon inverted and falling.

Important examples and sites include:

- Romanesque sculpture at Saint-Sernin, Toulouse, and Autun, where Simon's fall entered monumental church programs.
- Guido di Graziano, *Simon Magus Plummeting to Earth* (1280s), Pinacoteca Nazionale, Siena.
- Benozzo Gozzoli's Petrine cycle in the Camposanto, Pisa, including the contest with Simon (fifteenth century; damaged in World War II and restored).
- Filippino Lippi, *The Crucifixion of Saint Peter and the Dispute with Simon Magus*, Brancacci Chapel, Florence (completed in the 1480s), joining the magical contest to Peter's martyrdom.
- Francesco Solimena, *Fall of Simon Magus* (1689–1690), San Paolo Maggiore, Naples, deliberately paired with the fall/conversion of Paul.
- Pompeo Batoni and studio, *The Fall of Simon Magus* (c. 1745–1750), designed in relation to a mosaic altarpiece for St. Peter's; a version is held by the Cleveland Museum of Art.
- Santa Francesca Romana, Rome: local tradition of the apostles' knee-marked stone.

These works are evidence for reception and ecclesial memory, not for the occurrence of the flight.

## Death / Later Life / Present Status

### What can be stated

Simon is dead if he was a first-century historical person. His date, place, and cause of death are unknown. Acts' last scene leaves him alive and asking Peter to pray for him.

### Incompatible death traditions

| Tradition | Place | Cause | Source/status |
|---|---|---|---|
| No death narrated | Samaria | Not applicable | Acts 8; earliest account |
| Buried alive, fails to rise on third day | Unspecified | Suffocation/burial implied | *Refutation*; anti-resurrection legend |
| Falls over Rome; leg broken in three places; later dies under physicians | Via Sacra → Aricia → Terracina | Injuries and treatment | *Acts of Peter*; late-second-century legend |
| Falls from tower, body divided into four parts | Rome before Nero | Catastrophic fall | *Acts of Peter and Paul*; later legend |
| Falls mangled from demon-drawn aerial chariot | Rome | Fall after apostolic prayer | Cyril of Jerusalem; fourth-century retelling |

No version has independent corroboration. The flight narrative should not be assigned a calendar date under Nero as if it were a Roman execution record.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Simony

Simon's most concrete institutional legacy is lexical. “Simony” came to mean the purchase or sale of spiritual things and offices. The offense became central to canon law, the eleventh-century reform movement, disputes over ordination and benefices, and later civil ecclesiastical law. The semantic expansion went far beyond Simon's request in Acts.

### Heresiological genealogy

Irenaeus' placement of Simon at the head of an anti-apostolic succession profoundly shaped Christian historiography. Later catalogues could treat new opponents as branches of an original Simonian tree. The figure solved a narrative problem: if the church was originally one, diversity had to originate in a first corrupter.

### Magic versus miracle

Simon became the standard adversarial double of Peter. Both perform wonders; narrative authority decides which are divine miracles and which are demonic magic. The *Acts of Peter* makes the ambiguity explicit by staging matched feats before an audience. Simon therefore became a long-lived instrument for defining legitimate charisma, apostolic succession, sacramental authority, and the boundaries of religious expertise.

### Samaritan and gnostic histories

For historians of Samaria, Simon stands amid a poorly documented landscape of first-century sectarian, prophetic, baptist, and intermediary-power traditions. For historians of Gnosis, his importance lies less in proving a single origin than in the second-century communities, myths, and writings attached to his name. Modern scholarship increasingly studies “Simonians” rather than treating every later doctrine as the founder's own.

### Literature, folklore, and conspiracy narrative

The Simon-Paul identification, Simon-Marcion theories, hidden-origin reconstructions, and claims of suppressed Simonian Christianity continue to circulate. Their evidentiary value varies. The Pseudo-Clementines genuinely preserve anti-Pauline resonances; this supports literary-polemical analysis, not automatic identity. Modern retellings often reverse the victor and victim, reading Simon as defeated dissident and Peter as institutional enforcer. That reversal is itself a reception history built on the asymmetry of the sources: virtually everything about Simon was preserved by opponents.

### Historical remainder

After the layers are separated, a limited profile remains possible: a Samaritan named Simon acquired a substantial reputation as a religious wonder-worker; a Christian narrative remembered him as baptized through Philip's mission and rebuked by Peter after offering money for charismatic authority; second-century people called Simonians connected a divine figure Simon with a theology of Great Power and Helena/Ennoia; Christian authors made him the first heretic; apocryphal fiction transported the conflict to Rome and killed him in the sky. Everything beyond this profile requires explicit qualification.
