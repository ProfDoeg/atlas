## Works by Oscar Fingal O'Fflahertie Wills Wilde

### Novel and its materially different manifestations

- **1890:** *The Picture of Dorian Gray*. A novel first manifested in *Lippincott’s Monthly Magazine*; editorial intervention removed or altered material before publication.
- **1891:** *The Picture of Dorian Gray*. Wilde’s substantially revised and enlarged book version, with six additional chapters and a manifesto-like preface of aphorisms; it is not merely a reprint of the 1890 text.

### Plays and dramatic projects

- **1879–80; revised 1882:** *Vera; or, The Nihilists*. Wilde’s first completed play, a political melodrama intended for performance; its composition, revision, publication, and unsuccessful 1883 New York production have distinct dates.
- **1883:** *The Duchess of Padua*. A five-act verse tragedy commissioned for Mary Anderson, who declined it; first performed in New York in 1891 under the title *Guido Ferranti*.
- **1891–92:** *Lady Windermere’s Fan*. A four-act society comedy first performed in 1892; Wilde continued revising it through rehearsal and publication.
- **1892–93:** *A Woman of No Importance*. A four-act society comedy first performed in 1893.
- **1891; published 1893:** *Salomé*. A one-act play written in French and prohibited from English production by the Lord Chamberlain; it first appeared on stage in Paris in 1896.
- **1894:** *Salome*. The English manifestation traditionally credited as Lord Alfred Douglas’s translation, but extensively corrected or rewritten under Wilde’s supervision; the precise division of authorship remains disputed.
- **1893–94:** *An Ideal Husband*. A four-act society comedy first performed in 1895.
- **1894:** *The Importance of Being Earnest*. A four-act comedy as first written.
- **1895:** *The Importance of Being Earnest*. The performed three-act version, reshaped to accommodate the theatre’s programme; the four- and three-act texts are materially different dramatic objects.
- **1894–97, unfinished:** *La Sainte Courtisane; or, The Woman Covered with Jewels*. A surviving dramatic fragment, not a completed play; its status is chiefly that of an abandoned project reconstructed from manuscript and posthumous print.
- **1895–97, unfinished:** *A Florentine Tragedy*. A surviving verse-play fragment lacking its opening; Thomas Sturge Moore later supplied an opening for performance, but that addition is not Wilde’s work.

### Short fiction and fairy tales

- **1887:** *The Canterville Ghost*. A comic supernatural novella first serialized in *The Court and Society Review*.
- **1887:** *Lord Arthur Savile’s Crime*. A satirical short story first published in *The Court and Society Review*.
- **1887:** *The Sphinx Without a Secret*. A short story first published as *The Lady Alroy*; its later title is a revised manifestation.
- **1887:** *The Model Millionaire*. A short story about portraiture, performance, and mistaken economic identity.
- **1888:** *The Happy Prince*. A literary fairy tale.
- **1888:** *The Nightingale and the Rose*. A literary fairy tale.
- **1888:** *The Selfish Giant*. A literary fairy tale.
- **1888:** *The Devoted Friend*. A fable-like fairy tale whose nested storytelling makes its moral deliberately unstable.
- **1888:** *The Remarkable Rocket*. A satirical fairy tale.
- **1889:** *The Portrait of Mr W. H.*. A story-essay about a hypothetical dedicatee of Shakespeare’s sonnets; fiction, criticism, forgery narrative, and intellectual game are deliberately inseparable.
- **1889–93, unpublished in Wilde’s lifetime:** *The Portrait of Mr W. H.* (expanded version). A much longer revision surviving in manuscript; it is a distinct unfinished or unreleased project rather than simply another edition of the 1889 story.
- **1891:** *The Young King*. A literary fairy tale.
- **1891:** *The Birthday of the Infanta*. A literary fairy tale.
- **1891:** *The Fisherman and His Soul*. A literary fairy tale structured as a theological and aesthetic fable.
- **1891:** *The Star-Child*. A literary fairy tale.

### Prose poems

- **1894:** *The Artist*. A prose poem published as part of the collective manifestation *Poems in Prose*.
- **1894:** *The Doer of Good*. A prose poem recasting a biblical encounter as a paradox.
- **1894:** *The Disciple*. A prose poem and Narcissus fable.
- **1894:** *The Master*. A prose poem using a biblical setting.
- **1894:** *The House of Judgment*. A prose poem in the form of a divine interrogation.
- **1894:** *The Teacher of Wisdom*. A prose poem about knowledge, renunciation, and desire.
- **1894:** *Poems in Prose*. The collective title under which the six preceding pieces appeared in *The Fortnightly Review*; this periodical arrangement is itself a manifestation, not a seventh prose poem.

### Major poems and separately issued poetic objects

- **1875:** *Chorus of Cloud Maidens*. A translation or adaptation from Aristophanes and Wilde’s earliest known published poem.
- **1876:** *The Rose of Love*. An early poem later revised and retitled *Flower of Love*.
- **1878:** *Ravenna*. A long topographical and historical poem that won Oxford’s Newdigate Prize and was separately printed for public recitation.
- **1881:** *Hélas!*. A sonnet placed as the threshold poem of *Poems*.
- **1881:** *The Garden of Eros*. A long poem surveying modern English poetry and art.
- **1881:** *The Burden of Itys*. A long mythological and pastoral poem.
- **1881:** *Charmides*. A long narrative poem derived from Greek myth.
- **1881:** *Panthea*. A philosophical poem.
- **1881:** *Humanitad*. A long philosophical and political poem.
- **1885:** *The Harlot’s House*. A separately published poem presenting a nocturnal dance as an uncanny spectacle.
- **1894:** *The Sphinx*. A long decadent poem issued as an elaborately designed book; poem, typography, illustration, and binding approach a small Gesamtkunstwerk.
- **1898:** *The Ballad of Reading Gaol*. A long prison ballad first published under Wilde’s inmate designation, “C.3.3.”; later editions restored his name and altered the poem’s arrangement.

### Critical dialogues, essays, and manifesto-like texts

- **1879:** *The Rise of Historical Criticism*. Wilde’s Oxford Chancellor’s Essay, a long scholarly study; it was not published in full during his lifetime.
- **1885; revised 1891:** *The Truth of Masks*. An essay on costume and archaeological detail in Shakespearean performance, first published under another title and revised for *Intentions*.
- **1889; revised 1891:** *Pen, Pencil and Poison: A Study in Green*. A critical biography of Thomas Griffiths Wainewright that intentionally destabilizes distinctions between criticism, fiction, crime writing, and aesthetic manifesto.
- **1889; revised 1891:** *The Decay of Lying: An Observation*. A dialogue whose argument that life imitates art makes it function as an aesthetic manifesto.
- **1890; revised 1891:** *The Critic as Artist*. A two-part dialogue presenting criticism as an autonomous creative art; its original periodical title was *The True Function and Value of Criticism*.
- **1891:** *The Soul of Man under Socialism*. A political-aesthetic essay advocating individualism; it behaves partly as a manifesto rather than neutral description.
- **1894:** *Phrases and Philosophies for the Use of the Young*. A sequence of aphorisms; formally it resembles a protocol of conduct, but each instruction subverts conventional moral instruction.

### Lectures and lecture projects

- **1882:** *The English Renaissance of Art*. The principal lecture of Wilde’s North American tour; a performed and repeatedly adapted oral text rather than a single stable document.
- **1882:** *The Decorative Arts* / *House Decoration*. A lecture on domestic design delivered in variant forms; the surviving title and text do not represent every performance exactly.
- **1882:** *Art and the Handicraftsman*. A lecture on craft, labour, and beauty; like the other tour lectures, it survives through scripts and reports rather than one definitive utterance.
- **1883:** *Impressions of America*. A lecture fashioned from Wilde’s North American journey.
- **1883:** *Lecture to Art Students*. A lecture delivered at the Royal Academy Schools on artistic independence and the study of visible things.
- **1883–84:** *Personal Impressions of America*. A lecture-project related to, but not textually identical with, *Impressions of America*; Wilde varied its contents for different audiences.

### Art criticism, journalism, and editorial interventions

- **1877:** *The Grosvenor Gallery*. Wilde’s first substantial published art review.
- **1877:** *The Tomb of Keats*. A prose meditation on Keats’s grave incorporating Wilde’s sonnet *The Grave of Keats*.
- **1879:** *The Grosvenor Gallery* (1879 review). A second and distinct review of the gallery’s annual exhibition.
- **1882:** *L’Envoi*. Wilde’s critical introduction to Rennell Rodd’s *Rose Leaf and Apple Leaf*.
- **1882:** *Mrs Langtry as Hester Grazebrook*. A theatrical review written during Wilde’s American tour.
- **1884:** *More Radical Ideas upon Dress Reform*. A polemical article on clothing and bodily freedom.
- **1884:** *Woman’s Dress*. An article advocating dress reform.
- **1885:** *Mr Whistler’s Ten O’Clock*. A critical response to James McNeill Whistler’s lecture.
- **1885:** *The Relation of Dress to Art*. An essay linking clothing, everyday life, and artistic production.
- **1886:** *Keats’s Sonnet on Blue*. A bibliographical and critical note on a Keats manuscript.
- **1887:** *Art at Willis’s Rooms*. A report and critical notice of a lecture by Selwyn Image.
- **1887:** *Sermons in Stones at Bloomsbury*. An art-critical notice of ancient sculpture in the British Museum.
- **1887:** *The American Invasion*. A comic cultural essay about Americans in Britain.
- **1887:** *The Unity of the Arts*. A report on Selwyn Image’s lecture and an assertion of the arts’ interdependence.
- **1887–89:** *The Woman’s World*. A magazine edited and substantially reconceived by Wilde. It is not wholly authored by him, so it resists classification as a Wilde “work”; his made object was the editorial project—its title, programme, selection, commissioning, and arrangement.
- **1888:** *English Poetesses*. A survey of women poets.
- **1888:** *Mr Morris on Tapestry*. A report on William Morris’s Arts and Crafts lecture.
- **1888:** *Printing and Printers*. A report on Emery Walker’s lecture about typography and book production.
- **1888:** *Sculpture at the Arts and Crafts*. A critical lecture notice.
- **1888:** *The Beauties of Bookbinding*. A report on T. J. Cobden-Sanderson’s lecture and an argument about binding as decorative art.
- **1888:** *The Close of the Arts and Crafts*. A report on Walter Crane’s concluding exhibition lecture.
- **1889:** *London Models*. An essay about professional artists’ models.
- **1885–90:** *Anonymous reviews and notices*. A corpus of roughly one hundred journalistic pieces on fiction, poetry, theatre, history, translation, cookery, art, and social questions, established—sometimes tentatively—from anonymous periodical publication by the Oxford *Complete Works*. This is not one obra, and individual attributions vary in certainty; treating the corpus as a single titled “Reviews” conceals that resistance.
- **1890:** *Letters on The Picture of Dorian Gray*. Eight public letters answering hostile newspaper reviews; collectively they operate as a defence and aesthetic manifesto, although each originated as a separate intervention.
- **1892:** *Puppets and Actors*. A public letter about theatrical representation and the relative authority of playwright and performer.
- **1882–94:** *Letters to the Press*. A posthumously imposed collective label for Wilde’s separate public letters on art, controversy, attribution, and his own works; it is an archival corpus, not a work he designed as a book.

### Prison writing and public interventions

- **1895–97:** *Epistola: In Carcere et Vinculis*. Wilde’s long prison letter to Lord Alfred Douglas, written in sections under prison restrictions. It is simultaneously correspondence, confession, aesthetic essay, and an imposed writing protocol.
- **1905:** *De Profundis*. Robert Ross’s heavily abridged posthumous publication of the prison letter under a title not supplied by Wilde; it is therefore a consequential editorial manifestation, not the complete original object.
- **1949; complete text published 1962:** *The Complete Letters of Oscar Wilde*’s prison letter / *De Profundis* in full. The manuscript’s delayed publication history matters: the unabridged text and the familiar 1905 book are not identical works.
- **1897:** *The Case of Warder Martin: Some Cruelties of Prison Life*. A public letter to the *Daily Chronicle* calling for prison reform.
- **1898:** *Don’t Read This If You Want to Be Happy To-day*. A second *Daily Chronicle* letter on prison conditions, later collected under variant editorial titles.

### Books and other composite manifestations arranged by Wilde

- **1881:** *Poems*. Wilde’s first collected book and a deliberately arranged poetic object, revised through several editions; accusations of derivativeness accompanied its publication, but the poems’ canonical attribution to Wilde is not disputed.
- **1888:** *The Happy Prince and Other Tales*. A designed collection comprising five fairy tales; the collection is a distinct arrangement as well as a container for its component stories.
- **1891:** *Lord Arthur Savile’s Crime and Other Stories*. A collection bringing together four previously published stories; later impressions added *The Portrait of Mr W. H.*
- **1891:** *A House of Pomegranates*. A designed collection of four fairy tales, conceived in close relation to Charles Ricketts and Charles Shannon’s illustrations and book decoration.
- **1891:** *Intentions*. A carefully revised collection of four critical essays and dialogues; its sequence turns criticism into a sustained aesthetic project.
- **1892:** *The House of Pomegranates* presentation and deluxe manifestations. The bindings, page design, illustrations, and prose jointly make the volume more than a neutral carrier, though the visual work belongs substantially to Ricketts and Shannon rather than Wilde alone.
- **1894:** *The Sphinx* as a book-object. Wilde’s poem and Charles Ricketts’s typography and illustrations form a collaborative decorative object; authorship of the text is Wilde’s, but authorship of the Gesamtkunstwerk is shared.
- **1898:** *The Ballad of Reading Gaol* as “C.3.3.”. The pseudonymous first book manifestation converts a prison identification code into authorship; later signed issues materially alter that framing.

### Correspondence, inscriptions, and conversational creations

- **c. 1870s–1900:** *Letters*. More than a thousand surviving private, professional, romantic, legal, and prison letters are gathered in Merlin Holland and Rupert Hart-Davis’s scholarly *Complete Letters*. They are individual made texts, but most resist being called “works”: they were actions within relationships, not an oeuvre planned for publication.
- **c. 1870s–1900:** *Book inscriptions, presentation verses, dedications, visiting-card messages, and telegrams*. These survive unevenly in manuscripts and catalogues. Many are textual actions attached to material objects, not securely titleable autonomous works.
- **1870s–1900:** *Conversation, lectures as delivered, stories told orally, paradoxes, and epigrams*. Contemporary witnesses preserve many, but transmission through memory makes wording and authorship unstable; they are creations or performances rather than securely recoverable texts.

### Projects, personae, and mixed-media making

- **1882:** *The North American lecture tour*. A year-long performed project joining lectures, dress, interviews, publicity photographs, travel, and Wilde’s cultivated “Professor of Aesthetics” persona. No single transcript or photograph is the work; the project resists separation into text, performance, and publicity.
- **1882:** *Napoleon Sarony portrait sessions*. Wilde actively collaborated in staging his poses, clothing, props, and aesthetic identity, but Sarony made the photographs. They are therefore collaborative manifestations of Wilde’s self-fashioning, not photographs authored by Wilde.
- **1880s–90s:** *Oscar Wilde as public aesthetic persona*. Dress, speech, poses, interiors, journalism, and publicity constituted an ongoing performative project. It resists being called a discrete work because it has neither fixed boundaries nor a single material form.
- **1895:** *The Queensberry libel prosecution and Wilde’s courtroom speech*. Wilde’s testimony included highly wrought aesthetic and ethical argument, but it was compelled legal speech recorded through court reporting, not a literary work he authorized; treating it as an obra would erase that coercive setting.

### Rejected, doubtful, or misrepresented attributions

- **1880s, alleged:** *The Priest and the Acolyte*. This homoerotic story has repeatedly been attributed to Wilde but is now assigned to John Francis Bloxam; it should not enter Wilde’s oeuvre.
- **1893, anonymous:** *Teleny; or, The Reverse of the Medal*. Wilde may have helped circulate or encourage the manuscript, and later tradition associated him with a collaborative circle, but no reliable evidence establishes him as its author.
- **1899:** *Des Grieux*. A short prefatory text associated with *Teleny* and sometimes attributed to Wilde; the attribution is doubtful and it cannot safely be listed as his work.
- **1894, alleged:** *The Green Carnation*. Published anonymously by Robert Hichens and sometimes mistaken for Wilde’s work because it caricatures Wilde and Lord Alfred Douglas; it is not by Wilde.
- **Posthumously presented as Wilde’s:** *For Love of the King*. A play issued by Mabel Cosgrove as Wilde’s lost work; scholarly investigation treats the attribution as fraudulent.
- **Various dates:** Isolated aphorisms, jokes, and “Oscar Wilde quotations.” A large floating corpus is misquoted, paraphrased, or invented. Without manuscript or contemporary documentary evidence these are attributions, not traceable Wilde creations.
