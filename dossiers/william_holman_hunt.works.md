## Works by William Holman Hunt

### Oil paintings and temperas

- **1841:** *Self-Portrait at the Age of Fourteen*. An early oil portrait; Judith Bronkhurst’s catalogue raisonné is the governing source for Hunt’s paintings and their variant titles and dates.
- **1842:** *Portrait of William Pink*. An early portrait painting.
- **1844–45:** *Self-Portrait at Seventeen*. An oil self-portrait sometimes dated 1844 and sometimes 1845; the disagreement reflects inscription, exhibition, and completion evidence rather than two securely distinct works.
- **1846:** *Woodstock*. Hunt’s first sold painting; its present whereabouts are not securely established.
- **1846:** *Love at First Sight*. A literary subject painting.
- **1847:** *Frederic George Stephens*. A portrait of Hunt’s future fellow Pre-Raphaelite.
- **1847–97:** *Christ and the Two Marys in the Garden of Joseph of Arimathea*. A religious painting begun in 1847 but repeatedly reworked and not brought to its surviving state until about fifty years later; its long, discontinuous making makes it as much a sustained project as a single dated picture.
- **1848:** *The Eve of St Agnes: The Flight of Madeline and Porphyro during the Drunkenness Attending the Revelry*. A painting after Keats; older sources sometimes date it 1846, while modern cataloguing generally places its execution in 1848.
- **1848–49:** *Rienzi Vowing to Obtain Justice for the Death of His Young Brother, Slain in a Skirmish between the Colonna and Orsini Factions*. A historical-literary oil painting and an early work bearing the initials “PRB.”
- **1849:** *The Haunted Manor*. An oil landscape or architectural subject whose unfinished, enigmatic condition resists a settled narrative classification.
- **1849:** *Cornfield at Ewell*. A small plein-air landscape study in oil.
- **1849–50:** *A Converted British Family Sheltering a Christian Missionary from the Persecution of the Druids*. A large religious-history painting; early publications shorten or alter both “missionary” and “priest,” so its title is not wholly stable.
- **1850–53:** *Claudio and Isabella*. A Shakespearean oil painting after *Measure for Measure*, subsequently retouched in 1879.
- **1850–51:** *Valentine Rescuing Sylvia from Proteus*. A Shakespearean painting after *The Two Gentlemen of Verona*.
- **1851–52:** *The Hireling Shepherd*. A pastoral oil painting with an explicitly moral and biblical symbolic programme.
- **1852:** *Our English Coasts, 1852*. A landscape and animal painting later commonly called *Strayed Sheep*; the later name should not silently replace Hunt’s exhibited title.
- **1853:** *Portrait of Dante Gabriel Rossetti at Twenty-Two Years of Age*. A portrait made during a reciprocal portrait-drawing session among members of the Pre-Raphaelite circle.
- **1853–54:** *The Awakening Conscience*. A modern-life moral painting; Hunt later altered the woman’s expression and other passages, complicating the notion of a single finished state.
- **1851–53:** *The Light of the World*. The original oil version, now at Keble College, Oxford; it is one manifestation of a subject Hunt subsequently repeated.
- **1853–54:** *The Light of the World*. A smaller autograph version now at Manchester Art Gallery; it is a distinct painting, not merely an edition of the Keble picture.
- **1854:** *The Sphinx at Gizeh*. An Egyptian landscape or topographical study.
- **1854:** *Afterglow in Egypt*. A small oil landscape recording atmospheric light.
- **1854–56:** *The Lantern-Maker’s Courtship: A Street Scene in Cairo*. An Orientalist genre painting produced from Hunt’s first journey in Egypt and Palestine.
- **1854–56:** *The Scapegoat*. The principal oil painting made partly beside the Dead Sea; Hunt’s symbolic picture also depended upon direct topographical and zoological observation.
- **1854–56:** *The Scapegoat*. A smaller oil version with a darker goat and altered landscape; it is a separate manifestation rather than a mechanical copy.
- **1855:** *The Dead Sea from Siloam*. A topographical landscape in oil.
- **1854–60:** *The Finding of the Saviour in the Temple*. A monumental biblical painting begun in Jerusalem and completed in England after prolonged work.
- **1857:** *The School-Girl’s Hymn*. A modern devotional subject painting, also known through variant punctuation and capitalization.
- **1857–58:** *The Eve of St Agnes*. A later treatment of Keats’s poem, distinct from Hunt’s 1848 picture.
- **1859–66:** *Il Dolce far Niente*. An idealized female figure painting, retouched in 1874–75; its prolonged revision makes the terminal date conditional.
- **1860:** *Portrait of Robert Braithwaite Martineau*. A portrait of Hunt’s friend and fellow painter.
- **1861:** *Nazareth*. A Palestinian landscape, also circulated as *A Distant View of Nazareth*.
- **1861–62:** *The King of Hearts*. A small modern-life or costume subject painting.
- **1863–64:** *London Bridge on the Night of the Marriage of the Prince and Princess of Wales*. A contemporary crowd scene developed from drawings made during the celebrations of 10 March 1863; Hunt completed it in 1864 and retouched it in 1866.
- **1864:** *The Children’s Holiday*. A group portrait rather than a genre scene in the strict sense.
- **1864:** *Lady Fairbairn and Her Children*. A family portrait; the relationship between this title and *The Children’s Holiday* has not always been represented consistently in popular lists and should be checked against Bronkhurst’s separate catalogue entries.
- **1866:** *Morning Hunt*. A sporting or equestrian subject.
- **1866–67:** *Portrait of Fanny Holman Hunt*. A portrait of Hunt’s first wife, made around the time of her death.
- **1866–68:** *Isabella and the Pot of Basil*. A literary painting after Keats, retouched in 1886; the making therefore exceeds the date normally attached to it.
- **1867:** *Self-Portrait*. An oil self-portrait associated with Hunt’s mature public identity.
- **1867:** *The Ponte Vecchio, Florence*. A topographical cityscape.
- **1868:** *The Birthday: A Portrait of the Artist’s Wife, Edith*. An oil portrait whose title makes a private portrait function like an allegorical or commemorative picture.
- **1868:** *Bianca*. A tempera portrait or ideal head painted from an American sitter.
- **1870–73:** *The Shadow of Death*. A monumental religious painting of Christ in the carpenter’s workshop.
- **1873–74:** *Thomas Fairbairn*. A commissioned portrait in oil.
- **1875:** *The Festival of St Swithin, or The Dovecote*. A genre painting with an alternative title.
- **1875:** *The Ship*. A nocturnal seascape whose subject is subordinated to moonlight and atmosphere.
- **1876–87:** *The Triumph of the Innocents*. The first large conception of Hunt’s visionary Flight into Egypt; sources give differing boundaries because he reworked the subject for years.
- **1877:** *My Son Cyril*. A portrait of Cyril Benoni Holman Hunt.
- **1880:** *Cyril Benoni Holman Hunt*. A further portrait of Hunt’s son, distinct from the 1877 picture.
- **1883–84, retouched to 1897:** *The Triumph of the Innocents*. The principal surviving large oil version; continuing revision makes “completion” disputable.
- **1884:** *Amaryllis*. An ideal female figure or aesthetic subject rather than narrative history painting.
- **1884:** *The Bride of Bethlehem*. A female figure subject with biblical and ethnographic associations.
- **1880s:** *Sorrow*. An ideal or allegorical female figure; no more exact date should be imposed without the individual catalogue entry.
- **1886–1905:** *The Lady of Shalott*. A large oil painting developed from Hunt’s 1857 illustration; failing eyesight led Edward Robert Hughes to assist with its execution, so sole authorship of every painted passage should not be assumed.
- **1888–89:** *May Morning on Magdalen Tower*. A large ceremonial painting of the Oxford May Morning observance, commonly dated 1888–90 or 1890–91 because work and exhibition did not coincide neatly.
- **1892–99:** *The Miracle of the Holy Fire in the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, Jerusalem*. A large crowd painting based on Hunt’s observation of the Easter ceremony; it is also called *The Miracle of the Sacred Fire*.
- **1894–95:** *The Importunate Neighbour*. A biblical parable painting.
- **c. 1899–1904:** *The Light of the World*. The monumental St Paul’s Cathedral version; Hunt devised and supervised it, but Edward Robert Hughes materially assisted because of Hunt’s failing sight, making it a collaborative manifestation of Hunt’s project.
- **Undated:** *Asparagus Island*. A landscape whose exact date is not securely fixed in readily accessible summary sources.
- **Undated:** *Fairlight Downs: Sunlight on the Sea*. A coastal landscape; no precise date should be inferred from its subject.
- **Undated:** *Fishing Boats by Moonlight*. A nocturnal marine study or painting.
- **Undated:** *John Ruskin’s Dead Chick*. A small natural-history subject whose intimate, study-like character resists the status of a formal exhibition picture.
- **Undated:** *Stephen Lushington*. A portrait for which summary catalogues do not provide a dependable date.

### Watercolours, drawings, and sustained pictorial projects

- **1840s–1900s:** *Drawings and watercolours catalogued by Judith Bronkhurst*. This is not one work but a catalogue-defined corpus of individual portraits, landscapes, figure studies, compositional drawings, tracings, and studies for paintings. Bronkhurst devotes the second volume of the catalogue raisonné to these objects; collapsing them into their related oils would erase works that survive independently, but an item-by-item transcription cannot responsibly be reconstructed from abbreviated online lists.
- **1854–60:** *Studies for The Finding of the Saviour in the Temple*. A project-corpus comprising architectural, costume, figure, and ethnographic drawings and watercolours made in Palestine and England; individual sheets have separate catalogue identities.
- **1854–56:** *Studies for The Scapegoat and the Dead Sea landscape*. A dispersed project-corpus of animal and topographical studies rather than a single preparatory “text.”
- **c. 1876–97:** *Studies and versions for The Triumph of the Innocents*. A prolonged project encompassing drawings, watercolours, and several painted manifestations; no single sheet can stand for the project as a whole.
- **c. 1886–1905:** *Studies for The Lady of Shalott*. A multi-medium project growing from the 1857 wood-engraving design into drawings, a smaller painted treatment, and the late monumental oil.
- **Undated:** *Christ among the Doctors*. A large watercolour treatment distinct in composition and meaning from *The Finding of the Saviour in the Temple*.
- **1840s–1900s:** *Illustrated letters*. A corpus of letters in which verbal communication and original drawing form a single object; these are neither simply correspondence nor autonomous drawings and can reasonably be called miniature Gesamtkunstwerke. Bronkhurst catalogues them separately in an appendix rather than treating every doodle as a conventional work.

### Designs for printed illustrations

- **1850:** *A Converted British Family Sheltering a Christian Missionary from the Persecution of the Druids*. A design published in *The Germ*; the printed image is a reproductive manifestation made through an engraver rather than an autograph drawing by Hunt alone.
- **1850:** *The Light of Truth*. An emblematic illustration for *The Germ*.
- **1857:** *Recollections of the Arabian Nights: Drifting in a Boat*. One of Hunt’s designs for the illustrated Moxon edition of Tennyson’s *Poems*, translated into a wood engraving by another hand.
- **1857:** *Recollections of the Arabian Nights: The Man with the Scroll*. A second design for the same poem and publication.
- **1857:** *The Ballad of Oriana: The Lovers’ Farewell*. A Moxon Tennyson illustration design.
- **1857:** *The Ballad of Oriana: The Warrior’s Return*. A companion illustration design.
- **1857:** *Godiva*. A Moxon Tennyson illustration design.
- **1857:** *The Lady of Shalott*. A Moxon Tennyson design whose printed form is a wood engraving; Hunt later expanded the project into paintings, while Tennyson objected to liberties taken by the image.
- **1857:** *The Beggar Maid*. A Moxon Tennyson illustration design.
- **1860:** *The Lent Jewels*. A periodical illustration published in *Once a Week*.
- **1862:** *Active and Passive*. A printed illustration published in *Good Words*.
- **1862:** *Come and Go*. A printed illustration associated with *English Sacred Poetry*.
- **1865:** *Illustration for Parables from Nature*. A design for Margaret Gatty’s book; the bibliographical title of the individual image varies in secondary listings and should not be silently regularized.
- **1881:** *Design for the Dalziels’ Bible Gallery*. A biblical illustration whose printed manifestation involved the Dalziel Brothers’ reproductive process.

### Etchings and reproductive prints

- **1840s–1900s:** *Etchings catalogued by Judith Bronkhurst*. A small corpus listed in an appendix to the catalogue raisonné. Each printed impression is a manifestation of an etched matrix, so neither the plate nor any one impression alone exhausts the made thing.
- **1850s–1900s:** *Prints after Hunt’s paintings*. These engravings, photogravures, and popular reproductions helped make subjects such as *The Light of the World* internationally ubiquitous, but most were executed and published by other people. They resist inclusion as Hunt’s works unless Hunt designed, corrected, or authorized them; Bronkhurst’s separation of autograph etchings and published illustrations from reproductions is therefore essential.

### Sculpture, furniture, frames, and integrated objects

- **1840s–1900s:** *Sculpture catalogued by Judith Bronkhurst*. A small appendix-defined corpus. These objects belong to Hunt’s material practice but cannot responsibly be expanded into titled entries from general museum lists, which largely omit them.
- **1840s–1900s:** *Furniture designed by Hunt*. An appendix-defined corpus of designed furnishings rather than paintings; authorship may be divided between Hunt as designer and the unidentified or separately identifiable craft workers who fabricated them.
- **1850s–1900s:** *Artist-designed frames for Hunt’s paintings*. These frames often carry inscriptions, emblems, and architectural ornament integral to interpretation. Where Hunt designed them, painting and frame approach a Gesamtkunstwerk; fabrication nevertheless commonly belonged to professional frame-makers and should not be attributed wholly to Hunt without documentary evidence.

### Manifesto, memoir, polemic, and explanatory texts

- **1850:** *Two Subjects for “The Germ”*. Hunt’s prose contribution to the Pre-Raphaelite periodical; it functions partly as critical statement and partly as manifesto because it argues through examples for a new artistic practice.
- **1858:** *Jerusalem: Bishop Gobat in re Hanna Hadoub; with Original Documents Detailing the Case*. A sixty-page polemical pamphlet concerning a Jerusalem controversy, not an art book.
- **1873:** *Mr. Holman Hunt’s Picture, “The Shadow of Death”*. An exhibition pamphlet explaining and defending the painting’s subject and symbolism; it is an interpretive paratext and public argument rather than an independent narrative book.
- **1905:** *Pre-Raphaelitism and the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood*. A two-volume memoir, history, and apologia. It is not a neutral chronicle: Hunt uses recollection to establish the Brotherhood’s programme and his own role in it, so the work also behaves as a retrospective manifesto.
- **Throughout his career:** *Poems*. Hunt wrote poetry, reportedly chiefly in couplets and Spenserian stanzas, but abandoned sustained poetic production; no securely established authorial collection survives, and this unfinished practice resists treatment as a titled work.
- **Throughout his career:** *Letters*. Hunt produced a large correspondence, much of it now in archival collections and partly published in edited selections. The letters are made texts but not a single authored “work”; later editions such as his correspondence with Ruskin, John Lucas Tupper, or Ernest Chesneau are editorial creations rather than books Hunt himself made.

### Collective and collaborative projects

- **1848:** *The Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood*. A collective artistic project co-founded by Hunt with Dante Gabriel Rossetti and John Everett Millais and then enlarged to seven members. It is not Hunt’s sole work or a discrete object, but an intentional framework that generated manifestos, exhibitions, pictures, and *The Germ*.
- **1850:** *The Germ*. A four-number collective periodical created by the Pre-Raphaelite circle. Hunt contributed text and imagery, but the journal as a whole is a collaborative manifestation and should not be assigned to him alone.
- **1857:** *Poems by Alfred Tennyson* (the “Moxon Tennyson”). A collaborative illustrated book containing seven designs by Hunt alongside work by Rossetti, Millais, and other artists; Tennyson wrote the poems, engravers cut the blocks, and Moxon published the volume, so the book is not simply “by” Hunt.
- **1857–59:** *Oxford Union murals*. Hunt participated in the collective decorative project organized around Rossetti in the Oxford Union debating hall. Its unstable materials, divided authorship, unfinished passages, and later deterioration make it a project—and an attempted Gesamtkunstwerk—rather than a clean set of independently finished paintings.
- **c. 1857–59:** *The Death of Britomart*. Hunt’s contribution to the Oxford Union mural project, after Spenser; its condition and collaborative architectural setting resist treatment as an ordinary easel painting.
- **Late nineteenth century:** *Unfinished triptych including Christ the Pilot*. A religious project known from a surviving panel and related evidence but never completed as the intended whole. The project, rather than a nonexistent finished triptych, is the work.
