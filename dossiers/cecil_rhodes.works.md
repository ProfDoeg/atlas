## Works by Cecil John Rhodes

### Manifestos, testaments, and protocols

- **1877:** *Confession of Faith*. An unpublished imperial manifesto outlining Rhodes’s ambition to extend British rule through a secret society; it is a program for action rather than a descriptive essay. Scholars distinguish it from the first will, although both belong to the same moment and ideological project.

- **1877:** *First will*. A testamentary protocol directing Rhodes’s estate toward a secret society for British imperial expansion. Its traditional dating and even its independent existence have been questioned by later scholarship, so it should not be treated as an undisputed surviving document.

- **1882:** *Second will*. A revised testament naming N. E. Pickering as trustee and retaining the secret-society project.

- **1888:** *Third will*. A further testamentary version of Rhodes’s imperial project, composed while his mining and territorial enterprises were being consolidated.

- **1891:** *Fourth will*. A political testament entrusting the residue of Rhodes’s estate to executors including W. T. Stead; Stead subsequently helped formulate the projected society, so the work’s intellectual authorship is partly collaborative.

- **19 December 1892:** *Fifth will*. A transitional testament that began shifting the imperial association toward an educational mechanism. Its provisions are an antecedent—not yet the finished form—of the Rhodes Scholarships.

- **8 September 1893:** *Sixth will*. The first will to specify an organized scheme of Oxford scholarships for students from several settler colonies; it remained provisional and was revoked.

- **1 July 1899; codicils 1901–1902:** *Last Will and Testament*. A legal protocol establishing the principal Rhodes Scholarship scheme and making provisions for Groote Schuur, the Matopos estate, burial, memorials, and other beneficiaries. The operative object is the signed will together with its codicils, not W. T. Stead’s later edited book bearing the same title.

### Speeches and political texts

- **1881–1900:** *Political speeches*. A body of reported parliamentary, electoral, company, and public addresses collected during Rhodes’s lifetime in *Cecil Rhodes: His Political Life and Speeches, 1881–1900*. The collection claims comprehensiveness for speeches that were reported, not for every speech Rhodes delivered; wording often depends on newspaper or parliamentary reporting rather than authorial manuscripts.

- **30 July 1894:** *Speech on the Second Reading of the Glen Grey Act*. A legislative manifesto defending territorial segregation, taxation, compulsory wage labour, and restricted African political participation. It is separately nameable because of its historical importance, while also belonging to the collected political speeches.

- **1896–1897:** *Statements and examinations concerning the Jameson Raid*. Testimony, explanations, and parliamentary evidence through which Rhodes defended or obscured his role in the failed incursion. These are compelled or strategic utterances rather than a unified authored work, and their evidentiary status is contested.

- **Surviving correspondence, chiefly 1870s–1902:** *Letters and telegrams*. An archival corpus dispersed among the Bodleian Libraries’ Rhodes papers and other collections. It resists treatment as a single obra: the individual documents were made by Rhodes, but the corpus and its present organization were made by archivists and editors.

### Commercial and corporate obras

- **1873–1880:** *Rhodes and Rudd*. A diamond-mining partnership developed with Charles Rudd from accumulated Kimberley claims. It was a collaborative commercial construction rather than a formally authored object.

- **April 1880:** *De Beers Mining Company*. A joint-stock mining enterprise formed by Rhodes, Rudd, and associates to consolidate claims at the De Beers mine; it became one component of the later monopoly.

- **1887:** *The Gold Fields of South Africa Limited*. A gold-mining and finance company founded with Charles Rudd and other investors, later renamed Consolidated Gold Fields of South Africa. Rhodes was a co-maker, not a solitary founder.

- **12 March 1888:** *De Beers Consolidated Mines Limited*. The corporate amalgamation through which Rhodes, Barney Barnato, Alfred Beit, financiers, and shareholders concentrated much of the Kimberley diamond industry. Calling it simply “Rhodes’s creation” conceals its negotiated and collective authorship, as well as the coercive labour system on which it operated.

- **1888–1889:** *Central Search Association*. A short-lived concession-holding company formed by Rhodes and associates to receive and exploit the Rudd Concession. It was an instrumental corporate shell and therefore resists the status of an autonomous “work.”

- **1889:** *British South Africa Company*. A chartered company assembled from Rhodes’s group and the rival Exploring Company interests, then granted a royal charter. It combined company, government, army, and colonizing apparatus and consequently approaches a coercive political Gesamtkunstwerk rather than an ordinary business creation.

### Territorial, infrastructural, and political projects

- **1884–1885:** *British expansion into Bechuanaland*. A political project in which Rhodes helped secure British intervention and the establishment of the Bechuanaland Protectorate and British Bechuanaland. It was neither his sole creation nor a neutral administrative work: African rulers, Cape politicians, missionaries, and imperial officials acted independently within it.

- **1888–1890:** *Occupation of Mashonaland*. A BSAC project built upon the disputed Rudd Concession and implemented by the Pioneer Column. Rhodes designed and financed much of the scheme, but the resulting colonial territory was imposed through misrepresentation and armed occupation and resists being celebrated as a “work.”

- **1890–1902:** *Cape-to-Cairo telegraph and railway*. An imperial infrastructure project intended to link British-controlled territories across Africa. Only discontinuous sections were completed during Rhodes’s life, and the celebrated continuous railway remained an unrealized project rather than a made object.

- **1890–1896:** *South African federation*. Rhodes’s continuing project to unite southern African colonies and republics under British predominance. It never reached institutional form and should be catalogued as an unfinished political intention, not as a state he created.

- **1892:** *Franchise and Ballot Act*. Legislation promoted by Rhodes’s Cape ministry that raised property and literacy qualifications and disproportionately removed African voters from the rolls. It was collectively enacted law, not Rhodes’s individually authored text.

- **1893–1894:** *Conquest and administration of Matabeleland*. A corporate-colonial project conducted by the BSAC through war, land appropriation, and administrative rule. Rhodes directed and profited from it, but its violent, collective, and contested character makes “obra” an ethically unstable classification.

- **1894:** *Glen Grey Act*. A legislative system combining segregated African landholding, local councils, taxation, and pressure toward migrant labour. Rhodes was its principal political advocate, but the statute was drafted, debated, and enacted collectively and therefore cannot be assigned to him as sole author.

- **1895–1896:** *Jameson Raid*. A covert project to trigger an uprising and overthrow the South African Republic’s government, undertaken by Leander Starr Jameson and Rhodes’s associates with Rhodes’s support. Its precise chain of authorization remains disputed; the raid failed and ended Rhodes’s premiership.

- **1896:** *Matopos indabas*. A negotiated settlement associated with Rhodes’s meetings with Ndebele leaders during the uprising of 1896. It is better classified as a political performance and negotiated manifestation than as Rhodes’s work, since the settlement was made by multiple parties under conditions created by conquest and war.

- **1896–1902:** *Rhodesia*. The colonial territorial formation administered by the BSAC and named for Rhodes. Rhodes did not make a country by himself: this was an imposed and contested process involving company agents, British officials, settlers, and African polities whose sovereignty and land were taken.

- **1899–1900:** *Defence works during the Siege of Kimberley*. Rhodes used De Beers resources to organize labour, manufacture munitions, and build defensive equipment, including the field gun known as “Long Cecil.” The gun was designed by George Labram, not Rhodes, so it belongs here only as a financed company project and not as an object authored by Rhodes.

- **1899–1902:** *Rhodes Scholarships*. An educational and imperial-network project specified through Rhodes’s final will and implemented after his death by the Rhodes Trust. It is simultaneously a testamentary creation and a posthumous institution; later expansion beyond Rhodes’s racial, national, and gender restrictions is the work of subsequent trustees, not Rhodes.

### Architectural and landscape commissions

- **1891–1902:** *Groote Schuur estate*. Rhodes enlarged adjoining properties into a residential, agricultural, zoological, and scenic estate intended eventually for public and governmental use. This is a Gesamtkunstwerk of architecture, collecting, landscape, animals, and political symbolism, but its makers included Herbert Baker, gardeners, builders, labourers, and earlier occupants.

- **1893–1896:** *Groote Schuur house*. A reconstruction of the existing Rondebosch house commissioned by Rhodes from architect Herbert Baker after a fire. Rhodes was patron and program-maker, not architect; assigning the building to him without Baker’s name would misstate authorship.

- **1894–1896:** *The Woolsack*. A cottage on the Groote Schuur estate commissioned from Herbert Baker as a residence for writers and poets. It belongs to Rhodes’s cultural-patronage project, while the architectural authorship is Baker’s.

- **1890s:** *Groote Schuur zoo, lion enclosure, and landscape works*. A collection of animal enclosures, paths, plantations, and scenic interventions created across the estate. Several elements have uncertain individual dates and designers; the projected monumental “Lion House” remained unfinished at Rhodes’s death.

- **1897–1902:** *Matopos estate and burial landscape*. Rhodes acquired and designated land in the Matopos, including the hill he renamed “World’s View,” as a burial and memorial landscape. The place long pre-existed him and held Indigenous significance, so what Rhodes made was an appropriative memorial program—not the landscape itself.

- **1900–1904:** *Honoured Dead Memorial*. A memorial to those killed during the Siege of Kimberley, commissioned and financed by Rhodes and designed by Herbert Baker. It was unfinished when Rhodes died and completed posthumously; the architecture and sculpture therefore cannot be called Rhodes’s personal creation.
