# Johann Heinrich Wilhelm Tischbein: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier concerns the German painter, printmaker, collector, writer, and art administrator Johann Heinrich Wilhelm Tischbein (1751–1829), not the numerous similarly named members of the Tischbein artistic dynasty.

The family’s repeated use of “Johann,” “Heinrich,” and “Wilhelm,” together with the later shorthand “Tischbein,” has produced persistent cataloguing and attribution errors. Claims below are therefore distinguished as:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a contemporary record, archival catalogue, correspondence, or securely attributed object.
- **Reported fact:** stated by modern institutional scholarship but not independently verified here against the underlying manuscript.
- **Disputed attribution:** an artwork or document traditionally associated with Tischbein but questioned on chronological, stylistic, or documentary grounds.
- **Myth or reception history:** a story, symbolic interpretation, or popular identification whose historical effects are real even when its factual premise is weak.

Source references such as **[S1]** correspond in order to the URL list at the end.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Johann Heinrich Wilhelm Tischbein.

**Names and variants found in catalogues and records:**

- Wilhelm Tischbein
- Heinrich Wilhelm Tischbein
- Johann Wilhelm Tischbein
- Johann H. Tischbein
- J. H. W. Tischbein
- Henri or Guillaume Tischbein
- Guilelmus or Guillelmus Tischbein
- “Goethe-Tischbein”
- “Neapolitaner Tischbein” or “Neapolitan Tischbein”
- “Malerpoet,” a characterization attributed to contemporaries rather than a formal title

The Eutin death entry reportedly names him **Heinrich Wilhelm Tischbein**. “Goethe-Tischbein” is a retrospective identifying nickname derived from his portrait *Goethe in der römischen Campagna*. “Neapolitaner Tischbein” distinguishes him from the Kassel, Leipzig, Lübeck, and Hanau branches of the dynasty. [S1][S2][S3]

**Born:** 15 February 1751.

**Birthplace:** Haina, more precisely the hospital or former monastery settlement now called Haina (Kloster), in Hesse.

**Died:** 26 June 1829.

**Place of death:** Eutin, Holstein.

**Burial:** Eutin cemetery on Plöner Straße; the parish register records burial on 30 June 1829. February and July death dates found in older reference works are erroneous. [S1][S2]

**Religion:** Lutheran or Evangelical Protestant. [S1]

**Occupations and functions:**

- Portrait, history, landscape, and animal painter
- Draughtsman, watercolourist, etcher, and print designer
- Collector and dealer or vendor of works from his own collection
- Investigator and visual interpreter of classical antiquity
- Director of the Naples art academy
- Court painter and gallery inspector in Oldenburg-Eutin
- Drawing teacher and organizer of proposed art schools
- Writer and autobiographer
- Freemason

No patents, industrial inventions, corporations, or modern business holdings are associated with him.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and grandparents

Tischbein’s father was **Johann Conrad Tischbein** (1712–1778), a carpenter attached to the Haina hospital or monastic institution. Johann Conrad was one of the comparatively few men of his branch who did not become a professional painter.

His mother was **Helena Maria Christina Dehnhoff**, also recorded as Denhoff or Dehnhof (died 1781), of Haina.

His paternal grandparents were:

- **Johann Heinrich Tischbein** (1683–1764), hospital baker in Haina
- **Susanna Margaretha Hinsing** (1690–1772)

The extended Tischbein family became one of eighteenth-century Germany’s largest artistic dynasties. Five of the grandfather’s sons reportedly pursued painting. The family’s institutional base at Haina, court connections, workshop training, and movement among German principalities gave younger relatives an inherited professional network. [S1][S4]

### Artist relatives and siblings

Important relatives included:

- **Johann Heinrich Tischbein the Elder** (1722–1789), his uncle and first major teacher, court painter in Kassel.
- **Johann Jacob Tischbein** (1724/25–1791), uncle and teacher in Hamburg.
- **Johann Valentin Tischbein** (1715–1768), uncle and painter.
- **Anton Wilhelm Tischbein** (1730–1804), uncle, the “Hanauer Tischbein.”
- **Johann Friedrich August Tischbein** (1750–1812), cousin, later known as the “Leipziger Tischbein.”
- **Johann Heinrich Tischbein the Younger** (1742–1808), elder brother, painter and gallery inspector.
- **Heinrich Jacob Tischbein**, brother and painter; he appears with Wilhelm in the double portrait *Einer den anderen gemalt*.
- **Johanna Tischbein**, sister, who married the painter Johann Georg Pforr.
- **Franz Pforr** (1788–1812), nephew through Johanna; later an important Nazarene painter.

The house near the Haina monastery wall in which Wilhelm and his siblings are believed to have been born survives as a local memorial site. [S4][S5]

### Marriage and children

Tischbein married **Anna Martha Ketting**, also written Koetting (3 November 1775–17 November 1832), in Hamburg on 28 June 1806. She was a daughter of the Haina miller **Johann Reinhard Ketting** and **Eva Elisabeth Löwe**. [S1][S6]

Institutional and genealogical databases identify six children, although the names and treatment of an infant vary:

1. **Wilhelmine Karoline or Caroline Wilhelmine Tischbein**, reportedly born in 1800 and therefore before the recorded marriage; died 29 June 1872.
2. **Henriette Ernestine Tischbein**, born 3 August 1806 in Hamburg; died 2 April 1884 in Eutin.
3. **Angelika Tischbein**, apparently the child represented in one authority record; a genealogical compilation instead lists an unnamed child born and died in 1809.
4. **Konradine Katharina Maria Tischbein**, born 20 February 1810.
5. **Elisabeth Susanna Luise Tischbein**, born 1812, died 1906; married the Oldenburg court architect and painter **Heinrich Strack the Elder**, her second cousin.
6. **Peter Friedrich Ludwig Tischbein**, born 6 December 1813 in Eutin, died 5 October 1883; became a forester, entomologist, and naturalist.

The modern statement that the marriage produced “five daughters and one son” is consistent with the authority record, but the child born in 1800 and the 1809 infant require checking against Hamburg and Eutin parish registers before all maternity and legitimacy questions can be treated as conclusively resolved. There is no substantiated scandal in the surviving literature concerning the premarital birth. [S6][S7]

## Childhood and Early Life, 1751–1765

Tischbein grew up amid the workshops, stories, and professional contacts of the Haina painter family. His father’s work as an institutional carpenter placed the household within an artisanal rather than courtly economic setting. Later autobiographical accounts portrayed the family environment as naturally productive of artists, but these memoirs were written decades afterward and published posthumously.

No secure evidence found in the consulted sources documents formal elementary or university education. His formation was workshop-based. Later competence in literature, Homeric interpretation, antiquarian publishing, and correspondence developed through patrons, writers, collectors, and prolonged self-education rather than a recorded university course.

---

## Education and Formation, 1765–1779

### Kassel and Hamburg

At approximately fourteen, in 1765, Tischbein entered the Kassel workshop of his uncle **Johann Heinrich Tischbein the Elder**. In 1766 he moved to Hamburg and worked under another uncle, **Johann Jacob Tischbein**.

In Hamburg he also associated with his cousin **Johann Dietrich Lilly**, a painter, restorer, copyist, and art dealer. Lilly’s practice gave Tischbein access to old-master paintings and encouraged a shift from landscape toward history painting. Copying older works supplied an informal museum education at a time when systematic public art instruction was limited. [S1][S3]

### Wanderjahre

His early movements are reconstructed approximately as follows:

- Hannover during the late 1760s or early 1770s
- Bremen in 1771
- Holland in 1772 and again in 1773
- Haina and Hannover thereafter

Some accounts describe the Dutch journey as beginning in 1771; the NDB gives 1772 and 1773. The discrepancy likely reflects travel extending across more than one calendar year or reliance on differently dated reminiscences.

In Hannover in 1774, he met the poets **Johann Georg Jacobi** and **Johann Wilhelm Ludwig Gleim**. Modern scholarship credits these contacts with deepening his interest in Homer and classical antiquity. [S1]

### Berlin portrait practice

Through **Landgravine Philippine of Hesse-Kassel**, Tischbein obtained access to Berlin court society. From 1777 he worked successfully as a portraitist there.

On 18 May 1778, according to later biographical compilations, he was admitted to the Berlin Freemasons’ lodge **Zur Eintracht**. The admission is a documented institutional affiliation, not evidence by itself for covert political activity. Searches found later speculative claims that the pose or symbols of *Goethe in der Campagna* encode Masonic messages, but no consulted primary source establishes such an intended program.

## First Italian Journey and Zurich, 1779–1783

### Rome, 1779–1781

In 1779 Tischbein became the first recipient of a Kassel Academy travel stipend that enabled him to study in Italy. He travelled through Venice and Florence to Rome.

He studied:

- Ancient sculpture and monuments
- Raphael
- Early Italian painting
- Classical compositional models
- History painting
- The Roman landscape

The first Italian period marked his movement away from fashionable Rococo portraiture toward classicizing history painting. He attended or worked in the orbit of the sculptor **Alexander Trippel**, whose recommendation helped connect him to the Swiss theologian and physiognomist **Johann Caspar Lavater**.

Money ran short in 1781. Tischbein ended the Roman stay and travelled north rather than maintaining himself indefinitely in Italy. This is the clearest documented financial failure of his early career: artistic ambition exceeded the available stipend and income.

### Zurich, 1781–1782

In Zurich, Lavater employed Tischbein as a portrait draughtsman for the continuing project *Physiognomische Fragmente zur Beförderung der Menschenkenntnis und Menschenliebe*. Tischbein also entered the circle of the elderly writer and scholar **Johann Jakob Bodmer**.

Lavater’s belief that bodily and facial appearance could disclose character affected Tischbein’s portraiture and “character head” studies. Physiognomy was then presented as a system of human knowledge; later scholarship treats its claims as methodologically unreliable and historically entangled with stereotyping. Tischbein’s participation is documented as an artistic and intellectual collaboration, not as the invention of the theory.

Bodmer encouraged illustrations to Goethe’s *Götz von Berlichingen*. Through Lavater and correspondence, Tischbein came into indirect contact with **Johann Wolfgang Goethe** before the two met. [S1][S7]

## Second Italian Period, 1783–1799

### Return through patronage

Lavater and Goethe helped secure Tischbein a stipend from **Duke Ernst II of Saxe-Gotha-Altenburg**. He returned to Italy in 1783.

This patronage later became a source of strain. Goethe reportedly complained to Johann Gottfried Herder that Tischbein had received support for years without sending the duke the promised work. The underlying circumstances included Tischbein’s slow progress on ambitious history pictures and his diversion into other projects. This was a patronage dispute and complaint over non-performance, not a lawsuit or finding of fraud.

### Roman household and artistic circle

In Rome Tischbein shared or maintained lodgings on the Via del Corso with German-speaking artists including:

- Friedrich Bury
- Johann Heinrich Meyer
- Johann Heinrich Lips
- Johann Georg Schütz

These artists copied, drew, exchanged commissions, and accompanied foreign visitors. Tischbein’s Roman network joined court patronage, the expatriate artist colony, antiquarian study, and the early international market for classical imagery.

### Goethe, 1786–1787

Goethe arrived in Rome incognito in October 1786, travelling under the name “Philipp Möller.” Tischbein received him and initially shared his lodging. Their relationship included:

- Daily companionship
- Conversation on art, literature, and antiquity
- Tischbein’s instruction or assistance in Goethe’s drawing
- Numerous portrait studies
- Shared travel from Rome to Naples in 1787
- Plans for an onward journey to Sicily

Tischbein began studies for *Goethe in der römischen Campagna* in 1786 and worked on the large canvas in 1787. The portrait shows Goethe reclining among classical remains, with the Campagna presented as a landscape of memory, authorship, and antiquity.

Their friendship cooled during 1787. Tischbein settled in Naples and did not accompany Goethe to Sicily; Goethe instead travelled with the draughtsman Christoph Heinrich Kniep. Goethe felt neglected and subsequently made disparaging comments about Tischbein. The unfulfilled Gotha commission compounded the dissatisfaction. Their relationship was not permanently severed but remained intermittent for decades. [S1][S8][S9]

### Naples

Tischbein moved to Naples in July 1787. In 1789 he became director of the city’s royal academy of art, today the Accademia di Belle Arti di Napoli. His appointment brought a salary, institutional authority, students, court access, and a stable base for large publishing projects.

He met or worked with:

- **Jacob Philipp Hackert**, court landscape painter
- **Georg Abraham Hackert**
- **Sir William Hamilton**, British envoy and major antiquities collector
- Members of the Bourbon royal family and court
- Engravers, draughtsmen, and publishers involved with vase reproductions

Tischbein’s academy directorship lasted until the revolutionary and military crisis of 1799.

### Hamilton vase project

Hamilton commissioned or collaborated with Tischbein on the publication of his second collection of ancient painted vases. The resulting work bore the lengthy English title *Collection of Engravings from Ancient Vases of Greek Workmanship, Discovered in Sepulchres in the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies… Now in the Possession of Sir William Hamilton*.

The publication was issued in four volumes, with title-page dates beginning in 1791; bibliographical research indicates that the first volume may not actually have reached the market until 1794. Tischbein served as publisher/editor and supervised or supplied outline reproductions. Some work was executed by pupils and associated engravers, so the plates should not all be treated as drawings or engravings made solely by his hand. [S10][S11]

The project affected:

- Archaeological visualization
- Neoclassical design
- The market for Greek vases
- The transmission of ancient figure types to European artists and manufacturers

The reproductions were selective. Vase forms, dimensions, colour, damage, and stylistic particulars were not always recorded to modern archaeological standards.

### Homer project

By December 1792 Tischbein described a project for presenting Homer through images derived from ancient objects. He assembled drawings and copperplates of figures, narrative scenes, ornaments, and imagined landscapes.

A surviving British Museum album contains 81 proof prints on 78 sheets. Oldenburg archival inventories list 79 and 88 plates in his Naples studio in 1799. Recent research concluded that their arrangement was deliberate and that all were associated with the projected *Homer nach Antiken gezeichnet*, although only some reached the published instalments.

Tischbein struggled to obtain a suitable commentator. After returning to Germany, **Christian Gottlob Heyne** supplied explanations for early instalments. Later continuations involved **Ludwig Schorn** and the Cotta publishing house. [S12][S13]

### Flight from Naples, 1799

French troops occupied Naples and the Parthenopean Republic was proclaimed in January 1799. As an appointee of the Bourbon royal institution, Tischbein left the city.

He saved and carried at least part of his copperplate stock north. The flight ended a decade-long directorship and dispersed or stranded works and property. Available sources do not document imprisonment, formal prosecution, political violence committed by Tischbein, or a party role in either the Bourbon reaction or republican government.

## Return to Germany, 1799–1808

Tischbein travelled through Kassel, Hannover, and Göttingen.

### Göttingen

He established or participated in a **drawing academy for women** in Göttingen. His nephew Wilhelm Unger reportedly assisted between 1799 and 1801. The school is significant because women were commonly excluded from full academy training, although its finances, enrolment, and exact institutional status remain incompletely documented.

The Göttingen scholar **Christian Gottlob Heyne** collaborated on the first published sections of *Homer nach Antiken gezeichnet*.

### Hamburg

Tischbein settled in Hamburg in 1801. One source says he married that year, but the securely reported civil or church date is 28 June 1806.

He formed ties with **Philipp Otto Runge**, **Johann Friedrich Overbeck**, and younger artists associated with early Romanticism. Tischbein proposed a permanent drawing or art school in Hamburg. The Senate declined to finance it. This institutional project therefore failed.

His activity nevertheless included:

- Portrait commissions
- Literary and pedagogical projects
- Preparing the Homer publication
- Contacts with merchants and collectors
- Selling or negotiating the sale of his painting collection
- Family life

In 1804, **Peter Friedrich Ludwig**, regent and later duke of Oldenburg, purchased Tischbein’s painting collection as a foundation for an Oldenburg gallery. When Tischbein entered the duke’s service four years later, he became inspector of a collection that had previously been his own. The surviving sources do not give a reliable purchase price.

---

## Eutin and Oldenburg, 1808–1829

In 1808 Peter Friedrich Ludwig appointed Tischbein **court painter and gallery inspector**. Tischbein moved to Eutin, the ruler’s summer residence, and remained there for the rest of his life.

### Court duties

His responsibilities included:

- Caring for and arranging the ducal gallery
- Teaching drawing to members of the ruling family and court society
- Producing portraits and history paintings
- Designing decorative cycles
- Advising younger artists preparing for academy study
- Contributing to palace interiors

Pupils or students associated with him in Eutin included Ferdinand Flor, Nicolaus Lescow, Carl Andreas Goos, and Jacob Gensler.

### Decorative and manufacturing work

Tischbein collaborated with the Eutin stove manufacturer Niemann. He supplied classical and pictorial designs for ceramic stove tiles. Forty-three decorated stoves or related examples reportedly survive at Eutin Castle. This was applied art and workshop collaboration rather than ownership of the factory.

### Homer Room

For the Oldenburg court he devised the classicizing pictorial program now called the **Homer-Zimmer**. Its images connected palace decoration, ancient epic, moral exempla, and princely education.

### Idyll cycle

In 1819–1820 Tischbein produced a cycle of more than forty small paintings—often counted as forty-four—showing nymphs, shepherds, centaurs, fauns, dancing figures, and other pastoral or antique subjects.

In 1821 he sent Goethe watercolour summaries or sketches. Goethe supplied poems and prose descriptions, published in 1822 as *Wilhelm Tischbeins Idyllen*. Goethe did not inspect the original room; his literary response was based on the transmitted images. The collaboration marked their late reconciliation. [S1][S14]

### Writing and autobiography

Tischbein began recording autobiographical recollections in the 1810s, variously dated from 1810 or 1815. The work combined eyewitness memories, artistic anecdotes, moral tales, and retrospective self-fashioning.

It remained unpublished during his lifetime. Carl G. W. Schiller edited it posthumously as *Aus meinem Leben* in 1861. Because the printed text passed through an editor and appeared thirty-two years after Tischbein’s death, it must be used as an autobiographical source with attention to editorial intervention and retrospective memory.

## Artistic, Intellectual, and Religious Development

### Rococo to classicism

Tischbein began within the portrait and decorative traditions of his family. Roman study shifted his emphasis toward:

- Monumental history painting
- Classical bodily types
- Moral and political exempla
- Homeric subject matter
- Idealized landscape

### Physiognomy

Lavater’s physiognomic project encouraged Tischbein to connect visible form with character. His later animal-head and human-character studies similarly explored analogies between bodily appearance, temperament, and moral identity.

These works are historically important evidence of eighteenth-century classificatory thought. Their implied science of character is not supported by modern empirical psychology.

### Classicism and Romanticism

His mature work does not fit neatly into one category. It includes:

- Neoclassical moral history
- Arcadian and Homeric imagery
- Dutch old-master reception
- Anecdotal and comic drawing
- Animal studies
- Romantic or proto-Romantic interest in inwardness, poetry, and symbolic narrative
- Applied decorative art

The 2020–2024 Oldenburg research project explicitly reconsidered his position “between” inherited stylistic boundaries. [S15]

### Religion and Freemasonry

Tischbein remained institutionally Lutheran. His classical scenes coexist with Christian works and moral allegories. His documented Masonic membership places him within a late-Enlightenment network, but the consulted sources do not demonstrate sustained lodge office, revolutionary politics, occult practice, or participation in a conspiratorial organization.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Johann Wolfgang Goethe

Friend, housemate, travelling companion, sitter, intermediary to a patron, eventual critic, and late collaborator. The relationship passed through enthusiasm, estrangement, and reconciliation.

### Johann Caspar Lavater

Employer, intellectual influence, and connector to patrons and Goethe. Tischbein supplied portrait material for Lavater’s physiognomic undertaking.

### Johann Jakob Bodmer

Literary elder who encouraged historical-literary illustration, including designs related to *Götz von Berlichingen*.

### Duke Ernst II of Saxe-Gotha-Altenburg

Financed Tischbein’s second Italian period. The failure to send expected work generated complaints and damaged Tischbein’s standing with Goethe.

### Sir William Hamilton

Antiquities collector, British diplomat, and principal collaborator or patron for the vase publication. Hamilton’s collection gave Tischbein access to a major body of ancient painted imagery.

### Jacob Philipp and Georg Abraham Hackert

Colleagues in Naples. Hackert’s artistic, court, and manufacturing networks overlapped with Tischbein’s antiquarian publishing.

### Christian Gottlob Heyne

Classical scholar and commentator on early parts of *Homer nach Antiken gezeichnet*.

### Peter Friedrich Ludwig of Oldenburg

Buyer of Tischbein’s collection, employer, court patron, and sponsor of his long Eutin period.

### Philipp Otto Runge

Friend or close artistic contact in Hamburg. Runge and the younger Romantic circle influenced Tischbein’s later reception and possibly aspects of his late work.

### Duchess Anna Amalia of Saxe-Weimar

Correspondent. Their surviving correspondence is an important biographical source, later edited by Friedrich von Alten.

### Students and workshop associates

Numerous prints issued under Tischbein’s direction involved pupils or specialist engravers. “Published by Tischbein,” “designed by Tischbein,” and “engraved by Tischbein” are consequently not interchangeable authorship statements.

## Significant Successes

- Entry into Berlin court portraiture.
- First Kassel Academy stipend holder sent to Italy.
- Patronage from Lavater, Ernst II, Hamilton, and Peter Friedrich Ludwig.
- Appointment as director of the Naples academy.
- Completion and supervision of a major illustrated publication of Hamilton’s vases.
- Development of the ambitious Homer print project.
- Long court appointment in Eutin.
- Creation of the Homer Room and Idyll cycle.
- Production of *Goethe in der römischen Campagna*, one of the most reproduced German portraits.
- Establishment of a substantial archive of paintings, drawings, manuscripts, letters, and printed material.

---

## Failures, Reversals, and Unresolved Projects

- The first Roman stay ended because funds ran out.
- He did not satisfy the Gotha patron on schedule; Goethe complained about the absence of promised work.
- The planned Sicily journey with Goethe did not occur.
- The friendship with Goethe deteriorated during and after the Italian journey.
- The Naples directorship ended in flight during the 1799 revolution and invasion.
- The proposed Hamburg art school failed to obtain Senate support.
- The Homer publication remained incomplete and complicated. Cotta’s later continuation used plates acquired from the estate.
- *Goethe in der Campagna* appears to have remained unfinished by Tischbein and may have been completed by another hand.
- No comprehensive current catalogue raisonné exists; attribution problems persist.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Patronage dispute: documented complaint

Goethe reportedly complained to Herder that Tischbein had drawn the Duke of Gotha’s support for several years without sending a finished picture. The documentary core is a patron’s unmet expectation and Goethe’s complaint. No evidence found shows a fraud prosecution, civil suit, or formal dismissal for embezzlement.

### Goethe estrangement: documented relationship conflict

Goethe’s dissatisfaction involved Tischbein’s move to Naples, abandonment of the shared Sicily plan, and concerns over reliability. Tischbein’s perspective survives in later reminiscence and correspondence. The episode should not be reduced to a single betrayal narrative; artistic opportunity, court employment, finances, and competing projects all contributed.

### Academy departure: political displacement, not documented prosecution

Tischbein left Naples during the French occupation and Parthenopean upheaval of 1799. No evidence found establishes arrest, imprisonment, collaboration in political executions, espionage, or a criminal charge.

### Freemasonry and conspiracy claims

**Documented fact:** Tischbein joined the lodge *Zur Eintracht* in Berlin in 1778.

**Unverified interpretive claim:** internet discussions and symbolic readings have proposed Masonic coding in *Goethe in der Campagna*. The consulted discussions do not supply a contemporary statement by Tischbein, preparatory program, or lodge document demonstrating such intent.

### Criminal and financial misconduct

The dedicated searches combining Tischbein’s name with scandal, accusation, fraud, lawsuit, corruption, conspiracy, and criticism—including searches restricted toward 2025–2026 material—found no substantiated criminal prosecution, fraud proceeding, corruption investigation, money-laundering allegation, abuse accusation, or lawsuit involving Tischbein.

This is not proof that every private dispute is known; it is a finding that the accessible documentary and scholarly record is dominated by patronage, attribution, publication, and relationship controversies rather than criminal allegations.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “Tischbein’s Mozart”

**Story:** A portrait traditionally attributed to Tischbein was widely reproduced as an authentic likeness of Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart.

**Appearance and circulation:** The identification became prominent during the nineteenth century. A later owner promoted the portrait through copies and reproductions.

**Evidence against:** Mozart’s surviving son reportedly rejected the identification as early as 1856. More recent comparison with a drawing labelled Johann Franz Xaver Sterkel suggests that the sitter may be Sterkel or another unidentified person. A drawing dated Mainz, April 1788, cannot have been made there by Goethe-Tischbein because he was then in Italy. If a Tischbein relative made it, Anton Wilhelm Tischbein is chronologically more plausible.

**Status:** Disputed attribution and sitter identification. There is no documented meeting between Goethe-Tischbein and Mozart.

**Effect:** Reproduction helped shape popular expectations of Mozart’s appearance even after the identification was challenged. [S16]

### The perfectly finished *Goethe in the Campagna*

**Story:** Tischbein completed the famous monumental portrait exactly as it is now displayed.

**Evidence:** Technical and documentary discussions indicate that the picture was unfinished when Tischbein left Naples. The head, hat, cloak, and one foot appear more fully resolved than other parts. Another hand may have completed areas before its later sale. Tischbein did not sign it, and neither he nor Goethe is known to have seen the completed state.

**Status:** Tischbein’s authorship of the conception and principal portrait is secure; sole execution of every visible passage is disputed.

**Effect:** Reproductions converted a complicated studio object into a seamless national icon.

### The anatomically impossible Goethe

Critics and viewers have long observed that Goethe appears exceptionally long-legged and that the proportions seem inconsistent. Explanations include:

- An artistic error
- The joining of studies made at different stages
- Completion by another hand
- Deliberate monumentalization
- Symbolic distortion to make Goethe larger than ordinary life

No single explanation is conclusively established. The proportions became part of the painting’s folklore and parody history. [S9][S17]

### “Goethe-Tischbein” as his contemporary identity

The nickname is now ubiquitous but risks reducing a diverse seventy-year life to a single friendship and painting. “Neapolitaner Tischbein” better reflects how he was distinguished within the family in some older literature. Modern Oldenburg scholarship has explicitly attempted to recover the artist beyond the Goethe portrait.

### Arcadian Goethe as propaganda

The portrait has been interpreted as presenting Goethe as:

- A universal or “world” author
- A modern heir to classical antiquity
- A culturally sovereign German
- An Enlightenment intellectual whose education confers authority

These are reception-history interpretations. There is no evidence that the painting was commissioned as state propaganda. Its later use in museums, schoolbooks, anniversaries, and national literary culture gave it functions beyond the original private artistic exchange.

### Autobiographical self-mythology

*Aus meinem Leben* presents encounters and experiences through an elderly artist’s memory. It is indispensable evidence but not a stenographic record. Anecdotal shaping, hindsight, moral exemplification, omissions, and posthumous editing must be considered when it conflicts with dated correspondence or institutional records.

## Works by or Published Under Tischbein’s Name

A complete catalogue of individual paintings and drawings is not presently available. The following distinguishes major authored works, collaborative publications, posthumous editions, and representative artworks.

### Publications and literary works

1. **Collection of Engravings from Ancient Vases of Greek Workmanship… Now in the Possession of Sir William Hamilton**  
   Naples, title-page dates 1791–1795 and later volume dates extending into the early nineteenth century; four volumes. Tischbein was publisher/editor and artistic supervisor. Hamilton supplied collection ownership and observations; pupils and engravers participated. The first volume may actually have circulated only from 1794.

2. **Têtes de différents animaux dessinées d’après nature**  
   Naples, 1790s. Animal-head studies issued as prints. Exact issue sequence and bibliographical history vary among catalogues.

3. **Homer nach Antiken gezeichnet**  
   Göttingen, Heinrich Dieterich, instalments 1801–1805 or 1806; explanations by Christian Gottlob Heyne. Further Cotta instalments appeared in Stuttgart in 1821–1823 with text associated with Ludwig Schorn. The projected series was not fully realized. Plates included Tischbein’s own etched or engraved work and designs after ancient objects.

4. **Der Schwachmatikus und seine vier Brüder**  
   Fragment published in *Vaterländisches Museum*, volume 2, issue 1, Hamburg, 1811. Later expanded or edited under the title *Eselsgeschichte oder der Schwachmatikus und seine vier Brüder, der Sanguinikus, Cholerikus, Melancholikus und Phlegmatikus*, with twelve donkey representations; modern edited exhibition edition, Oldenburg, 1987.

5. **Wilhelm Tischbeins Idyllen**  
   1822. Collaborative publication: Tischbein supplied watercolour images or summaries from his Idyll cycle; Goethe wrote the accompanying verses and prose commentary. It is not solely authored by either man.

6. **Aus meinem Leben**  
   Written principally during the 1810s; posthumously edited by C. G. W. Schiller and published in Braunschweig by C. A. Schwetschke & Sohn, 1861, two volumes. Later edition by Lothar Brieger, 1922; annotated edition by Kuno Mittelstädt, 1956. Genuine autobiographical source, but posthumously edited rather than an author-supervised publication.

7. **Correspondence with Duchess Anna Amalia and other contemporaries**  
   Letters are genuine manuscript works but largely published in edited collections. Friedrich von Alten’s *Aus Tischbeins Leben und Briefwechsel mit Amalia Herzogin zu Sachsen-Weimar* appeared in Leipzig in 1872.

- *Goethe am Fenster der römischen Wohnung am Corso*, 1787, watercolour, chalk, pen, and pencil, Frankfurt Goethe Museum.

### Attribution cautions

- “Tischbein” without initials is insufficient attribution.
- Plates “published by” Tischbein were not necessarily engraved by him.
- Works associated with the Hamilton publication may be by pupils working under his direction.
- Several alleged portraits—including the so-called Mozart portrait—are insecure.
- A current comprehensive catalogue raisonné does not exist.

## Illness, Accident, and Death

No well-supported record was found of a chronic illness, addiction, psychiatric crisis, disabling accident, or specific terminal disease.

Tischbein died in Eutin on 26 June 1829, aged seventy-eight. The church entry gives the burial date as 30 June. The consulted summaries do not state a medically documented cause of death. Claims giving 26 February or 26 July are contradicted by the Eutin parish record.

Anna Martha Tischbein survived him until 17 November 1832.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Nineteenth century

Tischbein’s memoirs and correspondence established him as an anecdotal witness to Goethe’s Italian period. The Campagna portrait became increasingly famous through copies and prints; the original reportedly was not publicly prominent until the nineteenth century and entered the Städel as a gift in 1887.

His independent reputation suffered because:

- He was classified principally as “Goethe’s painter.”
- Neoclassical history painting went out of fashion.
- His work was dispersed.
- Publications remained incomplete.
- The many Tischbein relatives encouraged misattribution.
- His literary and applied-art output was less accessible than the portrait.

### National and educational icon

The Campagna portrait became a standard schoolbook and museum image of Goethe. It shaped the visual mythology of German classicism: travel, ruins, cultivated authority, Italy, and the solitary author amid antiquity.

### Twentieth- and twenty-first-century reassessment

Scholarship progressively recovered Tischbein as:

- A mediator between art and archaeology
- An antiquarian publisher
- A printmaker
- A collector
- A writer
- An applied designer
- A teacher
- A participant in physiognomic culture
- A transitional figure between classicism and Romanticism

The Oldenburg acquisition of the family estate in the 1980s and the 2020–2024 research project materially expanded the evidentiary base. The announced 2026–2027 retrospective emphasizes his anecdotal invention and work beyond Goethe.

### Continuing attribution problems

His name remains attached to works that may belong to relatives or later copyists. The “Tischbein Mozart” dispute is the clearest popular example. Recent research on the British Museum print album has also revised earlier beliefs about which plates belonged to Hamilton’s vases and which were conceived for Homer.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere contemporaneity, common nationality, or residence in the same city is excluded.

### Johann Wolfgang von Goethe — documented fact

Tischbein corresponded with Goethe before meeting him, received him in Rome in late 1786, shared lodgings with him, drew and painted him, taught or assisted him in drawing, and travelled with him from Rome to Naples in 1787. Their friendship deteriorated over the abandoned Sicily plan and Tischbein’s handling of the Gotha patronage but revived by 1817. Their late collaboration produced *Wilhelm Tischbeins Idyllen* in 1822.

### Johann Friedrich Overbeck — reported fact

Overbeck entered Tischbein’s Hamburg orbit during the first decade of the nineteenth century while preparing for his own artistic career. Institutional biographies describe direct contact concerning Tischbein’s proposed drawing school and the younger Hamburg artists. The precise frequency and intimacy of their meetings require fuller examination of correspondence.

### Franz Pforr — documented family relationship

The Nazarene painter Franz Pforr was Tischbein’s nephew, the son of Tischbein’s sister Johanna and the painter Johann Georg Pforr. This was a family and artistic-dynastic connection. The consulted sources do not establish that Tischbein was Pforr’s principal teacher.

### Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart — disputed attribution and myth, not a documented personal connection

A portrait traditionally called the “Tischbein Mozart” circulated widely. Mozart’s son questioned the identification in 1856, and chronological evidence makes Goethe-Tischbein’s authorship of a related Mainz drawing impossible. No documented meeting between Tischbein and Mozart was found. This is an Atlas connection only at the level of posthumous visual mythology, not biography.

### Arthur Schopenhauer — reported intellectual-artistic reception

A Schleswig-Holstein institutional account states that Arthur Schopenhauer was among writers who responded to Tischbein’s invitation to complete or accompany images with words. The precise work and documentary circumstances were not established in the sources consulted here; the connection should therefore remain labelled reported rather than fully documented.

### Johann Friedrich Overbeck and Franz Pforr’s broader Nazarene circle

The sources support individual contact with Overbeck and kinship with Pforr. They do not support turning Tischbein into a formal member of the later Nazarene brotherhood.

### No substantiated connection found

No direct, source-supported personal connection was established with Napoleon Bonaparte. Napoleonic warfare affected Tischbein’s political and professional environment, but no meeting, correspondence, patronage, or institutional relationship was found.
