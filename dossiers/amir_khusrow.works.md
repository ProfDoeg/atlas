## Works by Amir Khusrow Dehlavi

### Chronological lyric collections

- **c. 1271–73:** *Tuḥfat al-Ṣighar* (“Gift of Youth”). His first Persian *dīvān*: a deliberately organized collection of early qaṣīdas, ghazals, elegies, and other lyric forms, rather than a single continuous “book.” Sources differ between 1271 and about 1273 because they date either its poems or the collection’s compilation. [Schwartz](https://doi.org/10.1163/18747167-bja10062); [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/amir-kosrow-poet/)
- **c. 1279–84:** *Wasaṭ al-Ḥayāt* (“Middle of Life”). His second Persian *dīvān*, arranged as the work of a later life-stage; scholarship variously dates its compilation to 1279 or about 1284. [Schwartz](https://doi.org/10.1163/18747167-bja10062); [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/amir-kosrow-poet/)
- **1293–94:** *Ghurrat al-Kamāl* (“Prime/New Moon of Perfection”). His third Persian *dīvān*, with an extensive autobiographical and literary-critical prose preface; the preface also supplies important evidence that he composed in Hindavi. [Schwartz](https://doi.org/10.1163/18747167-bja10062); [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/41469/chapter/352858128)
- **1317:** *Baqiyya-yi Naqiyya* (“Pure Remnants”). His fourth chronological Persian *dīvān*, gathering later lyric and court poetry rather than constituting a single narrative work. [Schwartz](https://doi.org/10.1163/18747167-bja10062)
- **1325:** *Nihāyat al-Kamāl* (“The Perfect Conclusion” or “Utmost Perfection”). His fifth and final Persian *dīvān*, apparently assembled near the end of his life. The surviving five-*dīvān* corpus has never received a complete modern critical edition, so its exact contents and boundaries remain editorially unstable. [Schwartz](https://doi.org/10.1163/18747167-bja10062)

### The *Khamsa*: a five-poem project

- **1298–1302:** *Khamsa-yi Khusraw* (“Khusrow’s Quintet”). A consciously unified project of five Persian narrative *masnavīs* answering Niẓāmī’s *Panj Ganj*; it is simultaneously five independent poems and a larger obra, and later illustrated manuscripts made it a text-image Gesamtkunstwerk that should not be confused with Khusrow’s own original material object. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kamsa-amir-kosrow/)
- **1298–99:** *Maṭlaʿ al-Anwār* (“Rising Place of Lights”). The first poem of the *Khamsa*: a didactic *masnavī* of twenty discourses and illustrative tales, reportedly completed in a fortnight. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kamsa-amir-kosrow/)
- **1299:** *Shīrīn o Khusraw*. The second *Khamsa* poem, a romance about the Sasanian king Khusraw Parvīz and Shīrīn that deliberately reverses the order of the lovers’ names in Niẓāmī’s title. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kamsa-amir-kosrow/)
- **1299:** *Majnūn o Laylī*. The third *Khamsa* poem, Khusrow’s compact and substantially reshaped version of the Arabic-Persian romance of Qays/Majnūn and Laylī. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kamsa-amir-kosrow/)
- **1299–1300:** *Āʾīna-yi Iskandarī* (“The Alexandrine Mirror”). The fourth *Khamsa* poem, an Alexander romance that makes its hero an adventurer and investigator rather than Niẓāmī’s prophet-philosopher. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kamsa-amir-kosrow/)
- **1301–02:** *Hasht Bihisht* (“Eight Paradises”). The concluding *Khamsa* poem: an eight-story frame narrative about Bahrām Gūr, seven princesses, seven colored pavilions, and the days and planets. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kamsa-amir-kosrow/)

### Historical and courtly narrative poems

- **1289:** *Qirān al-Saʿdayn* (“Conjunction of the Two Auspicious Stars”). A Persian historical *masnavī* commemorating the meeting and reconciliation of Sultan Muʿizz al-Dīn Kayqubād and his father Bughra Khan; it mixes court panegyric, urban description, eyewitness history, and literary performance. [Mirza, SOAS](https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/output/394759); [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kamsa-amir-kosrow/)
- **1291:** *Miftāḥ al-Futūḥ* (“Key to the Victories”). A historical-panegyric *masnavī* celebrating four early victories of Jalāl al-Dīn Khaljī; it resists a clean division between chronicle and commissioned praise-poem. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kamsa-amir-kosrow/); [Inquiries Journal](https://www.inquiriesjournal.com/articles/1372/was-amir-khusrau-a-historian-examining-his-work-in-a-contemporary-framework)
- **1315–16, subsequently enlarged:** *Duval Rānī va Khiẓr Khān*, also called *ʿĀshiqā*. A historical romance in *masnavī* form about the Gujarati princess Duval Rānī and ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Khaljī’s son Khiẓr Khan. Khusrow enlarged the poem after Khiẓr Khan’s violent end, making its final form a revised project rather than the product of one composition date. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/amir-kosrow-poet/)
- **1318:** *Nuh Sipihr* (“Nine Heavens”). A nine-part historical-panegyric *masnavī*, each “sphere” employing a different meter, ostensibly celebrating Quṭb al-Dīn Mubārak Shah but also describing India’s languages, customs, sciences, fauna, music, and festivals. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/amir-kosrow-poet/)
- **1320–21:** *Tughlaq-nāma* (“Book of the Tughlaqs”). Khusrow’s last known historical *masnavī*, narrating Ghiyāth al-Dīn Tughlaq’s defeat of Khusraw Khan and accession. Sources variously give 1320 or 1321, reflecting the event, composition, and calendrical conversion rather than evidence for distinct works. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kamsa-amir-kosrow/); [Inquiries Journal](https://www.inquiriesjournal.com/articles/1372/was-amir-khusrau-a-historian-examining-his-work-in-a-contemporary-framework)

### Prose works and compilation projects

- **1283–1319:** *Iʿjāz-i Khusrawī*, also called *Rasāʾil al-Iʿjāz* (“Treatises on Inimitability”). A five-volume Persian epistolary and rhetorical obra containing model letters, literary exercises, and instruction in ornate composition: the first four treatises were completed in 1283 and the fifth in 1319. It is partly protocol, partly anthology, and partly a demonstration of the very verbal virtuosity it teaches. [Schwartz](https://doi.org/10.1163/18747167-bja10062); [McGill digitized edition](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:I%CA%BBj%C4%81z-i_Khusrav%C4%AB_%28IA_McGillLibrary-rbsc_isl_ijaz-i-khusravi_folioPK6335A651876_v1-2-19992%29.pdf)
- **1311–12:** *Khazāʾin al-Futūḥ*, also called *Tārīkh-i ʿAlāʾī* (“Treasures of Victory” / “History of ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn”). An ornate Persian prose chronicle and victory-panegyric covering ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Khaljī’s reign and campaigns through 711 AH; its rhetoric and courtly purpose prevent it from functioning as a neutral historical report. [Critical-edition description](https://www.scribd.com/document/705231704/Khusrau-Khazain-Al-Futuh-Ed-Mirza-Rpt-1976); [Dale](https://uzbekliterature.uz/sites/default/files/babur_stephen_dale_2.pdf)
- **Traditionally 1314–19; attribution disputed:** *Afżal al-Fawāʾid* (“Most Excellent Benefits”). A *malfūẓāt* compilation presenting the discourses of Khusrow’s Sufi master Niẓām al-Dīn Awliyā. Mirza’s standard bibliography accepted it, but later scholarship has questioned its authorship and internal historical reliability; it must therefore be treated as an attributed compilation, not an uncontested autograph work. [Mirza, SOAS](https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/output/394759); [Rekhta edition record](https://www.rekhta.org/ebooks/detail/afzal-ul-fawaid-ameer-khusrau-ebooks/)

### Hindavi poems, songs, and riddling forms

- **Before 1293–94:** *Hindavi verses embedded in or attested by the preface to Ghurrat al-Kamāl*. These scattered vernacular or Persian-Hindavi compositions are the safest textual evidence for Khusrow’s Hindavi making; they were never organized by him into a surviving Hindavi *dīvān*. [Mirza](https://www.scribd.com/document/813809623/Mohammad-Wahid-Mirza-The-Life-and-Works-of-Amir-Khusrau-Baptist-Mission-Press-1936); [Rare Book Society of India](https://www.rarebooksocietyofindia.org/book_archive/196174216674_10152039443146675.pdf)
- **Dates unknown; corpus and attribution unstable:** *Hindavi songs, dohas, riddles, kah-mukarnīs, do-sukhanas, macaronic ghazals, and devotional compositions traditionally transmitted under Khusrow’s name*. This is not one fixed text and may not be a “work” in the bibliographical sense: it is an expanding oral-manuscript manifestation in which authentic pieces, adaptations, anonymous folk material, and later attributions cannot consistently be separated. [Perveen and Ahmed](https://ojs.aiou.edu.pk/index.php/jssh/article/download/2210/1760/5649); [Mirza](https://www.scribd.com/document/813809623/Mohammad-Wahid-Mirza-The-Life-and-Works-of-Amir-Khusrau-Baptist-Mission-Press-1936)
- **Date unknown; probably composite and not singly authored:** *Khāliq Bārī*. A multilingual versified glossary or pedagogical vocabulary long attributed to Khusrow. Manuscript, linguistic, and structural evidence makes both a single original text and a single author doubtful; Walter Hakala argues that asking which individual wrote “the” work may itself misdescribe a composite textual tradition. [Hakala](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0019464614550764)
- **Date unknown; attribution rejected or unsubstantiated:** *Qiṣṣa-yi Chahār Darvēsh* (“Tale of the Four Dervishes”). Popular tradition makes Khusrow the teller or Persian author of this story-cycle, but it is absent from his secure medieval corpus and survives through much later recensions; it should be recorded as an attribution history, not as something demonstrably made by him. [Mirza](https://www.scribd.com/document/813809623/Mohammad-Wahid-Mirza-The-Life-and-Works-of-Amir-Khusrau-Baptist-Mission-Press-1936)

### Musical manifestations and invention traditions

- **Late thirteenth–early fourteenth century; no recoverable catalogue:** *Compositions and performance practices for Chishti samāʿ*. Khusrow was remembered as a poet-musician, and Persian and Hindavi material attributed to him remains in hereditary qawwāl performance; because medieval performances were not preserved in notation or recordings, this is a living repertorial manifestation rather than a finite, securely attributable list of compositions. [Brown](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/42286233/2004BrownSchofieldPhD.pdf); [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/amir-kosrow-poet/)
- **Traditional attribution; historically unverified:** *Qawl, qalbāna, tarāna, khayāl, and qawwālī as musical inventions or transformations*. Later tradition credits Khusrow with creating or synthesizing these vocal genres, but his own writings do not document most of these inventions and the surviving forms developed over centuries; they are better classified as foundation legends attached to his musical persona than as securely datable individual works. [Brown](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/42286233/2004BrownSchofieldPhD.pdf)
- **Traditional attribution; not accepted as a documented creation:** *Sitar and tabla*. Khusrow is popularly said to have invented both instruments, but they appear in recognizable form only in later textual and iconographic evidence. These are posthumous invention claims, not traceable obras by Khusrow. [Sikh Formations](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17448727.2015.1023105)
- **Traditional attribution; individual claims uncertain:** *Named melodies and rāgas associated with Khusrow*. Later musicological sources credit him with such forms or melodies as sāzgārī, aiman, ʿushshāq, muwāfiq, zilaf, farghāna, sarparda, and firodast, among others; because these claims arise in sources centuries after him and are absent from his writings, they cannot be entered as securely surviving compositions. [Survey summarizing Mirza and later musicological evidence](https://pragyata.com/amir-khusraus-contributions-to-indian-music-a-preliminary-survey/)

### Corpus limits, lost works, and false attributions

- **Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries; extent unknown:** *Lost or unidentified writings*. Pre-modern biographers sometimes credited Khusrow with ninety-nine or even many more books and hundreds of thousands of verses, but the standard scholarly corpus consists of five *dīvāns*, five *Khamsa* poems, five independent historical *masnavīs*, three principal prose attributions, and a problematic Hindavi corpus. Mirza concluded that some works may have disappeared but rejected the inflated traditional totals; unnamed losses cannot responsibly be converted into invented titles. [Mirza, SOAS](https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/output/394759); [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/amir-kosrow-poet/)
- **Later attributions:** *Miscellaneous Persian, Hindavi, and Urdu poems circulated under Khusrow’s name*. No closed catalogue can treat every such poem as his creation: later anthologies, oral performance, scribal supplementation, and devotional authority repeatedly enlarged the corpus. Where a piece lacks early manuscript support or a place in the five *dīvāns*, attribution remains a claim rather than evidence that Khusrow made it. [Mirza](https://www.scribd.com/document/813809623/Mohammad-Wahid-Mirza-The-Life-and-Works-of-Amir-Khusrau-Baptist-Mission-Press-1936); [Schwartz](https://doi.org/10.1163/18747167-bja10062)
