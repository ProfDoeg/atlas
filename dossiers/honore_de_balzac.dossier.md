# Honoré de Balzac — Research Dossier

**Subject:** Honoré de Balzac (1799–1850)  
**Primary field:** French literature; novel, novella, tale, drama, journalism, publishing, printing  
**Principal project:** *La Comédie humaine*  
**Prepared:** 19 August 2026  
**Method:** chronological reconstruction using institutional, archival, bibliographical, primary-text, scholarly, and contemporaneous sources. Claims are qualified where the documentary basis is uncertain.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

### Names

- **Birth name:** Honoré Balzac.
- **Usual literary name:** **Honoré de Balzac**. The nobiliary particle was not an inherited aristocratic title. His father had been born **Bernard-François Balssa** and transformed the family name into Balzac; no demonstrated genealogical connection exists between the Balzac family and the aristocratic Balzac d'Entragues line with which the name could suggest association. Honoré's increasingly regular use of **“de Balzac”** formed part of his social and authorial self-fashioning.
- **Early pseudonyms:**
  - **Lord R'Hoone / Lord R’hoone**, used on early commercial novels. The name plays on the letters/sound of “Honoré.”
  - **Horace de Saint-Aubin**, used on other early novels.
- **Occasional/private self-designation:** during the travels of 1845–1846 with Ewelina Hańska, her daughter Anna, and Georges Mniszech, Balzac jokingly called their group **“les Saltimbanques”** and himself **“Bilboquet.”**
- **Honor:** **Chevalier de la Légion d'honneur**, appointed 24 April 1845.
- **No securely inherited noble title:** “de Balzac” should not be read as proof of noble birth.

### Birth and death

- **Born:** 20 May 1799, Tours, France.
- **Died:** official BnF authority data gives **18 August 1850**, Paris. Some older narratives and the Maison de Balzac chronology give **19 August**, apparently reflecting a death late in the night and differing conventions for the date. The safest formulation is that he died in Paris on the night of 18–19 August 1850, with 18 August the date favored by the BnF authority record and by Octave Mirbeau’s later account.
- **Age:** 51.
- **Burial:** Père-Lachaise Cemetery, Paris.

### Principal occupations

The BnF authority file describes Balzac as novelist, playwright, journalist, printer, type founder, and publisher. Those categories are not merely retrospective labels: during the 1820s he attempted to make money directly through publishing, printing, and type founding; in the 1830s and 1840s he repeatedly entered newspaper and periodical ventures as author, editor, or proprietor.

### Literary position

Balzac is principally associated with the vast interconnected fiction eventually organized as **_La Comédie humaine_**, an attempt to describe French society across classes, professions, regions, institutions, households, and historical moments. In the 1842 *Avant-Propos*, he compared the differentiation of social “species” to natural history and explicitly named **Religion** and **Monarchy** as two organizing truths of his project. The work nevertheless contains extensive depictions of economic predation, social mobility, failed aristocratic institutions, speculation, corruption, sexual bargaining, crime, journalism, law, provincial life, bureaucracy, finance, and bourgeois consolidation.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

Balzac's father, **Bernard-François Balssa**, was born in 1746 into a peasant family in the Tarn. The Maison de Balzac chronology traces him through early clerical employment and then to Paris, where by the 1770s he was remaking both his career and his name. He converted **Balssa** into **Balzac**. This upward movement through administration, commerce, and state-connected provisioning became part of the social background against which his son's obsessive interest in rank, money, naming, inheritance, bureaucracy, and mobility developed.

The family name has sometimes encouraged retrospective claims of aristocratic ancestry. The institutional biography explicitly separates Bernard-François from the aristocratic **Balzac d'Entragues** family. The younger Balzac's “de” therefore belongs to a later strategy of presentation, not to a securely documented inherited title.

Bernard-François occupied several positions at Tours. He was connected to military provisioning and became director of provisions for the 22nd military division; he also held local civic responsibilities, including functions associated with the hospice, the justice of the peace, and municipal administration. His position gave the family a foothold in the educated administrative bourgeoisie.

A darker family episode entered Balzac's later consciousness: one of Bernard-François's brothers was executed at Albi in **1819** for murder. The Maison de Balzac chronology notes that the conviction may have been unjust. Balzac's later intense interest in criminal procedure and in the **Peytel affair** acquired an additional biographical resonance from this family history, although it would be reductive to make the uncle's execution a single cause of Balzac's legal preoccupations.

### Maternal line

Balzac's mother, **Anne-Charlotte-Laure Sallambier** (usually referred to in biographies as Laure Sallambier Balzac), came from a more prosperous Parisian textile/drapery family. She married Bernard-François in Paris on **30 January 1797**. He was approximately fifty-one; she was eighteen. The age difference, her later emotional relationships, her financial entanglement with her son, and the family's internal tensions became major elements of Balzac biography.

### Siblings

The household's children included:

- **Louis-Daniel Balzac**, born 20 May 1798 and dead after approximately thirty-three days.
- **Honoré Balzac**, born 20 May 1799.
- **Laure Balzac**, born 20 September 1800; later **Laure Surville**. She became Honoré's closest sibling, correspondent, defender, and first major family biographer.
- **Laurence Balzac**, born 18 April 1802. She married **Amand-Désiré Michaut de Saint-Pierre de Montzaigle**, an impecunious or financially unstable nobleman, and died in 1825 at only twenty-three. Her unhappy marriage contributed to the family archive of stories about marriages arranged across differences of money, rank, and expectation.
- **Henri-François Balzac**, born 21 December 1807. Institutional sources at the Musée Balzac describe him as the **presumed son of Jean de Margonne**, the owner of the Château de Saché and a close associate of the family, rather than the biological son of Bernard-François. This is a historical reconstruction rather than a documented legal acknowledgment. Henri later lived under difficult circumstances and died in Mayotte in 1864.

### Parent–child tensions

Honoré's relation with his mother was financially and emotionally complicated. She lent or advanced money into his ventures, monitored his debts, complained about his financial behavior, and remained entangled in his repeated attempts to escape insolvency. Much of the harshest portraiture of Madame Balzac comes through Honoré's own complaints and later family narratives; those sources should not be treated as neutral psychological dossiers. What is documentable is sustained financial interdependence and recurrent conflict.

His father died in Paris on **19 June 1829**, just as Balzac's signed literary career was beginning to take recognizable form.

### Children

Balzac had **no legitimate surviving children**.

There is, however, strong scholarly evidence for a probable biological daughter:

- **Marie-Caroline du Fresnay**, born **4 June 1834**, legally the child of the du Fresnay household. Scholarship based on private documents, letters, and Balzac's relationship with **Marie du Fresnay** has argued that Balzac believed himself to be Marie-Caroline's father. This is best classified as a **strong scholarly reconstruction**, not as a legal paternity record. Balzac's dedication of *Eugénie Grandet* to Maria du Fresnay in a later edition is one piece of the surrounding documentary context.

In **1846**, Balzac and Ewelina Hańska hoped for a child and discussed the projected name **Victor-Honoré**. The pregnancy did not produce a surviving child; the surviving correspondence supports expectation followed by loss, but the precise medical circumstances should not be embellished beyond the documentary record.

## Childhood and Early Life

### 1799–1803: Tours and Saint-Cyr-sur-Loire

Honoré was born in Tours on 20 May 1799. Like many children in families of means, he was sent out to a wet nurse and spent his earliest years away from the parental household, at **Saint-Cyr-sur-Loire**, remaining there until approximately age four.

The Maison de Balzac's detailed biographical chronology states that he “does not seem” to have been baptized. Because the wording itself is cautious and because baptismal documentation has been debated in older biographies, the correct status is **uncertain**, not “definitively unbaptized.”

### 1807–1813: Collège de Vendôme

In **June 1807**, Balzac entered the **Collège de Vendôme**, an Oratorian school, as a boarder. He remained until **April 1813**. The school was a decisive imaginative source. The regime was strict and isolating; Balzac read voraciously and was remembered as physically awkward, dreamy, and undisciplined. Later autobiographical transformations of school experience appear especially in *Louis Lambert*.

The exact reason for his withdrawal is not securely established. Accounts combine health problems, disciplinary difficulty, exhaustion, and family decision. It is safer to say that he left in a state that contemporaries and later biographers interpreted as physical or nervous collapse rather than to impose a modern psychiatric diagnosis.

### 1813–1814: Paris schooling and return to Tours

After Vendôme he was placed at a Paris boarding establishment connected with the **Hôtel du Marais** and attended the **Lycée Charlemagne**. He also spent a brief period back in Tours during the disruptions of 1814.

### 1814: family migration to Paris

The Balzac family moved to **Paris in November 1814**. The move placed Honoré in the capital's educational, legal, journalistic, publishing, and theatrical world—the infrastructures that would dominate his adult life.

## Education and Formation

### Classical schooling

Balzac's education at Vendôme and Charlemagne provided Latin, rhetoric, history, and the classical curriculum. He simultaneously formed an independent reading life that later biographies portray as unusually intense. His fiction displays knowledge of theology, medicine, law, finance, natural history, art collecting, music, typography, and bureaucracy; not all of that knowledge came from formal education, and he became an aggressive self-educator.

### Legal education, 1816–1819

Beginning in **1816**, Balzac studied law in Paris. Scholarly work on “Balzac juriste” places him in practical clerkships as well as formal study:

- He worked for the lawyer **Jean-Baptiste Guillonnet-Merville**, approximately November 1816 to March 1818 according to one scholarly reconstruction.
- He also worked in the notarial world, including with **Passez**.
- He obtained the **baccalauréat en droit** on **10 January 1819** according to legal-historical scholarship.

He did **not** pursue the expected notarial/legal career to completion. The distinction matters: Balzac was legally trained and had substantial practical exposure to offices, contracts, inheritance, debt, conveyancing, and litigation, but he was not a practicing lawyer in the later professional sense.

The law office became one of his great observational laboratories. Lawyers, notaries, clerks, creditors, marriage contracts, debt instruments, estates, guardianships, and procedural traps recur throughout *La Comédie humaine* because Balzac knew the documentary and institutional machinery from inside.

### Decision for literature

In **1819**, against his family's preference for a secure professional path, Balzac chose literature. The family allowed him a trial period. He moved into a poor attic room at **9 rue Lesdiguières**, Paris, while the family relocated to **Villeparisis**. The arrangement became one of the founding scenes of Balzac's self-mythology: the isolated young writer in a garret attempting monumental work while living cheaply.

His first major dramatic experiment, **_Cromwell_**, failed to convince the family or their literary adviser that he possessed immediate genius. He also worked on early prose such as **_Sténie_** and other fragments. The failure did not send him back permanently to law; it sent him into a commercial literary apprenticeship.

### Intellectual influences

Balzac's formation mixed traditions that later criticism sometimes separates too sharply:

- Catholic and monarchical social thought.
- Rousseau, whose presence in the youth novels has been documented by Raymond Trousson.
- Walter Scott and the historical novel.
- Gothic and popular fiction.
- Melodrama.
- Enlightenment and post-Enlightenment theories of human nature.
- Natural history, especially the model of classification and transformation associated with **Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire**, named in the 1842 *Avant-Propos*.
- Swedenborgian and mystical thought, especially visible in *Louis Lambert* and *Séraphîta*.
- Contemporary medicine, physiology, phrenological and “scientific” vocabularies, used in ways characteristic of the nineteenth century rather than modern science.
- The administrative, legal, financial, and journalistic institutions of Restoration and July-Monarchy France.

## Early Career

## 1819–1825: apprenticeship, pseudonyms, collaborations

Balzac's earliest fiction was not the grand autonomous production later associated with his name. It was commercial, collaborative, rapidly produced, and often pseudonymous. He wrote or co-wrote novels with figures including **Auguste Le Poitevin de L'Égreville** and others in the cheap-fiction market.

The principal pseudonyms were **Lord R'Hoone** and **Horace de Saint-Aubin**. The Maison de Balzac bibliography identifies eight early novels from 1822–1825 that later scholarship treats as the principal “romans de jeunesse”:

1. *L'Héritière de Birague*  
2. *Jean-Louis, ou la fille trouvée*  
3. *Clotilde de Lusignan, ou le beau Juif*  
4. *Le Centenaire, ou les Deux Béringheld*  
5. *Le Vicaire des Ardennes*  
6. *La Dernière Fée, ou la Nouvelle Lampe merveilleuse*  
7. *Annette et le Criminel*  
8. *Wann-Chlore*

Authorship within the earliest collaborative titles can be complicated. They should not all be treated as pure single-author Balzac texts in the same sense as the mature novels. Balzac later distanced himself from much of this material and excluded it from the architecture of *La Comédie humaine*.

### Laure de Berny

During this period Balzac formed the relationship that most biographies identify as his first decisive adult emotional and intellectual attachment: **Laure de Berny** (née Laure Hinner), approximately twenty-two years older than he was. She became lover, confidante, reader, critic, social guide, and financial supporter.

Her role should not be reduced to “older mistress.” She read manuscripts, helped stabilize him emotionally, lent or supplied money, and participated in the network that made his publishing experiments possible. Balzac continued to regard her as one of the formative persons of his life until her death in 1836.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

Balzac's financial biography is inseparable from his literary biography. He repeatedly tried to capture not only authorial income but also the profits of production and distribution. He understood that printing, paper, type, publishing contracts, serialization, and intellectual property determined what writers earned. His ventures usually increased rather than reduced his liabilities.

### 1825: publishing venture

In **1825**, Balzac entered publishing more directly. One venture involved compact or inexpensive editions of canonical authors including **Molière** and **La Fontaine**. It failed commercially.

The failure is often narrated as simple naïveté. The record shows something more structural: Balzac attempted to operate inside a capital-intensive trade requiring inventory, paper, printing, distribution, credit, and reliable demand, while possessing little capital and weak cash reserves.

### 1826–1828: printer

Balzac obtained a **printer's brevet in June 1826** and ran a printing establishment. The BnF/Gallica and Maison de Balzac material documents him as an actual printer, not merely an author who casually financed a press.

The shop printed his own or associated material as well as commercial jobs. Among the periodical or serial productions connected with the establishment were **_La Malle-poste_** (1826–1827) and four issues of **_Le Gymnase_** (1828).

### 1827–1828: type foundry and technical ambitions

Balzac expanded into type founding. A circular dated **6 December 1827** announced **“LAURENT, BALZAC ET BARBIER”**, reflecting an association formed from 1 August involving type founding, engraving on steel, copper, and wood, and polytypy.

He acquired equipment associated with **Gillé fils** and bought rights in or equipment for a stereotyping/type process called **“Fontéréotypie,”** developed by Durouchail and further worked by Laurent. The venture shows Balzac's technological curiosity and his desire to integrate the book-production chain.

It also magnified his exposure. By **1828**, the printing/type businesses had failed or been transferred out of his control. The resulting liabilities shadowed him for decades.

### Debt

**Documented fact:** Balzac was chronically indebted, borrowed from family and intimates, used future literary earnings to satisfy old obligations, concealed his whereabouts from creditors, and repeatedly entered new ventures before extinguishing old debts.

**Uncertain point:** popular biographies repeat exact totals for “Balzac's debt” as if one number described his entire life. The amount changed constantly, and nineteenth-century francs, business liabilities, personal debts, guarantees, advances, and property obligations are not interchangeable. No single headline number should be treated as definitive without a dated ledger reconstruction.

### Newspaper and periodical projects

Balzac's relation to journalism was ambivalent: he depended on newspapers and serial publication while attacking journalistic institutions in his fiction and essays.

Important ventures include:

- **_La Chronique de Paris_** (1835–1836): Balzac became heavily involved as editor/proprietor and contributor. The enterprise failed financially.
- **_La Revue parisienne_** (1840): a short-lived review that again ended as a commercial failure.
- Contributions to or relations with **_La Revue de Paris_**, **_La Caricature_**, **_Le Rénovateur_**, **_La Presse_**, **_Le Siècle_**, and other newspapers and reviews.
- The 1836 publication of *La Vieille Fille* in **_La Presse_** helped establish the large-scale newspaper serial novel in France.

### Intellectual property and authors' rights

Balzac took a sustained interest in literary property. The issue was personal—he lived on publication rights—and institutional. He participated in the early history of the **Société des gens de lettres** and served as its **president from 1839 to 1841**.

His conflict with **François Buloz** over *Le Lys dans la vallée* became an important authors'-rights case. Balzac objected to proofs being transmitted for publication abroad before authorized French book publication. He won his case in **June 1836**. Legal-historical scholarship treats the affair as significant in the development of the author's control over a work and what would later be conceptualized as moral rights.

### Les Jardies, Sèvres

In **1837**, Balzac acquired or developed **Les Jardies** at Sèvres. The property became another financial sink: building, furnishing, plans, and speculation exceeded the practical value of the house. He abandoned it within a few years.

Les Jardies became part of Balzac folklore because biographies used its unfinished or impractical features to symbolize his mixture of visionary scale and financial disorder. The symbolic reading is later; the expenditure and failure are historical.

### Sardinian silver-mine speculation, 1838

In **1838**, Balzac traveled through Marseille, Toulon, Corsica, and **Sardinia** to investigate a scheme involving old mining material or slag from which he believed silver could profitably be recovered. Letters show that he financed travel by pawning jewelry and obtaining loans from his mother and a cousin.

The project failed to produce the expected fortune. It belongs to a repeated pattern: Balzac imagined that a technically or commercially clever intervention would yield a rapid capital gain large enough to free him from debt and thereby allow literary independence.

### La Comédie humaine as publishing enterprise

The unification of his works under *La Comédie humaine* was an artistic project and a major publishing operation.

- The title was already in his planning by **January 1840**.
- A major publishing contract followed in **1841** with a consortium including **Hetzel, Paulin, Dubochet, and Sanches**, with **Furne** later becoming central.
- The first volume was announced and published in **1842**.
- Sixteen volumes had appeared by 1846; the first large edition ultimately reached seventeen volumes during 1842–1848, with posthumous additions and reconfigurations thereafter.
- Balzac obsessively revised proofs. The so-called **“Furne corrigé”** became a crucial textual witness because he continued writing changes onto the printed edition.

This project demonstrates how his “work” cannot be reduced to isolated novels. He continually renamed, revised, repositioned, linked, and retrofitted earlier texts into a changing total architecture.

### Rue Fortunée

In **September 1846**, Balzac bought a house on **rue Fortunée** in Paris (later renamed rue Balzac). He spent heavily furnishing it, in significant part preparing a domestic setting for life with Ewelina Hańska. The house became his final residence and the place where he died.

In his last years Hańska supplied or lent funds connected with property and investment. Their relationship therefore had a major financial as well as emotional dimension.

## Chronological Life History

## 1799–1819: childhood, school, law, and the decision to write

Balzac's first two decades moved from Tours to boarding school, then to Paris and legal training. The decisive break occurred in 1819 when he rejected the secure professional route.

The garret at rue Lesdiguières later became a foundational origin story: Balzac as the poor novice feeding himself cheaply, freezing, reading, and attempting tragedy. The material hardship was real, but later memoirs and Balzac's own self-dramatization helped turn it into legend.

## 1819–1828: commercial literature and failed industrial independence

The 1820s were years of experiment:

- tragic drama;
- Gothic/popular fiction;
- pseudonymous novels;
- literary collaboration;
- publishing;
- printing;
- typography;
- repeated borrowing.

The relationship with Laure de Berny connected these zones. She belonged simultaneously to his emotional life, editorial formation, and financing.

The printing collapse left Balzac with durable debts but also with unusual technical knowledge of the book trade. Printers' proofs, type, format, costs, deadlines, publishers' accounting, and the economics of editions remained material realities in his later career.

## 1828–1829: Brittany and the first mature signed novel

Balzac visited **Fougères** in Brittany and stayed with **General de Pommereul**, using the region to research the royalist Chouan insurgency. The result was **_Le Dernier Chouan ou la Bretagne en 1800_** (1829), later revised and retitled **_Les Chouans_**.

This was the decisive transition from pseudonymous commercial production toward the author “Balzac.” It combined historical documentation, political conflict, geography, romance, and social observation.

In **December 1829**, *Physiologie du mariage* appeared, a provocative pseudo-sociological treatment of marriage, sexuality, adultery, and domestic strategy. Its mixture of aphorism, satire, statistics, physiologie, and social taxonomy anticipates methods later dispersed through *La Comédie humaine*.

## 1830: revolution and rapid literary expansion

The July Revolution transformed the political environment. Balzac initially had liberal sympathies under the Restoration but moved toward **Legitimism** in the early July Monarchy.

In literary production, 1830 was explosive. Stories later absorbed into *Scènes de la vie privée* and other sections appeared at high speed. Balzac became a recognizable figure in Parisian letters.

His work increasingly shifted from isolated plots toward a system in which money, institutions, households, and social positions connected characters across stories.

## 1831: *La Peau de chagrin* and fame

**_La Peau de chagrin_** (1831) brought major public success. Its fantastic device—a magical skin that shrinks whenever its owner's desires are fulfilled—condensed a central Balzacian problem: desire as expenditure of life.

The novel also makes clear why “realist” is not an exhaustive label for Balzac. His mature writing includes the fantastic, occult, philosophical speculation, allegory, melodrama, satire, and social documentary.

### Political candidacy

Balzac attempted electoral politics in the early 1830s. He pursued candidacies without success and increasingly associated himself with Legitimist positions. His politics were monarchical and anti-parliamentary in important respects, but his fiction did not operate as a simple programmatic defense of aristocracy.

## 1832: Castries, “L'Étrangère,” and political realignment

Balzac pursued **Claire de Castries, marquise de Castries**, in 1832. He traveled to **Aix-les-Bains**, then outside France in the Kingdom of Sardinia, and onward toward **Geneva**. The hoped-for relationship did not develop as he desired.

That same year, a far more consequential relation began with an anonymous letter signed **“L'Étrangère”** (“the foreign woman”). Its author was **Ewelina/Eve Hańska**, a Polish noblewoman married to Wacław Hański and living on estates in the Russian Empire.

Their correspondence would continue for nearly two decades.

## 1833: Hanska in Switzerland; *Eugénie Grandet*; Marie du Fresnay

Balzac met Hańska in Switzerland in **1833**. Their relationship became romantic and sexual.

The same year brought **_Eugénie Grandet_**, one of the major provincial novels. It examines wealth not as abstract abundance but as hoarded capital, inheritance, family power, and emotional deprivation.

In the autumn of 1833 Balzac also had a relationship with **Marie du Fresnay**. Scholarship reconstructs him as the probable biological father of her daughter Marie-Caroline, born the following June. This should be distinguished from his legally documented family status.

## 1834: recurring characters and *Le Père Goriot*

Balzac's conception of reusing characters across independent works became decisive during the composition of **_Le Père Goriot_**. The technique allowed a figure to appear first at the margin, later at the center, or to age across novels. It converted separate fictions into a network.

At the **Château de Saché**, where he often retreated to work, his doctor **Nacquart** urged rest because of health problems. Balzac nevertheless engaged in extraordinarily intense writing schedules.

He offered *Le Médecin de campagne* for the **Prix Montyon** of the Académie française. It was rejected.

His increasingly conspicuous social persona included a costly **turquoise-studded cane**, commissioned around 1834. The cane was real; almost immediately it acquired a literary mythology of its own.

## 1835: *Le Père Goriot*, Vienna, Metternich, *Chronique de Paris*

**_Le Père Goriot_** appeared in book form in 1835 after serialization. The novel brought together boarding-house micro-society, aristocratic aspiration, filial exploitation, student ambition, crime, and finance.

Balzac traveled to **Vienna** and obtained an audience with **Prince Klemens von Metternich**, one of the central conservative statesmen of post-Napoleonic Europe. The meeting mattered to Balzac's self-image and political-social ambitions even though he never became a significant state political actor.

He also invested heavily in **_La Chronique de Paris_**, a newspaper/review venture. It collapsed in 1836 and increased his debts.

Around this period **Frances-Sarah Guidoboni-Visconti** became an important intimate in his social and romantic network. She and her husband also served as contacts in legal, financial, and travel matters.

## 1836: death of Laure de Berny; Buloz trial; National Guard detention; *La Vieille Fille*

Laure de Berny died in **1836**, a major personal loss.

Balzac also fought the **Buloz litigation** concerning *Le Lys dans la vallée*. François Buloz had caused proof material to circulate for publication outside Balzac's authorized schedule. Balzac sued and won in June. The affair is important both as personal literary combat and in the longer history of authors' rights.

In the same year Balzac spent approximately a week in the **Hôtel des Haricots**, the joking name for a detention site associated with disciplinary penalties for members of the **National Guard**. His offense concerned evasion or nonperformance of civic guard duty. This was not a criminal imprisonment for literary activity; it was a disciplinary consequence of civic-service obligations.

His *La Vieille Fille* was serialized in **_La Presse_**. It is frequently cited as the first French novel published in the modern daily newspaper feuilleton form, though the exact genealogy of serialization practices is broader than a single “first.”

## 1837–1838: *Illusions perdues*, Les Jardies, Italy, Sardinia, George Sand

The first part of **_Illusions perdues_** appeared in 1837, beginning a long publication history that would continue through 1843. The work anatomizes provincial aspiration, Paris journalism, publishing, paper manufacture, law, celebrity, and corruption of cultural value.

Balzac traveled extensively in Italy between **1836 and 1838**, including **Turin, Milan, Venice, Genoa, and Florence**, often mixing literary tourism, society, business, and errands for the Guidoboni-Visconti family.

In **1838**, he pursued the Sardinian mining scheme. The episode is one of the clearest cases in which the supposedly fantastical Balzac businessman is directly visible in correspondence: he raised money for travel, calculated possible extraction profits, and imagined a rapid reversal of his finances.

Later in 1838 he spent approximately a week at **Nohant** with **George Sand**. Their friendship linked two major but very differently positioned figures in French literature.

## 1839: Société des gens de lettres, Peytel, Académie, *Béatrix*

Balzac became president of the **Société des gens de lettres**, a position he held into 1841.

He intervened publicly in the **Peytel affair**. **Sébastien-Benoît Peytel**, a former journalist known to Balzac, was tried for killing his wife and servant. Balzac's *Lettre sur le procès de Peytel* appeared in *Le Siècle* over 27–29 September 1839.

Later scholarship complicates the popular summary “Balzac believed Peytel innocent.” Balzac argued about the character and legal classification of the acts and, in one scholarly reading, regarded Peytel as a killer but not an **assassin** in the technical sense requiring premeditation. Peytel was condemned and executed. Balzac's intervention failed to save him.

Balzac also sought admission to the **Académie française**. In 1839 he became a candidate for the seat of historian Joseph Michaud but withdrew when Victor Hugo entered the contest. Balzac would never be elected.

Major works in or around this period included **_Béatrix_**, portions of *Illusions perdues*, the music stories *Gambara* and *Massimilla Doni*, and the philosophical/mystical prose of the 1830s.

## 1840: *Vautrin*, censorship, Passy, *Revue parisienne*

Balzac's play **_Vautrin_** premiered at the Théâtre de la Porte-Saint-Martin on **14 March 1840**. It was removed after a single performance by censorship.

A famous explanation says actor **Frédérick Lemaître** appeared in makeup or costume resembling King Louis-Philippe, producing a politically explosive caricature. The suppression itself is documented; details about precisely what aspect of the performance triggered the ban entered theatrical anecdote and should be treated with caution rather than repeated as a single proven cause.

In 1840 Balzac settled in **Passy**, in the house now associated with the Maison de Balzac at **47 rue Raynouard**. He used concealment and alternate entrances/names in periods when creditors or National Guard obligations made visibility dangerous.

His companion/housekeeper **Louise Breugniot**, often called **Madame de Brugnol**, became part of the Passy household and was also Balzac's mistress. Ewelina Hańska was intensely jealous of her.

Balzac launched **_La Revue parisienne_** in 1840. It failed after only a few issues, adding another periodical defeat to his business record.

## 1841–1842: the architecture of *La Comédie humaine*

Balzac's scattered titles, series, and categories crystallized into the name **_La Comédie humaine_**. He negotiated a collective edition with publishers including Hetzel, Paulin, Dubochet, Sanches, and Furne.

The first volume of the major edition appeared in **1842**. The program divided the work broadly into:

1. **Études de mœurs**
   - Scènes de la vie privée
   - Scènes de la vie de province
   - Scènes de la vie parisienne
   - Scènes de la vie politique
   - Scènes de la vie militaire
   - Scènes de la vie de campagne
2. **Études philosophiques**
3. **Études analytiques**

The famous *Avant-Propos* of 1842 gave the project an explicit theoretical frame. Human beings, unlike animal “species,” change through social institutions, occupations, wealth, marriage, custom, and history; fiction could therefore act as an archive of “social species.”

The system was never static. Balzac altered titles, moved works between sections, reintroduced characters, and planned many texts he never completed.

## 1842: death of Wacław Hański and the possibility of marriage

On **5 January 1842**, Balzac learned that **Wacław Hański**, Ewelina's husband, had died. The event made marriage theoretically possible, but not immediately practical.

Several obstacles remained:

- Hańska's obligations and property position within the Russian Empire.
- The Russian state's rules concerning a noble landowner marrying a foreigner and retaining property.
- Balzac's debts and unstable finances.
- Her family's interests.
- distance and logistics.

Their correspondence became increasingly concerned with the practical conditions of union.

## 1843: Saint Petersburg

Balzac traveled by sea from Dunkirk to **Saint Petersburg**, a trip of roughly two weeks. He saw Hańska and her circle and returned through **Berlin and Dresden**.

His health was already problematic. The Maison de Balzac chronology reports a severe migraine or neurological episode described in period language as an **“accident méningé.”** That expression should not be converted into a precise modern diagnosis.

## 1844–1845: late productivity, travel, honor

Balzac continued revising the Furne edition and producing major new fiction. The late 1840s would include *La Cousine Bette*, *Le Cousin Pons*, and the final sections of *Splendeurs et misères des courtisanes*.

On **24 April 1845**, he was made **Chevalier de la Légion d'honneur**. This was his principal official state honor.

In 1845 Balzac traveled extensively with Hańska, her daughter Anna, and Anna's future husband **Georges Mniszech**. The party traveled in France and beyond. Balzac's letters jokingly named the traveling group “les Saltimbanques” and himself “Bilboquet.”

## 1846: Italy, Switzerland, pregnancy hopes, rue Fortunée

Travel continued through **Italy**, including **Rome**, and Switzerland. Balzac also indulged his appetite for paintings, furniture, antiquities, and luxury objects. These purchases were aesthetic investments and forms of domestic staging, but they also aggravated his financial position.

He considered buying the **Château de Moncontour** in Touraine but did not do so.

By May 1846, Balzac and Hańska believed or hoped that she was pregnant. They discussed a son, Victor-Honoré. No child survived.

In September Balzac bought the house on rue Fortunée and began furnishing it on a grand scale.

## 1847: Hańska in Paris; Wierzchownia

Hańska came to Paris in early 1847. The couple still did not marry. She left again for her estate at **Wierzchownia** (Wierzchownia/Verkhivnia, in present-day Ukraine).

Balzac traveled there in **September 1847** and remained until January 1848. The journey deepened his physical exhaustion.

## 1848: revolution, publishing disruption, worsening health, return to Ukraine

Balzac returned to France shortly before the **February Revolution of 1848**. The political and publishing crisis disrupted markets and theatrical plans.

At **Saché** in 1848, his health deteriorated seriously. Museum chronology points to signs of heart disease and increasing respiratory difficulty.

On **19 September 1848**, he left for Hańska's estate in the Russian Empire. He would remain there through prolonged illness until spring 1850.

## 1849: severe illness

By 1849 Balzac was describing a **hypertrophied heart**, extreme breathlessness, edema, and inability to manage stairs. These descriptions come from nineteenth-century observations and correspondence.

A modern medical-history study retrospectively interprets the terminal course as **congestive heart failure**, with severe respiratory problems, leg edema, stasis changes, and eventual gangrene. That is a retrospective reconstruction from texts, not the equivalent of a modern clinical workup or autopsy report.

## 1850: marriage and death

Balzac and **Ewelina Hańska** finally married on **14 March 1850** in the Russian Empire, at Berdychiv in present-day Ukraine. The marriage came after nearly eighteen years of correspondence and repeated meetings.

They left for Paris in April. The return became an ominous scene in later biographies: on arrival at the rue Fortunée house, the household was in disarray; a locksmith had to open the house; servants were ill or incapacitated. Balzac himself was gravely sick, breathless, visually impaired, and unable to resume normal work.

He deteriorated through the summer and died in August.

## Residences, Travels, and Geographical Movements

Balzac's life is often imagined as stationary writing in Paris, but his geography was wide.

### Principal residences and working bases

- **Tours** — birthplace and childhood family center.
- **Saint-Cyr-sur-Loire** — wet-nurse household in early childhood.
- **Vendôme** — boarding school, 1807–1813.
- **Paris** — from 1814, with many addresses.
- **Villeparisis** — family residence after 1819.
- **9 rue Lesdiguières, Paris** — famous early garret.
- **rue de Tournon** — early adult Paris residence.
- **rue Cassini** — important residence from 1828.
- **rue des Batailles** — used under concealment during a period of financial/civic pressure.
- **rue de Provence** — pied-à-terre.
- **Les Jardies, Sèvres** — property from 1837, soon abandoned.
- **Passy / 47 rue Raynouard** — principal hidden working residence from around 1840; now Maison de Balzac.
- **rue Fortunée** — bought 1846, final home; later renamed rue Balzac.

### Château de Saché

The **Château de Saché** in Touraine, owned by Jean de Margonne, was one of Balzac's most important refuges. The Musée Balzac documents roughly a dozen stays between **1825 and 1848**.

Functions of Saché:

- refuge from Paris and creditors;
- enforced rest recommended by Dr Nacquart;
- intense composition;
- access to Touraine landscapes and social observation;
- family contact.

Works composed or advanced there include parts of *Le Père Goriot*, *Illusions perdues*, *Séraphîta*, *César Birotteau*, *Maître Cornélius*, and other texts.

### Major travel sequence

- **Brittany/Fougères, 1828:** research for *Les Chouans*.
- **Le Croisic/Guérande, 1830:** travel associated with Laure de Berny; Balzac was away from Paris during part of the July Revolution.
- **Aix-les-Bains and Geneva, 1832:** pursuit of the marquise de Castries.
- **Switzerland, 1833:** meetings with Hańska.
- **Vienna, 1835:** Hańska and social/political contacts; audience with Metternich.
- **Italy, 1836–1838:** Turin, Milan, Venice, Genoa, Florence and related travel.
- **Corsica and Sardinia, 1838:** mining speculation.
- **Nohant, 1838:** George Sand.
- **Saint Petersburg, 1843:** Hańska.
- **France–Italy–Switzerland circuit, 1845–1846:** Hańska party; collecting and sightseeing as well as family plans.
- **Wierzchownia, 1847–1848 and 1848–1850:** long stays at Hańska's estate in the Russian Empire.
- **Berdychiv, 1850:** marriage.
- **Paris, 1850:** final return and death.

Balzac was not an exile in the political sense and was never subject to long-term imprisonment. His periods of concealment in Paris arose primarily from debt, creditor pressure, and civic obligations rather than exile by the state.

## Political / Religious / Intellectual / Artistic Development

### Political trajectory

Balzac's politics changed over time.

- Under the **Bourbon Restoration**, he could write from liberal or opposition positions.
- After the 1830 revolution and especially by the early 1830s, he moved toward **Legitimism**, defending hereditary monarchy and associating with Legitimist journalism.
- He sought elected office more than once but was not elected.
- He never held a major political office.

The *Avant-Propos* states his declared principles unusually clearly: he placed his social work under the signs of **Religion** and **Monarchy**. This does not eliminate contradictions between stated politics and fictional analysis.

### Politics versus fictional evidence

Balzac's later admirers on the left, especially **Friedrich Engels**, emphasized that the novels often revealed the decline, compromises, and historical weakness of Balzac's own favored aristocratic class more powerfully than his political preferences could conceal. In an 1888 letter to Margaret Harkness, Engels treated Balzac as a master of realism from whom he had learned more about French society than from many professional historians and economists.

Paul Lafargue later recalled **Karl Marx's** admiration for Balzac and Marx's desire to write a substantial study of *La Comédie humaine*. These recollections helped create a durable Marxist Balzac: a politically conservative author whose realism was considered socially revelatory.

That reception is historically important but should not be retrojected as Balzac's own ideology.

### Religion and mysticism

Balzac's religious world was heterogeneous:

- public and political defense of Catholic social order;
- attraction to mysticism;
- Swedenborgian influence;
- speculative metaphysics;
- fascination with magnetism, will, energy, and spiritual transformation.

**_Louis Lambert_** and **_Séraphîta_** are central documents. The latter's Swedenborgian and androgynous spiritual figure resists any simple description of Balzac as merely conventional Catholic moralist.

### Natural history and social classification

The 1842 *Avant-Propos* explicitly invokes **Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire** and the idea of natural species as a model for social differentiation. Balzac wanted to inventory professions, classes, households, regional types, behaviors, institutions, and their interactions.

His social taxonomy was not neutral “science” in a modern sense. It mixed empirical observation with nineteenth-century physiognomy, gender stereotypes, class assumptions, moral categories, and speculative theories. But the aspiration to make fiction a total social archive was self-conscious and documented.

### Art, collecting, and material culture

Balzac collected paintings, furniture, decorative arts, antiquities, and luxury objects. His collecting fed both fiction and self-presentation. Interiors in *La Comédie humaine* are economic documents: upholstery, silver, furniture, clothing, and architecture identify social position, old wealth, bad taste, counterfeit gentility, or debt.

The same sensibility shaped the furnishing of rue Fortunée. The boundary between investment, connoisseurship, theatrical self-fashioning, and extravagance was often porous.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Laure de Berny

**Role:** lover, mentor, reader, financier, maternal/intellectual figure.  
**Period:** especially 1820s–1836.  
**Importance:** among the most formative relationships of his life. Her death in 1836 was a major emotional rupture.

### Laure Surville

**Role:** sister, correspondent, defender, first family biographer.  
Her **_Balzac: sa vie et ses œuvres d'après sa correspondance_** (1858) is indispensable but not neutral. It preserved unique family testimony while also participating in posthumous reputation-making.

### Ewelina (Eve) Hańska

**Role:** correspondent from 1832, lover, patron/financial supporter in later years, wife in 1850.

The relationship generated one of the major authorial correspondence archives of the nineteenth century, later published as **_Lettres à l'Étrangère_**. It joined romance, literary criticism, gossip, financial reporting, travel planning, politics, jealousy, family negotiation, and publishing detail.

Because Hańska was a landholding noblewoman in the Russian Empire, marriage involved legal and property issues beyond private desire.

### Claire de Castries

**Role:** aristocratic romantic interest, 1832.  
Balzac pursued her intensely and traveled to Aix-les-Bains/Geneva. The relationship ended in disappointment and was later mined by biographers for parallels with aristocratic women in his fiction. Direct one-to-one identifications between fictional women and Castries should be treated cautiously.

### Frances-Sarah Guidoboni-Visconti

**Role:** intimate friend/lover and social ally from the mid-1830s.  
The Guidoboni-Visconti network connected Balzac to aristocratic society, Italy, and practical legal/financial errands.

### Marie du Fresnay

**Role:** lover, 1833; probable mother of Balzac's biological daughter Marie-Caroline.  
The paternity claim is a strong scholarly reconstruction rather than a formal legal record.

### Louise Breugniot / Madame de Brugnol

**Role:** housekeeper/companion and mistress during the Passy period.  
Her presence became a source of jealousy and conflict with Hańska.

### Zulma Carraud

**Role:** close friend, confidante, moral and political interlocutor.  
Balzac stayed with the Carraud family in Angoulême and Issoudun. Zulma's republican outlook made her a politically interesting counterpoint to his Legitimism.

### George Sand

**Role:** literary friend.  
Balzac stayed at Nohant in 1838. Their friendship is significant because later histories often position their politics and authorial personae as opposites.

### Théophile Gautier

**Role:** younger writer, collaborator, friend, memoirist.  
Gautier's later portrait of Balzac became one of the most influential sources for the physical and behavioral mythology of the writer: the robe, coffee, enormous working energy, laughter, and conversation.

### Victor Hugo

**Role:** peer, rival in prestige, Académie competitor, funeral orator.  
Balzac withdrew an Académie candidacy when Hugo entered the same contest. Hugo later delivered a celebrated funeral oration.

### François Buloz

**Role:** editor/publisher and legal adversary.  
Their conflict over *Le Lys dans la vallée* moved from the publishing economy into litigation.

### Pierre-Jules Hetzel, Furne, Dubochet, Paulin, Sanches

**Role:** publishing partners in the construction of *La Comédie humaine*.  
The collective edition depended on a commercial consortium. Balzac's relation with publishers was combative because he simultaneously required their capital and resisted their control.

### Frédérick Lemaître

**Role:** actor associated with *Vautrin*.  
The one-night 1840 theatrical scandal attached Lemaître to Balzac's censorship history.

### Dr Nacquart

**Role:** physician and close medical adviser.  
Nacquart repeatedly urged rest and became one of the main real doctors in Balzac's later health history—the opposite of the fictional **Horace Bianchon**, who belongs to Balzac mythology.

### Metternich

**Role:** political celebrity/contact, Vienna 1835.  
Balzac obtained an audience with the Austrian statesman, reflecting his attraction to high political society and European conservative power.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Financial recklessness versus fraud

Balzac's life contains extensive **debt, failed businesses, concealment from creditors, speculative schemes, aggressive contractual disputes, and dependence on loans**.

The sources consulted do **not** establish a criminal conviction for fraud, embezzlement, corruption, money laundering, or a comparable financial crime. It would therefore be inaccurate to convert chronic insolvency into “fraud.”

At the same time, his financial behavior caused real losses and burdens for others, including family members and business associates. A documentary treatment should retain both facts:

1. he repeatedly overextended himself and shifted financial risk onto intimates and partners;
2. this is not the same thing as a proven criminal fraud scheme.

### Buloz litigation, 1836

**Status:** documented civil/legal dispute; Balzac prevailed.

**Issue:** unauthorized circulation/publication of proof material from *Le Lys dans la vallée* in ways Balzac claimed violated his control over the work.

**Importance:** later legal scholarship places the decision within the history of literary property and authorial rights.

### National Guard detention, 1836

**Status:** documented disciplinary detention.

Balzac was confined for roughly a week at the **Hôtel des Haricots** for failing or refusing to perform National Guard obligations.

This should not be inflated into political imprisonment or persecution for writing. It arose from civic-military service regulations.

### Peytel affair, 1839

**Status:** public intervention in a murder trial; not a prosecution of Balzac.

Balzac knew **Sébastien-Benoît Peytel** through journalism and intervened publicly after Peytel's conviction for killing his wife and servant.

Balzac's published letter has often been simplified into “he believed Peytel innocent.” Scholarship shows a more complicated legal distinction: he could regard Peytel as having killed while contesting whether the act met the category of **assassination/premeditated murder** as charged.

Peytel was executed. Balzac's intervention became part of later debates about his judgment, loyalty, interest in criminal psychology, and suspicion of judicial narratives.

### *Vautrin* censorship, 1840

**Status:** documented theatrical suppression.

The play was performed once and then banned. The traditional story that Frédérick Lemaître's appearance was taken as a caricature of Louis-Philippe is plausible within the censorship context but contains anecdotal embellishment in later retellings.

### Literary labor and exploitative publishing structures

Balzac both suffered from and participated in the aggressive commercial culture he depicted:

- advance payments;
- proof-stage rewrites that dramatically raised typesetting costs;
- missed deadlines;
- multiple publication commitments;
- serial publication;
- contested territorial rights;
- repackaging and revision.

Publishers often complained that his endless proof corrections made production expensive. Balzac, conversely, complained of publishers treating writers as raw material. Neither side's complaints should be used as an exclusive account of the contractual reality.

### Personal relationships and overlapping liaisons

Balzac maintained overlapping emotional/sexual relationships while corresponding to Hańska in terms of exclusivity and destiny. His relation with Mme de Brugnol became a particular cause of jealousy.

These are documented aspects of his personal life, but hostile biographical narratives sometimes convert them into generalized moral character judgments. The documentary record is better used to establish dates, letters, co-residence, and financial relations.

### Sexuality claims

Balzac wrote unusually significant representations of homoerotic desire, gender ambiguity, cross-dressing, and non-normative sexuality—most famously in **_Sarrasine_**, the Vautrin cycle, and *La Fille aux yeux d'or*.

That literary material is not evidence by itself for a particular sexual identity of the author. In the sources consulted for this dossier, no archival basis strong enough to establish a hidden personal sexual identity was located. Literary interpretation and biographical claim should remain separate.

### Intelligence connections, organized political violence, money laundering

No reliable documentary evidence was located in the consulted archival, institutional, and scholarly sources establishing that Balzac engaged in intelligence work, organized political violence, money laundering, or systematic political corruption.

His contacts reached into aristocratic and diplomatic circles, but elite access is not the same thing as an intelligence relationship.

## Illnesses, Stimulants, Psychological Crises, Accidents, and Cause of Death

### Working pattern

Balzac became famous for extreme work routines: sleeping at unusual hours, rising in the night, writing in long sessions, and using strong coffee to sustain concentration. Contemporary memoirs and his correspondence make the general pattern credible.

The exact number of hours on a given day varied; anecdotes of 15–18-hour sessions should be understood as descriptions of peak periods rather than a medically measured lifelong daily average.

### Coffee

**Documented:** Balzac consumed large quantities of coffee and wrote about coffee as a stimulant. His **_Traité des excitants modernes_** (1839) discusses stimulants directly. His correspondence contains recurrent references to coffee and to awareness that it could damage his health.

**Legendary/unstable:** figures such as **“50 cups a day”** or a fixed lifetime total of **50,000 cups** circulate widely. The sourcing of these exact numbers is unstable, and they should not be repeated as secure quantitative facts.

**Causal myth:** “coffee killed Balzac” is too simple. Heavy stimulant use, sleep deprivation, and overwork may have stressed an already failing body, but the surviving medical descriptions point more directly to serious cardiac disease and terminal congestive complications.

### 1830s deterioration

The Musée Balzac's chronology places signs of heart and respiratory trouble in the 1830s. Dr Nacquart repeatedly sent or encouraged Balzac to go to Saché for recovery.

### 1837

During a stay at Saché, Balzac experienced chest inflammation or respiratory illness.

### 1843

After the Saint Petersburg journey he suffered an episode described by contemporaries in language that has been rendered as an **“accident méningé.”** It cannot be securely translated into a modern neurological diagnosis from the evidence available.

### 1848–1850

By 1848 the health decline was unmistakable. By 1849 Balzac described cardiac enlargement, inability to climb stairs, shortness of breath, and edema.

A 2016 medical-history article retrospectively interprets his terminal disease as **congestive heart failure**, associated with ventricular hypertrophy, severe respiratory symptoms, leg edema, stasis dermatitis, and ultimately gangrene.

This is the strongest modern synthetic medical reconstruction found for this dossier, but it remains **retrospective diagnosis**.

### Psychological descriptions

Balzac had periods of elation, grandiose commercial planning, exhaustion, fear, despair over money, sexual jealousy, and extraordinary productive drive. Retrospective psychiatric diagnosis from these behaviors is speculative. No modern clinical label should be imposed as established fact.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## 1. “De Balzac” as aristocratic birth

**Story:** Balzac was naturally or ancestrally an aristocrat, as the particle “de” suggests.

**Origin/circulation:** encouraged by Balzac's own social presentation, his fascination with nobility, and later readers who took the name literally.

**Evidence:** his father was born **Bernard-François Balssa** into a peasant family and adopted the name Balzac. Institutional research finds no demonstrated connection with the noble Balzac d'Entragues family.

**Assessment:** **self-created/status mythology**, not documented hereditary nobility.

**Effect:** the invented or aspirational nobiliary styling became inseparable from the public authorial name and mirrors one of Balzac's great subjects: how names, titles, property, and appearances manufacture social reality.

## 2. “What Napoleon could not finish with the sword, I will accomplish with the pen”

**Story:** Balzac placed a statuette or bust of Napoleon in his room and wrote beneath it words to the effect: what Napoleon failed to complete with the sword, Balzac would accomplish with the pen.

**Origin/circulation:** the anecdote appears in later nineteenth-century testimony, notably associated with **Virginie Ancelot**, and is repeated in histories of the Napoleonic literary myth.

**Evidence:** Balzac's fascination with Napoleon and literary conquest is well documented; the exact inscription is not preserved as an incontrovertible autograph in the sources consulted, and the wording varies.

**Assessment:** **plausible but not securely autograph anecdote**.

**Effect:** it became one of the most efficient images of Balzac's ambition: the writer as conqueror of society by textual totalization.

## 3. The magical turquoise cane

**Story:** Balzac possessed a jeweled cane that granted invisibility or occult powers.

**Origin/circulation:** unlike many later legends, this myth arose almost immediately. Balzac really commissioned and displayed a luxurious cane ornamented with turquoise. In **1836**, **Delphine de Girardin** published **_La Canne de M. de Balzac_**, a fantasy in which Balzac's cane becomes magical.

**Evidence for object:** the cane survives in the collections associated with the Maison de Balzac/Paris Musées.

**Evidence for magic:** literary fiction, not factual claim.

**Assessment:** **documented object + contemporaneous deliberate literary mythology**.

**Effect:** the cane turned Balzac's conspicuous consumption and social persona into a portable symbol. It is a rare case where the construction of an author's legend can be dated almost in real time.

## 4. Fifty cups of coffee a day

**Story:** Balzac habitually drank fifty cups of coffee every day.

**Origin/circulation:** modern biographies, lifestyle articles, coffee histories, and quotation culture.

**Evidence:** direct sources establish heavy coffee use but not a stable fifty-cup daily regimen. The numerical claim varies from source to source and is often uncited.

**Assessment:** **exaggerated popular legend built on a documented underlying habit**.

**Effect:** it has become a shorthand for pathological literary productivity and sometimes replaces serious discussion of his cardiac disease.

## 5. Coffee as sole cause of death

**Story:** Balzac worked and caffeinated himself to death.

**Evidence for:** extreme work, sleep disruption, and heavy coffee consumption are documented.

**Evidence against as sole cause:** the terminal symptom complex is consistent with severe chronic cardiac failure and congestive complications. No source establishes a direct single-cause chain “coffee → death.”

**Assessment:** **overcausal biographical myth**.

## 6. Calling for Dr Bianchon on his deathbed

**Story:** the dying Balzac asked for **Horace Bianchon**, the physician who exists only inside *La Comédie humaine*, as though his fictional doctor could save him.

**Circulation:** the anecdote appears in later Balzac legend and in discussions of the supposed collapse of the boundary between Balzac's invented world and ordinary reality.

**Evidence:** no contemporary eyewitness source strong enough to verify the episode was located in the materials consulted for this dossier.

**Assessment:** **unverified deathbed legend**.

**Effect:** enormously powerful symbolically: it portrays Balzac as having inhabited his fictional society so completely that its characters became real at the threshold of death.

## 7. The monk's robe / white dressing gown

**Story:** Balzac wrote like a monk, dressed in a distinctive robe.

**Evidence:** the work robe is well attested in portraits and memoirs. It became visually canonical.

**Later transformation:** **Auguste Rodin** made the enveloping robe central to the final monument to Balzac.

**Assessment:** **fact converted into icon** rather than simple legend.

## 8. The solitary genius who wrote *La Comédie humaine* alone

**Story:** Balzac's work emerged only from isolated genius and coffee.

**Evidence against simplification:** his career depended on a large network—lovers and lenders, compositors, printers, editors, publishers, illustrators, doctors, newspaper proprietors, creditors, proofreaders, correspondents, aristocratic contacts, lawyers, and household labor.

**Assessment:** **romantic simplification**.

**Effect:** it obscures the industrial and collaborative material history of nineteenth-century literature.

## 9. Mirbeau's scandalous death narrative

In **1907**, Octave Mirbeau published **_La Mort de Balzac_**, incorporating stories attributed to painter **Jean Gigoux** and giving an extremely hostile portrait of Ewelina Hańska around Balzac's death and widowhood.

Mirbeau himself presents Gigoux as his source. The account was written decades after the events and forms part of the polemical, eroticized, anti-bourgeois literature around Balzac's marriage. It cannot be treated as a neutral eyewitness report by Mirbeau.

**Status:** **late hearsay/polemical biography built on claimed testimony**.

Its importance lies as much in the history of Balzac's posthumous marital mythology as in factual reconstruction.

## Works by the Person

### Bibliographical caution

Balzac's bibliography is unusually difficult because:

- early novels were pseudonymous and collaborative;
- many mature works first appeared in newspapers or reviews;
- titles changed;
- texts were expanded between editions;
- sections were recombined;
- works migrated into *La Comédie humaine* under new classifications;
- Balzac revised printed copies after publication;
- unfinished works were posthumously completed by others;
- collected correspondence and notebooks are posthumous editorial constructions.

The list below therefore distinguishes early/pseudonymous works, the mature *Comédie humaine*, other essays and tales, drama, and posthumous editorial collections.

### Early manuscripts and experiments

- **_Cromwell_** — tragedy, written c.1819–1820; unsuccessful as the work with which Balzac hoped to establish himself.
- **_Sténie ou les erreurs philosophiques_** — early unfinished novel/manuscript.
- **_Falthurne_** — early prose fragment/unfinished work.

### Pseudonymous / commercial “romans de jeunesse,” 1822–1825

The following titles are among the eight youth novels identified in the Maison de Balzac bibliography. Collaboration/attribution varies in the earliest publishing history and should be checked title by title in critical editions.

- **_L'Héritière de Birague_** (1822) — associated with the pseudonym Lord R'Hoone and collaboration with Auguste Le Poitevin de L'Égreville.
- **_Jean-Louis, ou la fille trouvée_** (1822) — early collaborative/pseudonymous fiction.
- **_Clotilde de Lusignan, ou le beau Juif_** (1822).
- **_Le Centenaire, ou les Deux Béringheld_** (1822) — Horace de Saint-Aubin.
- **_Le Vicaire des Ardennes_** (1822) — Horace de Saint-Aubin.
- **_La Dernière Fée, ou la Nouvelle Lampe merveilleuse_** (1823).
- **_Annette et le Criminel_** (1824), later reworked/reissued in altered form.
- **_Wann-Chlore_** (1825).

These works are important for literary development even though Balzac did not incorporate them straightforwardly into the mature corpus.

### Major works outside or adjacent to the final *Comédie humaine* architecture

- **_Physiologie du mariage, ou Méditations de philosophie éclectique sur le bonheur et le malheur conjugal_** (1829) — later placed among the Études analytiques.

- **_Théorie de la démarche_** (1833).
- **_Traité des excitants modernes_** (1839), originally published in connection with a new edition of Brillat-Savarin's *Physiologie du goût*.
- Numerous journalistic articles, prefaces, political pieces, literary criticism, social “codes,” and short works now gathered in critical editions of **_Œuvres diverses_**.

### Journalism and political writing

Balzac wrote a large body of journalism, criticism, satire, and occasional prose, much of it anonymous or pseudonymous in first publication. Important areas include:

- theater and literary criticism;
- newspaper polemics;
- publishing and press economics;
- political commentary;
- social typologies;
- “physiologies” and codes;
- the **_Lettre sur le procès de Peytel_** (1839);
- essays later grouped as *Œuvres diverses*.

A truly exhaustive article-by-article bibliography requires specialized chronological bibliographies and critical editions rather than a conventional list of book titles.

### Correspondence and papers

#### *Lettres à l'Étrangère*

Letters to Ewelina Hańska written from **1832 to 1850**. These were not published by Balzac as a book. They appeared posthumously in edited collections beginning decades later and have been repeatedly re-edited.

They are primary evidence for:

- writing schedules;
- publication;
- debt;
- travel;
- sexual and romantic life;
- jealousy;
- politics;
- collecting;
- health;
- plans for marriage;
- self-mythologizing.

Because posthumous correspondence depends on editorial selection, dating, transcription, and provenance, modern critical editions are preferable to early popular selections where exact wording matters.

#### General correspondence

Balzac's correspondence with his family, publishers, writers, politicians, friends, doctors, and business associates is dispersed across posthumous **_Correspondance_** and **_Correspondance générale_** editions, including major editorial work by **Roger Pierrot**.

### The Furne corrected copy

Balzac's personal copy of the Furne edition, filled with manuscript corrections, is a major textual artifact. It demonstrates that “final text” is unstable: even after the supposedly definitive collected edition began appearing, he continued rewriting.

Located in the house where Balzac repeatedly stayed and worked. It preserves:

## Death / Final Months

Balzac's final two years were dominated by illness and the effort to marry Hańska and return to Paris.

### Marriage

**14 March 1850:** marriage to Ewelina Hańska at Berdychiv, in the Russian Empire (present-day Ukraine).

The marriage is sometimes romanticized as the triumphant culmination of an eighteen-year love story. The correspondence shows something more complicated: passion, literary intimacy, jealousy, money, property law, delays, family pressure, travel, illness, and bargaining all coexisted.

### Return to Paris

Balzac returned to the rue Fortunée house seriously ill. The much-retold arrival scene—with a locksmith needed to enter and a household in disorder—comes through later narrative sources but is consistent with the broader evidence of domestic collapse.

### Terminal disease

The final condition included:

- severe breathlessness;
- edema;
- inability to climb stairs;
- cardiac enlargement described by Balzac;
- progressive weakness;
- leg complications and gangrene in retrospective medical reconstruction.

The simplest responsible medical summary is **advanced cardiac failure with congestive complications**, while recognizing that the diagnosis is retrospective.

### Date dispute

- **BnF authority record:** 18 August 1850.
- **Maison de Balzac narrative chronology:** 19 August 1850.
- **Mirbeau's later retelling:** calls 18 August the day of death.

The discrepancy should be retained rather than silently erased.

### Funeral and burial

Balzac was buried at **Père-Lachaise**. **Victor Hugo** delivered the funeral oration. The event immediately participated in the conversion of a financially embattled, controversial working novelist into a national and European literary monument.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate memorialization

Balzac's death produced competing images:

- gigantic worker;
- comic debtor;
- vulgar social climber;
- visionary;
- reactionary Legitimist;
- anatomist of capitalism;
- romantic;
- realist;
- mystic;
- pornographic or immoral writer in hostile criticism;
- encyclopedist of society.

No single one describes the corpus adequately. The history of Balzac's reputation is a history of these competing appropriations.

### Family and friend biographies

Laure Surville, Gautier, Werdet, and later memoirists stabilized vivid anecdotes about his appearance, robe, laughter, coffee, debt, and work routines. Those accounts remain indispensable because they preserve observations no archive can replace, but they also constructed the “Balzac” later generations inherited.

### Realism

Balzac became one of the canonical founders of European literary realism. Yet the category can obscure the supernatural and speculative components of his writing. *La Peau de chagrin*, *Séraphîta*, *Louis Lambert*, *Melmoth réconcilié*, *Le Chef-d'œuvre inconnu*, and many short texts refuse a strictly documentary definition of realism.

### Marx and Engels

The Marxist reception is historically significant precisely because it crosses political lines. Engels admired the social truth he saw in Balzac despite Balzac's Legitimist sympathies. Marx's admiration, as remembered by Lafargue, made *La Comédie humaine* a privileged literary archive for later socialist criticism.

### Modernism and the novel

Balzac's recurring-character system and large social architecture influenced or challenged:

- Émile Zola's Rougon-Macquart project;
- Marcel Proust's social and recurring-character world;
- Henry James's theory and practice of fiction;
- later roman-fleuve and multivolume social cycles;
- modern television-style serial narrative and “shared universe” comparisons, though such analogies are retrospective.

### Psychoanalysis, semiotics, gender, queer criticism

Balzac's fiction became a major testing ground for twentieth-century theory:

- Freud and psychoanalytic traditions drew on the nineteenth-century novel as symptom and social archive.
- Barthes's *S/Z* made *Sarrasine* a canonical semiotic text.
- Gender and queer scholarship has paid particular attention to Vautrin, *Sarrasine*, *La Fille aux yeux d'or*, *Séraphîta*, marriage markets, cross-gender performance, and the instability of sexual categories.

These interpretive afterlives should not be mistaken for biographical facts about Balzac, but they are central to the history of his work.

### Rodin's *Monument to Balzac*

In **1891**, the Société des gens de lettres commissioned **Auguste Rodin** to make a monument to Balzac. Émile Zola was involved in the institutional context of the commission.

Rodin studied Balzac's appearance through surviving images, including the **Bisson daguerreotype**, and developed a radical monument in which a massive body is enveloped in a robe.

When exhibited in **1898**, the work was rejected by the Société des gens de lettres and provoked a major scandal. Writers and artists including supporters of modernism rallied around Rodin.

The rejection eventually became part of the monument's canonical status. Balzac's real work robe, already a piece of authorial iconography, was transformed by Rodin into a nearly abstract symbol of creative force.

### Museums and archives

Balzac's material afterlife is unusually rich:

- Maison de Balzac, Paris.
- Musée Balzac, Château de Saché.
- BnF/Gallica.
- Lovenjoul archive, Institut de France.
- Rodin museum collections.
- nineteenth-century newspapers, contracts, correspondence, and critical editions.

### Textual instability as legacy

Because Balzac revised compulsively, his canon poses fundamental editorial questions:

- Which version is “the work”?
- Should first newspaper publication, first book form, Furne text, or Balzac's manuscript corrections control?
- How should posthumous completions be separated?
- How should changing titles and classifications be represented?

Modern Balzac scholarship is therefore as much textual and bibliographical as biographical.

## Documentary Status Summary

### Securely documented or strongly established

- Birth in Tours, 20 May 1799.
- Family identity and parents' marriage.
- Vendôme schooling and Paris legal education.
- Pseudonymous youth fiction.
- 1825–1828 publishing/printing/type-founding ventures.
- Chronic debt.
- Laure de Berny relationship.
- Major publication chronology.
- Hańska correspondence beginning 1832 and marriage in 1850.
- Buloz litigation and Balzac's victory.
- National Guard disciplinary detention.
- Peytel intervention.
- Presidency of Société des gens de lettres.
- Failed Académie française candidacies.
- Légion d'honneur, 1845.
- extensive travel.
- severe terminal heart/respiratory illness.
- death in August 1850.
- Rodin monument controversy.

### Strong scholarly reconstruction, not formal legal record

- Jean de Margonne as probable biological father of Henri-François Balzac.
- Honoré de Balzac as probable biological father of Marie-Caroline du Fresnay.

### Uncertain or source-conflicted

- exact baptismal status.
- exact reason for removal from Vendôme.
- precise total of Balzac's debts at any one “definitive” moment.
- exact modern diagnosis of several earlier neurological/respiratory episodes.
- whether death should be dated 18 or 19 August in older narratives.

### Legend / later mythology

- guaranteed aristocratic ancestry implied by “de Balzac.”
- exact Napoleon statuette inscription as a securely autograph statement.
- magical powers of the cane.
- fixed “50 cups of coffee a day.”
- coffee as sole cause of death.
- deathbed appeal to fictional Dr Bianchon.
- Mirbeau/Gigoux scandal narrative as transparent eyewitness fact.

## Atlas Connections

### Emanuel Swedenborg

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Balzac wrote extensively about the earlier Swedish theologian in *Séraphîta* (composed 1833–1835), devoting a substantial passage to Swedenborg’s life, doctrines, visions, and reception. The novel was an important vehicle for introducing Swedenborgian ideas to Balzac’s French readership. [Persée](https://www.persee.fr/doc/caief_0571-5865_1963_num_15_1_2263), [Swedenborg Foundation](https://swedenborg.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/NCE_ScribeofHeaven.pdf)

### Napoleon Bonaparte

- **[T1-] Documented fact:** Balzac repeatedly wrote about the earlier emperor across *La Comédie humaine*, correspondence, and authorial self-presentation. Napoleon functions as a historical actor and as the defining model of post-Revolutionary ambition for characters such as Rastignac. The Fondation Napoléon accordingly catalogs Balzac among “authors of Napoleon” and has published a dedicated study of the Napoleonic myth constructed in his writing. [Fondation Napoléon bibliography](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/bibliographies/authors-of-napoleon/), [Fondation Napoléon study](https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/articles/napoleon-vu-par-balzac-un-mythe-edifie-par-un-futur-empereur-des-lettres/)

### Alexandre Dumas

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Balzac and Dumas met within the Paris literary and salon world, including Delphine de Girardin’s salon. Their relations were sufficiently personal that Dumas served as one of Balzac’s pallbearers at the Paris funeral in August 1850. [Maison de Balzac / Paris Musées](https://www.parismuseescollections.paris.fr/en/node/127), [contemporary-biographical account](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Honore_de_Balzac%2C_His_Life_and_Writings/Chapter_XVII)

### Victor Hugo

- **[T5] Documented fact:** Hugo and Balzac met in the Paris literary milieu, including Delphine de Girardin’s salon. Hugo also visited the dying Balzac at rue Fortunée in August 1850, acted as a pallbearer, and delivered the funeral oration at Père-Lachaise. [Maison de Balzac / Paris Musées](https://www.parismuseescollections.paris.fr/en/node/127), [contemporary-biographical account](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Honore_de_Balzac%2C_His_Life_and_Writings/Chapter_XVII)

- **[T1+] Documented fact:** As the later writer in this particular act of memorial writing, Hugo publicly interpreted Balzac’s life and achievement in the funeral address delivered in Paris on 21 August 1850; on Balzac’s dossier, Balzac is therefore the earlier, written-about figure. [Académie française](https://www.academie-francaise.fr/centenaire-de-la-mort-de-balzac-celebre-la-sorbonne)

### Gustave Flaubert

- **[T1+] Documented fact:** Flaubert discussed and evaluated the already-deceased Balzac in his correspondence, admiring the scale and social power of *La Comédie humaine* while criticizing Balzac’s prose style. The often-cited judgment that Balzac would have been a great man “if he had known how to write” belongs to this documented one-sided critical engagement, not to a personal meeting. [Cambridge University Press](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/15512/excerpt/9780521815512_excerpt.pdf)

### Karl Marx

- **[T1+] Documented fact:** Marx repeatedly read and wrote about Balzac after the novelist’s death. In an 1867 letter to Engels he praised *Le Chef-d’œuvre inconnu* and *Melmoth réconcilié* as masterpieces; other testimony records his projected study of *La Comédie humaine*. The surviving letter establishes the written engagement independently of the later report about the unrealized book. [Marx-Engels Gesamtausgabe](https://megadigital.bbaw.de/briefe/detail.xql?id=M0000231), [Cambridge University Press](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811070/66472/excerpt/9781107066472_excerpt.pdf)

### Marcel Proust

- **[T1+] Documented fact:** Proust made Balzac a sustained subject of literary criticism in the manuscripts and posthumously arranged texts collected as *Contre Sainte-Beuve*, including sections titled “Sainte-Beuve et Balzac” and “Le Balzac de M. de Guermantes.” This is a documented later writer’s analysis of the earlier novelist. [BnF resources on Proust](https://www.bnf.fr/fr/ressources-electroniques-sur-marcel-proust), [digitized contents of *Contre Sainte-Beuve*](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Contre_Sainte-Beuve)

### Roland Barthes

- **[T1+] Documented fact:** Barthes devoted *S/Z* (Paris: Seuil, 1970) to an intensive structural and semiotic analysis of Balzac’s 1830 novella *Sarrasine*. The BnF catalog identifies Balzac and *Sarrasine* as the work’s subject and notes that the volume incorporates Balzac’s text. [BnF catalogue](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark%3A/12148/cb35218566v)
