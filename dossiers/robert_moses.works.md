## Works by Robert Moses

### Authored texts

- **1914:** *The Civil Service of Great Britain*. Moses’s Columbia doctoral dissertation, published as a study of British public administration; it is a scholarly monograph rather than a planning project. [1][3]

- **1940:** *Theory and Practice in Politics: The Godkin Lectures, 1939*. A book derived from lectures at Harvard, setting out Moses’s theory of administration and political power. [3][4]

- **1956:** *Working for the People: Promise and Performance in Public Service*. A collection of arguments and retrospective essays about government, public works, recreation, housing, and administration; part memoir, part manifesto for executive power. [3][4]

- **1957:** *La Guardia: A Salute and a Memoir*. A political memoir and assessment of Mayor Fiorello H. La Guardia, written by an associate whose recollections are also acts of self-positioning. [3]

- **1962:** *A Tribute to Governor Smith*. A commemorative account of Alfred E. Smith and the administrative-reform milieu in which Moses acquired power. [3]

- **1970:** *Public Works: A Dangerous Trade*. Moses’s large retrospective compilation of memoir, correspondence, speeches, documents, and polemic. It is less a unified autobiography than a documentary self-portrait assembled to defend a career and a governing method. [3][4]

- **1974:** *Statement on The Power Broker*. A twenty-three-page typescript rebuttal to Robert A. Caro’s biography, circulated rather than conventionally published; it functions as a counter-manifesto, and its surviving archival form resists treatment as a finished book. [5]

### Administrative protocols, plans, and reports

- **1919–1922:** *New York State administrative-reorganization program*. A body of reports, draft laws, organizational charts, and constitutional proposals prepared with the state Reconstruction Commission. It is a collective governmental project rather than a singly authored work, although Moses was a principal draftsman. [2][6]

- **1924–1963:** *New York State park plan*. A continuously revised protocol for acquiring, connecting, financing, and administering state parks. Its obra is simultaneously textual, legal, territorial, and institutional. [2][6]

- **1924–1963:** *Long Island state park-and-parkway system*. A regional Gesamtkunstwerk joining beaches, parks, causeways, parkways, bathhouses, planting, signs, furniture, and administrative authorities. Moses directed it, but engineers, landscape architects, architects, legislators, contractors, and public labor made its individual components. [2][7]

- **1934:** *Plan for putting 80,000 men to work on 1,700 relief projects*. An emergency public-works program prepared for the La Guardia administration. The proposal became a generative protocol for parks, playgrounds, pools, roads, and civic improvements rather than a single built object. [1][2]

- **1934–1966:** *Triborough Bridge and Tunnel Authority “Green Books”*. Annual institutional compilations of reports, speeches, publicity, technical descriptions, and press material. The archive warns that their contents were prepared collectively and are not all directly attributable to Moses. [2]

- **1944:** *Postwar Works Program*. A coordinated construction agenda for New York City after the Second World War, combining arterial highways, recreation, hospitals, schools, housing, and other capital works. It is a project-document whose intended totality was never built exactly as proposed. [1][6]

- **1945:** *Master Plan of the Brooklyn Civic Center and Downtown Area*. A municipal planning report associated with Moses’s citywide reconstruction program; institutional authorship belongs to the City Planning Commission, so calling it solely “by Robert Moses” would overstate personal authorship. [4]

- **1949–1960:** *Title I slum-clearance program*. A protocol for condemning, clearing, transferring, and privately redeveloping designated urban areas. Twenty projects were pursued and sixteen reached some form of realization, but the resulting buildings were generally designed and developed by others. [1][6]

- **1960–1966:** *New York World’s Fair administrative and development plan*. An evolving ensemble of site plans, regulations, exhibit solicitations, financing devices, correspondence, and construction decisions. It is a collective institutional project presided over by Moses, not a fair personally designed by him. [2][8]

### Parks, beaches, and regional recreation works

- **1925–1963:** *Long Island state-park network*. The developed network included Belmont Lake, Bethpage, Caumsett, Captree, Hempstead Lake, Heckscher, Hither Hills, Jones Beach, Montauk Point, Orient Beach, Robert Moses, Sunken Meadow, Valley Stream, and Wildwood state parks, among other properties and facilities. The New York State Archives treats this as a changing administrative and construction corpus, not a fixed list of individually authored designs. [6]

- **1929 onward:** *Jones Beach State Park*. A beach park, architectural ensemble, engineered landscape, and transportation destination; Moses was its dominant client and organizer, while architects and engineers including Herbert Magoon, Aymar Embury II, and Gilmore D. Clarke shaped particular structures and landscapes. [1][6][7]

- **1930s:** *Jacob Riis Park redevelopment*. A beach, bathhouse, boardwalk, recreation, and approach-road ensemble substantially remade under Moses. Its buildings and landscape are collaborative public works, not Moses’s personal architectural designs. [1][7]

- **1934–1937:** *Orchard Beach*. An artificial crescent beach, bathhouse, promenade, parking field, and engineered shoreline in Pelham Bay Park. Moses initiated and controlled the project, but the physical design was produced by Parks Department architects, landscape architects, and engineers. [1][7]

- **1934–1960:** *New York City park reconstruction program*. A corpus of alterations to Central, Prospect, Riverside, Pelham Bay, Van Cortlandt, Crotona, Fort Tryon, Flushing Meadows, and numerous other parks. It resists classification as a single work because it combined hundreds of independent interventions, maintenance campaigns, demolitions, and new facilities. [1][2]

- **1934–1960:** *Neighborhood parks and playgrounds*. The Moses Parks Department built or reconstructed more than six hundred playgrounds. The standard scholarly catalogue records these as a large, incompletely separable corpus; treating every playground as Moses’s individual design would erase the department’s collective authorship. [1][7][9]

- **1936:** *Astoria Play Center*. A WPA swimming complex comprising bathhouse, pools, terraces, and landscape, made under Moses’s standardized aquatic-center program. [1][9]

- **1936:** *Betsy Head Play Center*. A WPA pool-and-bathhouse complex; a collaborative municipal work overseen by Moses rather than personally designed by him. [1][9]

- **1936:** *Crotona Play Center*. A WPA aquatic and recreational complex forming part of the coordinated eleven-pool program. [1][9]

- **1936:** *Hamilton Fish Play Center*. A bathhouse-and-pool ensemble inserted into an older park and made through the Parks Department/WPA partnership. [1][9]

- **1936:** *Highbridge Play Center*. A major swimming and diving complex beside High Bridge, produced as part of the citywide WPA pool project. [1][9]

- **1936:** *Jackie Robinson Play Center*—originally *Colonial Park Play Center*. A pool, bathhouse, recreation, and landscape complex; its later name is not its original project title. [1][9]

- **1936:** *McCarren Play Center*. A monumental bathhouse and swimming complex, designed within the Parks Department by a team led architecturally by Aymar Embury II. [1][9]

- **1936:** *Red Hook Play Center*. A WPA pool-and-bathhouse complex embedded in the larger Red Hook recreation area. [1][9]

- **1936:** *Sunset Play Center*. A pool, bathhouse, and terrace ensemble overlooking New York Harbor. [1][9]

- **1936:** *Thomas Jefferson Play Center*. A WPA aquatic complex in East Harlem, one manifestation of the common program adapted to an individual site. [1][9]

- **1936:** *Tompkinsville Pool*. The Staten Island component of the eleven-pool campaign; unlike most of the others, it was not integrated into a comparably monumental surviving play-center ensemble. [1][9]

- **1936–1939:** *Riverside Park and West Side Improvement*. An inseparable composition of park, railway covering, highway, paths, recreation grounds, retaining structures, and the 79th Street Boat Basin. Moses directed the public project, while Gilmore D. Clarke, Clinton Lloyd, and large engineering and architectural staffs designed its parts. [1]

- **1936–1939:** *Central Park Zoo reconstruction*. A compact zoo and architectural ensemble that replaced the earlier menagerie. Its later wholesale redesign means the Moses-era work survives only in altered fragments. [1]

- **1936–1939:** *Prospect Park Zoo reconstruction*. A zoo complex built under the unified Parks Department program; later redevelopment substantially changed the Moses-era manifestation. [1]

- **1939–1964:** *Flushing Meadows–Corona Park*. A former ash dump transformed first for the 1939–40 World’s Fair and then remade for the 1964–65 fair and permanent park. Moses’s obra here is the repeated territorial transformation and infrastructure, not every pavilion or sculpture placed on the site. [1][8]

- **1947–1950:** *Great Kills Park*—initially *Marine Park*. A reclaimed shoreline park and recreation project on Staten Island; its transfer into Gateway National Recreation Area changed its institutional identity after Moses’s involvement. [10]

- **1950s:** *Wollman Memorial Rink*. A Central Park skating facility delivered through Moses’s Parks Department; architects and engineers, rather than Moses personally, authored its physical design. [1]

### Parkways, expressways, and arterial systems

- **1927–1949:** *Southern State Parkway*. A staged limited-access landscaped road connecting western Long Island with the Jones Beach system. Its authorship is institutional and engineering-based, although Moses controlled its route, financing, and program. [6][7]

- **1929:** *Wantagh State Parkway*. A landscaped access road and causeway system built principally to carry motorists to Jones Beach. [6][7]

- **1931–1965:** *Northern State Parkway*. A parkway developed in successive sections across Long Island; its final form therefore has no single design date. [6][7]

- **1933–1964:** *Grand Central Parkway*. A limited-access route built and repeatedly extended or reconstructed under Moses’s state and city agencies. [1][7]

- **1934–1937:** *Henry Hudson Parkway*. A landscaped arterial and park-edge work tied to the West Side Improvement and Henry Hudson Bridge. [1][7]

- **1934–1940:** *Belt Parkway*. A circumferential system composed of the Shore, Southern, Laurelton, and Cross Island sections, conceived with adjacent parks, beaches, and green margins. It is better understood as a metropolitan landscape-and-mobility project than as one road. [1][7]

- **1935–1937:** *Interborough Parkway reconstruction*—now largely the Jackie Robinson Parkway. Moses’s agencies rebuilt and extended an earlier route; the work is therefore a transformation rather than an entirely new creation. [1][11]

- **1936–1956:** *Major Deegan Expressway*. A Bronx arterial completed in stages and linked to Moses’s regional bridge-and-highway network. [1][11]

- **1937–1964:** *Brooklyn–Queens Expressway*. A long, staged urban expressway incorporating new construction, older parkway segments, and the triple-level Brooklyn Heights cantilever. Moses was its principal promoter, but it cannot be reduced to a single design or opening. [1][11]

- **1941–1964:** *Gowanus Expressway*. An elevated and surface highway assembled in stages from the earlier Gowanus Parkway corridor. [1][11]

- **1947–1964:** *Harlem River Drive*. A limited-access conversion and extension of earlier river-edge roads along Manhattan. [1][11]

- **1948–1972:** *Cross Bronx Expressway*. A staged urban highway driven through the Bronx and completed after Moses had lost much of his authority. Dating and authorship are therefore distributed, while the associated displacement remains integral to what the project made. [1][11]

- **1952–1954:** *Sagtikos and Sunken Meadow State Parkways*. Linked Long Island parkways joining the east–west road network to Sunken Meadow State Park. [6]

- **1953–1960:** *Prospect Expressway*. A short Brooklyn arterial conceived as part of a larger route toward Ocean Parkway; only the built portion became an obra, while the proposed extension remained a project. [1][11]

- **1953–1964:** *Robert Moses Causeway*. A causeway and bridge system connecting Long Island to Captree and Fire Island; its present name should not be mistaken for proof of sole design authorship. [6]

- **1955–1972:** *Long Island Expressway*. A multi-administration highway built section by section; Moses powerfully promoted and shaped it but did not personally originate or complete every component. [7][11]

- **1958–1963:** *Sheridan Expressway*. A partially realized Bronx route; the longer connection envisioned by Moses was never completed. [1][11]

- **1959–1960:** *Clearview Expressway*. A Queens arterial constructed through an established residential corridor as part of the approaches to the Throgs Neck Bridge. [1][11]

- **1959–1964:** *Nassau Expressway*. Only portions of the larger planned route were built, making the surviving road the fragmentary manifestation of a more extensive project. [7][11]

### Bridges, tunnels, dams, and power works

- **1936:** *Triborough Bridge*. A three-bridge, viaduct, interchange, park, toll, and administrative complex joining Manhattan, the Bronx, and Queens. Moses rescued and institutionalized the project, but engineer Othmar Ammann and architect Aymar Embury II were among its principal designers. [1][2]

- **1936:** *Henry Hudson Bridge*. A toll bridge integrated with the Henry Hudson Parkway and the West Side park system; it is one component of a larger ensemble. [1]

- **1937:** *Marine Parkway Bridge*. A vertical-lift bridge linking Brooklyn with the Rockaway peninsula and Jacob Riis Park. [1][12]

- **1939:** *Bronx–Whitestone Bridge*. A suspension bridge and approach-road project promoted through the Triborough authority and engineered under Othmar Ammann; Moses’s role was that of power broker, client, and administrator, not structural designer. [1][2]

- **1939:** *Cross Bay Bridge*. A bridge and Rockaway-access project; the original span was later replaced, so the first Moses-era physical object no longer survives intact. [1][12]

- **1940:** *Queens–Midtown Tunnel*. A vehicular tunnel inherited from another authority and completed under a complicated institutional history; its attribution to Moses is administrative and political rather than one of original conception or engineering. [1][2]

- **1950:** *Brooklyn–Battery Tunnel*. A vehicular tunnel built after Moses’s preferred bridge at the Battery was rejected. He controlled its completion through the consolidated authority, but Ole Singstad and other engineers supplied the tunnel expertise. [1][2]

- **1957:** *Robert Moses Niagara Power Plant and associated Niagara power project*. A hydroelectric generating complex realized by the New York Power Authority under Moses’s chairmanship; it is an industrial and territorial public work designed by engineering organizations. [2][6]

- **1958:** *Kingston–Rhinecliff Bridge*. A Hudson River crossing developed by the New York State Bridge Authority with Moses’s influence; its inclusion in his corpus is more peripheral than that of the Triborough Authority crossings. [1]

- **1960:** *Throgs Neck Bridge*. A suspension bridge and approach-highway complex conceived to relieve the Bronx–Whitestone crossing; Othmar Ammann was responsible for its principal engineering design. [1][2]

- **1954–1958:** *St. Lawrence–Franklin D. Roosevelt Power Project*, including the later-named *Robert Moses Power Dam*. A binational hydroelectric and navigation undertaking in which Moses, as Power Authority chairman, directed New York’s institutional participation; sole authorship would be especially misleading. [2][6]

- **1959–1964:** *Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge*. A suspension bridge, approaches, toll complex, and metropolitan highway connection. Moses was its decisive promoter and institutional client, while Othmar Ammann was chief engineer; even the spelling of “Verrazzano” was disputed and officially corrected long after construction. [1][2]

- **1967–1970:** *Replacement Cross Bay Bridge*. Moses’s last major bridge undertaking, opened after the end of his dominant citywide role. [10][12]

### Housing, clearance, and civic redevelopment

- **1949–1960:** *Committee on Slum Clearance project corpus*. Projects at Washington Square, Lincoln Square, Harlem, North Harlem, Delancey Street, Corlears Hook, Williamsburg, Fort Greene, Pratt Institute, Brooklyn Civic Center, and other sites combined plans, condemnations, demolition, land transfer, housing, institutions, and private development. The corpus resists being called Moses’s “buildings”: he made the clearance-and-development mechanism, while many architects and sponsors made the structures. [1][2]

- **1949 onward:** *Washington Square Southeast redevelopment*. A superblock urban-renewal project whose manifestations included Washington Square Village and later New York University development. Moses’s larger plan was only partially realized, and the surviving buildings are not his architectural designs. [1][13]

- **1950s–1960s:** *Morningside–Manhattanville urban renewal*. A clearance and institutional-expansion project tied to housing and Columbia University. Its long, contested implementation exceeded Moses’s direct tenure and cannot be treated as a single finished work. [1]

- **1952–1969:** *Lincoln Square Urban Renewal Project*. A fifty-three-acre clearance and redevelopment undertaking whose most celebrated manifestation was Lincoln Center. Moses controlled the Title I project, but the performing-arts buildings were commissioned, financed, and designed by other institutions and architects. [1][13]

- **1956–1959:** *Washington Square Village*. A two-slab housing complex produced within Moses’s Washington Square renewal project. The planning framework belongs in his corpus, but architects Skidmore, Owings & Merrill, developer sponsors, and New York University complicate any claim that Moses “made” the buildings. [13]

- **1940s–1960s:** *Public-housing and housing-clearance program*. Moses’s roads, parks, clearance committees, and construction-coordinator’s office intersected with approximately 150,000 housing units, but the figure describes institutional output rather than a personal catalogue of designs. Many developments were works of the New York City Housing Authority or private Title I sponsors. [2][7]

### Civic complexes, fairs, and spectacles

- **1939–1940:** *New York World’s Fair site and permanent works*. Moses did not create the fair as a whole, but his Parks Department shaped Flushing Meadows and inherited permanent elements including the New York City Building, amphitheatre, roads, and park infrastructure. Attribution of individual pavilions to him would be false. [1][8]

- **1947–1952:** *United Nations headquarters siting and access works*. Moses participated in securing and servicing the Manhattan site and in associated street and roadway changes. The headquarters architecture belongs to the international Board of Design and its architects, not to Moses. [2][7]

- **1956:** *New York Coliseum*. An exhibition and convention complex at Columbus Circle, developed through the Triborough authority. Its demolition in 2000 means the obra now survives chiefly through documents, photographs, and its effects on the site. [1]

- **1962–1964:** *Shea Stadium*. A municipal stadium and World’s Fair-adjacent infrastructure project whose construction Moses facilitated. The architectural design was by Praeger-Kavanagh-Waterbury, so Moses’s authorship is institutional rather than formal. [1][8]

- **1964–1965:** *New York World’s Fair*. A temporary city of corporate, governmental, religious, technological, and amusement exhibits organized by a corporation headed by Moses. As a Gesamtkunstwerk it exceeds individual authorship; many constituent works were independent commissions, and the fair itself lacked official recognition by the Bureau International des Expositions. [2][8]

- **1964:** *Flushing Meadows permanent fair infrastructure*. The Unisphere precinct, Fountain of the Planets and Pool of Industry, marina, heliport, roads, utilities, landscaping, and reused buildings formed the intended permanent residue of the fair. Moses administered the ensemble, but its components had distinct designers. [8]

### Unrealized projects

- **1939–1941:** *Brooklyn–Battery Bridge*. A proposed bridge between Lower Manhattan and Brooklyn, accompanied by approaches that threatened Battery Park. Federal rejection converted the intention into the Brooklyn–Battery Tunnel; the bridge remains a project rather than a creation. [1][2]

- **1940s–1968:** *Lower Manhattan Expressway*. A repeatedly revised elevated or depressed highway intended to connect the Holland Tunnel with the Manhattan and Williamsburg bridges. Only approach fragments were built; the named expressway as a whole never appeared. [1][2][11]

- **1940s–1960s:** *Mid-Manhattan Expressway*. A proposed cross-town highway near 30th Street, at times imagined as an elevated structure passing through buildings. It remained a planning project and political instrument, not a built road. [1][11]

- **1940s–1960s:** *Cross-Harlem Expressway and Hudson River crossing*. A proposed connection across upper Manhattan toward New Jersey, sometimes linked to a new bridge or tunnel. Its configurations changed, and no single definitive built object corresponds to the title. [1][11]

- **1940s–1960s:** *Bushwick Expressway*. A proposed Brooklyn–Queens route intended to connect the Williamsburg Bridge corridor to southeastern Queens and the Nassau Expressway. It was not built. [1][11]

- **1940s–1960s:** *Cross-Brooklyn Expressway*. A proposed east–west Brooklyn arterial distinct from the Brooklyn–Queens Expressway. Its routes and names varied, so it is best classified as a family of unrealized plans. [1][11]

- **1940s–1960s:** *Shore Front Parkway*. A proposed limited-access road along the Rockaway oceanfront. The road remained unrealized although related beachfront and access improvements were made. [1][11]

- **1950s–1960s:** *Prospect Expressway extension*. A proposed continuation toward Ocean Parkway and southern Brooklyn. The existing Prospect Expressway is the truncated manifestation of this larger intention. [1][11]

- **1950s–1973:** *Oyster Bay–Rye Bridge*. A proposed Long Island Sound crossing with approach highways. Moses continued advocating it after losing most of his offices, but it remained an unbuilt project. [6][7]

- **1950s–1960s:** *Richmond Parkway system*. Portions were built on Staten Island, while the controversial extension through the Greenbelt was abandoned. The name therefore covers both an obra and an unrealized project and should not be classified wholly as either. [1][11]

- **1960s:** *Vieux Carré Riverfront Expressway*. A proposed elevated highway beside New Orleans’s French Quarter, developed during Moses’s work as an outside consultant. It was never built, and its authorship was shared with Louisiana highway agencies and consultants. [7]

### Archival corpora that resist work-status

- **1912–1980:** *Robert Moses papers*. Correspondence, memoranda, plans, reports, speeches, press releases, photographs, clippings, and office files preserved by the New York Public Library. This is a documentary remainder of work rather than a work deliberately composed as a whole, and the finding aid explicitly says it is not comprehensive. [2]

- **1924–1960:** *Housing files*. Letters, reports, plans, and publicity gathered from numerous sources across Moses’s offices. The archive classifies them as a series, but their mixed authorship prevents their treatment as a single Moses text. [2]

- **Career-long:** *Speeches and ceremonial addresses*. The surviving papers contain only a selection from Moses’s numerous openings, graduations, dedications, hearings, and professional addresses. This is an incomplete performative corpus: each speech acted within an occasion, but no authoritative collected-works edition establishes “everything” he delivered. [2]

- **1934–1968:** *Office-generated plans, maps, models, photographs, and publicity*. These objects helped obtain money, approvals, and public consent and therefore acted rather than merely described. They belong to Moses’s administrative machine, but most were made by unnamed or institutionally credited staff and cannot responsibly be reassigned to him as sole author. [1][2]
