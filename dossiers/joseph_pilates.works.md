## Works by Joseph Hubertus Pilates

### Contrology: method, repertoire, and living project

- **c. 1914–1967:** *Contrology*. Pilates’s evolving system of coordinated body-and-mind conditioning is an obra dispersed among exercises, apparatus, teaching, photographs, films, and writings—a practical Gesamtkunstwerk rather than a single fixed text. Its boundaries resist definitive cataloguing: contemporary Pilates includes later modifications, while the surviving archival record attributes hundreds of exercises to Joseph and Clara Pilates jointly.[^1][^2]
- **By 1945:** *The Return to Life mat sequence*. A protocol of thirty-four floor exercises, photographed and verbally instructed in *Return to Life Through Contrology*; it is the most firmly fixed sequence within the otherwise evolving method.[^3]
- **1926–1967:** *The Contrology studio / Pilates Studio, 939 Eighth Avenue, New York*. A jointly operated teaching environment made by Joseph and Clara Pilates, filled with apparatus, exercise charts, and individualized programs. It resists treatment as Joseph’s sole work because Clara participated substantially in its practice and development.[^1][^4]
- **1939–1951:** *Body-conditioning work at Jacob’s Pillow*. A recurring teaching project rather than a single work: Pilates conducted classes, devised programs for dancers, and documented apparatus and exercises there.[^1][^5]
- **1950s–1960s:** *Return to Life Club*. A community manifestation of Contrology—principally a men’s mat class in Lee, Massachusetts—rather than a separately codified exercise work.[^6]

### Books, manifesto-protocols, and promotional texts

- **1934:** *Your Health: A Corrective System of Exercising That Revolutionizes the Entire Field of Physical Education*. A manifesto for bodily reform: it argues for Pilates’s philosophy and social program more than it supplies a complete exercise protocol.[^1][^3]
- **1945:** *Return to Life Through Contrology*. Co-authored with William John Miller, this is both manifesto and protocol: its opening exposition defines Contrology, while its photographic section prescribes the thirty-four-exercise mat sequence. Miller’s precise share in the prose is not consistently distinguished in later editions.[^1][^3]
- **1957:** *Return to Life Through Contrology* (informational pamphlet). A promotional manifesto written with Frederick Rand Rogers and distributed to prospective students; because Rogers’s and Pilates’s contributions are not separately identified, sole authorship should not be assumed.[^7][^8]
- **1920s–1960s, various dates:** *Contrology advertisements, flyers, exercise charts, and apparatus instructions*. A dispersed body of functional texts—part publicity, part protocol—preserved through archival publications and private collections rather than as a settled bibliography of individually titled works.[^4][^9]
- **Undated and incomplete:** *Unpublished writings and designs*. The archive edited by Sean Gallagher and Romana Kryzanowska gathers Pilates’s photographs, writings, and equipment designs, but does not establish every surviving sheet as an autonomous work; many resist that designation and are better understood as working documents.[^4]

### Patented inventions and furniture

- **1922–1923:** *Device for eliminating or improving leg and foot defects or the like* (German patent no. 375001; later called the Foot Corrector). A spring-, pneumatic-, or hydraulically resisted foot-support apparatus and Pilates’s earliest documented successful patent; modern Foot Correctors differ from the patented configuration.[^10][^11]
- **1924–1927:** *Gymnastic Apparatus* (German priority; Swiss CH116170A, British GB254571A, and US1621477A). A movable recumbent carriage resisted by weights or elastic elements—the patented ancestor of the apparatus later called the Universal Reformer. “Universal Reformer” is a later product name, not the patent’s title.[^12][^13]
- **1928–1930:** *Catapult* (US1785876A). A hand-held spring game for throwing and catching balls, not an exercise machine; its presence in Pilates’s patent œuvre usefully resists reducing all his inventions to Contrology.[^14]
- **1930–1932:** *Chair* (US1874613A). A spring-supported reclining “easy chair” intended to promote relaxation and bodily support, distinct from the exercise chair now called the Wunda Chair.[^15]
- **1930–1932:** *Bed or Couch* (US1876743A). An adjustable V-shaped bed, divan, and folding couch designed to support the body along a longitudinal dihedral angle; surviving examples acquired exercise springs and straps not shown in the patent drawings.[^16][^17]
- **1931–1934:** *Chair* (US1969901A; subsequently called the Wunda Chair). Convertible furniture whose hinged, spring-loaded seat becomes an exercise pedal when the chair is placed on its back; the patented form differs markedly from later studio chairs.[^17][^18]
- **1937–1938:** *Exercising Apparatus* (US2132862A; commonly called the Tensometer or Tens-O-Meter). A calibrated compression-spring implement with interchangeable contact pieces for hands, feet, or limbs; it is related conceptually to, but not identical with, the later Magic Circle.[^17][^19]

### Unpatented or substantially redesigned apparatus

- **By the 1930s:** *Universal Reformer*. The manufactured spring-resistance carriage apparatus developed from the 1924 patent; surviving machines and the Smithsonian example show that the realized object evolved beyond the patent’s weight-resistance embodiment.[^13][^20]
- **By the mid-1930s:** *High Chair / Electric Chair*. An upright spring-pedal exercise chair. It belongs to the canonical apparatus but lacks a securely published invention date or separate patent in the consulted catalogue.[^2][^11]
- **1930s:** *Foot Corrector*. The studio realization and continued redesign of the 1922 German invention; it should not be assumed to reproduce the patent literally.[^10][^11]
- **1930s:** *Toe Corrector / Magic Square*. A small spring-resistance implement for toes and feet, described in some sources as the toe counterpart to the Tens-O-Meter; surviving names and configurations vary.[^2][^11]
- **Late 1930s:** *Magic Circle*. A flexible resistance ring for the limbs and torso. Its exact relation to the patented Tensometer and to an otherwise unidentified late-1930s invention advertised by Pilates remains uncertain.[^2][^17]
- **1930s–1940s:** *Spine Corrector*. A curved body-supporting barrel used for spinal articulation and conditioning; original examples and blueprints survive, but no fixed invention date is established.[^2][^21]
- **1930s–1940s:** *Small Barrel / Half Barrel*. A compact curved support, one of several barrel forms; its status is that of a design family rather than a single invariant object.[^2][^21]
- **1930s–1940s:** *Ladder Barrel*. A curved barrel paired with an adjustable ladder frame, combining support, leverage, and stretching functions.[^2][^11]
- **1930s–1940s:** *Cadillac / Trapeze Table*. A raised mat or table surrounded by a spring-and-bar frame. Its familiar form emerged through changes to separate arm- and leg-spring boards, so it resists assignment to one invention moment.[^2][^11]
- **1930s–1940s:** *Guillotine / Tower*. A tall doorway-like frame for spring exercises, also advertised as a “gym in a doorway”; terminology overlaps with later tower components of the Cadillac.[^11][^17]
- **1930s–1940s:** *Ped-O-Pul / Pedi-Pole*. A small standing platform with an upright pole and overhead springs for posture and balance work; contemporary sources preserve variant spellings.[^2][^11]
- **1940s:** *Resister*. A portable resistance apparatus made in at least two versions. Pilates also made two illustrated instructional charts for it; the existence of a patent is reported but not securely confirmed.[^21]
- **By 1952:** *Idealizer / adjustable Ped-O-Pul*. A Ped-O-Pul variant with overhead springs and an attachable bicycle-style seat; archival evidence identifies “Idealizer” with this apparatus and contradicts later origin stories.[^22]
- **1950s:** *Baby Chair*. A small spring-backed exercise chair included among the twelve apparatus conventionally treated as original Pilates equipment.[^2]
- **1950s:** *Breath-A-Cizer*. A handheld respiratory-training implement, likewise counted among the canonical twelve; it is a breath protocol embodied as an object rather than furniture.[^2]
- **1950s:** *Neck Stretcher / Neck Corrector*. A head-and-neck resistance device documented in the later apparatus repertoire; its name and precise configuration vary across accounts.[^11]
- **1950s:** *Head Harness / Headdress*. A suspended headpiece used for neck work and postural alignment, documented in photographs and in Pilates’s own film of Anna Woolley Shaffer.[^23]
- **1950s:** *Rejuvenating Bed / Automatic Posture Correcting and Rejuvenating Bed*. A portable cot fitted with a tower so that sleeping furniture could also serve as exercise apparatus; published descriptions do not provide a secure patent or construction date.[^17]
- **1958:** *Bednasium*. A home exercise-bed project promoted through a photographic exercise series. Its identity overlaps with Pilates’s rejuvenating-bed and spring-bed designs, so it is better treated as a product-system and publicity project than as one stable machine.[^9][^17]
- **1940s–1950s:** *Airplane Boards*. A set of at least five differently shaped boards used with spring apparatus; the surviving evidence establishes a family of objects, not one definitive design.[^21]
- **Undated:** *Sandbag roll-up device*. A weighted hanging device used for wrist, arm, and shoulder conditioning, visible in Pilates’s apparatus documentation but not securely dated.[^11]
- **Undated:** *Arm Springs Board and Leg Springs Board*. Wall-mounted spring stations whose development contributed to the later Cadillac or Trapeze Table; treating them as independent works or transitional components is necessarily interpretive.[^11]
- **Undated, late work:** *Devana*. A composite apparatus combining mat, Spine Corrector, barrel, Reformer, and Cadillac functions. It is described as one of Pilates’s final inventions, but neither a secure date nor a patent was found.[^17]

### Photographic and moving-image works

- **Early 1930s–1967:** *Contrology photographic archive*. Pilates systematically photographed exercises, apparatus, students, and “before-and-after” postural studies, sometimes developing the material in his own darkroom. This is a documentary corpus rather than a sequence of reliably titled individual works, and some photographs were made by identified outside photographers rather than by Pilates himself.[^4][^23]
- **1930s–1960s:** *Contrology exercise charts*. Photographic sequences arranged as studio instructions—among them sustained Wunda Chair documentation and two Resister charts—function as visual protocols rather than autonomous art photographs.[^5][^21]
- **1930s–1960s:** *Contrology film archive*. Pilates filmed demonstrations, apparatus, students, and his V-bed; surviving canisters and footage establish a moving-image corpus, but no complete scholarly filmography or stable set of original titles exists.[^17][^23]
- **1953:** *Anna Woolley Shaffer using the Headdress*. A filmed exercise demonstration made by Pilates in his New York studio. It is identifiable by subject and date, but no evidence establishes that Pilates assigned it a formal title.[^23]
- **Undated:** *V-bed rooftop demonstration*. A film showing Pilates’s V-shaped bed in different sleeping and relaxation positions; it survives as archival footage rather than a formally titled film.[^17]
