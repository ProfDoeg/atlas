# Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier reconstructs the documented life of the New Spanish writer and Hieronymite nun commonly known as Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz. Seventeenth-century records are incomplete, several foundational biographical claims derive from Sor Juana’s own retrospective account or from Diego Calleja’s posthumous biography, and major questions—especially her birth year, emotional relationships, and intellectual renunciation—remain disputed.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a surviving contemporary document, publication, inscription, or archival record.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a near-contemporary witness or later source but not independently documented.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** an argument derived from textual or historical evidence.
- **Disputed claim:** supported by competing interpretations or contradictory evidence.
- **Legend or myth:** a culturally important story not securely established.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Religious name:** Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz.

**Names found in scholarship and records:** Juana Inés Ramírez; Juana Ramírez; Juana Inés de Asuaje; Juana Inés de Asbaje; Juana Inés Ramírez de Santillana; Juana Inés de Asuaje y Ramírez de Santillana. “Asbaje” is a historically influential spelling, but recent documentary work favors **Asuaje**, the form associated with her father’s Canarian family.

**Honorific and literary titles:**

- *La Décima Musa*—the Tenth Muse.
- *El Fénix de México* or *Fénix de México*—the Phoenix of Mexico.
- *La Monja de México*—the Nun of Mexico.
- “Americana” and “poetisa americana” in early printed paratexts.
- “Minerva de América,” a later celebratory epithet.
- “The first feminist of the Americas,” a modern interpretive designation, not a title she used.

**Self-deprecatory formula:** “Yo, la peor del mundo” (“I, the worst woman in the world”), associated with the penitential profession renewal signed in blood in 1694. Its transformation into a summary of her identity is largely posthumous.

**Birth:** Almost certainly 12 November, but the year is disputed: **1648** or **1651**.

**Birthplace:** San Miguel Nepantla, in the jurisdiction of Amecameca, New Spain; now Nepantla de Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz, municipality of Tepetlixpa, State of Mexico.

**Death:** 17 April 1695, Convent of San Jerónimo/Santa Paula, Mexico City.

**Age at death:** Forty-six if born in 1648; forty-three if born in 1651.

**Occupation and institutional identity:** Poet, playwright, prose writer, theological controversialist, court intellectual, composer and music theorist, convent archivist and accountant, and professed nun of the Order of Saint Jerome.

**Political status:** Subject of the Spanish monarchy, born in the Viceroyalty of New Spain. She never held civil office.

---

## Birth-Date Problem

The traditional date, **12 November 1651**, comes principally from the Jesuit Diego Calleja’s biography in *Fama y obras póstumas* (1700). Calleja corresponded with Sor Juana but wrote after her death and included anecdotes that cannot always be verified.

A baptismal entry discovered in the twentieth century records the baptism at Chimalhuacán on **2 December 1648** of “Inés, hija de la Iglesia,” with godparents Miguel Ramírez and Beatriz Ramírez. Scholars including Guillermo Ramírez España and, later, Guillermo Schmidhuber and Olga Martha Peña Doria identified this child with Sor Juana. “Hija de la Iglesia” normally meant that the parents were not stated, often because the child was born outside marriage.

The identification is strong but not conclusive because the record does not give the child’s parents or surname. The alternatives are therefore:

- **1648: currently favored by much documentary scholarship**, based on the baptismal record and family reconstruction.
- **1651: traditional chronology**, based on Calleja and long-standing commemoration.
- **Exact year unresolved:** the most cautious conclusion because no record explicitly combines Sor Juana’s full identity with either birth year.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Mother:** Isabel Ramírez de Santillana, a New Spanish criolla associated with the Nepantla–Panoayan area. Surviving documents describe her as unmarried. Her family managed agricultural property in the region.

**Father:** Pedro Manuel de Asuaje y Vargas Machuca, generally identified as a native of the Canary Islands. Recent archival research reports baptismal and migration records for the Asuaje family and a 1598 license enabling members of the paternal family to pass from the Canaries to New Spain.

Sor Juana’s parents did not marry. She was therefore legally illegitimate under colonial categories. Her own writings largely omit her father. She grew up chiefly within her maternal family’s household and property network.

### Maternal grandparents

The maternal grandfather is usually identified as **Pedro Ramírez de Santillana**; the grandmother as **Beatriz Ramírez Rendón**. Sor Juana spent important childhood years at the grandfather’s hacienda of Panoayan, near Amecameca. His library became central to the story she later told about her education.

A recovered testament of the maternal grandmother and other parish and notarial records have complicated older accounts of a poor and isolated childhood. The family possessed land and agricultural resources, though it did not belong to the highest aristocracy.

### Siblings and half-siblings

Isabel Ramírez had several children by two relationships. Exact sibling lists vary among biographers because baptismal names, surnames, and later identities do not always align cleanly.

Frequently identified full sisters include:

- María Ramírez.
- Josefa María Ramírez.
- A sister named Inés has sometimes appeared in older reconstructions, contributing to confusion about the 1648 baptismal record.

After Pedro de Asuaje disappeared from the surviving family record, Isabel Ramírez had children with **Diego Ruiz Lozano**, usually identified as Sor Juana’s half-siblings. Documentary scholarship reports baptismal records for two half-brothers.

Sor Juana never married and had no documented children.

### Social and ethnic background

She was a Spanish-descended criolla born in New Spain. Claims that she was Indigenous or mestiza are not supported by currently known genealogical documentation, although she grew up in a multilingual rural environment and incorporated Nahuatl words, Indigenous performance conventions, and Afro-New Spanish speech into some dramatic and liturgical works. Modern symbolic identification of her with Mexican hybridity should be distinguished from genealogy.

## Childhood and Early Life, c. 1648/1651–1664

Sor Juana’s principal first-person account of childhood appears in the *Respuesta a Sor Filotea* (1691). It is invaluable but also rhetorical: she was defending her intellectual life to a bishop and fashioned childhood memories into evidence of an involuntary, divinely permitted hunger for knowledge.

### Nepantla and Panoayan

She was born at Nepantla and raised substantially at the nearby hacienda of Panoayan. The region lay southeast of Mexico City, between the capital and the Popocatépetl–Iztaccíhuatl landscape.

According to the *Respuesta*:

- At about three, she secretly followed an older sister to a girls’ school and persuaded the teacher to instruct her.
- She learned to read at a very young age.
- At six or seven she heard that Mexico City possessed a university and asked to be dressed as a boy so she could attend.
- She read freely in her grandfather’s library.
- She imposed punishments on herself while studying, cutting several finger-widths of hair when she failed to learn fast enough.
- She avoided cheese because she believed it impaired intelligence.
- She learned Latin grammar in approximately twenty lessons, though “lessons” may not mean twenty days.

These are **reported autobiographical facts**, not independently verified episodes. Their precision and memorable symbolism helped generate the later child-prodigy legend.

### Early writing

Calleja reported that she composed a eucharistic *loa* at approximately eight, connected with a contest at Amecameca. No securely authenticated manuscript of this childhood piece survives. It is therefore a **near-contemporary report**, sometimes embellished into the claim that she “won her first literary prize at eight.”

### Early intellectual environment

Her education was informal and self-directed rather than university-based. She later described learning through books, observation, music, cooking, geometry, and ordinary physical phenomena. She treated the entire material world as an object of inquiry: the behavior of eggs in fat and syrup, perspective, acoustics, machinery, medicine, astronomy, and the proportions of music.

Her library access and later convent resources distinguish her experience from that of most colonial women. Nevertheless, exclusion from the university and the narrow alternatives available to an unmarried illegitimate woman shaped her decisions.

---

## Move to Mexico City and Education

At an uncertain date, probably in the late 1650s or early 1660s, Juana moved to Mexico City to live with maternal relatives, commonly identified as her aunt **María Ramírez** and the aunt’s husband **Juan de Mata**.

She received private instruction in Latin from **Martín de Olivas**, reportedly mastering enough grammar after about twenty lessons to work directly with learned texts. There is no evidence that she formally enrolled at the Royal and Pontifical University of Mexico.

Her formation included:

- Latin scholastic theology and patristics.
- Spanish and classical poetry.
- Logic, rhetoric, philosophy, and biblical exegesis.
- Arithmetic and geometry.
- Astronomy and natural philosophy.
- Medicine as then understood.
- Music theory and composition.
- Emblem books, mythology, optics, magnetism, and mechanical curiosities.
- Works associated with Athanasius Kircher.
- Spanish dramatic traditions, especially Lope de Vega and Calderón.
- The elaborate poetic language of Luis de Góngora.

She was an autodidact with access to teachers, confessors, court scholars, books, and manuscript circulation—not an intellectually isolated prodigy.

---

## Viceregal Court, c. 1664–1667

Juana entered the court of Viceroy **Antonio Sebastián de Toledo Molina y Salazar, Marquis of Mancera**, and his wife **Leonor Carreto**, Marchioness of Mancera. The exact year is often given as 1664.

Leonor became her patron and protector. Sor Juana later celebrated her under poetic names including **Laura**. Juana served as a lady-in-waiting or favored court companion, though the precise household office is unclear.

### The examination by learned men

Calleja relates that the viceroy assembled approximately forty theologians, philosophers, mathematicians, historians, and poets to question the young woman. She supposedly answered them with such facility that the viceroy compared the spectacle to a royal galleon defeating small boats.

This is a **near-contemporary reported event**. No examination transcript or administrative record has been found. Later retellings turned it into a formal university-style oral examination; the evidence supports, at most, an elite courtly demonstration of learning.

### Court production and networks

At court she learned how literary patronage operated. Poetry was not merely private expression: it commemorated births, deaths, birthdays, religious feasts, dedications, political arrivals, and royal events. Her later convent career remained connected to this viceregal economy of commissions and favors.

## Religious Life

### Discalced Carmelites, 1667

On 14 August 1667, according to the established chronology, Juana entered the convent of **San José of the Discalced Carmelites** in Mexico City.

She left after approximately three months, in November 1667. The immediate reason was illness or inability to endure the exceptionally austere Carmelite rule. This departure was not a dismissal for misconduct.

### Why she became a nun

In the *Respuesta*, Sor Juana says she felt little inclination toward marriage and chose religious life because it appeared the least incompatible with her wish to study. This should not be reduced to the claim that she “pretended to be religious” merely to obtain a library. Her writings demonstrate sustained Catholic belief and theological engagement. At the same time, she explicitly identified intellectual freedom as a major reason for choosing the convent.

Possible motives operated together:

- Religious vocation.
- Refusal or disinclination to marry.
- Protection from the vulnerabilities of an unmarried woman.
- An institutional room, income, and community.
- Access to books and learned correspondence.
- A socially accepted identity permitting intellectual work.

### Hieronymite convent, 1668–1669

Juana entered the **Convent of Santa Paula of the Order of Saint Jerome**, conventionally called San Jerónimo, in 1668. On **24 February 1669** she professed as **Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz**.

A notarial renunciation/testament dated **23 February 1669** survives in the documentary record. Her convent profession is also preserved through the community’s *Libro de profesiones*.

Her dowry is usually reported as **3,000 pesos**, with assistance attributed to the wealthy patron **Pedro Velázquez de la Cadena**. Details of who supplied or guaranteed every component remain imperfectly reconstructed.

San Jerónimo was less austere than the Carmelite institution. Its nuns could maintain relatively spacious cells, own personal goods, receive visitors through grilles, employ servants, and participate in internal financial arrangements.

## Life in San Jerónimo, 1669–1695

Sor Juana remained at San Jerónimo for the rest of her life.

### Offices

She served the community as:

- Accountant or bookkeeper.
- Archivist.
- At least intermittently responsible for practical administrative work.
- Literary producer for convent, cathedral, court, and private patrons.

She was nominated more than once for prioress but reportedly declined. Some accounts say she was elected and refused; without the relevant election record, “nominated or chosen but unwilling to serve” is the safer formulation.

### Cell, library, instruments, and salon

Her quarters became famous for containing:

- A substantial personal library.
- Scientific and mathematical instruments.
- Musical instruments.
- Images, maps, curiosities, and writing materials.

The traditional number is approximately **4,000 books**. It comes through early biographical tradition and is plausible as an order of magnitude but not confirmed by a surviving complete inventory. The cell functioned as a semi-public intellectual space where authorized visitors could converse with her through convent arrangements.

The modern phrase “salon” captures the social role but can mislead if it implies an unrestricted secular gathering. Access remained conditioned by enclosure, rank, and convent regulation.

### Music and lost theory

Sor Juana composed music and wrote about musical proportion. She referred to a theoretical work called **El caracol** (“The Snail”), apparently proposing a new or spiral method of musical understanding. The treatise is lost. It is improper to describe it as a surviving invention or patent; no patent system or authenticated device is involved.

## Literary Career

### Early printed work

Before her collected volumes, Sor Juana’s poems circulated in manuscripts and occasional pamphlets. Her cathedral villancicos began appearing in print by the 1670s. A series for the Feast of the Assumption at Mexico City Cathedral was printed in **1676**.

Villancicos were commissioned, performative works divided into sections and sung in cathedrals. Those published under her name include pieces for Mexico City, Puebla, and Oaxaca. They use learned Latin, popular Spanish, Nahuatl-inflected diction, Portuguese, and stylized Black speech. Authorship of individual pieces can be complicated by collaborative performance and anonymous printing practices.

### Leonor Carreto’s death

The Marchioness of Mancera died in 1674 during the family’s return journey toward Spain. Sor Juana wrote memorial poetry for her former patron. Leonor’s death did not end Sor Juana’s ties to viceregal circles.

### Tomás de la Cerda and María Luisa Manrique de Lara

In 1680, **Tomás Antonio de la Cerda, Marquis de la Laguna**, arrived as viceroy with his wife **María Luisa Manrique de Lara y Gonzaga**, Countess of Paredes.

For the ceremonial entry Sor Juana designed the allegorical program **Neptuno alegórico**, interpreting the new viceroy through Neptune and an extensive learned iconography. The commission placed a cloistered woman at the center of an important civic-political spectacle.

María Luisa became Sor Juana’s strongest literary patron. Sor Juana addressed her under poetic names including **Lysi/Lisi** and wrote intense encomiastic and affectionate poems about her beauty, presence, absence, and portrait. The countess carried or arranged manuscripts in Spain and was instrumental in securing European publication.

### Drama

Sor Juana wrote courtly and religious drama:

- **Los empeños de una casa**, performed in 1683 as part of a larger festive theatrical sequence. It is a comedy of intrigue with unusually capable female characters and a celebrated male cross-dressing episode involving the servant Castaño.
- **Amor es más laberinto**, performed in 1689 for the birthday of Viceroy Gaspar de la Cerda. Sor Juana wrote the first and third acts; **Juan de Guevara** wrote the second.
- **El divino Narciso**, an auto sacramental using the Narcissus myth to dramatize Christ’s relation to Human Nature.
- **El mártir del Sacramento, san Hermenegildo**.
- **El cetro de José**.
- Numerous *loas*, *sainetes*, and short theatrical pieces.

### First collected volume, 1689

**Inundación castálida de la única poetisa, Musa Décima, sor Juana Inés de la Cruz…** appeared in Madrid in 1689, printed by Juan García Infanzón. It contained lyric poems, villancicos, and the *Neptuno alegórico* materials.

The collection was assembled and published through Spanish patronage, especially María Luisa’s network. Its success generated revised editions, often titled simply *Poemas*, in Madrid, Barcelona, Zaragoza, and Valencia.

### Segundo volumen, 1692

**Segundo volumen de las obras de Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz** was printed at Seville in 1692 by Tomás López de Haro and dedicated to Juan de Orúe y Orbieto. It brought together drama, religious pieces, prose, and major poetry, including *Primero sueño*.

### Publishing success

The three early collected volumes and their reprints produced approximately nineteen Iberian editions between 1689 and 1725, depending on bibliographic classification. This made Sor Juana an unusually successful transatlantic author. The precise sums she personally received from printing are unknown.

## Intellectual, Religious, and Artistic Development

### Catholic theology and learned inquiry

Sor Juana’s work remained within Catholic forms, but she insisted that theology required knowledge of logic, languages, history, music, mathematics, and natural philosophy. She argued that women’s education could protect women from ignorant teachers and permit older women to instruct younger ones.

Her defense was not a modern program of universal secular equality in present-day terms. It drew on scripture, patristic authorities, learned women of antiquity, female saints, and exceptional-vocation arguments. Later feminism expanded its implications.

### Baroque poetics

Her poetry employs:

- Paradox and antithesis.
- Góngora-style syntax and Latinate vocabulary.
- Neoplatonic love language.
- Scholastic distinction.
- Mythological allegory.
- Emblematic imagery.
- Scientific metaphor.
- Dramatic role-playing and unstable gendered voices.

### *Primero sueño*

*Primero sueño*, probably composed during the 1680s and published in 1692, is a philosophical poem of 975 lines. A sleeping body releases an intellect that attempts to comprehend the cosmos, first in a single intuition and then methodically, but fails; dawn returns the soul to the body.

It is sometimes described as the one poem Sor Juana wrote entirely for herself, based on her remark that it was the only work composed “por mi gusto.” Its sources and analogues include Góngora, classical dream journeys, scholastic psychology, Neoplatonism, hermetic imagery, optics, astronomy, and Athanasius Kircher. Scholarship identifies specific convergences with Kircher’s *Iter extaticum*, *Ars magna lucis et umbrae*, and musical works, while also emphasizing Sor Juana’s transformation and criticism of those models.

### Poetry about women and sexual double standards

The redondilla beginning **“Hombres necios que acusáis / a la mujer sin razón”** analyzes the contradictory sexual demands men impose on women: men solicit conduct they subsequently condemn. The poem circulated without the modern title often assigned to it.

Her comedies and poetry repeatedly represent intelligent women negotiating marriage markets, reputation, desire, and male hypocrisy.

### Race, empire, and colonial representation

Her work participates in the court culture of Spanish monarchy and Catholic evangelization. The *Neptuno alegórico* celebrates viceregal authority. *El divino Narciso* incorporates an allegorical Indigenous ritual and conversion narrative, granting Indigenous figures intellectual dignity while ultimately subordinating their religion to Christianity.

The villancicos’ stylized Black and Indigenous voices can be read both as incorporating marginalized speech into prestigious performance and as reproducing colonial theatrical stereotypes. These are not mutually exclusive findings.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Leonor Carreto, Marchioness of Mancera

**Documented/reported relationship:** Court patron and protector, approximately 1664–1674. Sor Juana memorialized her as “Laura.” Claims that Leonor personally arranged the famous examination are based primarily on Calleja’s account.

### Antonio Sebastián de Toledo, Marquis of Mancera

**Reported fact:** Viceregal patron. Calleja attributes to him the examination by learned men and an admiring comparison of Juana’s intellectual performance to naval combat.

### María Luisa Manrique de Lara y Gonzaga, Countess of Paredes

**Documented fact:** Patron, recipient of poems, owner of a portrait or images associated with Sor Juana, and an important facilitator of Spanish publication. Their relationship continued after María Luisa returned to Spain in 1688.

**Unresolved question:** The poems express intense love and bodily admiration. They demonstrate literary and emotional intimacy but do not establish a sexual relationship.

### Tomás Antonio de la Cerda, Marquis de la Laguna

**Documented fact:** Viceroy honored in *Neptuno alegórico*; part of the patronage system that protected Sor Juana.

### Antonio Núñez de Miranda, S.J.

Jesuit theologian, preacher, confessor to elite women, and Sor Juana’s spiritual director. Their relationship became conflictual.

The rediscovered **Carta al padre Núñez**, generally dated around 1681–1682, rebukes a confessor for criticizing her public literary reputation and interfering with her intellectual activity. Sor Juana declared that if he did not wish to continue directing her, he could cease doing so. The identification of Núñez as recipient is broadly accepted.

Núñez later resumed some spiritual relationship with her near the end of her life. He died in February 1695.

### Manuel Fernández de Santa Cruz

Bishop of Puebla and the person behind the female pseudonym **Sor Filotea de la Cruz**. He requested or obtained Sor Juana’s critique of António Vieira, printed it in 1690 as the *Carta Atenagórica*, and accompanied it with a letter praising her ability but admonishing her to give priority to sacred study.

Whether his intervention was protective, manipulative, disciplinary, or some combination remains disputed.

### Francisco de Aguiar y Seijas

Archbishop of Mexico, noted for personal austerity, almsgiving, opposition to theater, and severe views concerning women and worldly culture. He has frequently been cast as Sor Juana’s persecutor. No surviving decree records that he tried or formally censured her. His institutional authority and ideology made him a powerful factor in her final crisis, but a coordinated persecution remains a historical interpretation.

### Carlos de Sigüenza y Góngora

New Spanish polymath, mathematician, historian, and former Jesuit. He and Sor Juana inhabited the same learned courtly world and contributed rival but mutually respectful triumphal-arch programs in 1680. Later tradition frequently calls them friends. Direct correspondence has not survived, so the closeness of the friendship cannot be precisely measured.

### Juan de Guevara

**Documented collaborator:** Wrote the second act of *Amor es más laberinto*.

### Juan Ignacio de Castorena y Ursúa

Cleric and later bishop who edited **Fama y obras póstumas** in 1700, preserving the *Respuesta* and early testimonies.

### Diego Calleja, S.J.

Spanish Jesuit correspondent and first major biographer. His *Aprobación* and biographical narrative in the 1700 volume shaped almost every later life of Sor Juana. His proximity makes him indispensable, but his devotional and literary purpose requires caution.

## The Vieira Controversy and *Respuesta a Sor Filotea*

### Background

The Portuguese Jesuit **António Vieira** had preached a *Sermão do Mandato* about Christ’s “finezas,” or supreme demonstrations of love. At someone’s request, Sor Juana produced a learned critique of his reasoning.

### *Carta Atenagórica*, 1690

Fernández de Santa Cruz printed the critique at Puebla in late 1690 as **Carta Atenagórica de la madre Juana Inés de la Cruz**. “Atenagórica” suggested wisdom worthy of Athena.

**Documented fact:** The bishop published it with his own prefatory admonition under the pseudonym Sor Filotea.

**Disputed point:** Some scholarship says publication occurred without Sor Juana’s consent; other textual historians caution that manuscript transmission, authorial participation, and publicity cannot be reconstructed so simply. Sor Juana wrote that seeing it printed surprised and embarrassed her, but conventional modesty formulas and strategic self-presentation complicate literal interpretation.

### *Carta de Sor Filotea*

Fernández de Santa Cruz praised Sor Juana’s intelligence while urging her to devote it to scripture and salvation rather than secular letters. The use of a female religious persona allowed him to frame hierarchical correction as sisterly advice.

### *Respuesta*, 1 March 1691

Sor Juana’s response combines obedience, autobiography, scriptural argument, learned catalogues, and institutional criticism. She recounts her lifelong compulsion to learn, the obstacles imposed on her, and the hostility generated by a woman’s intellectual achievement.

She distinguishes teaching publicly in church—which Pauline texts restricted—from private study and writing. She cites biblical, classical, and Christian learned women, and argues for educated older women as teachers of girls.

The letter was not an open declaration against Catholic doctrine. It was a defense conducted inside Catholic rhetoric and hierarchy, but its documentary account of gendered restriction has made it foundational to histories of women’s intellectual rights.

## Property, Finances, and Material Interests

Sor Juana was not a commercial entrepreneur, officeholder, or patent holder. Her relevant economic activity took place within convent property structures and literary patronage.

### Convent assets

Nuns in San Jerónimo could hold and manage certain personal assets within corporate religious structures. Sor Juana:

- Entered with a substantial dowry.
- Held rights in her cell or rooms.
- Received gifts and commissions.
- Owned books, instruments, devotional objects, and other movable property.
- Worked as convent accountant.
- Participated in loans or investments recorded in notarial documentation.

Research cited in studies of her final years notes that she purchased or expanded her cell and made investments in **1691**, after the *Respuesta*. These transactions complicate a simple chronology in which the Church immediately stripped her of all intellectual and material agency following the Vieira dispute.

### Library disposal

Near the end of her life her books and instruments passed from her possession and their value was directed to charity under ecclesiastical administration. The exact mechanisms, timing, inventory, recipients, and degree of coercion remain disputed.

There is no surviving complete catalogue of her alleged four-thousand-volume library. Stories that every book was summarily confiscated by the Inquisition are unsupported.

### Literary income

Her works were financed and transmitted through patrons, cathedral commissions, and Iberian publishers. No complete royalty accounts survive. Publication brought prestige and possibly gifts, but modern royalty assumptions should not be imposed on seventeenth-century patronage.

## Final Crisis, Renunciation, and Silence, 1691–1695

This is the most contested part of Sor Juana’s biography.

### Documentary sequence

- The *Carta Atenagórica* appeared in 1690.
- Sor Juana completed the *Respuesta* on 1 March 1691.
- Her *Segundo volumen* appeared in Spain in 1692.
- She renewed or intensified penitential commitments in 1693–1694.
- On 5 March 1694 she signed a profession or protestation in blood.
- Her books and instruments were dispersed or sold, reportedly for alms.
- She died during an epidemic on 17 April 1695.

### Persecution thesis

Octavio Paz and many later writers interpret the sequence as the result of ecclesiastical pressure exerted by Aguiar y Seijas, Núñez de Miranda, Fernández de Santa Cruz, or a broader clerical establishment threatened by a woman entering theology.

Supporting considerations include:

- Núñez’s documented earlier opposition to her literary celebrity.
- The bishop’s public admonition.
- Aguiar’s hostility to theater and restrictive attitudes toward women.
- The abrupt penitential documents and loss of her library.
- The gendered hostility Sor Juana herself describes.
- The absence of known major secular writing after 1693.

### Voluntary-conversion thesis

Other scholars, particularly more confessional or revisionist biographers, argue that Sor Juana underwent a sincere spiritual conversion rather than being silenced.

Supporting considerations include:

- Her lifelong Catholic commitments.
- Conventional cycles of monastic renewal.
- Epidemics, famine, social crisis, and personal deaths in the early 1690s.
- Her reconciliation with Núñez.
- Language in her signed penitential documents.
- Lack of a surviving formal censorship order, trial, or Inquisition sentence.

### Mixed-pressure interpretation

A third interpretation sees neither free choice nor a documented institutional conspiracy as sufficient alone. Sor Juana could have acted sincerely within a coercive environment: religious conviction, communal crisis, aging, clerical pressure, material vulnerability, and strategic submission could coexist.

### What is not documented

There is no surviving evidence that Sor Juana was:

- Arrested.
- Imprisoned outside ordinary convent enclosure.
- Formally indicted or tried by the Inquisition.
- Convicted of heresy.
- Excommunicated.
- Subjected to a surviving written order explicitly prohibiting all writing.
- Physically forced to sign the blood declaration.

Claims of an “Inquisition conviction” should therefore be labeled false or unsupported. Claims of pressure and censorship remain historically plausible interpretations, not judicial findings.

## Illness and Death

A severe epidemic struck Mexico City’s convents in 1695. The precise pathogen is unknown. Later accounts often call it plague, typhus, or typhoid fever, but retrospective diagnosis is uncertain.

Sor Juana cared for sick sisters in San Jerónimo and contracted the disease. She died on **Sunday, 17 April 1695**, reportedly around four in the morning.

Calleja and convent tradition portray her death as charitable self-sacrifice. The basic circumstances—epidemic, nursing, infection, death—are strongly supported by near-contemporary testimony; devotional elaboration may have heightened the hagiographic pattern.

She was buried in the convent church.

### Alleged remains

Excavations at the former convent in the twentieth century uncovered burials, and bones were publicly associated with Sor Juana. The identification has never been conclusively demonstrated through secure archaeological provenance or genetic comparison. Descriptions of remains displayed at the Universidad del Claustro de Sor Juana properly say “attributed to” or “considered by some experts to be” hers.

## Controversies, Accusations, and Contested Questions

### 1. Was she born in 1648 or 1651?

**Status:** Unresolved documentary question. The 1648 baptismal entry is strong circumstantial evidence; 1651 rests on Calleja’s near-contemporary testimony.

### 2. Did she falsify or conceal her age?

**Status:** Speculation. No evidence establishes deliberate falsification. Confusion may derive from defective memory, biographical convention, or mistaken identification of the baptismal entry.

### 3. Was she coerced into silence?

**Status:** Disputed historical interpretation. Material and ideological pressure is well supported; a formal order or coordinated conspiracy is not documented.

### 4. Was she prosecuted by the Inquisition?

**Status:** Unsupported claim. Her writings operated in a censored religious society and the *Atenagórica* controversy carried risk, but no trial, charge, or conviction of Sor Juana has been identified.

### 5. Did Fernández de Santa Cruz betray her?

**Status:** Disputed. He exposed her critique to print and publicly admonished her, but he may also have attempted to protect her by directing criticism into an apparently private and moderate form. His motives cannot be conclusively recovered.

### 6. Did she have a sexual relationship with María Luisa?

**Status:** Unverified and probably unresolvable. Erotic or passionately affectionate poems are documented; physical sexual relations are not. Courtly poetic convention neither proves nor disproves personal desire.

### 7. Did she reject religion?

**Status:** Unsupported. Her criticism targeted restrictions on intellectual work and women’s learning. Her surviving corpus remains deeply Catholic, even where doctrinal rhetoric also served self-defense.

### 8. Was she simply a proto-modern scientist?

**Status:** Anachronistic if stated without qualification. She practiced natural inquiry, mathematics, astronomy, music theory, and experiment within seventeenth-century scholastic, Neoplatonic, hermetic, and Catholic frameworks.

### 9. Racialized villancicos

**Documented issue:** Some pieces imitate Black speech and assign comic or stereotyped roles; others give African and Indigenous voices unusual liturgical prominence. Current criticism debates whether they challenge hierarchy, reproduce it, or perform both functions.

### 10. Authorship of *La segunda Celestina*

A theatrical work left unfinished by **Agustín de Salazar y Torres** was said in the 1700 *Fama* to have been completed by Sor Juana. Octavio Paz and Guillermo Schmidhuber identified a discovered continuation as her work.

**Status:** Disputed attribution. The early testimony supports her completion of some Salazar work, but whether the extant text is precisely Sor Juana’s continuation remains debated.

### 11. Did she paint the famous portrait?

The image conventionally called a self-portrait survives through later copies and adaptations, notably Miguel Cabrera’s 1750 portrait. A Library of Congress record labels one image “Self portrait of Sor Juana Ines de la Cruz,” but cataloguing language does not establish physical authorship by Sor Juana.

**Status:** Traditional attribution, not securely proven.

### 12. Recent adversarial search, 2024–2026

Searches combining her name with scandal, fraud, crime, lawsuit, accusations, conspiracy, and criticism produced no credible new evidence of criminal or financial wrongdoing by the historical Sor Juana. Recent disputes concern representation: sexuality, church coercion, feminist appropriation, racialized language, theatrical adaptation, and the authenticity of attributed remains or works.

No recent archival finding located in this search establishes fraud, criminal activity, corruption, intelligence connections, or a hidden prosecution.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

### Child genius who taught herself everything

**Story:** She read at three, wrote prize-winning verse at eight, mastered Latin in twenty lessons, and defeated forty male scholars while still a teenager.

**Origins:** Sor Juana’s *Respuesta* supplies the reading, self-discipline, and Latin stories; Calleja supplies the examination narrative.

**Evidence:** Strong for extraordinary early learning; weak for literalized details such as a formal forty-professor examination.

**Reputation effect:** Established her as a miraculous exception and “Tenth Muse,” sometimes obscuring family resources and patronage networks.

### Disguise as a boy

**Story:** Sor Juana attended the university dressed as a man.

**Evidence:** She wrote that as a child she asked her mother to dress her as a boy so she might attend. She did not claim that the plan was carried out.

**Status:** Legend created by converting an unrealized childhood request into an event.

### The convent as a simple escape from marriage

**Story:** She became a nun only to avoid a husband and read books.

**Evidence:** She explicitly valued freedom from marriage and study, but also produced decades of serious religious writing and observance.

**Status:** Reduction of multiple documented motives to one.

### Secret lesbian romance with the vicereine

**Story:** Sor Juana and María Luisa were lovers.

**Circulation:** Modern biographies, feminist and queer criticism, novels, theater, María Luisa Bemberg’s film, and the television series *Juana Inés*.

**Evidence for:** Intense poems addressed to Lysi, admiration of the vicereine’s body and portrait, personal patronage, gift exchange, and continued manuscript relationship.

**Evidence against certainty:** No explicit confession, surviving private love correspondence, eyewitness testimony, or record of sexual contact. Erotic encomium and female friendship had conventional literary forms.

**Effect:** Made Sor Juana a major queer cultural symbol while producing counterclaims that modern identity categories are being retroactively imposed.

### A jealous archbishop destroyed her library overnight

**Story:** Aguiar y Seijas personally ordered the seizure of all her books and silenced her.

**Evidence:** He exercised authority, opposed worldly literature, and benefited from the charitable disposal. Her library did leave her possession. No surviving seizure order proves the dramatic version.

**Status:** Historically grounded persecution narrative with legendary compression.

### Voluntary saintly renunciation

**Story:** Sor Juana freely recognized the vanity of secular learning, gave everything to the poor, signed in blood, and died nursing her sisters.

**Origins:** Penitential documents, Calleja, Castorena’s 1700 editorial framing, and clerical memorial tradition.

**Evidence:** The signed declaration, disposal of goods, nursing, and death are real. Complete freedom from institutional pressure cannot be demonstrated.

**Effect:** Converted the troublesome intellectual into an obedient Catholic deathbed model.

### “Yo, la peor de todas” as autobiography

The phrase was a conventional monastic humility formula written in a specific penitential context. Later repetition—especially Bemberg’s film title—turned it into an emblem of institutional humiliation and Sor Juana’s entire life. Both uses exceed what the phrase alone proves.

### Four thousand books

Early tradition gives her library approximately four thousand volumes. No inventory survives.

**Status:** Plausible reported estimate, not an audited count.

### Correspondence with Isaac Newton

Occasional popular claims say Sor Juana corresponded with Newton. No letter, contemporary report, or archival trace supports this. Similar interests in optics and natural philosophy do not establish contact.

**Status:** Modern internet myth.

## Works by Sor Juana

Because many individual poems and occasional pieces circulated separately, the following is a structured bibliography rather than a claim that every leaf can be dated exactly.

### Authorial collections published during her lifetime

1. **Inundación castálida de la única poetisa, Musa Décima, sor Juana Inés de la Cruz…** Madrid: Juan García Infanzón, 1689.  
   First major collected volume; assembled through patronage in Spain.

2. **Poemas de la única poetisa americana, Musa Décima, Soror Juana Inés de la Cruz.** Madrid, 1690, and subsequent editions.  
   Retitled and revised successor to *Inundación castálida*.

3. **Segundo volumen de las obras de Soror Juana Inés de la Cruz, monja profesa en el monasterio del Señor San Jerónimo de la ciudad de México.** Seville: Tomás López de Haro, 1692.  
   Published during her lifetime.

### Posthumous early collection

4. **Fama y obras póstumas del Fénix de México, Décima Musa, poetisa americana, Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz.** Madrid: Manuel Ruiz de Murga, 1700; edited by Juan Ignacio de Castorena y Ursúa.  
   Posthumous compilation containing tributes, Calleja’s biography, and important prose. Editorial framing is not Sor Juana’s own.

### Major poetry

- **Primero sueño** or **El sueño**, composed probably in the 1680s; first printed in the 1692 *Segundo volumen*.
- **“Hombres necios que acusáis”**, undated redondillas; published in the first collected volume.
- Philosophical sonnets, including poems on appearance, portraiture, knowledge, mutability, and death.
- Love poetry addressed to poetic personae such as Lisi/Lysi, Laura, Fabio, Silvio, and others.
- Burlesque, satirical, encomiastic, funeral, and occasional poems.
- The portrait poem beginning **“Este que ves, engaño colorido.”**
- The epistemological sonnet beginning **“Detente, sombra de mi bien esquivo.”**
- The rational-choice sonnet beginning **“Al que ingrato me deja, busco amante.”**
- The celebrated philosophical lines beginning **“Finjamos que soy feliz.”**

### Theater

- **Los empeños de una casa**, performed 1683; genuine authorship.
- **Amor es más laberinto**, performed 1689; acts I and III by Sor Juana, act II by Juan de Guevara.
- **El divino Narciso**, written before its publication in 1690/1692; genuine authorship.
- **El cetro de José**, published 1692.
- **El mártir del Sacramento, san Hermenegildo**, published 1692.
- Numerous *loas*, including pieces for royal and viceregal celebrations.
- *Sainetes* and interludes associated with the larger theatrical festivities.
- **La segunda Celestina** continuation: attributed to Sor Juana but disputed in its extant form.

### Prose and theological writing

- **Neptuno alegórico, océano de colores, simulacro político**, Mexico, 1680; explanation of the triumphal arch for the Marquis de la Laguna.
- **Explicación del arco**, associated with *Neptuno alegórico*.
- **Carta al padre Antonio Núñez**, c. 1681–1682; private self-defense discovered in the twentieth century and commonly called the *Carta de Monterrey* or *Autodefensa espiritual*.
- **Carta Atenagórica**, Puebla, 1690; critique of António Vieira’s sermon. Sor Juana appears to have called the underlying work a *Crisis* or response; its printed title was editorial.
- **Respuesta de la poetisa a la muy ilustre Sor Filotea de la Cruz**, dated 1 March 1691; first printed posthumously in 1700.
- **Ejercicios devotos para los nueve días antes del de la Purísima Encarnación del Hijo de Dios Jesucristo**, devotional prose; publication and textual history are complex.
- **Ofrecimientos para el Santo Rosario de quince misterios**, attribution generally accepted within the devotional corpus, though editions reflect posthumous transmission.
- **Protesta de la fe** and profession-renewal documents, 1694.
- The blood-signed penitential declaration of 5 March 1694.

### Villancicos and sacred lyrics

Printed groups include villancicos for:

- The Assumption, Mexico City Cathedral, 1676 and later cycles.
- Saint Peter.
- Saint Peter Nolasco.
- Saint Catherine of Alexandria.
- The Nativity.
- The Immaculate Conception.
- Cathedral celebrations in Mexico City, Puebla, and Oaxaca.

Attribution of individual anonymous or collaboratively produced cathedral texts requires case-by-case bibliographical analysis.

### Lost, fragmentary, or reported works

- **El caracol**, music-theory treatise; lost.
- Childhood eucharistic *loa*; reported, not securely identified.
- Additional letters to patrons, relatives, confessors, and European correspondents; largely lost.
- Musical compositions: texts and references survive, but secure musical scores attributable to her are scarce or absent.
- A completion of a work by Agustín de Salazar y Torres; early attestation exists, surviving identification disputed.
- Library notes, scientific observations, and routine convent records may have been lost or remain unidentified.

### Standard modern edition

**Obras completas de Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz**, four volumes, edited principally by Alfonso Méndez Plancarte, with volume IV completed by Alberto G. Salceda, Fondo de Cultura Económica, 1951–1957. Antonio Alatorre later revised the personal lyric volume. Despite subsequent discoveries and corrections, this remains the standard comprehensive scholarly framework.

### Hostile or reductive interpretive traditions

- Clerical hagiography converted her late renunciation into proof that secular learning had been an error.
- Psychoanalytic studies diagnosed her from poetry and incomplete biography, sometimes treating intellectual ambition or attachment to women as pathology.
- Nationalist treatments transformed her into an uncomplicated precursor of the Mexican nation.
- Anti-clerical versions sometimes present a documented Inquisition prosecution that did not occur.
- Confessional revisionism sometimes treats the lack of a written censorship order as proof that no coercive pressure existed.

Each tradition preserves evidence selectively and should be separated from the underlying documents.

## Films, Television, Performance, and Archival Material

### Film and television

- **Yo, la peor de todas** (*I, the Worst of All*), 1990. Directed and co-written by María Luisa Bemberg; starring Assumpta Serna. Based substantially on Octavio Paz’s interpretation. It dramatizes ecclesiastical repression and attraction between Sor Juana and María Luisa. It is historical fiction, not a documentary.
- **Juana Inés**, 2016. Mexican television miniseries produced for Canal Once. It reconstructs Sor Juana’s life and explicitly dramatizes sexuality and political conflict. Some scholars and reviewers objected to its sexualization or speculative compression.
- Numerous Mexican educational documentaries and television biographies, including anniversary programming produced by cultural institutions and universities.
- Stage adaptations of **Los empeños de una casa**, **El divino Narciso**, and *Primero sueño* continue internationally.
- Contemporary dramatic and operatic works frequently merge Sor Juana’s voice with the “Yo, la peor” renunciation narrative.

### Visual archives

- The *Libro de profesiones* of San Jerónimo preserves the institutional record of her profession.
- Early editions of 1689, 1692, and 1700 survive in major rare-book collections and modern facsimiles.
- The Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes hosts texts, catalogues, studies, and digitized editions.
- UNAM repositories hold documentary studies and digital materials.
- The former convent is now the **Universidad del Claustro de Sor Juana**.
- Miguel Cabrera’s 1750 portrait became the canonical visual image: Sor Juana seated in her library, holding a book and wearing a medallion. It postdates her death by fifty-five years.
- A purported self-portrait tradition exists, but direct authorship is uncertain.
- The house and landscape at Nepantla have become memorial sites.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Eighteenth century

The early collected volumes continued to be reprinted through 1725. Castorena’s *Fama* created the canonical composite image: miraculous genius, American glory, learned nun, obedient penitent, and charitable victim of epidemic disease.

Later eighteenth-century literary taste became less receptive to high Baroque style, and her readership contracted.

### Nineteenth century

Mexican national literary histories recovered her as a colonial precursor. Her Catholic and Spanish imperial contexts were variously celebrated, minimized, or converted into national symbolism.

### Twentieth-century recovery

Philologists reconstructed the corpus. Dorothy Schons reframed Sor Juana as a woman confronting institutional barriers. Méndez Plancarte established the modern collected edition. Octavio Paz connected biography, court politics, sexuality, Mexican history, and ecclesiastical repression in an enormously influential synthesis.

Feminist criticism made the *Respuesta* a central document in women’s intellectual history. Queer criticism reexamined the Lysi poems and gendered poetic voices. Historians of science emphasized her optics, astronomy, mathematics, and Kircherian sources.

### Public commemoration

Sor Juana has appeared on Mexican currency, including the former 1,000-peso note and later the 200-peso note. Her name is borne by:

- Nepantla de Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz.
- Universidad del Claustro de Sor Juana.
- Premio de Literatura Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz, awarded at the Guadalajara International Book Fair.
- Schools, libraries, theaters, streets, and cultural institutions.

### Modern interpretive identities

She has been claimed as:

- A foundational Mexican writer.
- A major writer of the Spanish Golden Age.
- A criolla intellectual.
- A Catholic theologian.
- A precursor of feminism.
- A queer or lesbian cultural ancestor.
- A scientist or philosopher excluded by disciplinary canons.
- A victim of ecclesiastical patriarchy.
- A model of religious conversion.

None of these labels exhausts the documentary person. Some are analytical categories developed centuries after her death.

## Atlas Connections

The roster was checked for documented meetings, correspondence, collaboration, patronage, shared institutions, or demonstrable intellectual influence. Most names have no supported direct connection. The following meet a defensible threshold.

### Athanasius Kircher

**Evidentiary label: Documented textual influence / scholarly demonstration.**

Sor Juana owned or had access to works by Kircher and referred to his combinatory art in her poetry. Scholarship has identified specific relationships between *Primero sueño* and Kircher’s *Iter extaticum coeleste* and *Ars magna lucis et umbrae*, and between her music theory and *Musurgia universalis*. They did not meet or correspond as far as surviving evidence shows; Kircher died in Rome in 1680.

### Miguel de Cervantes Saavedra

**Evidentiary label: Documented literary influence, not personal contact.**

Sor Juana worked within the Spanish literary culture shaped by Cervantes and used comic, metatheatrical, and prose traditions to which Cervantes was central. No personal connection was possible: Cervantes died decades before her birth. The connection is literary and contextual, not a documented explicit discipleship comparable to Kircher.

### Plato

**Evidentiary label: Documented intellectual tradition / indirect influence.**

Her love poetry and philosophical writing employ Neoplatonic concepts transmitted through Christian and Renaissance authors. There was obviously no personal connection. Direct dependence on a particular Platonic edition is less certain than participation in the broader Neoplatonic tradition.

### Aristotle

**Evidentiary label: Documented intellectual tradition.**

Her logic, psychology, natural philosophy, rhetoric, and scholastic theology were formed in an Aristotelian educational world. The *Respuesta* describes study across these disciplines. This is mediated intellectual influence, not contact.

### Aurelius Augustinus

**Evidentiary label: Documented theological influence.**

Augustinian theories of illumination, memory, will, and Christian learning belong to the theological materials present in Sor Juana’s corpus and its scholarly reconstruction.

### Thomas Aquinas

**Evidentiary label: Documented scholastic influence.**

Sor Juana’s theological reasoning and the Vieira controversy operate in a scholastic tradition deeply shaped by Aquinas. Her texts invoke Thomistic distinctions, though she was not a formal university Thomist.

### Catherine of Alexandria

**Evidentiary label: Documented devotional and symbolic connection.**

Sor Juana wrote villancicos honoring Saint Catherine of Alexandria, celebrated as a learned woman who defeated male philosophers. Catherine served as an authorized Christian precedent for female erudition. The saint is a late-antique legendary figure, so the underlying biography of Catherine is itself historically uncertain.

### El Inca Garcilaso de la Vega

**Evidentiary label: Shared transatlantic intellectual field; direct link not established.**

Both became canonical American-born writers within Spanish letters, but no source consulted here proves Sor Juana’s direct acknowledgment of or dependence on Garcilaso. Mere inclusion in later histories of colonial literature is not a life connection. He is therefore not counted as a confirmed crossing.

### René Descartes

**Evidentiary label: Scholarly theory, not established direct influence.**

Some historians have proposed Cartesian influence on Sor Juana and Carlos de Sigüenza y Góngora. Evidence for Sor Juana’s direct reading of Descartes remains less secure than evidence for Kircher. This is an interpretive possibility, not a confirmed connection.

### Giambattista Vico

**Evidentiary label: No connection.**

A modern study compares Sor Juana’s spiral musical model with Vico’s later *ricorso*, but Vico developed the relevant work after her death. Similarity is not influence.

### Other roster members

No supported meeting, correspondence, patronage, family relationship, collaboration, or explicit influence was found for the overwhelming majority of the roster. Shared Catholic, colonial, philosophical, artistic, or national contexts alone were not treated as connections.
