# Thathanka Iyotake: Research Dossier

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Labels

**Subject:** Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake, conventionally rendered in English as Sitting Bull.

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary records, surviving documents, photographs, contracts, government files, or multiple convergent sources.
- **Reported fact:** preserved in a named witness account, later interview, family oral history, or responsible secondary source, but not independently established.
- **Disputed claim:** supported by conflicting evidence or incompatible accounts.
- **Allegation:** an accusation made by a contemporary or later claimant without sufficient proof.
- **Rumor or myth:** folklore, publicity, symbolic narrative, or a story whose documentary foundation is weak or absent.

Dates before Sitting Bull entered sustained contact with record-keeping institutions are often approximate. English-language transcriptions vary substantially.

| Field | Information |

| Roles | Spiritual practitioner or holy man, warrior, war-party leader, political spokesman, camp leader, diplomat, artist, performer |

| Age at death | Approximately 53–59, depending on the birth chronology used |

| Claimed later burial | Mobridge, South Dakota, following a disputed 1953 exhumation |

“Chief” is an imperfect English shorthand. Lakota authority was distributed among civil leaders, war leaders, society members, spiritual figures, and influential household heads; Sitting Bull was not a monarch or permanent commander of every Lakota band.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and siblings

The commonly reconstructed family identifies:

- **Father:** Jumping Bull, also said to have borne the name Sitting Bull before transferring it to his son.
- **Mother:** Her-Holy-Door.
- **Sister:** Good Feather, reportedly about six years older.
- Other siblings and half-siblings appear inconsistently in genealogies. Four Horns is often described as an uncle and important senior relative.

The Smithsonian’s later repatriation inquiry reconstructed a line through Sitting Bull’s descendants using documentary genealogy. Ernie LaPointe’s family history supplies additional names and relationships, sometimes differing from earlier biographers.

### Wives and partners

Sources give different lists because of deaths, sequential marriages, variant translations, and uncertainty over whether every woman named was a wife in the same sense. Names associated with his household include:

- Light Hair or Pretty Door
- Snow-on-Her
- Red Woman or Scarlet Woman
- Seen-by-Her-Nation
- Four Robes, Four Blankets Woman, or similar variants

By the Little Bighorn period, Seen-by-Her-Nation and her sister Four Blankets Woman are commonly identified as members of his household.

No reliable evidence establishes a sexual relationship with the activist Catherine Weldon. Their documented relationship was political and personal friendship. A proposal of marriage has been reported by Weldon’s biographer, but the surviving record does not support the romance portrayed in *Woman Walks Ahead*.

### Children and adopted relatives

Children attributed to Sitting Bull include:

- Crow Foot, a son killed with him in 1890
- Many Horses, a daughter
- Standing Holy, later Mary Sitting Bull
- Runs-Away-From-Him, later William Sitting Bull
- Several children who died young, including twins in some accounts

He also incorporated relatives into his household through customary adoption. **One Bull**, his nephew, is frequently called an adopted son. **John Sitting Bull**, born Refuses-Them and deaf and mute from birth, became his stepson when Sitting Bull married Seen-by-Her-Nation.

Claims that Annie Oakley was formally adopted as his daughter are discussed below. At minimum, he bestowed or popularized her stage name “Little Sure Shot.”

### Modern descendants

In 2007 the Smithsonian returned a lock of hair and wool leggings to Ernie LaPointe and his sisters after a multiyear documentary-genealogical inquiry recognized them as Sitting Bull’s closest lineal descendants.

A 2021 *Science Advances* study compared DNA extracted from the returned hair with Ernie LaPointe’s DNA and found support for a great-grandparent/great-grandchild relationship. This strengthened an already documented genealogy; it did not establish tribal citizenship for unrelated claimants or prove every published Sitting Bull family tree.

Numerous other people have claimed descent. Such claims must be evaluated individually; shared surnames, family lore, or consumer ancestry estimates are not independently sufficient.

## Childhood and Early Life

### Birth and environment

Sitting Bull was born before systematic civil registration. The conventional Grand River birthplace comes from twentieth-century biography and reservation-era testimony. Family oral tradition places the birth farther west in Yellowstone country. Neither location can be established conclusively.

He grew up in the northern Plains buffalo economy. His education was experiential and communal: hunting, horsemanship, religious observance, kinship obligations, generosity, warfare, oral history, and diplomacy. He received no Euro-American school education and never became literate in English. Later he learned to make a recognizable written signature.

### Childhood name and first buffalo

**Reported family tradition:** The boy was called Húŋkešni, “Slow,” because he acted deliberately rather than impulsively. He supposedly killed his first buffalo at approximately ten.

### First coup and renaming

**Reported tradition:** At about fourteen, during a raid or fight against the Crow, he rode ahead, struck an enemy with a coup stick, and returned safely. His father then transferred the name Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake to him and took the name Jumping Bull.

This is central to most biographies but comes from retrospectively collected Lakota testimony rather than a contemporary document. Details—including his age, the opponent, and the exact ceremony—vary.

### Warrior and society formation

As a young man he joined warrior societies, accumulated honors, led war parties, and developed a reputation for composure. He was associated with the Strong Heart society and later with the Kit Fox society in biographical reconstructions. His leadership grew through demonstrated courage, generosity, religious authority, and the ability to attract followers rather than through hereditary sovereignty alone.

---

## Education and Formation

Sitting Bull’s principal teachers were relatives, senior warriors, spiritual practitioners, and the ceremonial institutions of Hunkpapa life. Named influences include his father Jumping Bull, his uncle Four Horns, and other senior leaders whose instruction is preserved mainly through oral history.

His formation combined:

- Buffalo hunting and ecological knowledge
- Horse culture and mobile camp logistics
- Counting coup and warfare against Crow, Assiniboine, and other rivals
- Vision quests and the Sun Dance
- Pipe ceremonialism
- Dream and vision interpretation
- Public generosity and redistributive leadership
- Council oratory and consensus politics

He was not simply a military leader. Contemporary and later Lakota accounts consistently emphasize his spiritual discipline, dreaming, sacrifice, and responsibility toward poor families.

## Chronological Life History

## c. 1831–1850: Youth and emergence

Sitting Bull grew up during expanding trade, disease, intertribal competition, and accelerating American movement into the Plains. After his first coup he acquired additional war honors. His pictographic autobiographies later represented episodes against Crow and Assiniboine opponents, horse captures, and rescues.

His first marriage is often placed around 1851, possibly to Light Hair or Pretty Door. Domestic chronology remains uncertain.

## 1850s: Warrior reputation and widening American contact

The Lakota expanded their presence westward while traffic, military posts, and settler pressure increased along overland routes. Sitting Bull participated in raids and warfare customary to the region. The surviving pictographic record depicts violence against enemy men and at least one capture of a woman. These works are evidence of the deeds he chose to commemorate, though captions were supplied or mediated by later interpreters.

He gradually became identified with resistance to permanent American roads, forts, and settlements in Lakota hunting territory.

## 1862–1865: Dakota War aftermath and Sully campaigns

After the 1862 U.S.–Dakota War in Minnesota, the United States launched punitive expeditions against multiple Dakota and Lakota communities, including groups not responsible for the Minnesota uprising.

### 1863 fighting

Sitting Bull is associated in later accounts with resistance during Brigadier General Alfred Sully’s campaigns. Exact participation in each engagement is difficult to establish.

### Battle of Killdeer Mountain, 28 July 1864

**Documented context; reported individual role:** Sully attacked a very large encampment in present-day North Dakota with approximately 2,200 soldiers and artillery. National Park Service material describes this as the first time warriors such as Sitting Bull faced concentrated cannon and rifle fire. The camp was driven off and property and food were destroyed.

The experience contributed to his increasingly uncompromising opposition to military occupation.

### 1864–1865

He may have participated in subsequent resistance in the Badlands and Powder River region. Claims that he personally directed all “Sioux” resistance during these years exaggerate the decentralized organization of the Lakota.

## 1866–1868: Forts, Red Cloud’s War, and the Fort Laramie Treaty

Sitting Bull and Hunkpapa fighters opposed Fort Buford and other American installations. He was aligned in purpose, though not always under a unified command, with leaders resisting the Bozeman Trail forts during Red Cloud’s War.

The 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie established the Great Sioux Reservation, recognized the Black Hills within it, and designated unceded hunting territory farther west. Sitting Bull did **not** sign. He rejected dependence on agencies and continued living and hunting outside them.

Descriptions of him as elected “supreme chief of the whole Sioux nation” around this period derive especially from Stanley Vestal’s 1932 biography and interviews conducted decades later. Most modern historians regard the title as an overstatement or a mistranslation of a more limited ceremonial and political elevation. His broad influence is documented; universal constitutional authority is not.

## 1869–1874: Non-treaty leadership

Sitting Bull’s following included Hunkpapa and, at different times, people from other Lakota divisions and allied Northern Cheyenne. He emerged as one of the leading spokesmen for communities refusing agency life.

His network included Gall, Four Horns, Crow King, Crazy Horse, Black Moon, Rain-in-the-Face, and many others. Alliances shifted; Lakota leaders did not form a permanent general staff.

### 1874 Black Hills expedition

Lieutenant Colonel George A. Custer’s expedition, launched from Fort Abraham Lincoln, confirmed gold in the Black Hills. Miners entered land protected by treaty. Federal efforts to purchase the Hills failed, and the government did not effectively remove trespassers.

Sitting Bull rejected sale. At councils, he defended communal land and an independent buffalo-hunting life.

## 1875–1876: Ultimatum and Great Sioux War

In late 1875 the United States ordered “non-treaty” Lakota and Northern Cheyenne to report to agencies by 31 January 1876. Winter distance, communications, weather, and the communities’ rejection of federal authority made compliance implausible. Those who remained outside were classified as hostile, enabling military operations.

### Battle of the Rosebud, 17 June 1876

Sitting Bull’s broader coalition was nearby, but Crazy Horse is principally associated with battlefield leadership against General George Crook. Statements that Sitting Bull personally commanded the battle are insufficiently supported.

### Sun Dance and vision, June 1876

**Strong oral tradition, details disputed:** Before the Little Bighorn, Sitting Bull performed a Sun Dance sacrifice. After prolonged dancing and bleeding, he reportedly saw soldiers falling upside down into the Lakota camp “like grasshoppers,” with no ears—interpreted as soldiers unable to hear warnings.

Some versions add that victory was conditional on taking no spoils. The core vision was recorded through later Lakota testimony; precise wording and elaborate details differ among narrators. It became a major element of his prophetic reputation.

### Battle of the Little Bighorn/Greasy Grass, 25–26 June 1876

The large encampment contained Lakota, Northern Cheyenne, and some Arapaho families. Custer’s 7th Cavalry attacked it in separated battalions. Indigenous fighters defeated Marcus Reno’s initial assault and destroyed Custer’s immediate command.

**Documented:** Sitting Bull was the camp’s most internationally famous leader and had helped create the political and spiritual coalition.

**Best-supported reconstruction:** He did not exercise centralized tactical command and probably did not participate substantially in the direct fighting. He was beyond the usual prime fighting age, had weakened himself in the Sun Dance, and attended to spiritual or protective responsibilities and the safety of families. His nephews One Bull and White Bull fought.

**Hostile contemporary testimony:** Low Dog, who disliked him, later sneered that Sitting Bull would fight “if someone would lend him a heart.” This is evidence of rivalry and disagreement, not proof of cowardice.

**Myth:** Popular accounts made him the battlefield general who designed Custer’s destruction. Indigenous victory resulted from many autonomous fighters and leaders, including Crazy Horse, Gall, Crow King, Lame White Man, Two Moon, Low Dog, and others.

## 1876–1877: Military pursuit

After the American defeat, the government intensified operations, confiscated the Black Hills through coercive legislation, and pursued non-agency communities. Sitting Bull met Colonel Nelson A. Miles in October 1876. Their negotiations failed.

Miles later suggested Sitting Bull contemplated treachery during a parley. This was never judicially established and should be treated as a self-interested military allegation.

Sitting Bull’s forces fought Miles at Cedar Creek and were pressured through winter campaigns, the destruction of resources, and the declining buffalo herds. Groups progressively left him for agencies.

## 1877–1881: Canadian exile

Sitting Bull entered the North-West Territories, now Saskatchewan, in 1877; some followers had crossed earlier. His following at its greatest numbered several thousand people, although estimates fluctuate.

### James Morrow Walsh

North-West Mounted Police superintendent James Walsh informed Sitting Bull that he would receive the protection of British law if his people obeyed Canadian law and refrained from cross-border raids. A relationship of mutual respect developed between them.

Canadian archival and diplomatic records show continual concern about:

- American demands for his return
- Rumors of raids or a southward movement
- Food shortages
- Relations with local First Nations and Métis traders
- Whether the Lakota would be granted land or rations

Canada did not grant Sitting Bull a reserve or sustained government support. Buffalo scarcity produced severe hunger. Ottawa’s refusal of long-term material assistance functioned as pressure to leave.

### U.S. commission, October 1877

American commissioners traveled to Canada and offered terms for return. Sitting Bull rejected the offer and asserted that the United States had broken its agreements. Canadian records contradict recurrent American rumors that he was assembling a new invasion force.

### Nez Perce refugees

After the 1877 Nez Perce War, small groups, including people associated with White Bird, reached Sitting Bull’s Canadian camp. This is a documented cross-tribal refugee connection.

### Jean-Louis Légaré

Métis trader Jean-Louis Légaré traded with the Lakota and ultimately helped provision and guide Sitting Bull’s remaining group to Fort Buford. Later claims that Légaré “captured” or single-handedly induced the surrender simplify a longer process driven principally by starvation, political isolation, and the breakup of the buffalo economy.

## 1881–1883: Return and imprisonment

On 19 July 1881 Sitting Bull arrived at Fort Buford with approximately 186 relatives and followers. The formal surrender ceremony occurred on 20 July. He had his young son Crow Foot hand over his rifle.

A widely reported statement says he wished it remembered that he was the last of his people to surrender his rifle. The translation and polished English wording should not be assumed verbatim, but the substance fits contemporary surrender reporting.

The group was transported through Bismarck to Fort Randall. Sitting Bull remained a **prisoner of war** for roughly twenty months, separated from his preferred home community because officials feared his influence.

He was returned to Standing Rock in May 1883. Agent James McLaughlin tried to reduce his political authority by controlling travel, visitors, trade, and access to him.

## 1883–1884: Public appearances and contested speeches

At the September 1883 Northern Pacific Railway celebration in Bismarck, Sitting Bull delivered remarks in Lakota through an interpreter. A popular story says he insulted the assembled officials as thieves and liars while the interpreter substituted a polite welcome.

**Disputed:** Versions appeared through later recollection, and no reliable contemporaneous verbatim Lakota transcript survives. It is plausible that an interpreter softened politically hostile remarks; the theatrical modern quotation cannot be treated as certain.

In 1884 promoter Alvaren Allen obtained permission for a touring exhibition commonly called the “Sitting Bull Combination” or “Sitting Bull Connection.” Sitting Bull traveled in the northern United States and Canada.

### Annie Oakley

He met the young marksman Annie Oakley in Minnesota in 1884. He admired her shooting and called her **Watanya Cicilla**, conventionally “Little Sure Shot.”

**Reported fact:** Oakley and her husband later maintained that Sitting Bull ceremonially adopted her as a daughter.

**Qualification:** The adoption story is documented primarily through Oakley, Frank Butler, and later publicity; independent contemporary Lakota evidence is limited. Their acquaintance and his conferral of the name are substantially better supported than every detail of the ceremony.

## 1885: Buffalo Bill’s Wild West

On 6 June 1885 Sitting Bull signed a contract at Standing Rock with John M. Burke for one summer with Buffalo Bill Cody’s Wild West.

The surviving contract provided:

- $50 per week for Sitting Bull
- Monthly wages for five Indigenous men, three women, and interpreter William Halsey
- A $125 gift
- Two weeks’ advance pay
- Sole right for Sitting Bull to sell his photographs and autographs
- A four-month season, extendable on the same terms

This is the clearest documentary evidence concerning his personal finances.

He joined the show in Buffalo, New York, and appeared as a celebrated living symbol rather than reenacting a reliably documented battlefield command. He rode around the arena and sold signed photographs. The tour took him through eastern and Canadian cities, including Montreal, where he was photographed with Cody.

Reports say he gave money to poor children and returned with relatively little. The exact amounts are unverified. He left after one season and did not travel with the show to Europe.

### Relationship with Buffalo Bill Cody

Their relationship combined commerce, publicity, mutual fascination, and some personal regard. Cody benefited from Sitting Bull’s fame; Sitting Bull obtained cash, mobility, contact with large urban audiences, and a view of American demographic and industrial power. Later stories making them uncomplicated best friends are romanticized.

### “Cursing the audience”

A recurrent legend claims Sitting Bull denounced or cursed white spectators in Lakota while audiences applauded, unaware of his meaning. Historian Robert Utley rejected the story as unsupported. It may be a conflation with the disputed 1883 railroad speech.

## 1886–1888: Reservation politics and land resistance

Back at Standing Rock, Sitting Bull lived near the Grand River, raised livestock, sold autographs and photographs, and remained an attraction for tourists. His celebrity did not translate into unrestricted political authority or wealth. Indian Service regulations controlled movement and economic choices.

He opposed allotment and reduction of the Great Sioux Reservation. In 1888 he joined a delegation to Washington, D.C., and met President Grover Cleveland. Negotiations concerned federal efforts to obtain millions of acres. Sitting Bull pressed for higher compensation and rejected attempts to secure quick assent.

He did not hold an American elected office, formal federal appointment, corporate interest, patent, or conventional investment portfolio. His most clearly documented commercial assets were livestock, personal property, photographic rights, performance earnings, and autograph sales.

## 1889–1890: Catherine Weldon and the Ghost Dance crisis

### Catherine Weldon

Susanna Faesch Schlatter, using the name Catherine Weldon, was a Brooklyn artist and member of the National Indian Defense Association. She began corresponding with Sitting Bull after the 1888 negotiations and traveled to Standing Rock in 1889 and again in 1890.

She served as an advocate, secretary, adviser, and intermediary; supplied maps and political information; gave material assistance; and painted four portraits of him.

Their alliance deteriorated when the Ghost Dance crisis intensified. Weldon feared the movement would provoke military repression and urged him to distance himself. She left Standing Rock before his death.

**Disputed relationship:** Biographer Eileen Pollack reports evidence that Sitting Bull proposed marriage. Weldon did not accept, and no sound evidence supports the film version of a consummated romance.

### Ghost Dance

The movement originated with the Northern Paiute prophet Wovoka and promised moral renewal, reunion with the dead, restoration of the world, and—across some Lakota interpretations—the return of buffalo and disappearance of colonial domination.

Kicking Bear and others brought the teachings to Standing Rock. Sitting Bull permitted followers to practice and reportedly said that if the movement’s claims proved true he wanted to know it. Evidence portrays him as receptive or strategically protective rather than its founder.

**False or exaggerated official claim:** Agent McLaughlin represented Sitting Bull as a principal instigator of a dangerous uprising. The National Archives now notes that officials incorrectly blamed him for the movement’s rise. No reliable evidence shows that he planned an armed rebellion in December 1890.

## Death

## Arrest order

General Nelson Miles sought Sitting Bull’s removal. On 14 December 1890 McLaughlin ordered Lieutenant Bull Head and the agency police to arrest him before he could allegedly leave for Pine Ridge.

The written order is preserved in National Archives Record Group 75. McLaughlin believed arrest by Lakota police would be less provocative than an immediate military seizure. U.S. troops followed as support.

## Fatal confrontation, 15 December 1890

Before dawn, approximately forty agency policemen surrounded Sitting Bull’s cabin. They awakened, dressed, and led him outside. A crowd of his followers assembled.

The sequence thereafter differs by witness:

1. Sitting Bull initially submitted or at least did not physically resist inside the cabin.
2. Outside, followers urged resistance.
3. Catch-the-Bear fired and wounded Lieutenant Bull Head, according to McLaughlin’s official account.
4. Bull Head shot Sitting Bull in the chest or side.
5. Red Tomahawk shot him in the head as the exchange continued.
6. A general gunfight followed.

Sitting Bull and his son Crow Foot died. Agency policemen Bull Head, Shave Head, Little Eagle, Hawk Man, Afraid-of-Soldiers, and others were killed or mortally wounded; several of Sitting Bull’s supporters also died. Totals vary because some wounded died later.

McLaughlin’s report is indispensable but interested: he ordered the operation and defended it afterward. Charles Eastman’s later account supplies a partly different, family-informed narrative. “Murder,” “assassination,” “death resisting arrest,” and “killed in a gun battle” encode different interpretations. The narrow documentary description is that he was killed by agency police during a federally directed arrest that escalated into gunfire.

### Immediate aftermath

Followers fled toward Spotted Elk/Big Foot’s Miniconjou community. Fourteen days later, on 29 December 1890, the 7th Cavalry killed Spotted Elk and large numbers of Lakota men, women, and children at Wounded Knee.

There is no evidence that Sitting Bull masterminded an armed Ghost Dance conspiracy. Officials regarded his symbolic influence as dangerous, and the attempt to neutralize that influence caused the fatal encounter.

## Body, Autopsy, and Burial

Sitting Bull’s body was taken to Fort Yates. Army physician Horace Deeble removed hair and leggings—and contemporary reports or later investigations also associate him with other body parts or objects—without family consent or lawful authority.

The Smithsonian later concluded that the hair and leggings had been improperly acquired. They were returned to the LaPointe family in 2007.

Sitting Bull was buried at Fort Yates. In April 1953 a Mobridge group, including Clarence Grey Eagle, excavated what they claimed were his remains and reburied them near Mobridge beneath a memorial by Korczak Ziolkowski.

**Unresolved:** North Dakota authorities maintained that the raiders removed the wrong remains or recovered very little. Other traditions say the real body had already been moved secretly, possibly to Canada. No publicly established forensic chain of custody proves that the Mobridge grave contains Sitting Bull.

The 2021 DNA study used authenticated hair, not bones from either claimed grave. It therefore did not resolve the burial-location dispute.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Sovereignty and land

Sitting Bull’s recorded political position was consistent:

- Land was held through collective obligation, not treated simply as an alienable commodity.
- The Black Hills could not legitimately be sold by a limited set of negotiators.
- Agency dependence threatened political and material autonomy.
- U.S. promises were unreliable when enforcement benefited settlers or miners.
- Peace was possible without accepting unrestricted federal control.

He negotiated repeatedly, rejected some offers, and adapted when military and ecological conditions made independent buffalo life impossible.

### Religion

He practiced Lakota religion through prayer, pipe ceremony, sacrifice, dreams, visions, the Sun Dance, and generosity. “Medicine man” is an approximate and sometimes reductive translation.

Claims that he converted to Catholicism are unsupported. Missionaries interacted with him, but evidence does not show baptism or abandonment of Lakota ceremonial life.

### Violence

He participated in intertribal warfare and resistance to U.S. expansion. His pictographic autobiographies celebrate violent deeds and captures. Conversely, no evidence supports indiscriminate command responsibility for every killing committed by Lakota fighters or the popular description of Little Bighorn as a “massacre” orchestrated by him.

### Adaptation

His 1885 show-business employment, autograph sales, livestock ownership, and use of photography demonstrate selective engagement with the cash economy. These did not mean acceptance of allotment or cultural assimilation.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Family and Hunkpapa circle

- **Four Horns:** senior relative and mentor; helped elevate Sitting Bull’s leadership.
- **One Bull and White Bull:** nephews; fought at Little Bighorn and later supplied important recollections.
- **Gall:** prominent Hunkpapa leader and sometime ally whose reservation politics later diverged from Sitting Bull’s.
- **Crow King and Black Moon:** Hunkpapa associates in the non-treaty coalition.
- **Rain-in-the-Face:** fellow Hunkpapa warrior; frequently joined to Sitting Bull in popular mythology.

### Interband allies

- **Crazy Horse:** Oglala war leader and major military ally in the 1876 coalition. No evidence makes either the permanent subordinate of the other.
- **Low Dog:** Oglala leader at Little Bighorn; later an openly hostile critic.
- **Spotted Elk/Big Foot:** Miniconjou leader whose community received refugees after Sitting Bull’s death.
- **Kicking Bear:** brought Ghost Dance teachings to Standing Rock.
- **White Bird and Nez Perce refugees:** sought refuge in the Canadian Lakota camp.

### Government and military adversaries or negotiators

- **Alfred Sully:** commanded 1864 punitive operations.
- **George A. Custer:** never established as having met Sitting Bull personally; their names became permanently joined through Little Bighorn.
- **Nelson A. Miles:** battlefield opponent, negotiator, and later advocate of arrest.
- **James McLaughlin:** Standing Rock agent; controlled Sitting Bull’s reservation life and ordered his arrest.
- **Bull Head and Red Tomahawk:** agency policemen involved in his fatal shooting.
- **James Morrow Walsh:** Canadian police officer who developed a respectful working relationship with him.
- **Jean-Louis Légaré:** trader and intermediary in the return from Canada.
- **Grover Cleveland:** met Sitting Bull’s 1888 delegation.

### Performers, promoters, and advocates

- **Buffalo Bill Cody:** employer, promoter, and public associate.
- **John M. Burke:** contracted him for the 1885 Wild West season.
- **Annie Oakley:** marksman he named “Little Sure Shot”; reported adopted daughter.
- **Catherine Weldon:** correspondent, political advocate, secretary, material supporter, and portraitist.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Sitting Bull did not form companies or hold conventional securities, patents, or industrial investments.

Documented economic and institutional relationships included:

- **Buffalo Bill’s Wild West:** a four-month 1885 employment contract at $50 weekly, with a separate allowance structure for his party.
- **Photograph and autograph sales:** the contract reserved these rights to him.
- **Livestock and household property:** maintained at Standing Rock.
- **Standing Rock Agency:** controlled rations, travel, policing, and official economic opportunities.
- **Northern Pacific Railway:** used his presence in public spectacle surrounding railroad expansion; he opposed the underlying land dispossession.
- **National Indian Defense Association:** Catherine Weldon’s advocacy network, not an organization Sitting Bull formally directed.
- **U.S. Indian Service and Army:** held him as a prisoner of war and later regulated his residence.
- **North-West Mounted Police:** governed his legal position during Canadian exile.

Popular claims that he became wealthy with Buffalo Bill are unsupported. His earnings were significant relative to reservation cash incomes, but he toured for only one season and reportedly distributed or spent much of the money.

## Controversies, Allegations, Violence, and Evidentiary Findings

### Was he the commander at Little Bighorn?

**Popular claim/myth:** Sitting Bull devised and directed Custer’s defeat.

**Finding:** He was a coalition-builder and spiritual authority but not a centralized battlefield commander. Most strong reconstructions place him outside the principal fighting.

### Was he a coward?

**Hostile allegation:** Low Dog’s 1881 interview ridiculed his failure to fight.

**Finding:** The remark documents factional hostility. Sitting Bull’s earlier war record and pictographic autobiographies contradict a generalized claim of cowardice.

### Was Little Bighorn a massacre?

“Custer massacre” was a common U.S. propaganda formulation. The engagement began with a military attack on an Indigenous village and became a battlefield defeat of Custer’s command. Lakota and Cheyenne fighters also killed soldiers in flight or after units disintegrated. The term “massacre” is therefore a political characterization, not a neutral description of Sitting Bull’s personal conduct.

### Did he order mutilations?

Bodies at Little Bighorn were mutilated according to several accounts, but acts varied and involved individual combatants. No reliable evidence establishes a general order from Sitting Bull.

### Was he responsible for the Ghost Dance crisis?

**Official allegation in 1890:** He fostered a militant conspiracy.

**Finding:** He allowed and protected the practice but did not originate it, and evidence of a planned armed uprising is lacking. Officials treated influence and potential movement to Pine Ridge as sufficient danger.

### Did he plan to kill Nelson Miles at a parley?

**Military allegation:** Miles later implied treachery.

**Finding:** Unproven and dependent on adversarial testimony. No legal adjudication or independent documentary confirmation exists.

### Was he anti-white?

He repeatedly denounced invasion, treaty violations, and federal policy. He also negotiated with American and Canadian officers, hired non-Lakota intermediaries, toured with white performers, befriended or worked with Walsh, Cody, Oakley, and Weldon, and gave public audiences. Sweeping racial formulations often come from translated or disputed speeches.

### Did he curse Wild West audiences?

**Myth or disputed anecdote:** He supposedly insulted audiences in Lakota while interpreters rendered praise.

**Finding:** No dependable contemporary transcript substantiates it. It likely borrows from the equally uncertain Northern Pacific speech story.

### Catherine Weldon romance

**Modern dramatization:** They became lovers.

**Finding:** Unsupported. Friendship and political collaboration are documented; a reported proposal is possible but not securely established.

### Annie Oakley adoption

**Reported fact with publicity overlay:** Sitting Bull adopted her and named her Little Sure Shot.

**Finding:** The naming and acquaintance are well attested; details of a formal ceremony rest mainly on Oakley-Butler publicity and recollection.

### Artifact fraud and false provenance

Artifacts allegedly owned by Sitting Bull command high prices. Litigation has involved dealers accused of selling bogus objects attributed to him and other leaders. Such cases concern the modern artifact market, not criminal conduct by Sitting Bull. Provenance requires a documented chain of custody; attached names and oral dealer representations are insufficient.

### Recent adversarial-search finding, 2024–2026

Searches pairing his names with “controversy,” “scandal,” “allegations,” “lawsuit,” “fraud,” “accusations,” “conspiracy,” and “criticism” found no credible recent evidence of a newly discovered personal crime, financial fraud, or secret organization attributable to him. Recent contested material instead concerns:

- Artifact authenticity and commercial exploitation
- Competing descendants and genealogical claims
- The location of his remains
- Representation in film and public memorials
- Misleading claims that DNA newly “discovered” an otherwise unknown descendant
- Persistent inflation of his command role at Little Bighorn

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Supreme chief of the Sioux nation”

- **Story:** A grand council appointed him supreme chief of all Sioux.
- **Appearance:** Best known through Stanley Vestal’s 1932 biography based on interviews conducted around 1930.
- **Evidence:** His wide influence is supported; a permanent pan-Lakota office with sovereignty over every band is not.
- **Effect:** Encouraged portrayals of him as king or commanding general.

### The prophetic vision

- **Story:** Soldiers fell upside down into camp, foretelling Little Bighorn.
- **Circulation:** Lakota oral accounts, family testimony, later biographies.
- **Evidence:** Strongly rooted oral tradition; wording, timing, sacrifice counts, and prohibition on taking spoils vary.
- **Effect:** Established him in popular memory as the prophet of Custer’s defeat.

### “Last man to surrender his rifle”

- **Story:** At Fort Buford he proclaimed himself the last of his people to surrender.
- **Evidence:** Contemporary surrender reporting supports the substance, but polished English versions are translations.
- **Effect:** Became a defining statement of uncompromising resistance.

### “I hate all white people”

- **Story:** He opened the Northern Pacific ceremony with an insulting speech that was deliberately mistranslated.
- **Evidence:** Later recollections, no surviving dependable Lakota transcript.
- **Effect:** Used alternately to depict fearless defiance, interpreter deception, or racial hostility.

### Secret removal of his body

- **Story:** Relatives removed him from Fort Yates before the 1953 raid, perhaps burying him in Canada.
- **Evidence:** Oral claims without a verified chain of custody.
- **Against:** Official burial records and the 1953 excavation claim.
- **Status:** Unresolved.

### The Mobridge body

- **Story:** South Dakotans recovered and reburied Sitting Bull in 1953.
- **Evidence:** Participant accounts and a memorial site.
- **Against:** Poor excavation documentation, North Dakota objections, uncertainty about grave placement, and allegations that the recovered bones belonged to someone else.
- **Status:** Unresolved; DNA from the claimed bones has not publicly settled it.

### Buffalo Bill friendship

- **Story:** Former enemies became intimate lifelong friends.
- **Evidence:** A contract, shared photographs, one season of travel, and later accounts of mutual regard.
- **Qualification:** The relationship was also commercial and structured by colonial spectacle.
- **Effect:** Supplied an enduring reconciliation narrative.

### Famous quotations

Many internet quotations—especially statements about ownership, poverty, money, environmentalism, or the disappearance of the buffalo—lack traceable contemporary texts. Because Sitting Bull spoke primarily in Lakota through interpreters, exact English quotation marks should be used only when a source, interpreter, date, and transmission history can be identified.

## Works by or Attributed to Sitting Bull

Sitting Bull did not author a conventional book. The genuine corpus consists primarily of visual autobiographies, signatures, dictated or translated speeches, interviews, and statements recorded by others.

### Pictographic autobiographies

1. **Sitting Bull pictographic autobiography, Smithsonian National Anthropological Archives, MS 1929-b**  
   Twenty-two surviving ledger drawings attributed to Sitting Bull, depicting battles, coups, captures, and horse-taking. His signature accompanies drawings. Genuine authorship is accepted, though identifications and captions were mediated by collectors or interpreters.

2. **Other pictographic autobiographical sequences**  
   Multiple sets have been attributed to him. M. W. Stirling published *Three Pictographic Autobiographies of Sitting Bull* in 1938 with forty-six plates. Provenance and individual-sheet attribution should be checked collection by collection.

3. **Saville Ledger drawings attributed to Sitting Bull**  
   Reportedly produced at or associated with Red Cloud Agency/Fort Robinson. Attribution and provenance depend on the particular ledger record.

### Speeches and diplomatic statements

Recorded through interpreters and therefore not autograph texts:

- Statements during councils concerning Fort Buford and American encroachment, 1860s–1870s
- Interviews with the U.S. commission in Canada, October 1877
- Fort Buford surrender statement, 20 July 1881
- Northern Pacific Railway address, Bismarck, September 1883—wording disputed
- Reservation and land-agreement councils, 1880s
- Statements during the 1888 Washington delegation
- Remarks to journalists and Wild West audiences, 1884–1885

### Interviews

His words appeared in newspapers and military or agency reports, frequently translated twice—from Lakota through an interpreter and then edited by a reporter. No collected edition can be assumed to reproduce an unmediated voice.

### Modern collections

- **Bob Blaisdell, editor, *Sitting Bull: The Collected Speeches* (1998).** Posthumous edited collection; not a book composed by Sitting Bull.
- **Mark Diedrich, *Sitting Bull Speaks* (2025).** Archival reconstruction centered on recorded words; posthumous scholarly compilation rather than autobiography.
- **M. W. Stirling, *Three Pictographic Autobiographies of Sitting Bull* (1938).** Scholarly publication of visual works.

### Letters and signatures

Some documents bear his handwritten signature, including the 1885 Wild West contract. Letters attributed to him were generally drafted by interpreters, advocates, or clerks and should be described as dictated, translated, signed, or issued in his name—not assumed to be personally written prose.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Sitting Bull’s reputation has passed through several distinct constructions:

1. **Late nineteenth-century U.S. enemy image:** “hostile chief,” architect of the “Custer massacre,” obstacle to civilization.
2. **Wild West celebrity:** a defeated but marketable warrior presented within commercial frontier mythology.
3. **Early biography:** the tragic “last chief” or noble adversary.
4. **Lakota and Indigenous memory:** spiritual leader, defender of land, provider, and symbol of survival.
5. **Modern scholarship:** a political and religious leader operating within decentralized Lakota institutions rather than a conventional general.
6. **Global icon:** used in advertising, tourism, sports trophies, film, protest imagery, environmental quotation culture, and anti-colonial symbolism.

Institutions and landmarks bearing his name include Sitting Bull College at Standing Rock, schools, monuments, roads, commercial products, and the former University of North Dakota–University of South Dakota Sitting Bull football trophy.

His life remains relevant to Black Hills land claims, treaty-rights politics, the meaning of the Ghost Dance crisis, museum repatriation, Indigenous remains policy, and debates about who controls Native history.

The Smithsonian’s 2007 return of objects removed from his corpse became an important case in correcting museum acquisitions made without consent. The DNA work later demonstrated a method for kinship testing from degraded historical hair, while also raising ethical questions about genetics, ancestry, tribal sovereignty, and scientific authority.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the researched sources are included.

### Low Dog — documented fact / reported hostility

Low Dog, an Oglala Lakota war leader, was present in the allied village at the Little Bighorn on 25 June 1876 and participated in the fighting. He and Sitting Bull therefore belonged to the same wartime coalition, although neither was under the other’s permanent command.

In an interview published in the *Leavenworth Weekly Times* on 18 August 1881, Low Dog was explicitly described as unfriendly to Sitting Bull. Asked what role Sitting Bull had taken in the battle, he replied sarcastically that Sitting Bull would fight “if someone would lend him a heart.” The statement records a direct rivalry or factional hostility and is also important evidence against treating Indigenous accounts as a single unanimous tradition.

Both later lived at Standing Rock and were among leaders invited to the 4 July 1889 Bismarck constitutional-convention parade.

### Abraham Lincoln — no documented personal connection

Sitting Bull and Abraham Lincoln were contemporaries for approximately three decades, but no source consulted documents a meeting, correspondence, acknowledged influence, or personal relationship.

Lincoln’s administration conducted the Civil War-era campaigns that formed part of Sitting Bull’s early conflict with the United States, including the wider military response following the 1862 Dakota War. That is a governmental and historical context, not a documented personal connection. Fort Abraham Lincoln, from which Custer’s 1874 and 1876 expeditions departed, was named after the president; the shared name does not establish a relationship.
