## Works by Sir Thomas More

### Poetic and commemorative obras

- **c. 1492–1504:** *English Poems*. A composite poetic œuvre rather than one book: verse devised for painted hangings in More’s father’s house, Fortune poems, devotional and occasional pieces, the lament for Queen Elizabeth, and *A Merry Gest of a Sergeant and a Friar*. Individual dates are often conjectural, and the canon survives through manuscripts and later printings rather than an authorially fixed collection. [CW 1; CTMS]

- **c. 1500:** *Verses for William Holt’s Lac Puerorum*. Commendatory Latin verse attached to Holt’s grammar; it is a small paratextual intervention, not an independent book. [CW 3.2; CTMS]

- **c. 1496–1523:** *Latin Poems / Epigrammata*. A changing corpus of epigrams, verse letters, translations, political poems, devotional poems, and family verse, repeatedly enlarged and rearranged in printed manifestations; the 1523 collection contains 253 poems, while the modern *Essential Works* counts 291 poems across More’s Latin and English poetic œuvre. Calling it a single work conceals its serial, occasional character. [CW 3.2; EW]

- **1509:** *Coronation Ode for Henry VIII and Katherine of Aragon*. A Latin ceremonial poem made for a dynastic occasion and incorporated into the larger poetic corpus. [CW 3.2; CTMS]

- **1513:** *Epigrams against Germain de Brie*. A sequence produced during the quarrel over Brie’s *Chordigera*; several poems act polemically and collectively rather than forming a neutral lyric collection. [CW 3.2; CTMS]

- **1517–1522:** *Poems to and about More’s children*. Latin occasional and pedagogical verse embedded in family correspondence and the *Epigrammata* tradition; the boundaries between poem, letter, and educational exercise are porous. [CW 3.2; CTMS]

- **c. 1512 and 1532:** *More family epitaph*. More made an earlier Latin verse epitaph and in 1532 composed the Latin prose inscription installed on his Chelsea tomb. It is simultaneously text, monument, family memorial, and autobiographical public statement, so it resists treatment as merely a literary “work.” [CW 3.2; IMB]

### Translations and adaptive works

- **c. 1504; published 1510:** *The Life of John Picus*. An English translation and devotional adaptation of Gianfrancesco Pico’s Latin life of Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, supplemented with translated writings attributed to Pico; it is transformative compilation rather than a wholly original biography. [CW 1; CTMS]

- **1505–1506:** *Cynicus*. More’s Latin translation of Lucian’s Greek dialogue, made within a collaborative translation project with Erasmus. [CW 3.1]

- **1505–1506:** *Menippus, or the Descent into Hades*. A Latin translation of Lucian, also transmitted under the title *Necromantia*. [CW 3.1]

- **1505–1506:** *Philopseudes, or The Lover of Lies*. A Latin translation of Lucian’s dialogue. [CW 3.1]

- **1505–1506:** *Tyrannicida*. A Latin translation of Lucian’s rhetorical exercise on tyrannicide. [CW 3.1]

- **1505–1506:** *Declamatio in reply to Lucian’s Tyrannicida*. More’s original counter-declamation answering the translated exercise; it is both a creative imitation and the surviving product of literary competition with Erasmus. [CW 3.1]

- **1506:** *Dedicatory Letter to Thomas Ruthall*. The prefatory letter for More’s Lucian translations explains and performs the humanist translation project; although formally a letter, its publication makes it part of the constructed book. [CW 3.1]

### Histories and political thought-experiments

- **c. 1513–1518:** *The History of King Richard III / Historia Richardi Tertii*. An unfinished historical project surviving in related English and Latin manifestations, neither simply a translation of the other. Its stopping point and the relationship between versions remain matters of scholarly discussion; the work was not published by More. [CW 2; CW 15; CTMS]

- **1515–1516; published 1516:** *Utopia*. A Latin dialogue, travel narrative, political thought-experiment, satire, and invented commonwealth, issued with collaborative letters, verses, a map, an invented alphabet, and marginal glosses. More made the central text and several paratexts, but the first printed object is a humanist Gesamtkunstwerk involving Erasmus, Peter Giles, printers, artists, and other contributors. [CW 4]

### Humanist letters that became works

- **c. 1504:** *Letter to John Colet*. An extended Latin humanist letter concerning education, theology, and Colet’s biblical lectures; its argumentative scale makes it more than routine correspondence. [Corr.; CTMS]

- **1515:** *Letter to Martin Dorp*. A public defense of Erasmus, humanist learning, Greek, and imaginative literature; although epistolary in form, it operates as a manifesto for Christian humanism. [CW 15]

- **1518:** *Letter to the University of Oxford*. An intervention against hostility to Greek studies, functioning as an institutional manifesto for the new learning. [CW 15]

- **c. 1519:** *Letter to Edward Lee*. A humanist-theological intervention in the controversy over Lee’s criticisms of Erasmus’s New Testament. [CW 15]

- **1519–1520:** *Letter to a Monk*. A long defense of Erasmus and humanist biblical scholarship addressed to an unidentified religious; the recipient’s identity and exact date are not securely fixed. [CW 15]

- **1520:** *Letter to Germain de Brie*. The prose intervention that closes, or attempts to close, the quarrel generated by More’s epigrams against Brie. [CW 3.2; CTMS]

- **1526; published 1568:** *Letter to Johannes Bugenhagen*. An anti-Reformation polemical tract cast as an open letter; delayed publication means its principal public manifestation was posthumous. [CW 7; CTMS]

### Polemical and confessional interventions

- **1521:** *Assertio septem sacramentorum* (*Defence of the Seven Sacraments*). This is conventionally Henry VIII’s book, not More’s: More later said that he helped “set the matters in order” and advised on papal language, but the extent of his contribution is uncertain. It belongs here only as a disputed collaboration that resists admission to More’s canon, and the Yale *Complete Works* consequently does not print it as his obra. [Cambridge, “Two Issues”; SAL]

- **1523:** *Responsio ad Lutherum*. A large Latin answer to Martin Luther’s attack on Henry VIII, published under the pseudonym “Guilielmus Rosseus.” It is polemic intended to act—defending ecclesiastical authority and discrediting an opponent—rather than detached theological description. [CW 5; CTMS]

- **June 1529; revised 1531:** *A Dialogue Concerning Heresies*. An English dialogue defending Catholic teaching, clerical authority, pilgrimage, images, and traditional interpretation against evangelical reform; More substantially revised the work for its second edition, so the two printings are authorial manifestations of one developing obra. [CW 6; CTMS]

- **September 1529:** *The Supplication of Souls*. A satirical prosopopoeia in which the dead answer Simon Fish’s *Supplication for the Beggars*; it is a polemical counter-manifesto voiced through fictional speakers. [CW 7; CTMS]

- **1532–1533:** *The Confutation of Tyndale’s Answer*. A vast, serial rebuttal of William Tyndale, published in installments and ultimately organized as nine books; the final book was left incomplete. Its scale and evolving publication make it a polemical project as much as a finished book. [CW 8; CTMS]

- **December 1532; published December 1533:** *Letter against John Frith*. An open letter on the Eucharist aimed at Frith and his readers; its publication under epistolary form turns correspondence into doctrinal action. [CW 7; CTMS]

- **April 1533:** *The Apology of Sir Thomas More*. More’s public defense of his controversial writing and conduct, combining autobiography, legal argument, and religious polemic. [CW 9; CTMS]

- **October 1533:** *The Debellation of Salem and Bizance*. A polemical answer to Christopher St German, whose fictional realms “Salem” and “Bizance” More appropriates and symbolically defeats; the title declares the text’s intended action. [CW 10; CTMS]

- **December 1533:** *The Answer to a Poisoned Book*. A lengthy Eucharistic polemic against a work published anonymously and now generally associated with George Joye; More’s title presents the opposing text as a dangerous material object requiring an antidote. [CW 11; CTMS]

### Unfinished meditation and devotional protocols

- **c. 1522:** *The Four Last Things / Quattuor Novissima*. An unfinished English meditation on death, judgment, hell, and heaven; only the treatment of death was completed. Its incompleteness is intrinsic evidence of an abandoned devotional project, not a defect to be silently repaired. [CW 1; CTMS]

- **1534:** *A Treatise upon the Passion*. A Tower meditation on Christ’s Passion, probably written under conditions of restricted access to books; it is devotional exposition and a protocol for sustained contemplation. [CW 13; CTMS]

- **1534–1535:** *A Treatise to Receive the Blessed Body of Our Lord*. A short Eucharistic devotional instruction, surviving among the Tower writings. It is more nearly a protocol for reception and prayer than a speculative treatise. [CW 13; CTMS]

- **1534:** *A Dialogue of Comfort against Tribulation*. A three-book English dialogue set against an imagined Ottoman threat to Hungary but written during More’s imprisonment; it is literary dialogue, spiritual counsel, and a practical technology for governing fear. [CW 12; CTMS]

- **1535:** *De tristitia Christi* (*The Sadness, Weariness, Fear, and Prayer of Christ before His Capture*). More’s final sustained Latin work, a meditation on Gethsemane left unfinished when his writing materials were removed. The surviving manuscript makes the interrupted project materially visible. [CW 14; CTMS]

### Prison instructions, prayers, and marginal creations

- **1534–1535:** *Imploring Divine Help against Temptation*. A compact devotional instruction from the Tower writings; its editorial title describes its function rather than certifying an authorial title. [CW 13; CTMS]

- **1534–1535:** *A Godly Instruction on How to Treat Those Who Wrong Us*. A brief ethical-devotional protocol; the familiar title is posthumous and descriptive. [CW 13; CTMS]

- **1534–1535:** *A Godly Meditation*. An English prayer written in More’s own hand in the margins of his printed Book of Hours. It is not a separate book but an autograph intervention that turns a pre-existing printed object into a new composite devotional object. [CW 13; Beinecke; CELM]

- **1534–1535:** *A Devout Prayer Collected out of the Psalms of David*. A prayer assembled by selecting and ordering verses in the margins of More’s Psalter; it is a cento and a protocol of recombination rather than wholly new verbal composition. [CW 13; CELM]

- **1534–1535:** *Notes and marks in More’s Psalter*. Latin annotations and pen marks connected with the making of *A Dialogue of Comfort against Tribulation*. These working traces resist being called an autonomous work, but as material evidence of composition they are part of what More made. [Beinecke; Folger]

- **July 1535:** *A Devout Prayer before Dying*. A final prayer associated with More’s execution and preserved among the concluding instructions and prayers; its title is editorial or transmissional rather than securely authorial. [CW 13; CTMS]

### Correspondence and collaborative testimony

- **1499–1535:** *Correspondence in Latin and English*. A dispersed corpus of approximately 280 known or recorded letters: private notes, family letters, prison letters, official communications, verse epistles, dedications, and public polemics. No complete critical edition exists—Rogers prints or lists 218—and therefore this corpus remains an incompletely gathered œuvre rather than one fixed book. [Corr.; SEP; CTMS]

- **1517–1522:** *Letters to More’s children and their tutors*. A pedagogical sub-corpus setting out More’s educational project and participating in the children’s learning; several items blur domestic correspondence, curriculum, and public humanist self-presentation. [Corr.; CTMS]

- **1534:** *Letter from Margaret Roper to Alice Alington*, commonly called *A Dialogue on Conscience*. The document presents itself as Margaret’s report of conversations with her father, and scholarship disputes whether More composed it, collaborated in it, or is only its reported speaker. It should therefore not be silently converted into a sole-authored More work. [Corr.; Kearney; CTMS]

- **1534–1535:** *Prison letters*. The surviving exchanges with Margaret Roper and letters to family, clergy, royal officers, and friends form a situational corpus rather than a designed book. Their making was collaborative and constrained: some survive through copies, reports, concealment, or transmission by recipients. [Corr.; EW; CELM]
