# Txillardegi: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier reconstructs the life of José Luis Álvarez Enparantza, known principally as **Txillardegi**, from documentary, scholarly, institutional, journalistic, bibliographic, and oral-history sources. It distinguishes his own recorded statements; contemporary records; later recollections by relatives and associates; scholarly interpretation; partisan memorialization; and hostile or polemical claims.

Several recurring summaries require qualification:

- **Birth year:** Euskaltzaindia, the Txillardegi biographical archive, and specialist Basque sources give **27 September 1929**. Several agency-derived obituaries printed **1927** and called him 84 at death. The 1929 date is the strongly documented version; he was 82 when he died.
- **ETA’s foundation:** participants long dated the change from Ekin to ETA to **July 1959**, while later accounts have sometimes assigned it to late 1958 or to 31 July 1959. The evidence supports a process: the name was discussed and adopted around 1958–59, and the new designation was publicly used by July 1959. No securely documented single “founding day” is universally accepted.
- **“Father of Euskara Batua”:** Txillardegi was an initiator and principal practical architect of the Bayonne proposals of 1963–64. Koldo Mitxelena prepared the decisive report adopted by Euskaltzaindia at Arantzazu in 1968; Euskaltzaindia collectively institutionalized and subsequently elaborated the standard. “Father” is commemorative shorthand, not an exclusive authorship finding.
- **Break with ETA:** he left in 1967, before ETA’s first generally accepted killing in June 1968. He nevertheless helped construct the organization and its ethnolinguistic doctrine, accepted revolutionary violence in principle under some conditions, and never issued an unconditional moral condemnation of ETA. At different moments he criticized armed struggle as tactically damaging and advocated mass civil disobedience.

### Origin of “Txillardegi”

The pseudonym came from a house called **Txillardegi**, about fifty metres from the Álvarez family’s Antigua home. He later explained his use of a house-name as a continuation of the Basque practice by which a toponym attached to one’s birthplace or household could eclipse the official patronymic. The pen name is usually dated to about 1954, though he had other names for clandestine and literary publication.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

The surname **Álvarez** came from Madrid. A great-grandfather reportedly moved from Madrid to Tolosa before the Second Carlist War. His grandfather, **Federico Álvarez**, lived in Tolosa and, after the early death of his wife, moved to the Antigua district of San Sebastián. Federico built a house called **Casa Victoria** and established a lithography workshop.

The workshop later failed. During Txillardegi’s childhood his father went to **Cuba** for work, and the children lived for a period with their grandmother on Calle San Martín. The family was back in Antigua by the outbreak of the Spanish Civil War.

### Maternal line

His mother was **María Enparantza**, from a family with roots in **Oñati**. She knew Basque but did not ordinarily use it with her children. Txillardegi recalled hearing her speak it with a maid and a milk seller; some cousins and children from nearby Aiete also spoke it. This domestic pattern—Basque present but socially restricted—became central to his later interpretation of language loss.

María played piano and encouraged her son to study it. Piano remained a major private practice throughout his life. His daughter Maddi recalled that he played very well, spent long periods at the instrument, and seemed to use it to order his thoughts and release emotional tension; the family could infer his mood from the music he chose.

### Sibling

His brother **Juan Mari Álvarez Enparantza** appears in Txillardegi’s account of his first return across the Spanish border in 1976. Juan Mari knew the civil governor and obtained an assurance that his brother could enter without arrest. The consulted sources do not establish a complete sibling list.

### Marriage and household

Txillardegi met **Jone Forcada** at a romería in San Sebastián’s Plaza Nueva on **19 January 1950**, the eve of San Sebastián Day. Both groups of young people had gravitated to one another because they were speaking Basque. Forcada’s father, from a right-wing family in Tudela, had moved to Errenteria for work and learned Basque; Jone trained as a teacher and worked with the pioneering educator **Elbira Zipitria**.

Jone was not merely an accompanying spouse. She taught, helped build ikastolas, handled the family’s legal papers when Txillardegi was clandestine or irregularly resident, supported the family financially when he could not work, and moved abroad with three very young children in 1961. A 2025 family account describes their marriage as a joint project joining language revival, political activism, education, and family survival.

The first two children, twins **Joseba and Jon**, were born in 1959. A daughter, **Maddi**, was born eleven months later. The family later referred to four siblings and five grandchildren. **Joseba Álvarez Forcada** became a prominent Herri Batasuna/Batasuna politician and language-normalization activist. Father and son reportedly disagreed sharply at times over the proper role of violence.

The exile imposed repeated moves and separations. When the twins were about nine, the parents sent them to their grandparents in Donostia so that they would remain rooted in the Basque Country; they rejoined the family in Hendaia in 1972. Joseba recalled being told that, if questioned, he should describe his father as a friend of his mother. Maddi later described childhood memory gaps and uprooting produced by constant changes of home, school, and country.

After returning south, Txillardegi and Jone eventually moved to a small farmhouse, **Bidarte**, at Amasa. In old age they sold it and returned to Donostia’s Parte Vieja. According to their children, they required the farmhouse’s purchaser to know Basque, rejecting other offers until a Basque-speaking buyer appeared. The piano and a large portion of Txillardegi’s library remained at Bidarte.

No reliable public evidence located for this dossier establishes significant inherited wealth, large investments, business ownership, patents, or elaborate financial structures. The available picture is of an educated but repeatedly precarious activist-intellectual household sustained at several points by teaching and Jone’s income.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1929–1948

Txillardegi was six when the Civil War began. He later said he did not fully understand the conflict at the time. Postwar San Sebastián, repression of Basque public life, and the conspicuous marginalization of the Basque language shaped his formation.

Although he did not grow up speaking Basque, he felt the Basque world was “his” before he knew it directly. In 1947, during a month-long illness at home, he questioned his mother and constructed a small grammar in a notebook from his father’s lithography business. That same year he later dated his first political action: distributing PNV propaganda under doors in Donostia’s Parte Vieja.

Accounts vary slightly on whether he “began” learning Basque in 1947 or 1948. The reconstruction is compatible: self-directed work began during the illness, and in 1948 he began sustained study and met teachers and activists including **Jon Etxaide** and **Elbira Zipitria**. He said he did not fully master the language until about 1955. His life therefore became a powerful symbolic case: a Spanish-speaking urban **euskaldunberri** who made acquired Basque the axis of nationhood.

## Education and Formation

### Engineering

In 1949 he moved to Bilbao and continued engineering preparation at the **Academia Necoechea**, later completing industrial-engineering studies. He entered a milieu of university students dissatisfied with what they regarded as the PNV’s passivity under Franco.

Military service was divided into periods: Zamora in 1952 and 1954 and a final six months at Ferrol in 1956. At Ferrol he wrote his first novel, *Leturiaren egunkari ezkutua*.

After military service he worked at a motor factory in Donostia. During his first exile he also worked as an engineer in Paris. The public sources consulted do not reliably identify the motor manufacturer or Paris employer.

### Linguistics, phonetics, and doctorate

He pursued linguistics at the University of Paris/Sorbonne and received a linguistics degree in **1975**. Some short biographies incorrectly imply completion in 1961; the detailed chronology places his Paris degree in 1975.

He began teaching phonology in Donostia’s EUTG in 1976–77. In 1981–82 he spent an academic year at **UCLA** studying phonetics and phonology. He completed a doctoral thesis on Basque accent at the **Autonomous University of Barcelona** in 1983; later notices date the doctorate formally to 1984 and describe it as *sobresaliente cum laude*. The thesis became *Euskal azentuaz* (1984).

From 1982 he taught at the **University of the Basque Country (UPV/EHU)**, including sociolinguistics and Basque phonology, and later held emeritus status.

### Philosophical and literary formation

His early intellectual field included **Søren Kierkegaard, Miguel de Unamuno, Jean-Paul Sartre, Albert Camus, Bertrand Russell**, existentialism, structuralism, and later sociolinguistics. In 1955 he received an Oiargi cultural prize for *Kierkegaard-en hazia Unamuno-gan lore* (“Kierkegaard’s seed flowers in Unamuno”).

He was raised in a Catholic social world but developed an agnostic or heterodox position and repeatedly treated religion as an existential and political problem. His fiction departed from the rural, confessional, costumbrista modes associated with much earlier Basque prose. His characters were urban, alienated, sexually and religiously uncertain, and philosophically self-conscious.

## Early Career and the Reinvention of Basque Prose, 1950–1958

In 1950 he began publishing Basque articles in the bulletin of **Eusko Ikasleen Alkartasuna**. He was arrested that year, accused of belonging to **Jagi-Jagi**. The consulted evidence records the arrest but not a resulting conviction.

In 1952 he and fellow students created **Ekin** (“to act,” “to undertake,” “persist”). Ekin was initially a clandestine political-cultural study group: its members educated themselves in Basque history, language, and nationalism. Its principal early figures included Txillardegi, Julen Madariaga, Benito del Valle, José Manuel/Manu Agirre, David López Dorronsoro, Alfonso Irigoien, Iñaki Gainzarain, Rafael Albisu, and others whose exact membership varies by account.

Ekin later merged uneasily with the PNV youth organization EGI. The younger group accused the old party leadership of passivity and excessive attachment to prewar formulas; the PNV leadership distrusted Ekin’s independence and growing appetite for direct action. The rupture produced the organization that became ETA.

In 1956 Txillardegi translated or adapted short works, published essays in *Egan*, completed military service, and wrote *Leturiaren egunkari ezkutua*. Published in 1957, the book is commonly designated the first modern Basque novel. The phrase is a critical consensus, not a literal claim that no earlier modernizing fiction existed: its importance lies in a decisive shift toward existential interiority, the diary form, urban space, and European philosophical contemporaneity.

Euskaltzaindia named him a corresponding member (**euskaltzain urgazlea**) on **26 July 1957**. In 1958 he began teaching Basque literature for the Gipuzkoa provincial institution; his arrest in 1960 ended that work.

## Chronological Life History

### Ekin becomes ETA, 1958–1959

The most defensible reconstruction is that Ekin’s separation from EGI and transformation into **Euskadi Ta Askatasuna (ETA, “Basque Country and Freedom”)** occurred across late 1958 and 1959. Txillardegi is broadly credited—including by institutional and hostile sources—with proposing the name.

One frequently repeated origin story says the group first considered **Aberri Ta Askatasuna** (“Homeland and Freedom”) but rejected the acronym **ATA** because *ata* meant “duck” in a dialect; Txillardegi then proposed *Euskadi Ta Askatasuna*. Accounts locate the decisive meeting in Deba and name Txillardegi, Benito del Valle, Julen Madariaga, and Iñaki Larramendi. This is plausible participant tradition, but details were fixed retrospectively.

Historian Santiago de Pablo has shown that founders’ own statements for roughly two decades consistently dated the name change to **1959**, often July, while a later 1958 anniversary narrative became common. A letter to lehendakari-in-exile Jesús María Leizaola dated 31 July 1959 is often cited as the first public use. The dossier therefore treats July 1959 as the strongest conventional date, without pretending a unique incorporation event existed.

Txillardegi became the visible intellectual of ETA’s cultural/ethnolinguistic current. In the organization’s early conception, national identity was detached from Sabino Arana’s biological/genealogical criteria and reattached to language, political will, and cultural recovery. This was an opening to acquired identity—Txillardegi himself lacked the “pure” surname credentials of older nationalism—but it also produced exclusionary definitions of the non-Basque-speaking or non-nationalist resident.

### Arrests, prison, and flight, 1960–1961

In August 1960 police arrested him. A Basque biographical chronology says he spent several months in prison; another specialist entry connects the second arrest to a public letter signed by 339 Basque priests. He was held at **Martutene**. No consulted record establishes conviction for homicide, bombing, financial crime, or direct participation in a lethal attack.

On New Year’s Day 1961 he crossed into the Northern Basque Country. He first stayed in **Hazparne/Hasparren**, then went to Paris for engineering work. Jone followed with their three small children. Txillardegi’s exile ultimately lasted until 1977, with a first protected visit south in 1976.

ETA attempted to derail a train carrying Franco supporters to San Sebastián on 17 July 1961. No one was killed. Txillardegi was then in exile. The operation belonged to the organization he had helped create, but the consulted sources do not document that he planned or executed it.

### Exile: Hasparren, Bayonne, Paris, Belgium, Bordeaux, Hendaia, 1961–1977

In 1962 he returned to Hasparren and taught mathematics at **Collège Saint-Joseph**, directed by **Piarres Xarriton**. Oral-history archives preserve testimony about his dismissal from the school in 1965. He developed durable relationships across the northern/southern border, especially with Xarriton, Jean-Louis Davant, Telesforo Monzón, Martin Ugalde, Pierre Lafitte, and activists connected to Enbata.

In 1963 the **Euskal Idazkaritza** was established in Bayonne and placed Txillardegi in charge of its language work. The group met repeatedly—later accounts say weekly for eight months—and included, in varying attendance, Telesforo Monzón, Juan Solaun, Eneko Irigarai, Jean-Louis Davant, the priests Andiazabal and Roger Idiart, and others.

Txillardegi submitted a resignation letter to Euskaltzaindia through *Enbata* in 1963, accusing the Academy of inertia and demanding action on written unification, Basque in schools and churches, spelling, and a dedicated congress. Euskaltzaindia did not accept the resignation and replied that a congress would be held.

The **Bayonne Congress of 29–30 August 1964**, under Pierre Lafitte’s patronage, adopted a practical written standard for immediate use by writers and publications. It dealt with orthography, morphology, auxiliary forms, and disputed letters—most famously **h**. The proposals became the working bridge to Arantzazu in 1968.

French authorities subsequently required Txillardegi to leave the border area, and he moved to Belgium. The family lived in several Belgian homes; the record refers to Brussels and Waterloo. From Brussels he launched **Branka** in 1966, a review for exiled nationalists that continued until 1971. It carried his political, linguistic, and anti-Marxist/anti-dogmatic arguments under several signatures.

### Break with ETA, 1966–1967

ETA’s internal conflict pitted “culturalist” or ethnolinguistic nationalists, workerist Marxists, Third World revolutionaries, and an emerging military logic against one another. The labels were fluid and polemical. Txillardegi opposed reducing the Basque national question to class struggle and rejected the Marxist-Leninist direction established around the Fifth Assembly.

In 1967 he and several associates resigned from ETA by letter. He was not leaving because he had adopted pacifism. A February 1967 text associated with his current argued that a violent and wholly illegal phase might ultimately be necessary to reach a socialist Basque state, but that making ETA a guerrilla organization **then** was indefensible. His critique was strategic, demographic, geopolitical, cultural, and anti-dogmatic.

ETA’s first generally accepted killing occurred on **7 June 1968**, when Txabi Etxebarrieta shot Civil Guard traffic officer **José Antonio Pardines**. ETA killed police official and documented torturer Melitón Manzanas two months later. Txillardegi had left before both events. That chronological fact limits claims of operational responsibility; it does not erase his part in creating the organization or legitimating revolutionary counterviolence in principle.

### Literature and political work in exile, 1968–1976

He wrote *Elsa Scheelen* in Brussels and won the **Domingo/Txomin Agirre Prize** in 1968. In 1969 he received an **Andima** award in Caracas connected to mathematics teaching materials. That year he broke Belgian confinement and returned to the Northern Basque Country.

In 1970 he worked with **Telesforo Monzón** on an attempted broad nationalist front; the effort did not consolidate. He became an assistant teacher at the University of Bordeaux and settled in Bordeaux. From the beginning of the **Udako Euskal Unibertsitatea (UEU)** in 1972, he participated in the attempt to create a modern Basque-language university culture.

His family moved closer to the border, eventually spending five relatively stable years in **Hendaia**. Jone helped establish an early ikastola there. In 1975 Txillardegi completed his Paris linguistics degree. During 1976–77 he began teaching at EUTG in Donostia while his legal return was being normalized.

### Return, ESB, Chiberta, and Herri Batasuna, 1976–1980

He crossed south for the first time in 1976 after his brother obtained assurances from the civil governor. Txillardegi recalled a policeman at Irun greeting him first to Spain and then, correcting himself, to the Basque Country. He returned definitively in 1977.

With **Iñaki Aldekoa** and others he helped found or reorganize **Euskal Sozialista Biltzarrea–Partido Socialista Vasco (ESB-PSV)**. Histories place ESB’s creation in 1975–77 because it emerged through a merger and operated illegally before formal political life. Txillardegi participated in the **Chiberta Summit** and other unsuccessful attempts to create a single abertzale front in the transition.

ESB became one of the components that formed **Herri Batasuna (HB)** in 1978. Txillardegi’s recurrent project was a front placing national liberation and self-determination ahead of disputes among Basque parties. Critics regarded this as subordination of plural politics to maximalist nationalism and, in HB’s case, to ETA’s strategic influence.

He was expelled from ESB in 1980. Friendly biographical accounts attribute the break to the place of Basque within the party; ESB histories also describe factional struggle, electoral failure, debt, disagreement over HB, and the party’s collapse. These explanations are complementary rather than mutually exclusive.

### Language institutions, university, and research, 1977–2002

In the late 1970s Txillardegi co-founded or helped build **Euskal Herrian Euskaraz (EHE)**, a movement demanding the practical right to live in Basque. He participated in **Euskal Kulturaren Batzarrea (EKB)** from its creation in 1983 until it disappeared into Kontseilua in 1999.

His technical work covered phonology, accent, dialectology, mathematical sociolinguistics, language territoriality, and standard pronunciation. He attempted to model bilingual language use mathematically, later collaborating with **Xabier Isasi** on *Soziolinguistika matematikoa* (1994). These were scholarly projects, not commercial technologies or patents.

He helped found **Bat Soziolinguistika Aldizkaria** in 1989 and directed it until 2002. In 1993 he joined Euskaltzaindia’s newly formed pronunciation commission. International appearances included conferences on minority linguistic rights in Finland (1994) and European languages at Santiago de Compostela (1995).

Euskaltzaindia passed over him for full membership. Detailed pro-Txillardegi chronology says he was proposed in 1980, twice in 1987, and lost respectively to Patxi Altuna, Juan Mari Lekuona, and Ibon Sarasola. The commonly repeated claim that he was excluded “for political reasons” is plausible testimony and interpretation, not a published institutional finding. He remained a corresponding member and was made an honorary academic in 2004.

### Senator for Herri Batasuna, 1986–1992

He was elected senator for Gipuzkoa on the HB list in **1986** and again in **1989**. The second term ran from 29 October 1989 to 12 May 1992. HB’s institutional abstention meant that representation did not resemble ordinary parliamentary participation; Txillardegi wrote publicly as a senator but shared the coalition’s refusal to normalize Spanish constitutional institutions.

He was not found in the consulted sources to have been charged or convicted for ETA membership, violent crime, extortion, corruption, fraud, or money laundering during this later period. His political responsibility remains a matter of historical interpretation: HB operated in strategic proximity to ETA and was later adjudged part of ETA’s political structure, while Txillardegi’s own relationship to the armed organization had formally ended in 1967.

### Aralar, tactical opposition to armed struggle, and final candidacy, 1998–2008

After the failure of the Lizarra-Garazi process, Txillardegi argued that ETA’s renewed attacks damaged abertzale unity and blocked self-determination. Around 2000 he advocated massive, rigorous **Gandhian civil disobedience** as an alternative. He distinguished this tactical judgment from an absolute condemnation of counterviolence.

He joined **Aralar** in 2001. Aralar emerged from the abertzale left but explicitly rejected ETA’s violence. Txillardegi left in 2007. Contemporary summaries give two proximate reasons: Aralar’s alliance with Ezker Batua and its participation in a Basque Government act of solidarity with ETA’s victims. The second reason became central to later criticism of him. His departure demonstrates that his tactical rejection of armed struggle did not become acceptance of the dominant victims-memory framework.

In 2008 he appeared on **EAE-ANV’s proposed Senate list for Gipuzkoa**. The judicial suspension of the party prevented the candidacy from proceeding normally; he was not elected.

### Final years and death, 2009–2012

In his final years he continued to write, lecture, attend mobilizations for Basque-language rights and self-determination, and receive commemorations. EHE honored him after a national demonstration in Donostia in February 2011; recordings preserve the tribute and one of his final speeches.

He learned shortly before death that ETA had announced the definitive end of armed activity in October 2011. According to Pako Sudupe’s synthesis, Txillardegi responded that the Spanish government now had to abandon political-military imposition. He did not live to see ETA’s 2018 dissolution.

He died at home in Donostia around midday on **14 January 2012**, after an illness reported to have lasted almost a year. The exact diagnosis and medical cause of death were not disclosed in the sources consulted. A later family account describes his dying as a long process that prompted extensive family discussion of autonomy and death.

## Companies, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Institutions and projects

- **Family lithography workshop:** established by his grandfather in Antigua; failed during Txillardegi’s childhood. It supplied the notebook in which he made his first Basque grammar.
- **Motor factory, Donostia:** engineering employment after military service; employer not established.
- **Engineering work, Paris:** employment during early exile; company not established.
- **Collège Saint-Joseph, Hasparren:** mathematics teacher from 1962; later dismissed.
- **University of Bordeaux:** assistant teacher from 1970.
- **EUTG, Donostia:** taught phonology from 1976–77.
- **UPV/EHU:** professor from 1982; taught sociolinguistics and Basque phonology; later professor emeritus.
- **Euskal Idazkaritza:** led the language work that produced the Bayonne standardization proposals.
- **Branka:** founder/editor in Brussels, 1966–71.
- **UEU:** participant from its 1972 beginnings; honorary member in 1999.
- **EKB, EHE, Kontseilua-related language activism:** institution-building around public use of Basque.
- **Bat Soziolinguistika Aldizkaria:** co-founder and director, 1989–2002.

### Technical contribution

His most consequential “technology” was social and linguistic infrastructure: a reproducible written standard, pedagogical grammars, technical vocabulary, phonological description, and quantitative models of bilingual usage. He did not invent Euskara Batua alone, but his 1959 proposals, Bayonne organizing, and insistence on a usable standard helped transform Basque from a collection of literary/dialectal practices into a language capable of mass schooling, administration, broadcasting, science, and university instruction.

No patents or profit-seeking technology enterprises were located.

### Property and finances

The sources document a family house/workshop in Antigua, repeated rented or temporary homes in exile, the later Bidarte farmhouse at Amasa, and a final residence in Donostia. Jone’s family testimony says she often carried the household economically when Txillardegi could not work legally. ESB’s institutional history includes party debt and collapse, but no evidence located makes Txillardegi personally liable for fraud or enrichment.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Language as the nation

Txillardegi’s characteristic formula was that **language, more than race or surname, constituted the nation**. His own biography made this doctrine both emancipatory and paradoxical: a person could become Basque through learning and commitment, yet a native resident who rejected Basque nationalism could be defined as foreign or denationalized.

He was influenced by linguistic relativity and argued that language structured thought and worldview. Later scholarship has criticized some of these formulations as deterministic. Politically, he translated them into a program: territorial language rights, a common standard, education in Basque, and skepticism toward stable bilingualism. Mathematical sociolinguistics became a means to argue that formally bilingual societies tend toward unequal functional distribution unless public institutions intervene.

### From Catholic milieu to agnostic existentialism

Religion remained an intellectual wound rather than a simple abandoned identity. His early work brought Kierkegaard into dialogue with Unamuno; his novels treated God, death, guilt, meaning, and freedom without returning to conventional Catholic resolution. He published *Erlijioa hauzipean* (“Religion on trial”) in 1971 and wrote on Buddhism, Camus, Sartre, and Russell.

### Socialism without Marxist-Leninist subordination

He called for a socialist Basque state but opposed the Spanish workerist claim that class struggle superseded national liberation. He criticized both fascism and communism, defended pluralism, supported aspects of Castro’s revolution while criticizing bureaucratization, celebrated the Prague Spring, and condemned the Warsaw Pact invasion of Czechoslovakia. His politics combined anti-colonial language, socialism, civil-libertarian impulses, national exclusivity, and strategic acceptance of revolutionary violence.

### Literature

His fiction established a modern Basque subject who was urban, solitary, European, philosophically divided, and not reducible to folklore. *Leturiaren egunkari ezkutua* became a canonical rupture text. *Peru Leartzako*, *Elsa Scheelen*, *Haizeaz bestaldetik*, *Exkixu*, *Putzu*, and *Labartzari agur* extended the inquiry into commitment, exile, militancy, identity, and loss.

## Controversies, Allegations, Political Violence, and Criminal Questions

### 1. Founding ETA

**Documented fact:** Txillardegi helped create Ekin, participated in the break that produced ETA, proposed or decisively promoted the name, and shaped its early cultural doctrine.

**Documented limit:** no consulted evidence connects him personally to a killing. He left in 1967; ETA’s first generally accepted murder was in 1968.

**Historical responsibility claim:** victims’ organizations and anti-ETA writers argue that founders bear responsibility for creating the ideological and organizational project that later killed hundreds. This is an attribution of political/moral causation, not a criminal judgment against Txillardegi.

### 2. Position on armed struggle

The common statement that he “left ETA and rejected violence” compresses several positions:

- He left because of Marxist-Leninist/workerist direction and strategic differences, not because he became an absolute pacifist.
- In 1967 he regarded immediate guerrilla war as irresponsible but retained a possible future violent phase.
- Under Franco he warned that an action–repression spiral could be nationally suicidal.
- Around 1999–2001 he argued that renewed attacks and street violence damaged Lizarra-Garazi, Udalbiltza, and nationalist unity; he advocated mass civil disobedience.
- Pako Sudupe concludes that Txillardegi never unconditionally condemned ETA’s struggle, though he repeatedly criticized it and sometimes demanded that it stop.

This record supports neither “lifelong operational terrorist” nor “consistent pacifist.”

### 3. Immigrants, ethnicity, and accusations of xenophobia

Txillardegi rejected biological racism and blood/surname criteria. He substituted a language-centered, voluntarist ethnicity. Historians Gaizka Fernández Soldevilla and Raúl López Romo argue that this remained xenophobic or ethnocentric.

Their evidence includes:

- His contrast between “ethnic patriots” of the new movement and “political patriots” of the old PNV.
- ETA executive reports of 1965–66 describing Spanish immigration as denationalizing and, in one Txillardegi phrase, an effective “fifth column” against liberation.
- The claim that immigrants who adopted the national cause could become Basque, neutral residents could be tolerated, but active non-abertzale opposition could be treated as foreign collaboration.
- A 1978 polemic in *Punto y Hora* in which the Coordinadora de Emigrantes accused him of racism.

The same study explicitly states there is no evidence that ETA planned systematic expulsion or elimination of immigrants in this early doctrine. It interprets his position as a movement from biological racism to ethnolinguistic assimilationism. Sympathetic interpreters instead emphasize that language-learning opened Basque identity to anyone and that Francoist repression made demographic and linguistic fears historically intelligible. Both descriptions should be retained: the doctrine was more open than surname racism and could still deny equal national legitimacy to non-abertzale or Spanish-speaking residents.

### 4. Euskaltzaindia exclusion

Supporters say political hostility caused the Academy to reject him as a full member three times. The election results are documented; a formal record proving a coordinated political veto was not located. He was later made honorary academic. The episode fed the durable narrative of institutional erasure.

### 5. Herri Batasuna and institutional proximity to ETA

Txillardegi helped found HB, served in its national leadership environment, and represented it in the Senate. He later said he often felt that decisions were controlled by actors beyond HB’s formal national table, an allusion to KAS/ETA influence. HB and its successors were later dissolved judicially as part of ETA’s political complex. Those later rulings do not retroactively constitute a personal criminal conviction of Txillardegi.

### 6. Departure from Aralar and victims commemoration

His 2007 departure was linked in contemporary reporting both to Aralar’s electoral alliance with Ezker Batua and to its participation in an official act for ETA’s victims. COVITE and other critics use this episode to argue that later cultural tributes sanitize his refusal to recognize victims within a shared civic framework. Admirers respond by separating his linguistic and literary achievement from the politics, or by regarding state-sponsored memory as partial. The departure itself is documented; claims about inner motive beyond his stated disagreements remain interpretation.

### 7. Financial and personal criminal allegations

No substantial evidence was located of personal corruption, fraud, money laundering, extortion, private enrichment, addiction, sexual scandal, or abuse. He was arrested and imprisoned by the Franco regime for clandestine nationalist activity and accused in 1950 of Jagi-Jagi membership. No consulted source records a conviction for a violent offense.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### Myth 1: “Txillardegi invented ETA in one meeting and named it because ATA meant duck”

1. **Story:** At a Deba meeting, Aberri Ta Askatasuna was rejected because ATA meant “duck,” and Txillardegi instantly supplied Euskadi Ta Askatasuna.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** Founder recollections, press profiles, encyclopedias, and commemorative retellings from the transition onward.
3. **Evidence:** He is strongly attested as proposer of the name. The duck anecdote is repeated but rests chiefly on retrospective testimony. The foundation was a process rather than a single incorporation scene.
4. **Effect:** It makes him “the father of the initials,” concentrating ETA’s origin in an elegant linguistic anecdote ideally suited to both admiration and blame.

### Myth 2: “31 July 1959 was ETA’s exact birthday”

1. **Story:** ETA was founded on St Ignatius Day, exactly sixty-four years after the PNV.
2. **Circulation:** Anniversary culture, ETA histories, media chronologies.
3. **Evidence:** A 31 July letter provides a documented public marker. Founders also described July 1959 generally. Evidence for a single founding act on that day is weaker.
4. **Effect:** It supplies ritual symmetry and a usable anniversary.

### Myth 3: “Txillardegi single-handedly created Euskara Batua”

1. **Story:** His Bayonne plan was simply adopted, making him sole father of unified Basque.
2. **Circulation:** Posthumous tributes, documentaries, campaign for a Txillardegi library, family and activist memory.
3. **Evidence for:** He initiated and led decisive practical work; Joan Mari Torrealdai calls his authorship of the Bayonne decisions undisputed.
4. **Evidence against exclusivity:** Earlier unification efforts existed; the Bayonne group was collective; Mitxelena authored the decisive 1968 report; Euskaltzaindia and later congresses selected and elaborated the standard.
5. **Effect:** Corrects genuine underrecognition but can invert it into another simplification.

### Myth 4: “Koldo Mitxelena stole Txillardegi’s work”

This sharper hostile-to-institutions version appears in some memorial discourse. Evidence supports continuity between Bayonne and Arantzazu and the political advantage of Mitxelena’s authority while Txillardegi was exiled. It does not support plagiarism in the ordinary sense. Mitxelena’s scholarly contribution and institutional mediation were substantive.

### Myth 5: “He abandoned ETA because he opposed killing”

The timing—before ETA’s first accepted murder—encourages this hagiographic inference. His own strategic texts and later statements contradict absolute pacifism. He left over ideological direction and strategy, while retaining conditional acceptance of revolutionary counterviolence.

### Myth 6: “Founder equals perpetrator of ETA’s later 800-plus murders”

Hostile commemorative politics sometimes collapses organizational founding, ideological influence, political support, and operational perpetration. Txillardegi undeniably shares the first two categories and, in complex fashion, the third. No evidence located places him in the fourth. Treating all as identical obscures the historical record.

### Myth 7: “Politics erased him from official Basque culture”

This narrative has factual supports: failure to attain full Euskaltzaindia membership, arguments over memorial naming, and frequent attribution of Batua primarily to Mitxelena. It is not complete erasure: Euskaltzaindia made him a corresponding member in 1957 and honorary member in 2004; UPV/EHU made him emeritus; major language institutions, publishers, and public bodies repeatedly honored and archived him.

## Death and Posthumous Reputation

Txillardegi’s death produced tributes from the abertzale left, language institutions, writers, universities, and public officials. His reputation is divided across three overlapping figures:

1. **Founder of modern Basque prose** — anchored in *Leturiaren egunkari ezkutua* and the existential break with costumbrismo.
2. **Practical architect of Euskara Batua and sociolinguistic militant** — increasingly emphasized after 2012 as archives documented the Bayonne process.
3. **Founder and ideologue of ETA/HB** — invoked either as revolutionary pedigree or as a barrier to unqualified civic commemoration.

Campaigns to name a Donostia library or other public space after him repeatedly encounter this division. Supporters describe the absence of major official naming as erasure of a foundational linguist. Victims’ organizations describe honor without full political context as “whitewashing.” The documentary record supports commemoration only if all three figures remain visible.

His influence on Basque is structural rather than merely reputational. Standard Basque now underlies mass education, publishing, government, media, technology, and university work. Later digital projects—including Basque language technologies and large language models—operate in the standardized linguistic space he helped make possible, even when they do not descend technically from his research.
