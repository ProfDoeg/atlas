# Henryk Erlich Research Dossier

**Subject:** Henryk (Henrik) Erlich / Ehrlich, born Hersh-Wolf (Hersh-Volf) Erlich  
**Life:** 13 April 1882–15 May 1942  
**Principal fields:** law, journalism, Jewish labor politics, Russian and Polish social democracy  
**Prepared:** 25 August 2026  

## Scope, Method, and Evidentiary Note

This dossier reconstructs Erlich's life from biographical dictionaries, family testimony, Bundist publications and memorial literature, archival descriptions, contemporaneous press and protest material, institutional histories, and the scholarship based on the Soviet security files opened near the end of the USSR. The strongest continuous biographical source is the entry derived from the *Leksikon fun der nayer yidisher literatur*, now translated and annotated by the Congress for Jewish Culture. The principal modern corrective for the last three years is Łukasz Hirszowicz's publication of NKVD documents and Gertrud Pickhan's analysis of those files. The 1951 Yiddish memorial biography, translated and revised by Samuel A. Portnoy in 1990, remains unusually rich but is a commemorative Bundist work and predates or only indirectly anticipates the archival opening.

Several older catalogs and encyclopedias give **1881**, **1882 without a day**, or a death year of **1941**. The detailed Yiddish lexicon gives 13 April 1882; modern archival scholarship gives 15 May 1942. The Soviet government announced in February 1943 that both Erlich and Wiktor Alter had been executed. Opened NKVD records instead document Erlich's suicide in prison in May 1942 and Alter's execution in February 1943. The older paired formula—"Erlich and Alter, executed in 1941"—is therefore retained below as a major commemorative and propaganda history, not as the best reconstruction of Erlich's physical death.

### Name and dating problems

The Germanizing spelling **Ehrlich** is frequent in English, Polish, and older reference works; **Erlich** is used by his sons, the 1990 English memorial volume, YIVO's English encyclopedia, and this dossier. **Henrik Erlikh** is the standardized Yiddish transliteration. The lexicon also records **Hersh-Volf**. There is no clear evidence that this was a clandestine pseudonym rather than his Jewish given name.

Catalog records often assign both Erlich and Alter the death year 1941 because their disappearance after the joint rearrest on 4 December 1941 was long treated as their death and later became fixed in memorial bibliography. Some reference works give Erlich 1881. The day-specific lexicon entry, 13 April 1882, is the most precise accessible record; Sofia's scholarly biographical entry also exhibits the 1881/1882 discrepancy. No birth certificate was located online.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and household

Erlich was born into a prosperous Jewish family in Lublin. His father, **Moyshe (Moses) Erlich**, owned a mill. The surviving biographical notice makes the father's intellectual itinerary important: as a young man he was an ardent **Gerer Hasid**, then left Hasidism, entered the orbit of the Jewish Enlightenment, became a **Ḥovev Tsiyon** (Lover of Zion), joined **Bene Moshe**, and followed **Ahad Ha'am**. That movement—from Hasidic piety through cultural Zionism—placed several competing Jewish futures inside Erlich's childhood home. Henryk eventually rejected political Zionism but retained the language, collective life, and cultural claims of the Jewish diaspora.

The accessible sources do not securely name his mother or siblings. Claims about a wider Erlich genealogy should therefore remain unassigned unless supported by civil or communal registers.

### Marriage and the Dubnov family

Erlich met **Sofia Dubnov** in Saint Petersburg and married her in 1911. Sofia, born in Mstislavl in 1885, was the daughter of the historian and diaspora-nationalist theorist **Simon Dubnov** and Ida Friedlin Dubnov. She had studied history and philosophy, joined Russian social democracy and the Bund, wrote Russian and Yiddish poetry, criticism, translation, and educational journalism, and did not merely function as a political spouse. Her intellectual position joined socialism to Jewish cultural autonomy. Her unpublished or privately circulated memoir *Bread and Matzah* is an important family source; her study of her father later appeared in English as *The Life and Work of S. M. Dubnow: Diaspora Nationalism and Jewish History*.

Erlich and Dubnov were political interlocutors as well as son-in-law and father-in-law. In 1938 Erlich publicly answered Dubnov on Zionism and the status of the Jewish people. Their disagreement was substantive but not a family rupture: Erlich rejected the territorial-national solution while defending Jewish collective existence in the diaspora; Dubnov's autonomism helped make that position intellectually possible.

### Sons

- **Alexander Erlich** (1912–1985) became an economist, a specialist in Soviet economic history, and a professor at Columbia University.
- **Victor Erlich** (22 November 1914–29 November 2007), born in Petrograd, became a major scholar of Russian Formalism and modern Russian literature, ultimately Bensinger Professor at Yale. His memoir, *Child of a Turbulent Century* (2006), is an essential family witness. Victor and his wife Iza escaped through Lithuania and Japan, reached Canada and then the United States; he served in the U.S. Army during the war.

In 1939 Sofia and the sons escaped Warsaw toward Vilna while Henryk, following a separate Bund route, was arrested. The family never reunited. Sofia survived the war and lived until 1986. The destruction of her family was divided between the two occupying regimes: her father Simon Dubnov was murdered in Riga by the Nazis in December 1941; her husband died in an NKVD cell the following May.

### Property and finances

The mill-owning father and descriptions of Erlich's family as well-to-do establish a comfortable childhood. No reliable inventory of Henryk and Sofia's Warsaw property, income, investments, or estate was found. His adult livelihood combined legal practice, editorship, and political work. There is no evidence of business ownership, patents, speculative investments, corruption, hidden wealth, or financial crime. The absence of such material is evidentiary, not a claim that complete personal accounts survive.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1882–1902

Lublin was both a major center of traditional Jewish life and a provincial capital within the Russian imperial system. Erlich began **heder** at six. From age nine he studied privately with a Lithuanian Jewish teacher while a separate tutor taught secular subjects. His formation was therefore deliberately double: religious texts and Hebrew-Jewish learning beside the curriculum required for Russian secondary education.

In 1896 he entered the third class of a gymnasium. He graduated in 1902 with a **gold medal**, evidence of exceptional academic performance. In his final school year he entered an illegal student organization created to assist Jewish graduates who were barred or restricted by the imperial **numerus clausus** from higher education. In summer 1902 he attended a Moscow organizing meeting; at a subsequent conference of Polish sections near Warsaw he was elected chair of the Polish central committee. His first political work thus joined mutual aid, resistance to legal discrimination, and clandestine organization.

His father's Jewish nationalism initially coexisted with Erlich's growing socialist sympathies. The decisive move was not from Jewishness to assimilation but from Zionist or ethnonational remedies toward Bundist socialism, Yiddish mass politics, equal citizenship, and national-cultural autonomy where Jews already lived.

## Education and Formation, 1902–1908

Erlich entered the law faculty of the **Imperial University of Warsaw** in 1902. Soon afterward he joined a protest in the Warsaw municipal theater during a performance of *Złoty Cielak* (*The Golden Calf*), a play Jewish students regarded as antisemitic. Arrested, he received three weeks in jail.

He joined the **Bund in 1903**. In winter 1904 he was again arrested, this time after taking part in the funeral demonstration for the Bundist leader Birntsvayg. He spent roughly three months in prison and was expelled from Warsaw University. He then studied political economy and political science at the **University of Berlin**.

After the 1905 Revolution he returned to Warsaw. With **Bronisław Grosser** and others he helped edit the illegal Polish-language Bund organ *Nasze Hasło* (*Our Watchword*). In 1906–1908 he studied law at **Saint Petersburg University**, graduated, and qualified for legal work. The combination of law, political economy, clandestine journalism, repeated imprisonment, and close work with Grosser constituted his practical political education. No single teacher is documented as a personal mentor; the strongest formative figures visible in the record are Moyshe Erlich, Grosser, the Bund's collective leadership, and—after 1911—Simon and Sofia Dubnov.

## Early Career and Underground Politics, 1908–1914

After graduation Erlich settled in Warsaw and practiced law, although party activity increasingly dominated his time.

- **1909:** Imperial authorities arrested him for belonging to the Social Democratic movement. After more than three months in prison he was administratively expelled from Poland. He lived for a time in Paris.
- **1910:** Permission was granted for his return to Warsaw.
- **1911:** A prosecution was initiated against Erlich and Grosser for Bund membership. Grosser was arrested; Erlich hid in Kiev Province and then went to Munich. When the case was discontinued, Erlich moved to Saint Petersburg.
- **1911:** He met and married Sofia Dubnov.
- **1912:** He began contributing to the important Russian newspaper *Den'* (*The Day*).
- **1913:** The Bund co-opted him to its Central Committee. He became its representative on the organizing committee attempting to reconstruct the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party and in relations with the Social Democratic faction of the Fourth State Duma.
- **1913:** With Dovid Zaslavski, Beynish Mikhalevitsh, and later Yitskhok Rafes, he joined the editorial board of the new Bundist organ *Di Tsayt* (*The Times*).
- **Summer 1913:** Arrested in Moscow during an All-Russian Conference of commercial employees, he spent another approximately three months in prison.

The repeated prosecutions were political cases under the tsarist regime. They document unlawful-under-imperial-law party membership and agitation, not conventional criminality. No evidence located connects him to theft, fraud, assassination, or individual terror.

## War and Revolution, 1914–1918

During the First World War the authorities closed *Di Tsayt*. Erlich and colleagues produced the Russian-language monthly *Evreiskie vesti* (*Jewish News*). He also worked with the labor bureau of **ORT**, the organization promoting vocational training and skilled trades among Jews.

### February Revolution and the soviets

After the February/March Revolution of 1917, Erlich entered the executive committee of the Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies. At the First All-Russian Congress of Soviets in June he was elected to the All-Russian Central Executive Committee. The Bund at this stage generally occupied the democratic-socialist/Menshevik wing: it supported revolutionary democracy, opposed restoration and reaction, but resisted Bolshevik monopoly rule.

### The failed Stockholm peace initiative

Erlich joined Goldenberg, Smirnov, Rozanov, and Rusanov in a Soviet delegation intended to assemble an international socialist peace conference at Stockholm. He traveled through **Sweden, Norway, Britain, France, and Italy**. Entente governments obstructed or refused travel by their socialist representatives, and the conference never achieved its intended universal form. Erlich returned to Petrograd in early August 1917. This journey was his most extensive documented international mission before the interwar Socialist International congresses.

### Pre-Parliament and opposition to Bolshevik seizure

Following the Democratic Conference, Erlich was selected for the **Provisional Council of the Russian Republic**, usually called the Pre-Parliament. In October, with the Bundist and Menshevik factions, he withdrew from its second session amid the revolutionary crisis. He also stood for the Constituent Assembly, first on a list in Kherson Province and second in Kiev Province, but did not become a nationally prominent assembly deputy.

From November 1917 he served on the editorial board of the Menshevik central paper *Rabochaia gazeta*. His post-October position became a durable anti-Bolshevik socialist one: he rejected the one-party dictatorship while retaining a revolutionary Marxist vocabulary and organization. Soviet interrogators later treated this long public opposition as counterrevolutionary conspiracy.

### Return to Poland

In August 1918 Erlich, Sofia, Alexander, and Victor traveled to Lublin; two months later the family settled in Warsaw. This was a relocation from revolutionary Russia to the emerging Polish state, not an abandonment of Jewish or international socialist politics.

## Chronological Life History in Independent Poland, 1918–1939

### 1918–1920: municipal entry and the Polish-Soviet War

Erlich initially worked in the Warsaw workers' council. In March 1919 he headed a Bund municipal list that placed four Bundists on the **Warsaw City Council**. He remained one of the Bund's central municipal figures until the German invasion.

He edited or published the Polish-language weekly *Głos Bundu* (*Voice of the Bund*). Polish authorities confiscated issues and courts twice sentenced him as responsible editor—once to six months and once to two months—but amnesties extinguished both judgments.

During the **Polish-Soviet War of 1920**, Erlich spoke in the city council against the war. He was arrested and held for approximately three months. A prosecution premised on Bund membership continued for three years before dismissal. His opposition to the war did not entail political allegiance to Bolshevik rule; his public line was internationalist and antiwar while critical of both Polish nationalism and Soviet dictatorship.

At the Bund's 1920 Cracow unification conference he entered the Central Committee and emerged as its leading political voice. **Wiktor Alter**, also on the Central Committee from the reconstituted Polish Bund's early years, became his closest public counterpart. Their division of labor was recognizable: Erlich emphasized theory, public law, journalism, municipal politics, and international representation; Alter emphasized unions and cooperatives. Their pairing was organizational before it became martyrological.

### The press

From 1921 Erlich wrote for the Warsaw Yiddish daily *Folkstsaytung*, later *Naye Folkstsaytung* and also known as *Unzer Folkstsaytung*. He became editor and eventually editor-in-chief. He wrote almost every day, especially unsigned or topical political commentary that formulated Bund responses to rapidly changing Polish and European events. This creates a bibliographical difficulty: his actual article corpus is far larger than the small number of signed pamphlets cataloged under his name.

The newspaper was not merely a vehicle for personal opinion. It was the communications center of an integrated Bundist counterculture: trade unions, the TSYSHO secular Yiddish schools, the youth organization Tsukunft, the SKIF children's organization, the Morgnshtern sports movement, cooperatives, libraries, and cultural associations. Erlich's institutional project was political power without a state: an organized Jewish working public inside Poland.

### Municipal and communal politics

Erlich's Warsaw office operated on two intersecting planes:

1. **City government:** elected in 1919 and repeatedly prominent in the Bund's council group; municipal work focused on labor, welfare, civil equality, schooling, and opposition to antisemitic discrimination.
2. **Jewish community government:** elected to the Warsaw *kehilla* council in **1924** and **1936**. In 1924 the Bund won five of fifty seats; Erlich stood for president against Agudath Israel's Eliahu Kirshbraun. In 1936 the Bund won fifteen of fifty seats. Erlich received sixteen votes for president, Zionist historian Yitzhak Schipper ten, and Agudist Jacob Trokenheim won with nineteen.

The *kehilla* contests exposed the conflicts among secular socialist, Zionist, and Orthodox models of Jewish authority. After the 1936 election Erlich accused Zionist leaders, including Yitzhak Grünbaum and Ze'ev Jabotinsky, of aiding antisemitic agitation by accepting the premise that Polish Jews should emigrate. The accusation produced a public incident and became one of the most durable fragments of his later reputation.

### Lawyer and advocate

Erlich maintained a legal practice. He appeared in political trials and defended Jews prosecuted after resisting antisemitic violence. A complete case list has not been located, and it would be unsafe to assign him counsel roles based only on later recollection. The combination of courtroom defense and editorship, however, explains the contemporary image of him as the Bund's chief advocate in both senses of the word.

### International socialism

Erlich represented the Bund at congresses of the Socialist International in **Hamburg (1923)**, **Marseilles (1925)**, and **Vienna (1931)**. The Polish Bund had resisted joining an international body while the Bund still existed as an all-Russian organization; after Soviet suppression eliminated that organization, Erlich was central to gaining admission to the **Labour and Socialist International** in 1930–1931. Erlich and Alter then served on or represented the Bund before its Executive Committee.

His international line attempted to hold together class politics and minority autonomy. He pressed Polish socialists to recognize Jewish national-cultural rights, rejected separate Jewish statehood, and warned west European social democrats against accommodation with authoritarianism and fascism. A story repeated in recent Bund historiography has him confronting German SPD leader Otto Wels around 1931, warning that deals with industrial and conservative power would leave workers to fascism, and being laughed off. The episode is plausible and symbolically exact, but the accessible retelling does not provide minutes or a contemporary transcript; it should be cited as reported recollection rather than verbatim fact.

### The 1930s: revolutionary socialism, anti-fascism, and electoral growth

Erlich opposed Józef Piłsudski's authoritarian **Sanacja** regime, Polish nationalist antisemitism, fascist movements, Zionism, and Stalinism. His 1934 pamphlet *In kamf farn revolutsyonern sotsyalizm* argued against reformist passivity on one side and Communist dictatorship on the other. "Revolutionary socialism" in his usage meant democratic mass action, independent labor organization, and political freedoms, not Bolshevik command.

The Bund's position strengthened during the depression and the antisemitic radicalization of Polish public life. It became the dominant Jewish labor party and won major gains in the 1936 *kehilla* elections and the 1938–1939 municipal cycle. Its growth did not translate into control of the Polish state or of the Warsaw *kehilla*: electoral rules, divisions among Jewish parties, Polish socialist ambivalence over minority autonomy, and the looming destruction of the republic constrained its victories.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### From household Zionism to Bundism

Erlich inherited a house shaped by Hasidism, Haskalah, Ḥovevei Zion, Ahad Ha'am, and secular education. He did not adopt religious observance as his public program. Nor did he treat Jewishness as a private confession. His adult position was secular Jewish socialism: Jews constituted a living collectivity with a language and cultural institutions, but their emancipation required equality and socialism in their countries of residence.

### *Doikayt* and autonomy

The word later used to condense Bundism is **doikayt**—"hereness." Erlich's own writings emphasized that Jewish workers should organize where they lived rather than regard diaspora life as a waiting room for Palestine. This entailed:

- full civic and political equality;
- Yiddish cultural and educational institutions;
- national-cultural or nonterritorial autonomy;
- solidarity with non-Jewish workers through socialist parties and unions;
- opposition to clerical control of the *kehilla*;
- opposition to nationalist hierarchy, including Jewish nationalist hierarchy.

### Anti-Zionism and the Dubnov exchange

Erlich's best-known surviving essay is the October 1938 Yiddish response to Simon Dubnov, commonly translated as **"Is Zionism a Liberating Democratic Movement?"** He argued that the Bund was organically Jewish without accepting an undifferentiated *klal yisroel* that erased class and ideological conflict. He associated Zionism with the normal logic of national struggle, criticized statements by David Ben-Gurion, Yitzhak Grünbaum, and Jabotinsky that appeared to validate the antisemitic demand for Jewish departure, and predicted that a Jewish state would be organized by permanent external and internal conflict with Palestinian Arabs and by pressure against non-Hebraized Jewish languages and cultures.

The much-circulated formula that Zionism was the "Siamese twin" of antisemitism belongs to this polemical context. It is documentary, not a later paraphrase, but excerpts often omit his direct answer to Dubnov: the Bund claimed Jewish peoplehood, not Jewish disappearance. His earlier 1933 essay, generally rendered **"No, We Are Not the Chosen People!"**, applied the same universalist claim against assumptions that Jewish nationalism would be morally exempt from the dangers of other nationalisms.

### Communism and the Soviet state

Erlich's hostility to Bolshevik dictatorship was open and longstanding. The Bund itself fractured after 1917; Communist Bundists entered the Soviet system, while the Polish Bund developed as an independent democratic-socialist party. Erlich's 1934 writing attacked the Communist International's subordination of labor movements and the suppression of socialist democracy. This history was central to the NKVD case against him, although it did not demonstrate espionage or terrorism.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Erlich created no known commercial company and held no patents. His projects were political-institutional:

- **Bund press:** *Nasze Hasło*, *Di Tsayt*, *Evreiskie vesti*, *Głos Bundu*, *Folkstsaytung/Naye Folkstsaytung*, and contributions to *Den'* and Polish socialist papers.
- **Legal defense:** representation in political cases and cases arising from antisemitic violence.
- **ORT labor bureau:** vocational and employment work during the First World War.
- **Warsaw City Council:** municipal socialist representation from 1919 to the destruction of normal city politics in 1939.
- **Warsaw *kehilla* council:** Bundist attempts to secularize and democratize Jewish communal administration.
- **Labour and Socialist International:** incorporation of the Polish Bund into an international socialist structure.
- **Proposed international Jewish anti-Hitler committee (1941):** a plan developed with Alter after their release. Soviet authorities initially encouraged it but then arrested its authors. A state-controlled Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee later emerged under Solomon Mikhoels; it was not institutionally identical to the independent world committee Erlich and Alter proposed.

## War, Flight, and First NKVD Imprisonment, 1939–1941

### Departure from Warsaw and arrest

German forces invaded Poland on 1 September 1939. Following the Bund Central Committee's general advice, Erlich left Warsaw on **6–7 September** and traveled east, intending to reach Vilna for a leadership meeting. After the Soviet invasion of eastern Poland he was arrested at **Brześć nad Bugiem/Brest-Litovsk**. Sources give **4 October** and **6 October 1939**; the two-day discrepancy remains unresolved. A later Bundist account says local Jewish Communists denounced him, but a denunciation document has not been identified in the accessible archival scholarship.

After approximately two weeks in Brest prison, he was taken to Moscow's **Lubyanka**, and later held in **Butyrka**. Prison testimony describes isolation, sleep deprivation, continuous interrogation, and at least one personal interrogation by Beria. The NKVD required him to produce extensive narratives of Bund history and personnel. Pickhan treats these coerced prison manuscripts as remarkable sources—documents written under extreme duress whose factual detail must be separated from the interrogators' imposed categories.

### Charges and prosecution

The accusations shifted but included:

- anti-Soviet and counterrevolutionary organization;
- terrorism;
- collaboration with Polish authorities or intelligence;
- contacts with hostile foreign organizations;
- in the most grotesque later public form, service to Hitler or the Gestapo.

Erlich had publicly opposed Soviet dictatorship and worked within Polish and international socialist institutions. Those facts were real. The conversion of lawful party networks and polemics into terrorism or Nazi espionage was not supported by independent evidence. No public, procedurally credible trial occurred.

The transfer record dated **25 July 1941** places him in the NKVD prison at Saratov. On **2 August 1941**, a Soviet tribunal sentenced him to death. He made a long defense denying the charges. After roughly two weeks expecting execution, the sentence was commuted to ten years' imprisonment or forced labor. Some older accounts misdate the trial to 1940; the archival chronology and wartime transfer favor 1941.

### Release under the Polish-Soviet agreement

Germany's invasion of the USSR forced a diplomatic reversal. The **Sikorski–Mayski Agreement** of 30 July 1941 restored Soviet relations with the Polish government-in-exile and provided an "amnesty" for Polish citizens held in Soviet territory. The Polish embassy intervened for Erlich and Alter. Erlich was released on **11 September 1941**, Alter on 12 September.

Beria reportedly acknowledged a "fatal error" and treated their prison time as time served. Soviet officials proposed that the two internationally connected Bundists lead a world Jewish anti-fascist effort. Erlich and Alter drafted a plan and a letter to Stalin. Their concept relied on an independent global Jewish and socialist network; Soviet officials wanted an organization subject to Soviet direction. That structural conflict is the likeliest political setting for their renewed arrest, although the precise decision process remains incompletely documented.

In late September Erlich wrote to the Bund in New York that NKVD officials were suddenly solicitous because they hoped to use the men's international standing. As Moscow came under threat, the two were evacuated to **Kuibyshev** and housed under privileged conditions at the Grand Hotel or in accommodations connected with the Polish diplomatic community. Surveillance records show that their private discussions were monitored. They discussed Soviet policy, Polish prisoners, and reports concerning missing Polish officers—an issue that intersected with what later became known as the Katyn massacre.

## Rearrest, Prison, and Death, 4 December 1941–15 May 1942

On the night of **3–4 December 1941**, Erlich and Alter were summoned or seized by the NKVD in Kuibyshev and disappeared into custody. The arrest immediately followed the Soviet declaration of 1 December that inhabitants of annexed eastern Poland whom Moscow classified as Ukrainian, Belarusian, or Jewish could be treated as Soviet rather than Polish citizens. At the time, Erlich was expected in some accounts to travel with General Władysław Sikorski and possibly join Polish political work in London. Soviet authorities gave no transparent contemporaneous reason.

International labor and socialist organizations mounted a campaign. The American Federation of Labor's William Green pressed the case; appeals were associated with **Eleanor Roosevelt** and **Albert Einstein**; Bund representatives in New York and London sought information. Moscow remained silent or deceptive.

NKVD records opened decades later state that Erlich, exhausted, ill, and subjected to harsh renewed interrogation, hanged himself from the bars of his cell on **15 May 1942**. One Russian retelling dates the act to the night of 13–14 May, and another gives 14 May; the formal and most widely accepted date is 15 May. The instrument is variously described as a belt or improvised ligature. No independent autopsy, body, grave, or family-witnessed identification is known. The archival record makes suicide substantially better supported than execution, but it was a death in coercive secret-police custody after unlawful imprisonment and mistreatment.

Alter remained alive. Soviet documentation places his execution on **17 February 1943**. On **23 February 1943**, Ambassador **Maxim Litvinov** informed William Green that Erlich and Alter had been executed as enemy or Hitler agents by Soviet order. The statement erased the nine-month difference in death and converted Erlich's prison suicide into a state execution. It also supplied a Nazi-espionage accusation unsupported by a credible evidentiary proceeding.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Political Violence

### Tsarist and Polish prosecutions

Erlich repeatedly violated imperial bans on socialist organization and press activity. The factual basis—Bund membership, underground editing, demonstrations, antiwar speech—is well documented. The political character of the offenses and repeated dismissals/amnesties should not be confused with absence of illegality under the regimes involved. Conversely, no evidence links him to personal enrichment or ordinary violent crime.

### Soviet allegations

| Claim | Status | Evidentiary assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Erlich opposed Bolshevism and maintained foreign socialist contacts | Documented fact | Shown by his publications, offices, and public activity |
| He organized counterrevolutionary activity | Soviet accusation | Broadly defined from lawful anti-Bolshevik politics; no credible independent proof of a covert violent organization |
| He practiced terrorism | Formal Soviet charge | No substantiating evidence located; denied by Erlich; produced in secret-police proceedings |
| He collaborated with Polish intelligence | NKVD allegation | Political and diplomatic contacts were real; espionage was not proved |
| He was a Nazi/Gestapo agent | Soviet propaganda claim announced after his death | Contradicted by his record as an anti-fascist socialist and by the Soviet attempt to recruit him for Jewish anti-Hitler work; no credible evidence |
| He knew or discussed the Soviet killing of Polish officers | Monitored-conversation evidence indicates awareness of rumors | Knowledge of or discussion about missing Polish officers is not participation in espionage; details depend on NKVD surveillance records |

### Anti-Zionist polemics

Erlich's descriptions of Jabotinsky, Zionism, and Jewish nationalism were deliberately severe. Zionists viewed the Bund's refusal of mass emigration and territorial statehood, particularly after Hitler's rise, as dangerously impractical; Bundists argued that evacuation could not solve the condition of millions and that equal citizenship could not be surrendered to antisemites. His 1938 prediction about a Jewish state's conflict with Arabs has given the essay a renewed political afterlife. Modern circulation often removes the interwar Polish setting, the family debate with Dubnov, or the Bund's affirmative program. The text is authentic; claims that it straightforwardly predicts every later Israeli development are interpretations.

### Responsibility and limits

Erlich was the chief political voice of a party that failed to prevent catastrophe, did not win control of Jewish communal institutions, and could not reconcile Jewish organizational autonomy with the PPS on stable terms. These are documented political failures, not evidence of personal fraud. The record contains no substantiated scandal concerning sexual misconduct, addiction, corruption, money laundering, or private financial abuse.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### 1. "Erlich and Alter were shot together in December 1941"

**Story:** The two leaders were executed immediately or soon after their joint disappearance.  
**Appearance and circulation:** Bundists, socialist newspapers, wartime ghetto notices, later memorial books, monuments, and reference catalogs repeated variants. Soviet silence made a December 1941 death a reasonable inference; Litvinov's February 1943 announcement asserted a joint execution without truthful dates.  
**Evidence:** NKVD records document Erlich's suicide on 15 May 1942 and Alter's execution on 17 February 1943.  
**Effect:** It created the indivisible martyr-name **Erlich–Alter**, preserved their case when facts were inaccessible, and also obscured the distinct mechanisms of their deaths.

### 2. "Both were Hitler agents"

**Story:** Soviet authorities lawfully eliminated spies working for Germany.  
**Circulation:** Soviet diplomatic communications and pro-Soviet defenses in 1943; the allegation was repeated amid wartime alliance politics.  
**Evidence:** Their long anti-fascist records, the Soviet proposal that they organize Jewish anti-Nazi mobilization, the absence of a credible open trial, and later rehabilitation all weigh against it.  
**Effect:** The charge divided parts of the international left, forced socialists to choose between Soviet wartime loyalty and solidarity with imprisoned comrades, and furnished critics of Stalinism with a paradigmatic case of political murder.

### 3. "The fathers of the Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee"

**Story:** Erlich and Alter originated the Soviet Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee and were killed after creating it.  
**Evidence:** They did propose an international Jewish anti-Hitler committee after their September 1941 release and negotiated with Beria. The later state-controlled Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee led by Solomon Mikhoels had related mobilizing aims but a different institutional form and leadership.  
**Effect:** The story correctly restores their initiative but can overstate direct organizational continuity. It also connects their fate to the later Soviet destruction of Mikhoels and the Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee.

### 4. The prophetic anti-Zionist

**Story:** Erlich foresaw that a Jewish state would exist in permanent war with Arabs, subordinate Arabs, and suppress diaspora Jewish culture.  
**Origin:** His genuine 1938 response to Dubnov, with an allied 1933 essay against Jewish exceptionalism.  
**Evidence:** The words and argument are authentic in the Yiddish publication and Portnoy translation. Calling them "prophecy" is retrospective interpretation.  
**Effect:** Since the late twentieth century, Erlich has been recovered by diaspora socialists and anti-Zionists; quotations circulate far beyond Bund history, sometimes without his democratic-Marxist and Yiddish-autonomist framework.

### 5. "Murdered by the NKVD" versus suicide

**Story:** Descriptions frequently say Erlich was murdered, shot, or executed by the NKVD.  
**Evidence:** The immediate cause documented in the files is self-inflicted hanging. The surrounding facts are secret detention, fabricated capital charges, abusive interrogation, and state concealment.  
**Effect:** "Murdered" functions as a political-causal description of a custodial death, while "suicide" states the physical mechanism. A precise account must preserve both levels without substituting one for the other.

### 6. The inseparable pair

Erlich is "always named beside Alter" because they shared the Polish Bund Central Committee, international representation, arrest, release, anti-fascist proposal, rearrest, global defense campaign, Soviet defamation, rehabilitation, and monuments. Yet the pairing compresses differences: Erlich was eight years older, a lawyer-theorist-editor formed in the Russian revolutionary underground and Petrograd Soviet; Alter was an engineer and organizer especially associated with unions and cooperatives. Their deaths were neither simultaneous nor identical.

## Works by Henryk Erlich

### Books and pamphlets securely attributed

1. **_*In kamf farn revolutsyonern sotsyalizm*_** (אין קאַמף פֿאַרן רעוואָלוציאָנערן סאָציאַליזם; *In the Struggle for Revolutionary Socialism*). Warsaw: Naye Folkstsaytung, 1934. 104 pp. A programmatic collection or pamphlet of political argument. Digitized by the Yiddish Book Center.
2. **_*The Struggle for Revolutionary Socialism*_**. New York: Bund Club of New York, 1934. English translation by Haim Kantorovitch and Anna Bercowitz, 62 pp. Translation of the preceding work; not a separately composed English book.
3. **_*Der iker fun Bundizm*_** (דער עיקר פֿון בונדיזם; *The Essence of Bundism*). New York: Bundisher Klub, 1935. Cataloged at 16 or 18 pages depending on whether wrappers are counted. Digitized by the Yiddish Book Center.
4. **_*Der “Forverts” un der “Bund”*_** (דער „פֿאָרווערטס“ און דער „בונד“; *The Forward and the Bund*). New York: Bundisher Klub, 1935. Cataloged as 48 or 50 pages. Jointly associated in cataloging with Raphael R. Abramovitch; the exact division of authorship/editorial responsibility should be checked against the title page. Digitized by the Yiddish Book Center.
5. **_*Manifest fun “Bund” tsu di yidishe arbetndike masn in Poyln*_** (*Manifesto of the Bund to the Jewish Working Masses in Poland*). Warsaw, 1937, 21 pp. An official party manifesto rather than safely classifiable as Erlich's sole-authored book; contemporary Bund sources associate him with its formulation. Reportedly circulated in more than 100,000 copies.

### Major essays and speeches identifiable by title or context

- **"Neyn, mir zaynen nisht dos oysderveylte folk!" / "No, We Are Not the Chosen People!"** (1933). Essay against Jewish nationalist exceptionalism. Exact original issue and pagination require periodical-level verification.
- **"Is Zionism a Liberating Democratic Movement? A Reply to Professor Simon Dubnow"**. Originally in Yiddish in *Di Tsukunft*, October 1938. English translation by Samuel A. Portnoy appears in *Henryk Erlich and Victor Alter*, pp. 253–264 and is reproduced online by Libcom.
- **Speech against the Polish-Soviet War**, Warsaw City Council, 1920. The speech occasion and prosecution are documented; a complete authoritative transcript was not located.
- Speeches and interventions at Labour and Socialist International congresses and executive meetings, 1923–1930s. Minutes and socialist press reports should be consulted for a complete corpus.
- **Letter/project memorandum to Stalin on an international Jewish anti-Hitler committee**, 1941, jointly developed with Wiktor Alter. Archival/documentary rather than originally published work.
- **Prison histories and statements about the Bund**, 1939–1941. Composed under NKVD interrogation. These are genuine texts by Erlich in the physical sense but coerced documents, not voluntary publications; Pickhan analyzes their evidentiary character.

### Periodical corpus

Erlich wrote, edited, or participated in:

- *Nasze Hasło* (illegal Bundist Polish organ), after 1905;
- *Den'* (Russian daily), from 1912;
- *Di Tsayt* (Yiddish Bundist organ), editorial board from 1913;
- *Evreiskie vesti* (Russian monthly), during World War I;
- *Rabochaia gazeta* (Menshevik central organ), editorial board from November 1917;
- *Głos Bundu* (Polish weekly), Warsaw after 1918;
- *Folkstsaytung / Naye Folkstsaytung / Unzer Folkstsaytung* (Warsaw Yiddish daily), contributor from 1921 and later editor-in-chief;
- Polish socialist press and international Bund publications.

A complete article bibliography is not presently reconstructable from online catalogs. Many newspaper leaders were unsigned; Warsaw and YIVO holdings would require issue-by-issue attribution. The 1951 *Henrik Erlikh un Viktor Alter* contains a section of selected Erlich writings, "Fun Henrik Erlikhs pen," and a bibliography at pp. 459–472.

### Posthumous collections

- **_*Henrik Erlikh un Viktor Alter*_**. New York: Farlag Unzer Tsayt, 1951, 472 pp. (Yiddish Book Center scan: 494 digital pages). Compiled by Victor Shulman with an editorial committee. This is a memorial biography/document collection, not a jointly authored book by its two subjects; it includes selected genuine writings.
- **_*Henryk Erlich and Victor Alter: Two Heroes and Martyrs for Jewish Socialism*_**. Hoboken: KTAV / New York: Jewish Labor Bund, 1990, viii + 386 pp. Revised English translation by Samuel A. Portnoy of the Yiddish memorial volume. ISBN 0-88125-357-X.

## Death, Disclosure, and Rehabilitation

For nine months after Erlich's death the USSR disclosed nothing. On 23 February 1943 Litvinov gave the false paired-execution account to William Green. Because Alter had in fact been shot only six days earlier, the announcement joined a current execution to a concealed earlier custodial death. International socialist protest followed, but wartime alliance with the USSR limited governmental consequences.

News reached occupied Poland in fragmentary form. In the Warsaw and Vilna ghettos, underground notices and diary entries preserved the paired names **"Henryk Wiktor."** This was both a coded personal memorial and the political condensation of the two missing leaders. The full mechanism of death remained inaccessible for decades.

On **8 February 1991**, Erlich's son Victor was informed that the men had been **rehabilitated** and the repression declared unlawful. The rehabilitation nullified the Soviet criminal case; it did not recover a body or settle every detail of prison treatment. Subsequent publication of NKVD documents corrected the dates and mechanisms of death.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Bundist martyr and institutional memory

The international Bund made Erlich and Alter its central paired martyrs: democratic socialists destroyed by Stalin after escaping Hitler's invasion. Memorial books appeared in Buenos Aires in 1943 and New York in 1951. Their names helped define the postwar Bund's simultaneous opposition to fascism, Stalinism, and Zionist state-centered politics.

### Warsaw monument

A symbolic monument/cenotaph to Erlich and Alter was erected in the Jewish cemetery on Okopowa Street in Warsaw and dedicated on **17 April 1988**. The inscription used the then-standard formula that both had been executed in the Soviet Union. Communist Polish authorities had obstructed open commemoration; **Marek Edelman**, Bundists, and Solidarity-linked activists enabled it. More than 3,000 people reportedly attended. The monument was thus simultaneously a Jewish-socialist memorial, a recovery of suppressed Polish history, and an anti-Stalinist civic demonstration.

### New York and transnational memory

Memorial references and plaques in New York's Bundist milieu, including the Cooperative Village/Lower East Side world, extended the pairing into the diaspora institutions that published their work. Montreal's 1982 memorial recording documents the same living Yiddish commemorative culture.

### Intellectual afterlife

Erlich's most durable concepts are:

- socialism with political pluralism rather than one-party dictatorship;
- Jewish collective life without territorial sovereignty;
- Yiddish culture as a modern public culture rather than residue;
- minority rights secured through democratic institutions and transnational labor solidarity;
- the warning that nationalist movements reproduce the coercive patterns they attribute only to enemies.

His limitations are equally part of the record. Bundist power was concentrated in urban Jewish working life and could not compel the Polish state to guarantee equality. Its nonterritorial solution confronted regimes destroying the very civic arena it required. It also underestimated the degree to which physical flight, rather than political transformation in place, would become the sole survival route for many individuals. These historical outcomes generated later Zionist claims that Bundism was futile; postwar Bundists answered that Nazi genocide could not retrospectively validate nationalism or abolish the meaning of the culture destroyed.

### Recent reinterpretation

The opening of Soviet archives shifted Erlich from an undifferentiated "executed in 1941" martyr into a specifically documented victim of coercive detention who died by suicide in May 1942. At the same time, contemporary debates about Israel/Palestine and diaspora Jewish identity have moved his 1933 and 1938 anti-Zionist writings back into circulation. Some presentations make him a solitary prophet; historically he was the chief theoretician of a large movement and wrote within a collective platform. Others reduce him to a Soviet victim; that erases four decades as a lawyer, editor, municipal politician, Russian revolutionary democrat, and organizer of Jewish working-class institutions.
