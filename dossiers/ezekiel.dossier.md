# Ezekiel son of Buzi: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Limits

Ezekiel son of Buzi is a sixth-century BCE Judahite priest and prophet whose name and career are preserved principally in the biblical Book of Ezekiel. No presently known contemporary inscription, Babylonian administrative tablet, royal chronicle, or archaeological artifact names Ezekiel individually.

The reconstruction must therefore distinguish four evidentiary levels:

- **Textual self-presentation:** what the Book of Ezekiel says about its protagonist.
- **Historically corroborated context:** events or social conditions independently supported by Babylonian records and archaeology.
- **Historical inference:** conclusions scholars draw from the book’s language, dates, and setting.
- **Later tradition, legend, or reception:** rabbinic, Christian, Islamic, pilgrimage, mystical, artistic, or popular claims that do not constitute contemporary biographical evidence.

The atlas dates “c. 623–after 571 BCE” are possible but not secure. The birth estimate depends on interpreting Ezekiel 1:1’s unexplained “thirtieth year” as Ezekiel’s age. The text’s latest dated oracle falls in 571/570 BCE, establishing only that the literary prophetic career—or its chronological framework—extends that late.

## Historical Setting

Ezekiel’s career is embedded in the terminal crisis of the kingdom of Judah.

Babylonian king Nebuchadnezzar II captured Jerusalem in 597 BCE, removed King Jehoiachin, installed a replacement ruler, and carried tribute and part of Judah’s court, military, craft, and administrative population to Babylonia. The Babylonian Chronicle BM 21946 independently records the capture of “the city of Judah” and its king on 2 Adar, conventionally calculated as 16 March 597 BCE. It does not name Ezekiel, but it corroborates the deportation in which the book implicitly places him. The object is catalogued by the [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1896-0409-51).

Zedekiah, installed by Babylon, later rebelled. Nebuchadnezzar’s forces besieged Jerusalem, destroyed the city and temple in 587/586 BCE, deposed Zedekiah, and imposed a more extensive deportation. Ezekiel’s pre-destruction preaching predicts this catastrophe; the later part of the book assumes that it has occurred.

Cuneiform documentation from the sixth and fifth centuries BCE, especially the Āl-Yāhūdu tablets, independently establishes that Judahite deportees and their descendants lived in Babylonian rural communities, leased or cultivated royal land, paid taxes, entered contracts, lent and borrowed goods or silver, served as witnesses, and retained Yahwistic personal names. These records do not identify Tel Abib or Ezekiel, but they make the book’s broad social geography credible. Tero Alstola’s documentary study is available through the [University of Helsinki](https://tuhat.helsinki.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/129018053/2020_Alstola_Judeans_in_Babylonia.pdf).

The book locates Ezekiel beside the “river” Kebar. This was probably a navigable irrigation canal rather than a natural river. An Akkadian waterway called *nāru kabari* is documented in tablets from the Nippur region. Whether the biblical Kebar is exactly the same canal remains likely but not demonstrable.

Tel Abib itself is unidentified. The name may reflect Akkadian *til abūbi*, “mound of the flood/deluge,” rather than the later Hebrew reinterpretation “hill of spring.” It has no demonstrated relationship to the modern Israeli city of Tel Aviv except through later literary naming.

## Family and Ancestry

### Buzi

Ezekiel 1:3 identifies him as “Ezekiel the priest, son of Buzi.” Nothing else securely historical is known about Buzi. The biblical text does not provide Buzi’s ancestry, office, residence, deeds, or dates.

Calling Ezekiel “the priest” may grammatically designate either Ezekiel himself or, less probably, his father. Most interpreters understand Ezekiel as the priest. Because priesthood was hereditary, Buzi would ordinarily be presumed to belong to a priestly family, but this is an inference.

### Claimed priestly lineage

Ezekiel’s repeated concern with sanctuary boundaries, sacrificial regulation, ritual impurity, priestly conduct, Zadokite status, measurements, and graded holiness strongly supports priestly education or identity. Some scholars place him specifically within a Zadokite tradition. The book never supplies a complete genealogy linking him to Zadok or Aaron, however.

### Rabbinic ancestry

**Later tradition:** Megillah 14b associates Ezekiel and Jeremiah with descent from Joshua and the convert Rahab. This is theological genealogy rather than contemporary documentation.

**Later tradition:** Some rabbinic statements make Buzi a name or epithet for Jeremiah, explaining it from a root associated with contempt: Jeremiah was supposedly “despised” or shamed. Under this construction Ezekiel becomes Jeremiah’s son. This conflicts with the plain presentation of Buzi and Jeremiah as differently named persons and lacks early historical corroboration.

### Wife

Ezekiel 24:15–24 reports the death of Ezekiel’s unnamed wife, described as “the delight of your eyes.” He says that she died in the evening after he had addressed the people in the morning. He was commanded not to perform customary public mourning rites; his restrained behavior became a sign of the population’s coming inability to mourn openly for the destroyed sanctuary and their dead.

This is the sole explicit information about her. No name, ancestry, marriage date, occupation, children, or grave is supplied.

The passage may preserve a personal bereavement subsequently incorporated into prophetic performance. Alternatively, scholars treating the book as heavily literary or pseudobiographical regard even this episode as a constructed sign-act. There is no independent confirmation.

### Siblings, children, and descendants

None are identified in the Book of Ezekiel or in reliable contemporary sources. Claims assigning descendants to him belong to later legend.

## Childhood and Early Life

No source describes Ezekiel’s childhood.

If the “thirtieth year” of Ezekiel 1:1 means that he was thirty years old in 593 BCE, he was born around 623 BCE, during Josiah’s reign and near the conventional date of Josiah’s religious reform. That reconstruction would place his youth during:

- Assyria’s decline;
- Josiah’s centralizing religious program;
- Josiah’s death at Megiddo in 609 BCE;
- Egyptian domination of Judah;
- Babylonian ascendancy after Carchemish in 605 BCE;
- the political instability that ended in the deportation of 597 BCE.

This sequence is historically plausible as formative background, but the book does not say that Ezekiel witnessed specific events before his deportation.

Other explanations of “the thirtieth year” include an otherwise unknown era, a regnal calculation, an editorial remnant, or a scribal dating system. Consequently, neither thirty as his age nor c. 623 BCE as his birth year can be treated as certain.

## Education and Formation

No teachers or schools are named. His education must be reconstructed from the book attributed to him.

### Priesthood and temple knowledge

The text displays detailed acquaintance with:

- Jerusalem’s temple precincts and entrances;
- altar and sacrificial practices;
- ritual purity and pollution;
- distinctions among sacred and ordinary space;
- priestly dress and behavior;
- Zadokite claims;
- calendrical and festival regulations;
- land allotment and sanctuary administration.

This suggests formation within a literate priestly environment. Whether Ezekiel had actually officiated in the Jerusalem temple before 597 BCE is unknown. If he was only about twenty-five at deportation, later priestly age conventions might imply that he had not yet begun full service.

### Israelite literary traditions

Ezekiel’s language shows engagement with legal, prophetic, historical, lament, priestly, and mythic traditions. Particularly important are:

- covenant and curse traditions;
- holiness and purity systems related to Levitical materials;
- Exodus and wilderness narratives;
- traditions concerning Noah, Daniel/Danel, and Job;
- royal and Davidic imagery;
- Eden, cherubim, cosmic mountain, and temple symbolism;
- prophetic predecessors, especially themes also found in Hosea, Isaiah, and Jeremiah.

Direct literary dependence, common oral tradition, and later editorial cross-pollination cannot always be separated.

### Babylonian intellectual environment

The book contains imagery and knowledge compatible with Babylonia:

- the divine chariot’s composite beings resemble aspects of Mesopotamian guardian figures and iconography;
- precise calendrical notices resemble scribal practice;
- extensive measurements and architectural planning suit learned administrative culture;
- descriptions of trade, nations, precious materials, and imperial geography reflect international knowledge;
- loanwords or culturally embedded vocabulary point toward Mesopotamian surroundings.

Michael Jursa and other scholars have argued that the book’s author or scribal tradents knew Babylonian learned and administrative culture. The formulation “Babylonian intellectual” is an analytical reconstruction, not an attested job title. A useful synthesis is [Jonathan Stökl and Michael Jursa’s discussion](https://www.thetorah.com/article/ezekiel-a-jewish-priest-and-a-babylonian-intellectual).

## Deportation and Migration

### Jerusalem or Judah to Babylonia, 597 BCE

**Historically plausible textual inference:** Ezekiel’s dates are reckoned from Jehoiachin’s exile, and he is already among deportees beside the Kebar by 593 BCE. He was therefore probably deported in 597 BCE with Jehoiachin and the Jerusalem elite.

The Book of Ezekiel never narrates his capture, the route east, detention, or arrival. A likely imperial route would have led north through Syria and then southeast along the Euphrates, but assigning that itinerary to Ezekiel personally would be conjectural.

### Nature of the exile

The older image of Judeans living in prison-like mass captivity is misleading. Ezekiel had a house, received elders, and lived within a community possessing internal leadership. Babylonian archives show deportees farming crown land and participating in ordinary legal-economic activity, although within coercive imperial resettlement and service obligations.

Ezekiel’s personal income, landholding, debts, taxes, or wealth are not recorded. Possession or occupancy of a house does not prove affluence or legal ownership.

### Travels after deportation

No physical journey outside the Babylonian settlement is securely recorded. Journeys to Jerusalem in Ezekiel 8–11 and to the valley of dry bones in chapter 37 are explicitly visionary transports. They should not be entered as ordinary travel.

No evidence shows that he returned to Judah after Cyrus conquered Babylon in 539 BCE. If born around 623 BCE he could conceivably have lived to see that event, but nothing establishes that he did.

## Chronological Life History

### Before 597 BCE: probable life in Judah

The earliest reconstructable stage is a probable priestly upbringing in Judah, perhaps Jerusalem. The detail of the temple visions is consistent with personal knowledge, though a later author or editor could also possess inherited plans and traditions.

No employment record, initiation, property, patron, teacher, political office, or public prophecy survives from this phase.

### 597 BCE: deportation under Nebuchadnezzar II

Nebuchadnezzar captured Jerusalem and removed Jehoiachin. Ezekiel appears to belong to this deportee community. The exile removed him from the Jerusalem sanctuary around which priestly identity had been organized.

This dislocation is central to his subsequent religious development: the opening vision relocates divine glory to Babylonia, demonstrating that the deity is not geographically confined to Jerusalem.

### 593 BCE: inaugural vision and commission

In the fifth year of Jehoiachin’s exile, conventionally 593 BCE, Ezekiel says that the heavens opened beside the Kebar and he saw “visions of God.”

The vision includes:

- a storm cloud, fire, and radiance;
- four living creatures, each with multiple faces and wings;
- intersecting wheels described as “a wheel within a wheel”;
- eyes or eye-like structures around the rims;
- a crystal-like expanse;
- a throne-like structure;
- a humanlike radiant figure representing divine glory.

Ezekiel falls on his face. He is addressed as *ben-ʾadam*, “mortal,” commissioned to confront a “rebellious house,” and made to eat a written scroll containing lamentation and woe. He is then brought to the deportees at Tel Abib, where he sits stunned for seven days.

The seven-day interval resembles priestly consecration and mourning periods, though its exact meaning is debated.

He receives the “watchman” commission: failure to warn the wicked makes him accountable for their blood, while warning discharges his responsibility. A related commission reappears after Jerusalem’s fall in chapter 33.

### 593–592 BCE: confinement, muteness, and sign-acts

The text reports that Ezekiel would be bound, confined in his house, and rendered unable to speak except when given a divine oracle. Interpretations include:

- literal involuntary confinement by the community;
- ritual withdrawal;
- symbolic description of restricted prophetic speech;
- a narrative mechanism controlling when he speaks;
- symptoms retrospectively characterized by some physicians as catatonia or another disorder.

No diagnosis is possible from an ancient literary text.

Ezekiel stages or narrates a sequence of sign-acts:

1. He inscribes Jerusalem on a brick and constructs a model siege around it.
2. He lies on his left and right sides for specified periods, bearing symbolic years of Israel’s and Judah’s guilt.
3. He eats rationed food cooked as under siege conditions. He protests an instruction to use human excrement as fuel on purity grounds and is permitted cow dung instead.
4. He shaves his head and beard with a sword, dividing and dispersing the hair to symbolize deaths by plague, famine, sword, and exile.
5. He packs an exile’s baggage, breaks through a wall, and departs with face covered, signifying Zedekiah’s attempted escape and capture.
6. He trembles while eating and drinking, embodying fear during devastation.
7. He refuses conventional mourning after his wife’s death.

Whether these were all publicly enacted, privately enacted, visionary, or literarily constructed remains disputed.

### 592 BCE: vision of Jerusalem’s corruptions and departing glory

While elders sit in his house, Ezekiel reports visionary transport to Jerusalem. He sees:

- an “image of jealousy” in the temple;
- elders performing illicit rites;
- women mourning Tammuz;
- men worshiping the sun;
- executioners marking or killing inhabitants;
- divine glory moving from the cherubim through the temple’s thresholds and ultimately departing the city.

The vision links the temple’s destruction to accumulated ritual, social, and political offenses. It also solves a theological problem of exile: Jerusalem can fall because divine glory has abandoned it, not because Babylon’s god defeated an immobilized national deity.

### 591–589 BCE: consultations by elders

Community elders repeatedly come to Ezekiel’s house. This indicates that he possessed recognized or at least sought-after authority. The book also portrays the audience as treating him like an admired performer whose words they enjoy but do not obey.

Names attached to the community include Jaazaniah son of Shaphan and Pelatiah son of Benaiah in the Jerusalem vision, though these are not necessarily personal acquaintances in ordinary space. Pelatiah dies within the vision, causing Ezekiel to intercede.

Ezekiel rejects claims that exile has severed the deportees from the deity. He calls the deity a temporary or “little sanctuary” among them, while challenging people who remained in Jerusalem and claimed exclusive possession of the land.

### 589/588 BCE: announcement of Jerusalem’s final siege

Ezekiel dates an oracle to the day on which the Babylonian siege of Jerusalem began. He uses the image of a corroded cooking pot to describe the city.

The same chapter reports his wife’s death. He performs no public lament, using the event as a sign of the shock that will follow destruction of the temple, “the delight of your eyes.”

The ethical and biographical implications are severe: the narrative subordinates an unnamed woman’s death and the husband’s mourning to prophetic symbolism. Whether the prohibition and event are historical, literary, or theological cannot be independently resolved.

### 587/586 BCE: Jerusalem destroyed

A fugitive eventually arrives with the report, “The city has fallen.” Before the messenger’s arrival, Ezekiel’s mouth is opened; the imposed muteness ends.

The fall changes the book’s dominant orientation. Earlier warnings of unavoidable destruction give way to restoration, communal reconstitution, and renewed land and sanctuary.

### Oracles against foreign nations

Ezekiel issues oracles against Ammon, Moab, Edom, Philistia, Tyre, Sidon, and Egypt. They reflect imperial geopolitics and Judah’s failed search for alliances.

The extended Tyre cycle depicts the city as:

- a merchant ship carrying the products of an international trade network;
- a beautiful but doomed maritime center;
- a ruler elevated in pride;
- a cherubic being in Eden in the lament over the king.

The passages later contributed to Christian representations of Satan’s primordial fall, although their immediate literary targets are Tyre and its ruler.

### Tyre, Nebuchadnezzar, and the disputed failed prophecy

Ezekiel 26 predicts that Nebuchadnezzar will destroy Tyre, breach its walls, kill its population, and take its wealth. Nebuchadnezzar besieged Tyre for approximately thirteen years but apparently did not plunder and erase the island city as described.

Ezekiel 29:17–20, dated to 571/570 BCE, says Nebuchadnezzar’s army labored against Tyre until heads were bald and shoulders rubbed raw, yet neither king nor army obtained wages. Egypt is therefore promised as compensation.

Evidentiary classifications:

- **Documented textual fact:** Ezekiel 26 predicts Babylonian devastation and plunder.
- **Documented textual fact:** Ezekiel 29 acknowledges that Nebuchadnezzar obtained no material reward from Tyre and redirects compensation toward Egypt.
- **Probable historical fact:** Babylon’s long siege ended through accommodation or submission rather than the complete annihilation described.
- **Critical scholarly judgment:** many interpreters regard this as an unfulfilled prophecy revised by a later oracle.
- **Alternative interpretation:** some scholars and confessional interpreters treat the destruction language as conventional hyperbole, apply portions to mainland settlements or later conquerors, or understand the prophecy as fulfilled through Tyre’s loss of political-economic dominance.
- **Unresolved point:** evidence for Nebuchadnezzar’s later Egyptian operations is fragmentary and does not establish the total conquest, forty-year desolation, or population dispersal described in Ezekiel 29–30.

A recent accessible discussion is [“When prophecy doesn’t come true”](https://www.pthu.nl/en/bibleblog/2024/10/when-prophecy-doesnt-come-true/). A confessional attempt to read the language non-literally appears in [Cody Baker’s 2024 dissertation](https://repository.sbts.edu/handle/10392/7456).

### Restoration program

Following Jerusalem’s destruction, Ezekiel develops a program including:

- replacement of exploitative “shepherds” by divine care and a future Davidic shepherd;
- purification and a “new heart” and “new spirit”;
- collective restoration represented by dry bones;
- reunion of Judah and Joseph/Ephraim as two sticks made one;
- defeat of Gog of Magog;
- a measured temple and reordered land;
- strict sanctuary access;
- elevated status for Zadokite priests;
- a life-giving river flowing from the temple;
- a renamed city: “YHWH is there.”

The dry-bones vision explicitly interprets the bones as “the whole house of Israel,” whose national hopes have died. It originally functions principally as restoration metaphor. Second Temple and rabbinic readers increasingly interpreted it as bodily resurrection; some traditions treated it as an event that had literally occurred.

### 573/572 BCE: vision of the measured temple

In the twenty-fifth year of exile, fourteen years after Jerusalem fell, Ezekiel is transported in a vision to a high mountain. A supernatural guide measures gates, courts, chambers, altar, and sanctuary.

Divine glory returns through the east gate, reversing its earlier departure. Temple, royal land, priestly territory, city, and tribal allotments are reconfigured to prevent earlier forms of contamination and exploitation.

No known historical temple was built exactly according to this design. Interpretations include:

- a practical blueprint for restoration;
- an ideal constitution;
- scribal-priestly utopia;
- symbolic cosmic sanctuary;
- program for limiting monarchy and protecting sacred space;
- later eschatological or millennial temple.

### 571/570 BCE: last dated oracle

The oracle concerning compensation for Nebuchadnezzar is the latest date explicitly given. Ezekiel’s activities afterward, the length of his life, and the circumstances of his death are unknown.

## Employment, Institutions, Property, and Finances

### Profession

Ezekiel is identified as a priest and functions as a prophet, visionary, teacher, watchman, performer of signs, interpreter of catastrophe, and designer or transmitter of a sanctuary program.

There is no documentary record of:

- temple salary;
- sacrificial income;
- Babylonian administrative employment;
- agricultural tenancy;
- commercial activity;
- investments;
- slaves or servants;
- debts;
- tax payments;
- inherited property;
- patents, inventions, or business organizations.

### House and community status

Ezekiel possesses or occupies a house where elders assemble. This suggests a settled deportee household and recognized public role. It cannot establish exact property rights or wealth.

### Political position

He held no known formal Babylonian or Judahite office. His political theology generally treats Babylon as an instrument of divine judgment and condemns Judah’s rebellion, diplomatic dependence on Egypt, royal oath-breaking, elite violence, land seizure, and corrupt leadership.

This stance aligned pragmatically with submission to Babylon but does not prove employment as a Babylonian propagandist, collaborator, or intelligence asset. No evidence supports intelligence-service or paid-patronage theories.

## Religious and Intellectual Development

### From Jerusalem-centered priest to mobile-glory theologian

The opening vision makes divine glory mobile. The deity appears in Babylonia on a wheeled throne, later abandons Jerusalem, and eventually returns to a purified temple. The sequence relocates sacred presence without discarding the temple as an ideal.

### Responsibility and repentance

Ezekiel rejects the proverb that children’s teeth are set on edge because their parents ate sour grapes. Chapter 18 asserts that each person is accountable for his or her conduct and may turn and live.

This is not modern individualism: Ezekiel also employs corporate history, inherited patterns, collective punishment, and national restoration. The book contains tension between individual responsibility and intergenerational communal judgment.

### Israel’s history as rebellion

Chapters 16, 20, and 23 retell Israel’s history as persistent rebellion beginning in Egypt or infancy. This differs sharply from idealized national origin stories.

Chapter 20 even states that the deity gave the people “statutes that were not good” after rebellion—a notoriously difficult passage variously interpreted as:

- punitive surrender to destructive practices;
- reference to child sacrifice;
- polemic against rival law traditions;
- rhetorical shock;
- later editorial intervention.

### Monarchy and the “prince”

The restored order has a *nasiʾ*, usually translated “prince,” not an unrestricted king. His land and offerings are regulated; he may not dispossess the people. This appears designed to prevent royal abuses associated with the former monarchy.

### Priesthood and exclusion

Ezekiel 40–48 privileges the descendants of Zadok and demotes other Levites for past cultic service. It also bars foreigners described as “uncircumcised in heart and flesh” from the sanctuary.

These rules constitute an exclusivist priestly settlement, though the land vision also grants resident aliens inheritance among Israelite tribes in Ezekiel 47:22–23. The book therefore contains both restrictive sanctuary policy and an unusual provision for integrating resident foreigners into landholding Israel.

### Violence and purification

Divine judgment is expressed through siege, famine, plague, sword, exposure, mutilation, burning, and mass death. The text treats catastrophe as deserved purification, while also recording Ezekiel’s protests when complete destruction appears imminent.

Modern trauma-oriented scholarship interprets the book as an attempt to construct meaning after forced migration, political collapse, atrocity, and cultural humiliation. That framework may illuminate the rhetoric but does not clinically diagnose the author or neutralize the violence.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Buzi

Father or ancestral identifier. Nothing beyond the patronymic is securely known.

### Unnamed wife

The only documented intimate relationship in the literary biography. Her narrative presence is confined to her death and symbolic function.

### Jehoiachin

Ezekiel dates his career by Jehoiachin’s deportation and appears to share his exile. The book does not record a meeting. Continued dating by Jehoiachin may recognize his legitimacy or simply employ the deportee community’s chronological system.

### Zedekiah

Target of hostile prophecy as the oath-breaking ruler installed by Babylon. No face-to-face meeting is recorded.

### Nebuchadnezzar II

Imperial conqueror responsible for the deportation setting. Ezekiel calls him the agent through whom judgment falls on Jerusalem and foreign states. No personal meeting is reported.

### Deportee elders

They visit Ezekiel’s house and request oracles. Their names are generally not supplied in these scenes. They constitute his clearest institutional audience.

### Fellow deportees at Tel Abib

The book alternately portrays them as rebellious, resistant, curious, and attracted to Ezekiel’s performances. They are his principal known audience.

### Jerusalem prophets and priests

Ezekiel attacks unnamed rival prophets, diviners, priests, princes, and elders. Chapter 13 condemns male and female prophetic practitioners for false visions and manipulative rites. Specific historically verifiable rivalries cannot be reconstructed.

### Jeremiah

Jeremiah is not on the supplied atlas roster, but historically he is Ezekiel’s most important prophetic contemporary. Their books share themes—Babylonian domination, corrupt shepherds, individual responsibility, restoration, and new covenantal transformation—without recording a meeting or correspondence.

### Daniel/Danel

Ezekiel 14 and 28 name “Daniel” alongside Noah and Job as a model of righteousness and wisdom. Many scholars identify this figure not with the later biblical Daniel but with the ancient wise hero Danel known from Ugaritic tradition. The identification is disputed.

## Controversies, Allegations, Criticism, and Adversarial Findings

A dedicated search combining Ezekiel’s name with scandal, accusations, fraud, crime, conspiracy, lawsuit, corruption, and recent controversies found no credible evidence of personal financial crime, litigation, sexual misconduct, official investigation, espionage, fraud, or criminal prosecution. That absence reflects the nature and age of the evidence, not an affirmative proof about every action of the historical person.

The principal controversies concern the text attributed to him.

### Authorship and historical existence

Traditional interpretation treats most or all of the book as Ezekiel’s words, organized by the prophet or disciples. Modern positions range across a spectrum:

1. A substantially authentic first-person record expanded by disciples.
2. A sixth-century Ezekielian core subjected to extensive scribal supplementation.
3. A literary persona constructed by an exilic or early Persian-period school.
4. More radical theories relocating substantial composition to Jerusalem or later periods.

Reasons supporting a historical sixth-century core include:

- the dense sequence of dates;
- knowledge of events between 597 and 571 BCE;
- accurate Babylonian setting and vocabulary;
- the unusual admission concerning Nebuchadnezzar’s lack of spoil from Tyre;
- coherent priestly personality and recurring style.

Reasons for positing editorial growth include:

- duplicate commissions and judgments;
- abrupt changes of addressee and chronology;
- explanatory expansions;
- tension between passages;
- differences among the Masoretic Text, Old Greek, and other witnesses;
- signs that later scribes systematized or reapplied Ezekielian material.

No scholarly method can recover a transcript of Ezekiel’s speech or assign every verse confidently to one historical individual. The 2025 study [“The Composition of the Book of Ezekiel in Light of Babylonian Sources”](https://www.degruyter.com/document/doi/10.1515/zaw-2025-2007/html) argues from archival practices that the book’s composition should be assessed in a Babylonian scribal context.

### Sexualized and gendered violence

Ezekiel 16 and 23 portray Jerusalem and Samaria as sexualized women punished for political and religious “prostitution.” The punishments include stripping, exposure, mutilation, stoning, and killing.

Evidentiary distinctions:

- **Documented textual fact:** the chapters use explicit sexual imagery and describe gendered violence.
- **Critical interpretation:** feminist scholars have called this rhetoric misogynistic, pornographic, “pornoprophetic,” victim-blaming, or analogous to domestic and sexual violence.
- **Counter-interpretation:** other scholars argue that the obscenity is a deliberate shock strategy directed at male political-religious audiences and that the women are national personifications rather than actual female victims.
- **Continuing problem:** allegorical intent does not erase the text’s use of women’s bodies and sexual punishment as vehicles for public shame.

Important treatments include [Athalya Brenner’s “Pornoprophetics Revisited”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/030908929602107006), [Bryan Bibb’s translation study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/0034637314554382), [David Zucker and Moshe Reiss on Ezekiel and misogyny](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146107916664053), and [Rhiannon Graybill’s 2024 analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00209643241244451).

### Ethnic invective

Ezekiel’s oracles celebrate or command devastating violence against neighboring peoples. Edom, Ammon, Moab, Philistia, Tyre, Sidon, and Egypt are assigned collective guilt and punishment. These passages have been read as wartime rhetoric, imperial interpretation, theological retaliation, or identity formation by a traumatized minority.

There is no evidence that Ezekiel personally commanded troops or carried out violence.

### Child sacrifice and collective culpability

The book accuses Jerusalem of sacrificing children and shedding innocent blood. Archaeological and textual evidence confirms that child sacrifice was a real subject of ancient Levantine religious controversy, but no independent source verifies Ezekiel’s particular accusations against named individuals.

Chapter 20’s “statutes that were not good” has produced accusations that its deity mandated child sacrifice. The Hebrew remains debated; interpretations range from divine punitive abandonment to a polemic against laws or practices Ezekiel rejected.

### Alleged mental illness

Ezekiel’s visions, muteness, confinement, paralysis-like behavior, food practices, grief restrictions, and elaborate sign-acts have prompted retrospective diagnoses including schizophrenia, temporal-lobe epilepsy, catatonia, psychosis, dissociation, and post-traumatic disorder.

- **Documented fact:** the text reports unusual perceptual experiences and behavior.
- **Medical hypothesis:** Edwin Broome and later authors proposed psychiatric explanations.
- **Counterassessment:** other clinicians find ecstatic or culturally structured visionary behavior without evidence adequate for a modern diagnosis.
- **Methodological limit:** the source is an ancient edited theological text; duration, voluntary control, functional impairment, medical history, and direct examination are unavailable.

The dispute is summarized in [George Stein’s psychiatric review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-psychiatrist/article/psychiatry-in-scripture-sacred-texts-and-psychopathology/398A762532FF0A8FE67EE27EC2DE2C7D) and [“Did Ezekiel have catatonia?”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/did-ezekiel-have-catatonia/75116B97B36F0EB5292389DCD3CAABED).

### Prophetic failure

The Tyre and Egypt predictions are the clearest historically testable controversy. The text itself admits that Babylon received no spoil from Tyre. No surviving evidence demonstrates Egypt’s complete conquest and forty-year desolation.

Calling Ezekiel a conscious fraud goes beyond the evidence. An unfulfilled prediction, conventional ancient war rhetoric, editorial preservation, and theological reinterpretation are different claims. There is no evidence he fabricated a prophecy for financial gain.

### The unrealized temple and restoration

The measured temple was not built as described, the land was not redistributed into the ideal parallel tribal bands, and Gog’s invasion did not occur in an identifiable near-term event. Later readers variously spiritualized, postponed, or eschatologized these promises.

Historically, this is evidence that the program was ideal or unrealized—not evidence of a completed architectural project.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### Ezekiel resurrected literal dead bodies

**Story:** The dry bones were actual dead persons whom Ezekiel restored to life; some traditions say they stood, sang, returned to Judah, married, and had children.

**Appearance and circulation:** Second Temple interpretation, Pseudo-Ezekiel, and rabbinic debate transformed the national metaphor into a resurrection narrative. Talmudic sages disagreed over whether the event was literal or “a true parable.”

**Evidence:** Ezekiel 37 explicitly identifies the bones as “the whole house of Israel,” favoring collective metaphor in its immediate literary setting. Later resurrection belief supplied a literalizing frame.

**Reputational effect:** Ezekiel became a major scriptural authority for Jewish and Christian belief in resurrection. See the historical survey at [TheTorah.com](https://www.thetorah.com/article/the-valley-of-dry-bones-and-the-resurrection-of-the-dead).

### Ezekiel was murdered by a Judahite prince

**Story:** A leader of the exiles killed Ezekiel because the prophet rebuked him for idolatry; Ezekiel was then buried in the field of Maour or in the tomb associated with Shem and Arphaxad.

**Appearance:** The pseudepigraphic *Lives of the Prophets*, in forms transmitted in late antiquity and later Christian manuscripts.

**Evidence:** No contemporary or biblical support. The story follows a hagiographic martyrdom pattern.

**Effect:** It supplied a dramatic end absent from the canonical book and supported the image of the persecuted prophet.

### Ezekiel’s tomb at al-Kifl

**Story:** Ezekiel was buried at al-Kifl, south of Hillah in present-day Iraq. A shrine, synagogue, inscriptions, associated graves, and pilgrimage complex developed around the claimed tomb.

**Earliest firm attestation:** Medieval Jewish and Islamic geographical and travel traditions. Benjamin of Tudela in the twelfth century gives an extensive account; Pethahiah of Regensburg also describes pilgrimage. The surviving architecture is medieval and later, not sixth-century BCE proof.

**Circulators:** Babylonian Jews, medieval travelers, Muslim communities identifying the site with Dhū al-Kifl, shrine custodians, Iraqi historians, and modern heritage organizations.

**Evidence for:** Very old regional tradition; continuous sacred geography; Jewish inscriptions and documented pilgrimage.

**Evidence against identification as the actual grave:** no excavated, securely dated burial inscription naming Ezekiel; a gap of more than a millennium before detailed testimony; overlapping and competing Muslim identifications.

**Effect:** The site became one of Iraqi Jewry’s most important pilgrimage centers. Medieval accounts describe enormous festival gatherings, commerce, charity, and supernatural protection. Modern changes to the site have generated disputes over preservation of Jewish inscriptions and its characterization as synagogue, mosque, or shared shrine.

Benjamin’s translated account is available in [*The Itinerary of Benjamin of Tudela*](https://wpd.ugr.es/~proyecto_viajeros/recursos/textos/judios/tudela_adler_1907.pdf).

### Miraculous gate and inviolable shrine

**Story:** The tomb’s small gateway expanded miraculously to admit heavily laden camels; assailants or rulers who threatened the shrine were punished; neither Muslim nor Jewish attendants would be harmed even during disturbances.

**Appearance:** Medieval pilgrimage literature, especially traditions collected around Benjamin and Pethahiah.

**Evidence:** Folklore and hagiography; no independent demonstration.

**Effect:** These stories established the shrine as a protected zone and enhanced its economic and intercommunal importance.

### Ezekiel as Dhū al-Kifl

**Story:** The Qur’anic righteous figure Dhū al-Kifl, mentioned in Qur’an 21:85–86 and 38:48, is Ezekiel.

**Evidence:** The Qur’an does not call Dhū al-Kifl Ezekiel and supplies too little biography for identification. Muslim commentators have proposed Ezekiel, Job’s son Bishr, Elijah, Joshua, Zechariah, or an otherwise unknown righteous man.

**Status:** Later identification, common but unproved.

**Effect:** It allowed the al-Kifl shrine to operate as overlapping Jewish-Muslim sacred geography. A recent survey of competing identifications appears in the [Integrated Encyclopedia of the Qur’an](https://iequran.com/pdf/v3_toc/ieq_v3_dhul_kifl.pdf).

### The chariot was an extraterrestrial spacecraft

**Story:** Ezekiel witnessed a technologically advanced aircraft or alien vehicle. Some versions interpret living creatures as astronauts, wheels as rotors, and fire as propulsion.

**Origins and circulation:** Modern UFO literature, including Morris K. Jessup’s *UFO and the Bible* (1956), Erich von Däniken’s ancient-astronaut writing, and Josef F. Blumrich’s *The Spaceships of Ezekiel* (1974). Television programs and internet videos continue the claim.

**Evidence offered:** literalized mechanical readings of “wheels within wheels,” movement in multiple directions, fire, sound, and ascent.

**Evidence against:** the imagery belongs to ancient Near Eastern throne, storm-theophany, cherub, temple, and divine-glory traditions; the narrative calls the object “the appearance of the likeness of the glory” of Israel’s deity; no artifact or external record establishes machinery.

**Status:** Modern pseudohistorical or speculative interpretation, not an archaeological finding.

**Effect:** It made Ezekiel a central figure in ancient-astronaut culture and inspired technical illustrations. Blumrich’s engineering diagrams do not authenticate an ancient spacecraft.

### “Wheel within a wheel” as a mechanical invention

Ezekiel did not file or receive a patent. Modern omnidirectional-wheel designs have sometimes been associated in popular accounts with attempts to reconstruct his vision. Such modern engineering reception does not make Ezekiel an inventor of a vehicle.

### Pulp Fiction’s “Ezekiel 25:17”

**Story:** Jules Winnfield’s execution speech in Quentin Tarantino’s *Pulp Fiction* is frequently treated as a quotation of Ezekiel 25:17.

**Evidence:** Only its closing language about vengeance and recognition of the Lord resembles the verse. Most of the speech derives from an embellished film tradition, especially the English opening of the Sonny Chiba vehicle *The Bodyguard*, with language assembled from broader biblical motifs.

**Status:** Misquotation or fictional adaptation.

**Effect:** For many modern audiences this invented passage is Ezekiel’s most recognizable supposed quotation. The transformation has even produced later public misquotations of the film as scripture.

## Works by or Attributed to Ezekiel

### The Book of Ezekiel — ספר יחזקאל (*Sefer Yeḥezqel*)

- **Original language:** predominantly Biblical Hebrew, with features associated with late biblical, priestly, and Babylonian contexts.
- **Traditional attribution:** Ezekiel son of Buzi.
- **Historical-critical attribution:** a substantial sixth-century Ezekielian corpus, often understood as preserved, expanded, organized, and edited by one or more scribal hands; the scale and dating of redaction remain disputed.
- **Original publication date:** not recoverable in modern terms. Oracles are dated between 593 and 571/570 BCE; formation of the extant book may have continued into the exilic or early Persian period.
- **Structure:**
  - Chapters 1–3: inaugural vision and commission.
  - Chapters 4–24: judgment on Jerusalem and Judah.
  - Chapters 25–32: oracles against nations.
  - Chapters 33–39: renewed commission and restoration.
  - Chapters 40–48: temple, cult, land, and city vision.
- **Forms:** visions, prose reports, prophetic speeches, laments, allegories, riddles, legal disputations, historical retrospectives, sign-acts, temple plans, and dated notices.
- **Textual witnesses:** Masoretic Hebrew; Old Greek/Septuagint; fragments among the Dead Sea Scrolls; Peshitta; Targum; Vulgate; later manuscripts.
- **Textual problem:** the Old Greek is shorter in a number of places and may reflect a Hebrew edition different from or earlier than the Masoretic form. Papyrus 967 preserves important ordering and textual differences. See [The Oxford Handbook’s textual overview](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/35418/chapter-abstract/303164850).

### Individual oracles and performances

The book attributes all major visions, speeches, laments, and symbolic actions to Ezekiel, but none survives as a separately authenticated autograph, tablet, scroll, or contemporaneous transcript.

### *Apocryphon of Ezekiel* / *Pseudo-Ezekiel*

- Jewish works or fragments extending Ezekiel-related traditions, including resurrection material.
- Not genuine works demonstrably authored by the historical prophet.
- Survive through quotations and Qumran fragments.
- Best classified as pseudepigraphic reception.

### *Lives of the Prophets: Life of Ezekiel*

A post-biblical biographical legend, not written or dictated by Ezekiel.

### “Ezekiel’s tragedy” by Ezekiel the Tragedian

The Hellenistic Jewish dramatist Ezekiel the Tragedian was a different person, probably active in the second century BCE. His Greek play *Exagōgē* concerns Moses and must not be attributed to Ezekiel son of Buzi.

## Films, Documentaries, Recordings, Art, and Archival Material

### Film and television

- *Pulp Fiction* (1994), directed by Quentin Tarantino. Its fabricated or composite “Ezekiel 25:17” speech is a major modern reception phenomenon, not a faithful quotation.
- *The Bodyguard* / *Karate Kiba* (1976), associated with the earlier cinematic form of the pseudo-Ezekiel speech later adapted in *Pulp Fiction*.
- *Ancient Aliens* and numerous UFO documentaries have presented the chariot vision as possible spacecraft evidence. These are popular speculative productions, not historical documentation.
- Various Bible-film and television anthologies dramatize the dry bones, the chariot, or Jerusalem’s fall, but there is no single historically authoritative cinematic biography of Ezekiel.

### Music and oral culture

- The African American spiritual “Ezekiel Saw the Wheel,” documented in multiple twentieth-century arrangements and recordings, transforms the throne vision into communal religious song.
- “Dem Bones” or “Dry Bones,” commonly credited in its popular form to James Weldon Johnson’s brother J. Rosamond Johnson, draws on Ezekiel 37.
- The phrase “dry bones” became a recurring idiom in preaching, gospel, political oratory, and civil-rights discourse.
- Leonard Cohen’s “The Story of Isaac” and many other modern works participate in broader prophetic reception but should not automatically be classified as direct Ezekiel adaptations without explicit evidence.

### Visual art

Important visual traditions include:

- synagogue and Christian manuscript depictions of the chariot;
- medieval illuminations of Ezekiel’s visions;
- Raphael’s *The Vision of Ezekiel* (c. 1518);
- William Blake’s prophetic imagery and illustrations engaging Ezekiel;
- Marc Chagall’s biblical treatments of Ezekiel and the dry bones;
- modern architectural reconstructions of the visionary temple;
- technical spacecraft diagrams inspired by Josef Blumrich.

### Archaeological and archival objects

- Babylonian Chronicle BM 21946, British Museum: independent evidence for Jerusalem’s capture in 597 BCE.
- Āl-Yāhūdu tablets, dispersed among collections: economic and legal evidence for Judean communities in Babylonia.
- Murašû archive tablets from Nippur: later evidence for the canal-based agrarian-commercial environment.
- Dead Sea Scroll fragments of Ezekiel and Pseudo-Ezekiel: evidence for textual transmission and resurrection reception.
- Papyrus 967: important Greek witness to a differing edition/order of Ezekiel.
- Medieval and modern photographs, inscriptions, Torah installations, and pilgrimage records from al-Kifl.

## Death and Burial

The canonical book does not narrate Ezekiel’s death.

The last dated oracle is placed in the twenty-seventh year of Jehoiachin’s exile, around March/April 571 or 570 BCE. This supplies the only defensible lower boundary for his life if the date is authentic.

Competing end-of-life traditions include:

- natural death in Babylonia;
- murder by an exiled prince whom he reproved;
- burial at al-Kifl;
- burial near Baghdad or other Mesopotamian locations;
- alternative shrines in northern Iraq or elsewhere.

None can be confirmed archaeologically. The al-Kifl identification is the most historically influential, not the most demonstrable.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Formation of the biblical canon

Ezekiel became the third of the Latter Prophets in the Jewish ordering and one of Christianity’s major prophets. Rabbinic tradition attributes the writing or final transmission of his book to the Men of the Great Assembly, a theological-scribal attribution rather than a verifiable publication record.

A rabbinic story says Hananiah ben Hezekiah used three hundred jars of oil while reconciling Ezekiel’s temple laws with the Torah, thereby preventing the book from being withdrawn or suppressed. The story records ancient awareness that Ezekiel’s sacrificial and legal rules differ from Pentateuchal norms.

### Merkabah mysticism

The throne-chariot vision became the foundation of *Maʿaseh Merkabah*, “the Work of the Chariot,” one of the central subjects of early Jewish esoteric speculation. Rabbinic texts restrict public exposition of the chariot because of its danger and complexity. Later Hekhalot and merkabah literature developed heavenly ascent, throne-palace, angelic, and liturgical systems around related imagery.

Maimonides later allegorized the chariot philosophically, treating it as encoded metaphysics and cosmology.

### Resurrection

The dry-bones vision helped form Jewish and Christian language for bodily resurrection, despite its immediate national interpretation. It influenced:

- Pseudo-Ezekiel;
- rabbinic resurrection debate;
- Christian Easter vigil readings;
- funerary art;
- spirituals and sermons;
- political rhetoric concerning collective revival.

### Christianity

The Book of Revelation extensively reuses Ezekiel:

- throne and living creatures;
- scroll eating;
- measuring;
- Gog and Magog;
- divine presence;
- river and trees of life;
- renewed city.

The dependence is literary and theological, not a personal relationship between Ezekiel and Revelation’s author. A detailed recent study is [Laszlo Gallusz and Peter-Ben Smit’s reception analysis](https://wp.ces.org.tw/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Dik-John-Reception-and-Transformation-of-Ezekiels-Throne-Vison-in-the-Apocalypse-of-John.pdf).

Christian traditions also reread:

- Ezekiel’s humanlike enthroned figure christologically;
- the closed eastern gate of the temple as a Marian symbol;
- the shepherd and “David” passages messianically;
- cleansing water and new spirit sacramentally;
- Tyre’s cherub lament as Satan’s fall;
- the temple eschatologically.

### Judaism

Ezekiel contributed enduring concepts of:

- divine presence in exile;
- individual repentance;
- restored Israel;
- resurrection;
- chariot mysticism;
- priestly and temple ideals;
- Gog and Magog;
- the future reunification of Israel.

The prophetic reading for Shavuot includes the chariot vision in many traditions, while chapter 37 is read in connection with Passover in some rites.

### Islam and Iraqi sacred geography

Ezekiel is respected in Islamic historiography under forms of Ḥizqīl and often associated with Dhū al-Kifl. The al-Kifl tomb became a shared and contested shrine. Jewish mass emigration from Iraq in the mid-twentieth century transformed its custodial and ritual life; restoration and Islamization claims after 2003 generated controversy over erased, concealed, restored, or replaced Hebrew inscriptions.

The shrine remains evidence of Ezekiel’s place in Iraqi Jewish memory and Muslim sacred geography, but not proof of the burial.

### Political and cultural afterlives

Ezekiel’s imagery has been used in:

- Jewish national restoration;
- Christian Zionist temple expectations;
- apocalyptic interpretations of Russia, modern states, or military alliances as Gog and Magog;
- African American religious and liberation culture;
- sermons about personal renewal;
- nationalist and war rhetoric;
- environmental readings of the life-giving river;
- disability and trauma interpretation;
- feminist criticism;
- UFO and ancient-astronaut culture.

These applications frequently detach images from their sixth-century context.

## Atlas Connections

Only supported intersections with people on the supplied roster are included.

### Nabucodonosor II — Nebuchadnezzar II

- **Nature:** Imperial conqueror responsible for the 597 BCE deportation setting; named repeatedly as the instrument of Jerusalem’s destruction and as attacker of Tyre and Egypt.
- **Dates and places:** Jerusalem and Babylonia, 597–571 BCE context.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented historical-context connection; textual political connection.**
- No personal meeting is recorded.

### David ben Jesse

- **Nature:** Ezekiel invokes David as the archetype or name of the future shepherd-prince who will reunify and govern restored Israel.
- **Dates and places:** Literary influence in exilic Babylonia, especially Ezekiel 34 and 37.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented literary-symbolic influence; no personal connection.**

### King Solomon

- **Nature:** Ezekiel’s priestly formation presupposes the Jerusalem temple traditionally associated with Solomon; his visionary temple replaces and transforms that institution.
- **Dates and places:** Jerusalem temple tradition; Babylonian vision c. 573/572 BCE.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented institutional-literary relationship; no personal connection.**

### Moses

- **Nature:** Ezekiel engages Torah traditions concerning Exodus, wilderness rebellion, covenant, purity, priesthood, land, sacrifice, and sanctuary. Scholars dispute whether he used written Pentateuchal texts, common priestly traditions, or materials later edited in both corpora.
- **Dates and places:** Intellectual formation in Judah and Babylonia.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented textual influence/traditional relationship; no historical meeting.**

### Aaron the Levite

- **Nature:** Ezekiel’s priestly identity and sanctuary law operate within an Israelite hereditary-priesthood tradition associated with Aaron, although Ezekiel particularly privileges Zadokites.
- **Evidence label:** **Traditional institutional connection; no recoverable genealogical proof or meeting.**

### Abraham

- **Nature:** Ezekiel 33:24 cites the remaining population’s appeal to Abraham—“Abraham was one, yet he possessed the land”—and rejects their inference that they are therefore entitled to it.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented literary reference; no personal connection.**

### Noah son of Lamech

- **Nature:** Ezekiel 14 names Noah with Danel/Daniel and Job as paradigms whose righteousness could save only themselves amid judgment.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented literary-symbolic reference; no personal connection.**

### Flavius Josephus

- **Nature:** Josephus later recounts Ezekiel’s prophecy and says he left “two books,” an enigmatic testimony in *Antiquities* 10.
- **Dates and places:** First century CE literary reception.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented posthumous reception; no personal connection.**

### Maimonides

- **Nature:** Maimonides interpreted Ezekiel’s chariot vision as esoteric philosophical-cosmological teaching in *The Guide of the Perplexed*.
- **Dates and places:** Twelfth century CE, Egypt.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented posthumous intellectual influence.**

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **Nature:** Christian scholarship has proposed Ezekielian backgrounds for Jesus traditions concerning “son of man,” shepherd imagery, water and spirit, temple, and resurrection. “Son of man” in Ezekiel is an ordinary divine address to a mortal and is not automatically identical to the later Gospel title.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented shared scriptural tradition; specific direct influence varies from probable to disputed.**

### Paul the Apostle

- **Nature:** Scholars sometimes connect Paul’s temple, spirit, new-heart, and resurrection language with Ezekiel, often through the broader Jewish scriptural tradition.
- **Evidence label:** **Literary influence theory; no explicit personal acknowledgment and no personal connection.**

### William Blake

- **Nature:** Blake explicitly engaged biblical prophets, including Ezekiel, in his art and prophetic poetics. In *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell*, the narrator famously converses imaginatively with Isaiah and Ezekiel.
- **Dates and places:** Britain, late eighteenth to early nineteenth century.
- **Evidence label:** **Documented posthumous artistic influence and literary mythmaking.**

### Remaining roster

No source consulted supports a personal meeting, correspondence, kinship, patronage, employment, rivalry, or collaboration between the historical Ezekiel and other named members of the supplied roster. Mere contemporaneity—especially with Cyrus the Great—or later reuse of generic prophetic imagery has not been counted as a connection.
