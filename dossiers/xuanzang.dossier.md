# Xuanzang: Research Dossier

## Scope and source criticism

Xuanzang was a seventh-century Chinese Buddhist monk, translator, scholar, diplomat, traveler, and author associated with the transmission of Indian Buddhist literature to Tang China. This dossier concerns the historical Xuanzang—identified in Chinese sources as a member of the Chen family, commonly called Chen Hui or Chen Yi before ordination—not the fictionalized “Tang Monk” of *Journey to the West*.

The principal narrative sources are:

1. Xuanzang’s *Great Tang Record of the Western Regions* (*Da Tang Xiyu ji*), completed in 646 from his reports and compiled in writing by Bianji.
2. Huili and Yancong’s *Biography of the Tripiṭaka Master of the Great Ci’en Monastery*, completed in its surviving form around 688.
3. Daoxuan’s shorter biography in the *Continued Biographies of Eminent Monks*.
4. The early funerary biography or “account of conduct” attributed to Mingxiang.
5. Tang imperial correspondence, prefaces, memorials, translation colophons, catalogues, and inscriptions preserved in Buddhist and historical collections.

These sources are unusually rich but not neutral. Xuanzang’s biography is a Buddhist hagiography written by disciples. The *Western Regions* was produced for Emperor Taizong and addressed Tang political, geographical, and religious interests. Modern scholarship cautions that it combines observation, textual learning, oral reports, sacred geography, political argument, and literary conventions. Statements below are therefore labeled where necessary as documented fact, contemporary report, hagiographic report, disputed reconstruction, or legend.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Religious name:** Xuanzang, Chinese 玄奘; modern Mandarin Xuánzàng.
- **Common older romanizations:** Hsüan-tsang, Hsuan-tsang, Yuan Chwang, Yuan Chuang, Hiouen-thsang, Hiuen Tsiang, Hiuen Tsang.
- **Lay surname:** Chen, 陳.
- **Lay personal name:** Usually given as Yi, written 禕 or 褘 in different textual traditions; modern popular accounts also call him Chen Hui. The precise original graph and pronunciation remain disputed because the forms may reflect textual corruption.
- **Indian honorifics:** Mokṣadeva, “Deva of Liberation,” and Mahāyānadeva, “Deva of the Mahāyāna,” reportedly bestowed by Indian Buddhist communities representing different doctrinal affiliations.
- **Chinese titles:** Sanzang fashi, 三藏法師, “Tripiṭaka Master” or “Master of the Three Collections”; Datang Sanzang, “Tripiṭaka Master of the Great Tang”; “Master of the Law.”
- **Popular and fictional identity:** Tang Sanzang, Tang Seng, or the “Tang Monk,” the scripture pilgrim subsequently transformed into the protagonist of *Journey to the West*.
- **Posthumous title:** Dabianjue, 大遍覺, conventionally translated “Great Universal Enlightenment,” attributed to later imperial commemoration.
- **Birth:** Traditionally 602, sometimes 600 or dates between approximately 596 and 602.
- **Death:** Fifth or sixth day of the second lunar month in the first Linde year, commonly converted as 7 March 664; another calculation gives shortly after midnight on 8 March.
- **Birthplace:** Chenhe/Chenbao Valley near Goushi County, Luozhou, in the vicinity of modern Yanshi District, Luoyang, Henan. Some English publications loosely give Chenliu or Luoyang.
- **Deathplace:** Yuhua Monastery, converted from the former Yuhua Palace, in present-day Tongchuan, Shaanxi.
- **Professions and functions:** Buddhist monk, lecturer, debater, translator, translation-project director, compiler-author, geographer, diplomatic intermediary, and adviser to the Tang court.
- **Religious affiliation:** Mahāyāna Buddhist, particularly the Yogācāra tradition transmitted through Śīlabhadra and the Dharmapāla lineage; also extensively trained in Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma, Buddhist logic, Madhyamaka, Vinaya, and non-Buddhist Indian systems.

### Birth-date problem

**Disputed reconstruction.** The date 602, sometimes specified as 6 April, is traditional and now standard. Li Rongxi’s English translation of the medieval biography uses approximately 600–664. Passages reporting that Xuanzang described himself as sixty-five shortly before death have led some Chinese scholars to propose a birth between 596 and 600. East Asian age reckoning, copying errors, and inconsistent biographical chronologies prevent certainty. “602–664” remains the conventional identification, not an independently documented civil registration.

## Family and Ancestry

### Ancestry

**Hagiographic and genealogical report.** Huili’s biography presents Xuanzang as descended from an educated and official family. The lineage is traced to Chen Shi, 陳寔, a celebrated Eastern Han official and moral exemplar. Such genealogies established status and virtue, and the exact descent cannot now be independently demonstrated.

Xuanzang’s great-grandfather and grandfather were reportedly officeholders. His grandfather Chen Kang was associated with the Northern Qi court and held an academic or instructional appointment. These reports place the family among the provincial scholarly elite rather than among merchants or peasants.

### Parents

- **Father:** Chen Hui, 陳惠. The biography describes him as tall, handsome, learned in the Confucian classics, and formerly an official. He reportedly withdrew from service amid the disorder and dynastic transition at the end of the Sui, preferring private study.
- **Mother:** Traditionally identified as a woman of the Song family of Guangping, reportedly the daughter of the Sui official Song Qin. Her personal name is not securely preserved.

Descriptions of the father’s physical appearance and cultivated retirement are conventional components of elite biography as well as possible family memory.

### Siblings

Xuanzang was reportedly the youngest of four sons.

His most consequential sibling was an elder brother who became the monk Changjie, 長捷. Changjie entered Jingtu Monastery at Luoyang and brought the young Xuanzang into the monastic environment. The names and careers of the other brothers are poorly documented in accessible sources.

### Marriage, partners, and children

No spouse, sexual partner, or child is documented. Xuanzang entered Buddhist monastic life while young and is presented in all early sources as celibate. Later fiction creates emotional and occasionally erotic temptations around the Tang Monk, but these are literary episodes, not biographical evidence.

### Wealth and property

There is no evidence that Xuanzang accumulated private commercial wealth. His later career depended on imperial funding, monasteries, scribes, paper, residences, transport, and a large translation staff. These resources belonged to court and monastic institutions rather than to a personal business estate. Hagiographic accounts describe him refusing valuable gifts or redirecting them toward religious purposes, but exact personal finances cannot be reconstructed.

## Childhood and Early Life, c. 602–618

Xuanzang grew up during the final years of the Sui dynasty, a period marked by costly military campaigns, political disintegration, rebellion, and the establishment of the Tang in 618.

**Hagiographic report.** He is portrayed as an exceptionally grave and studious child, uninterested in ordinary play. He studied Confucian works under his father and allegedly imitated ritual conduct described in the classics. These childhood-prodigy motifs may preserve genuine early education but also conform to the literary construction of eminent monks.

After his father’s death—whose date is uncertain—Xuanzang followed Changjie to Jingtu Monastery in Luoyang. There he heard Buddhist lectures and studied scriptures before he was legally old enough for full ordination.

During a state-sponsored examination for admission to the Buddhist clergy, officials reportedly objected that he was underage. The supervising official Zheng Shanguo was impressed by his answer that he intended to perpetuate the Buddha’s teaching and glorify it in the future. Xuanzang was allowed to enter the clergy as an exception. The incident is preserved in his disciple’s biography; the dialogue may be literary reconstruction.

He became a novice at approximately thirteen. The exact year depends on the disputed birth date, usually placed around 612 or 613.

## Education and Formation in China

### Luoyang

At Jingtu Monastery, Xuanzang studied foundational Mahāyāna and Abhidharma texts. Teachers named in the biographical tradition include:

- **Jingtu:** associated with instruction in the *Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra*.
- **Yan:** associated with the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha* or related scholastic literature.

The collapse of Sui authority made Luoyang unsafe. Xuanzang and Changjie moved west, first toward Chang’an and then into Sichuan.

### Chang’an and Sichuan

The brothers reportedly found the capital’s monastic community disrupted by warfare. They traveled through Qinzhou and other western locations to Chengdu, where Buddhist institutions were more stable.

At Chengdu, Xuanzang studied and lectured on:

- the *Abhidharmakośa*;
- the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*;
- the *Saṃyuktābhidharmahṛdaya* tradition;
- the *Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra*;
- Yogācāra and tathāgatagarbha-related literature.

He received full monastic ordination at Chengdu at approximately twenty years of age, conventionally around 622.

### Search for teachers across China

Xuanzang left Sichuan, reportedly against his brother’s preference, and traveled down the Yangzi. His itinerary included Jingzhou, Xiangzhou, Zhaozhou, and eventually Chang’an. Teachers connected with this period include:

- **Huixiu**, under whom he studied Abhidharma;
- **Daoshen**, associated with the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*;
- **Daoyue** and **Fachang**, prominent teachers at Chang’an;
- other specialists in Yogācāra, Abhidharma, Vinaya, and the *Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra*.

Names and exact locations vary between reconstructions.

### Motive for the western journey

**Consistent early-source report.** Xuanzang encountered contradictions among Chinese translations and interpretations, particularly concerning Yogācāra doctrine, the stages of practice, Buddha-nature, consciousness, and the relationship between several scholastic systems. He concluded that Chinese materials were incomplete or inconsistent and sought:

1. fuller Sanskrit manuscripts;
2. authoritative oral instruction;
3. the *Yogācārabhūmi Śāstra* and associated works;
4. clarification from Indian centers of Buddhist learning.

The journey was therefore neither simply tourism nor a single-purpose effort to fetch “the Buddhist scriptures.” China already possessed a large translated canon. Xuanzang sought particular missing texts, better recensions, philological authority, and living instruction.

## Illegal Departure from Tang China, c. 627–630

### Date

The traditional departure year is 629. Some chronologies favor 627 or 628 because dates embedded in the biography and western political history do not align perfectly. “629–645” remains the conventional travel period.

### Legal status

**Documented core, embellished narrative details.** Emperor Taizong’s early government restricted unauthorized foreign travel, partly because of war with the Eastern Türk empire. Xuanzang reportedly petitioned for permission and received no approval. He nevertheless left Chang’an secretly.

Calling the departure “illegal” is substantially justified: the biography says he traveled without authorization and evaded frontier controls. It does not imply conviction in a criminal trial. No surviving court record shows a prosecution.

### Gansu corridor and the desert

Xuanzang moved through Liangzhou toward Guazhou. Officials learned of the unauthorized journey. A warrant or order to detain him reportedly arrived, but sympathetic Buddhists and local personnel helped him continue.

At the Yumen frontier, guides showed him routes around watchtowers. One guide allegedly threatened him and then withdrew. He crossed the Gobi toward Hami, at one point losing his water supply. The biography says he nearly died after several days without water, refused to turn back, prayed to Avalokiteśvara, collapsed, and was revived by a cooling wind before his horse carried him toward water.

- The desert crossing is historically credible.
- The precise conversations, miraculous wind, and providential behavior of the horse are **hagiographic reports**.
- The later fictional Sandy and dragon-horse episodes are not present as such in the early biography.

## Chronological Life History: Central Asia and India

## Gaochang, c. 629–630

Xuanzang reached Hami and then the kingdom of Gaochang near modern Turfan. King Qu Wentai, a Buddhist ruler, received him.

**Contemporary-biographical report.** Qu Wentai became so attached to Xuanzang that he attempted to retain him as court preceptor. Xuanzang refused and reportedly began a hunger strike. The king relented, supplied money, clothing, attendants, letters of introduction, and diplomatic gifts, and asked Xuanzang to remain in Gaochang for several years on his return.

Xuanzang and the king reportedly became ritual brothers. This relationship was vital patronage rather than employment in a modern sense. Gaochang’s support converted an unauthorized refugee-pilgrim into a traveler carrying politically useful credentials.

Xuanzang never fulfilled the promised return residence. Gaochang was conquered by Tang forces in 640 while he was abroad, and Qu Wentai died during the crisis.

## Northern Silk Road

His westward itinerary, reconstructed from the biography and *Western Regions*, included:

- Karashahr;
- Kucha;
- Aksu;
- the Bedel or a comparable Tian Shan pass;
- the Issyk-Kul region;
- Suyab;
- Tashkent or Chach;
- Samarkand;
- the Iron Gates;
- Tokharistan/Bactria.

At Kucha he encountered a flourishing Sarvāstivāda Buddhist establishment and debated or conversed with learned monks. The biography stresses doctrinal disagreements while acknowledging their scholarship.

Near Suyab, he met the Western Türk ruler conventionally identified as Tong Yabghu Qaghan. The qaghan provided protection and letters or escorts. The exact chronology is difficult because external evidence suggests that Tong Yabghu may have died earlier than the biography’s dating permits.

**Disputed historical problem:** Either Xuanzang met a successor or another Türk ruler, the Chinese biography’s chronology is displaced, or the external chronology requires revision. The meeting cannot safely be assigned to Tong Yabghu without qualification.

At Samarkand, the ruler initially gave him a cool reception but later accepted Buddhist teaching and supported him. The claim that hostile Zoroastrians attempted to burn or assault Buddhist monks belongs to the disciple-biography and cannot be independently verified.

## Bactria, Bamiyan, and Kapisa

Xuanzang crossed the Iron Gates into Bactria, visited Balkh and the great monastery traditionally identified as Nava Vihāra, and studied with monks including Prajñākara.

He visited Bamiyan, whose enormous standing Buddha statues he described centuries before their destruction in 2001. His measurements are approximate and embedded in premodern units, but the description became an important source for the site’s history.

Crossing the Hindu Kush, he reached Kapisa, where he studied and debated across doctrinal traditions. The journey involved repeated shifts between rulers, monasteries, languages, and ecological zones rather than a single direct “Silk Road.”

## Gandhāra and Kashmir

Xuanzang traveled through regions corresponding to parts of modern Afghanistan and Pakistan, including:

- Nagarahāra;
- Peshawar/Puruṣapura;
- Pushkalavati;
- Uḍḍiyāna;
- Taxila;
- Kashmir.

He found many Gandhāran Buddhist sites diminished or ruined but also recorded active monasteries and sacred traditions. His account is a major source for the later geography of Gandhāra, though modern scholars warn that a monastery described as ruined may represent literary contrast, limited observation, or local testimony rather than a comprehensive census.

In Kashmir he spent approximately two years studying Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma and other subjects. The scholar Saṃghayaśas is among the teachers associated with this period. Royal support gave him access to manuscripts and scribal assistance.

## Northern India and Nālandā

Xuanzang proceeded through the Punjab and the upper Gangetic plain, visiting Mathurā, Śrughna, Kanyākubja, Ayodhyā, Kauśāmbī, Śrāvastī, Kapilavastu, Lumbinī, Kuśinagara, Vārāṇasī, Vaiśālī, and Bodh Gayā. Not every reported site was necessarily visited personally; the *Western Regions* includes lands known through reports.

At or near Ayodhyā, the biography says river pirates or worshippers of Durgā seized him and selected him for human sacrifice. A sudden storm allegedly scattered the assailants and saved him.

- Travel danger and robbery are plausible.
- The sacrificial selection and providential storm are **hagiographic reports without independent confirmation**.
- Later retellings often present the episode as established fact.

### Nālandā, c. 630s

Xuanzang reached Nālandā, the major monastic and scholastic complex in Magadha. He was received by the elderly master Śīlabhadra, an authority in Yogācāra and heir to the interpretive tradition of Dharmapāla.

The biography reports that Śīlabhadra had suffered severe illness and had contemplated suicide, but a dream or vision instructed him to remain alive to teach a Chinese monk who would arrive. This is **hagiographic prophecy**, serving to portray the meeting as predestined.

Xuanzang studied under Śīlabhadra for several years. Subjects reportedly included:

- the *Yogācārabhūmi*;
- the *Madhyāntavibhāga*;
- the *Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra*;
- the *Abhidharmasamuccaya*;
- Buddhist logic and epistemology;
- Sanskrit grammar;
- Vedic and Brahmanical systems;
- medicine;
- Sarvāstivāda and other Buddhist scholastic traditions.

He was reportedly among a small elite allowed to hear advanced instruction and was maintained at institutional expense. Numerical claims that Nālandā housed 10,000 monks or that only a handful mastered its entire curriculum should be treated as contemporary literary estimates, not audited enrollment data.

Nālandā gave Xuanzang both textual resources and an intellectual lineage. He did not merely collect manuscripts: he learned how Indian teachers organized and adjudicated competing commentaries.

## Travels within the subcontinent

Xuanzang left Nālandā for extensive study and pilgrimage. His route included parts of:

- Bengal and Assam;
- Orissa;
- Andhra;
- Kanchipuram in the Tamil south;
- Maharashtra;
- Gujarat;
- Sindh;
- the Indus valley;
- central and northern India.

He intended to visit Sri Lanka, an important center of Buddhist learning. Political disorder and reports from Sri Lankan monks led him not to cross the sea. Claims that he personally visited Sri Lanka are therefore erroneous.

### Encounter with Bhāskara Varman

Bhāskara Varman, king of Kāmarūpa in Assam, requested Xuanzang’s presence. Xuanzang initially resisted because he was studying, but the king’s increasingly forceful summons brought him east. Bhāskara treated him as an honored religious and diplomatic guest.

### Harṣavardhana

Emperor Harṣa, ruler of much of northern India, learned that Xuanzang was at Bhāskara’s court and demanded that the monk be sent to him. Tension between the rulers was resolved, and Xuanzang met Harṣa.

Xuanzang’s description of Harṣa is highly favorable: energetic rule, religious generosity, support for Buddhism, and public benefaction. The representation is politically situated. Xuanzang was Harṣa’s guest and later helped connect his court to Tang China.

## Kannauj assembly, c. 642–643

Harṣa convened a large religious assembly at Kanyākubja/Kannauj. Xuanzang was given a prominent position and presented a Mahāyāna thesis.

The biography says that thousands of monks, Brahmins, and rulers attended; Xuanzang posted a written challenge and remained undefeated for eighteen days because nobody dared answer. It also reports that opponents plotted to kill him and burn the assembly structure.

- A major court-sponsored assembly and Xuanzang’s honored participation are **well-supported early reports**.
- Attendance numbers, universal silence, assassination plots, and absolute victory come from laudatory narrative and lack independent documentation.
- The event should not be described uncritically as an objectively adjudicated international championship.

## Prayāga assembly

Xuanzang accompanied Harṣa to a quinquennial donation festival at Prayāga. Harṣa is reported to have distributed accumulated treasure to religious communities and the poor, eventually giving away his clothing and jewels. The account is important evidence for royal religious display but may idealize the scale and self-emptying generosity.

At these assemblies Xuanzang’s position was simultaneously religious, scholarly, ceremonial, and diplomatic.

---

## Return Journey, c. 643–645

Xuanzang declined requests to remain in India. Harṣa and other rulers provided transport, attendants, gifts, and protection.

His collection reportedly included:

- 657 Sanskrit Buddhist texts or “works,” a figure whose cataloguing unit is not entirely clear;
- Buddha images;
- approximately 150 relics;
- other religious objects.

The books were transported on pack animals, commonly said to number twenty horses. While crossing the Indus, part of the cargo reportedly fell into the river. Xuanzang halted and obtained replacement copies where possible. Exact losses cannot be reconstructed.

The return route ran through:

- the Indus and upper Indus regions;
- the Pamirs;
- Kashgar;
- Yarkand;
- Khotan;
- the southern Tarim route;
- Dunhuang;
- the Gansu corridor.

At Khotan, rather than enter Tang territory furtively, he sent a memorial to Emperor Taizong acknowledging his unauthorized departure and announcing his return. Taizong pardoned him and organized his reception.

Xuanzang reached Chang’an in early 645. Crowds reportedly filled the streets as the manuscripts, images, and relics were displayed. The exact crowd descriptions are celebratory but the public and imperial reception is well supported.

## Relationship with the Tang Court

## Emperor Taizong

Xuanzang met Taizong at Luoyang soon after his return. Their interests overlapped but were not identical:

- Xuanzang sought support for translation and Buddhist institutions.
- Taizong wanted geographical and political intelligence on Central Asia and India.
- Xuanzang’s contacts with Harṣa and Bhāskara were diplomatically valuable.
- The court could present his return as evidence of Tang cosmopolitan prestige.

Taizong offered him secular office or asked him to accompany military and administrative projects. Xuanzang declined, citing his monastic vocation, but remained closely tied to the emperor.

At imperial command, he produced the *Great Tang Record of the Western Regions*. Bianji shaped Xuanzang’s oral and written material into its twelve-fascicle form. The book was completed and presented in 646.

Taizong wrote the celebrated “Preface to the Sacred Teachings” for Xuanzang’s translations. Crown Prince Li Zhi, the future Gaozong, wrote a supplementary preface. The court did not thereby become doctrinally Buddhist; patronage existed within a state that balanced Buddhist, Daoist, and Confucian interests.

### Diplomatic consequences

Harṣa’s contact with Xuanzang contributed to exchanges between his court and Tang China. Tang missions traveled to India. Xuanzang therefore operated as an informal diplomatic intermediary, although he held no permanent foreign ministry position.

## Emperor Gaozong

After Taizong’s death in 649, Gaozong continued patronage. Xuanzang worked at court-supported monasteries and occasionally traveled with the imperial household.

He repeatedly memorialized the court on Buddhist affairs, requested support for translation, and sought benefits for relatives or religious institutions. His career illustrates dependence on imperial favor rather than separation between monastery and state.

## Translation Bureaus and Institutional Career, 645–664

### Hongfu Monastery

Translation began at Hongfu Monastery in Chang’an in 645. The first completed works included texts belonging to the *Mahāratnakūṭa* complex and Yogācāra materials.

### Great Ci’en Monastery

In 648 or 649, Xuanzang became associated with the newly built Great Ci’en Monastery, founded by Crown Prince Li Zhi in memory of his mother, Empress Wende. Xuanzang directed a state-supported translation bureau there.

The bureau was a collaborative institution. Roles included:

- Xuanzang reading or orally translating the Sanskrit;
- monks verifying Sanskrit wording and meaning;
- scribes recording the Chinese;
- literary revisers regularizing syntax;
- doctrinal specialists checking interpretation;
- editors and officials preparing final copies.

Important collaborators included:

- **Kuiji**, principal Chinese Yogācāra disciple and commentator;
- **Woncheuk/Yuance**, Korean monk and influential interpreter;
- **Puguang**, Abhidharma specialist;
- **Shentai**;
- **Fabao**;
- **Shenfang**;
- **Jia Shang**;
- **Bianji**, compiler of the *Western Regions*;
- **Huili**, later biographer;
- **Yancong**, editor-completer of Huili’s biography.

Xuanzang’s translations were therefore neither anonymous committee products nor solitary compositions. He controlled their doctrinal and philological direction, while organized teams produced and revised the Chinese texts.

### Great Wild Goose Pagoda

Xuanzang requested a structure to preserve scriptures and images. The Great Wild Goose Pagoda was erected at Ci’en Monastery in 652. Its original form differed from the later rebuilt structure now standing in Xi’an.

The familiar image of Xuanzang personally carrying every brick is devotional elaboration. His documented role was as petitioner, planner, institutional leader, and participant in ceremonial labor.

### Translation scale

Traditional catalogues credit him with seventy-five works in 1,335 fascicles. Some counts give 1,338 or comparable totals because cataloguing and textual boundaries differ. Only part of the 657 imported titles was translated.

His output included:

- vast Prajñāpāramitā collections;
- Yogācāra scriptures and treatises;
- Sarvāstivāda Abhidharma;
- Buddhist logic;
- Madhyamaka texts;
- devotional sūtras;
- dhāraṇīs;
- Vinaya material;
- texts on rulers and ethics;
- a Vaiśeṣika work from a non-Buddhist Indian school.

### Translation method

Xuanzang’s work is often characterized as more literal than earlier translations. This is only partly adequate. His bureau developed technical vocabulary, maintained closer morphological correspondence with Sanskrit, used transliteration strategically, and compared manuscripts. Yet translations also contain doctrinal interpretation, harmonization, Chinese stylistic choices, and the influence of oral commentarial traditions.

His work marked the conventional beginning of the “new translations,” in contrast to “old translations” associated with Kumārajīva, Paramārtha, and others.

## Intellectual and Religious Development

## Before India

Xuanzang was formed amid disagreements between Chinese interpretations of Yogācāra-related literature. Earlier translations by Bodhiruci, Ratnamati, Paramārtha, and others did not yield a single coherent system.

His central question concerned the structure of consciousness and the path by which cognition is transformed into awakening.

## Indian formation

Under Śīlabhadra, Xuanzang adopted the Yogācāra interpretation associated with Dharmapāla. Important concepts included:

- **Eight consciousnesses:** five sensory consciousnesses, mental consciousness, afflicted mind, and store consciousness.
- **Ālayavijñāna:** the “store” or foundational flow of consciousness containing karmic seeds.
- **Three natures:** imagined, dependent, and perfected modes of experience.
- **Vijñaptimātra:** “representation-only” or “cognition-only.”
- **Transformation of the basis:** the conversion of deluded cognitive structures into wisdom.
- **Five-gotra theory:** a classification of differing spiritual capacities, including a disputed category said to lack the seed of complete liberation.

Calling this a claim that “nothing exists except my mind” oversimplifies the system. Modern scholars disagree over whether Yogācāra is metaphysical idealism, phenomenological analysis, epistemic critique, soteriological therapy, or a combination.

## *Cheng weishi lun*

The *Cheng weishi lun*, 成唯識論, “Demonstration/Establishment of Consciousness-Only,” completed in 659, synthesizes commentaries on Vasubandhu’s *Triṃśikā*. Tradition says Xuanzang first intended to translate ten Indian commentaries separately but, following Kuiji’s advice, produced a synthetic text privileging Dharmapāla.

This authorship is layered:

- Vasubandhu supplied the root verses.
- Ten Indian commentators supplied interpretations.
- Dharmapāla’s position dominates.
- Xuanzang translated and synthesized.
- Kuiji may have influenced organization and recorded oral explanations.

It should therefore be identified as a translation-compilation with significant editorial authorship, not simply an original monograph or a transparent translation of one Sanskrit book.

## Faxiang school

Later historiography calls Xuanzang and Kuiji founders of the Faxiang or “Dharma Characteristics” school, also called Chinese Yogācāra or Weishi. The designation can exaggerate how institutionally separate the movement was during Xuanzang’s life. His teachings spread through a network of textual study rather than a centralized sect with membership rolls.

The school had major influence in China, Korea, and Japan. Japanese Hossō lineages traced themselves through students connected to Xuanzang’s circle.

## Decline of the school

As an independent Chinese doctrinal lineage, Faxiang lost ground to Huayan, Chan, Tiantai, and other movements. Reasons included its technical scholastic vocabulary, internal disputes, dependence on a narrow textual curriculum, political changes, and competitors’ capacity to incorporate Yogācāra concepts into broader syntheses. This was not disappearance: Xuanzang’s vocabulary and translations became embedded throughout East Asian Buddhism.

## The Final Years at Yuhua, 659–664

Xuanzang’s health and workload became increasing concerns. In 659 he requested permission to withdraw from Chang’an to Yuhua Monastery, north of the capital, in a former imperial palace. A dated inscription confirms his presence there.

His principal final undertaking was translation of the enormous *Mahāprajñāpāramitā Sūtra* collection. Colleagues reportedly suggested abridgment because of its length. Xuanzang refused after dreams or signs convinced him that the work must be translated in full. The resulting text comprises 600 fascicles and occupies several volumes of the modern Taishō canon.

The translation was completed late in 663. He then began or prepared to translate the *Ratnakūṭa* collection but concluded that his strength was exhausted.

### Illness and accident

The biography says that in early 664 Xuanzang stumbled or fell while crossing a ditch or water channel and injured his leg or shin. He subsequently became ill. Edema or swelling is mentioned in some accounts. No modern medical diagnosis is possible.

The sources describe:

- worsening physical weakness;
- withdrawal from translation;
- meditation on Maitreya;
- instructions about his funeral;
- predictions of his approaching death;
- visionary experiences reported by disciples.

There is no evidence of addiction, psychiatric illness, deliberate self-harm, poisoning, homicide, or execution.

---

## Death and Burial

Xuanzang died at Yuhua Monastery on the fifth or sixth day of the second lunar month of 664. The conventional Gregorian date is 7 March 664; a calendrical calculation placing death after midnight yields 8 March.

The biography records final devotional utterances and visions of rebirth in Maitreya’s Tuṣita heaven. These are **hagiographic reports**, not independently observable facts.

His body was returned toward Chang’an. Gaozong ordered an official funeral. Vast crowds were said to attend; exact figures are rhetorical.

He was initially buried at Bailuyuan, the White Deer Plain, east of Chang’an. Because the grave was visible from the imperial residence and caused Gaozong grief, the remains were reportedly moved in 669 to a site at Fan River valley, later associated with Xingjiao Monastery.

### Cause of death

The strongest reconstructable sequence is:

1. chronic exhaustion and declining health;
2. a fall and leg injury;
3. subsequent systemic illness and swelling;
4. death at approximately sixty-two to sixty-five.

A precise cause—fracture complication, infection, cardiovascular disease, renal disease, or another illness—cannot be established.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Changjie

Elder brother and first monastic guide. He brought Xuanzang into the Luoyang monastery and accompanied him during the flight from wartime disorder.

### Chinese teachers

Huixiu, Daoshen, Daoyue, Fachang, and other lecturers gave Xuanzang mastery of the competing Chinese traditions whose contradictions prompted his journey.

### Qu Wentai

King of Gaochang; patron, protector, and reported ritual brother. Their conflict over Xuanzang’s freedom to depart ended in extensive material support.

### Western Türk ruler

A qaghan at Suyab provided protection. Identification with Tong Yabghu is traditional but chronologically contested.

### Śīlabhadra

Xuanzang’s decisive Indian teacher at Nālandā. Their relationship transmitted the Yogācāra interpretation associated with Dharmapāla. The prophecy of their meeting is hagiographic; the teacher-student bond is central and credible.

### Prajñākara and other Indian scholars

Prajñākara in Bactria and numerous monks in Kashmir, Nālandā, and elsewhere broadened Xuanzang’s training. The sources sometimes suppress disagreements by converting debates into narratives of his victory.

### Bhāskara Varman

King of Kāmarūpa; patron and political host. He competed with Harṣa for access to Xuanzang before participating in Harṣa’s assembly.

### Harṣavardhana

Imperial patron in India. Xuanzang’s description of Harṣa shaped later histories of seventh-century northern India and helped establish diplomatic contact with Tang China.

### Emperor Taizong

Patron, interrogator, and political user of Xuanzang’s geographical knowledge. Taizong supported the translation project and authored a preface but failed to induce Xuanzang to accept secular office.

### Emperor Gaozong

Continued court sponsorship, supplied resources, and ordered funerary honors.

### Bianji

Monk-editor who transformed Xuanzang’s material into the *Great Tang Record*. Bianji was executed in 649 after accusations of an illicit relationship with Princess Gaoyang.

There is **no evidence that Xuanzang participated in Bianji’s alleged affair or prosecution**. The episode nevertheless removed a close literary collaborator and demonstrates the vulnerability of monastic scholars within court politics.

### Kuiji

Principal disciple and major systematizer of Chinese Yogācāra. Later sectarian narratives elevate him as the exclusive orthodox heir.

### Woncheuk

Korean monk and major interpreter of Xuanzang’s translations. Later Chinese and Korean-Japanese traditions constructed rivalry between Woncheuk and Kuiji. Some stories accuse Woncheuk of secretly overhearing lectures reserved for Kuiji.

These accusations are **later sectarian narratives**, not securely documented contemporary misconduct. Modern scholarship treats the two as distinct but overlapping interpreters within Xuanzang’s broader network.

## Institutions, Projects, and Material Interests

### Institutions

- Jingtu Monastery, Luoyang: novice formation.
- Chengdu monasteries: higher study and full ordination.
- Nālandā: advanced Indian study.
- Hongfu Monastery, Chang’an: initial translation bureau.
- Great Ci’en Monastery: main imperial translation center.
- Great Wild Goose Pagoda: scripture and image repository.
- Ximing Monastery and other court institutions: associated teaching and translation activity.
- Yuhua Monastery: final translation residence.
- Xingjiao Monastery: later burial and commemorative site.

### Technologies and intellectual techniques

Xuanzang did not invent mechanical technologies or hold patents. His important techniques were institutional and philological:

- multilingual manuscript comparison;
- division of translation labor;
- controlled technical vocabulary;
- systematic transliteration;
- doctrinal adjudication among commentaries;
- large-project management;
- state-supported copying and editing;
- geographic recording based on itineraries, distances, reports, and textual traditions.

### Financial structure

His projects required substantial state expenditure, but no reliable budget survives in the consulted sources. Funding took the form of:

- monastic endowments;
- imperial grants;
- lodging and food;
- paper and copying materials;
- salaried or maintained personnel;
- transport;
- construction;
- gifts from Central Asian and Indian rulers.

Xuanzang’s political access was a material precondition for his scholarly production.

## Controversies, Allegations, Criticism, and Unresolved Questions

## 1. Unauthorized departure

**Documented fact in the early biographical tradition:** Xuanzang left despite the absence of imperial permission and evaded frontier restrictions.

**Qualification:** No surviving judgment shows that he was tried or formally convicted. Taizong pardoned or accepted the act upon his return.

## 2. Reliability of the *Great Tang Record*

**Modern scholarly criticism:** The work was long mined as a straightforward eyewitness gazetteer. Max Deeg and other scholars argue that this misunderstands its genre. It is an imperial, Buddhist, didactic, and literary representation of the western regions.

Problems include:

- countries Xuanzang described but probably did not visit;
- information drawn from Indian Buddhist and Brahmanical texts;
- oral reports merged with observation;
- idealized kings;
- sacred legends presented alongside geography;
- schematic measurements;
- political lessons intended for Taizong;
- discrepancies between its sectarian history and other Buddhist works.

This criticism does not make the text fraudulent. It means every entry must be assessed according to source type.

## 3. Chronology and Tong Yabghu

**Unresolved historical problem:** The claimed meeting with Tong Yabghu conflicts with some chronologies for the qaghan’s death. Proposed solutions include an earlier departure date, misidentification of the ruler, displaced chronology, or errors in external records.

## 4. Triumphs in debate

**Hagiographic claim:** Xuanzang defeated all opponents at Kannauj and faced assassination by jealous rivals.

**Evidence:** His honored role at a major assembly is credible; complete intellectual victory, universal refusal to debate, and murder plots are preserved mainly in disciple-centered narrative.

## 5. Religious violence and human sacrifice

The biography reports a near-sacrifice to Durgā and hostile attacks by non-Buddhists.

**Status:** Unverified hagiographic reports. They may reflect real banditry, religious polemic, local practice, or narrative motifs. They should not be generalized into claims about Hinduism or Indian society.

## 6. Sectarian bias

Xuanzang favored the Dharmapāla-Śīlabhadra Yogācāra lineage. The *Cheng weishi lun* gives that lineage interpretive priority over other Indian commentators. Modern scholarship has identified inconsistencies between sectarian histories in the *Western Regions* and those in texts Xuanzang himself translated.

**Status:** Documented interpretive preference, not fraud.

## 7. “Author” versus translator

Many works popularly listed as “by Xuanzang” are translations of Indian scriptures or treatises. The *Cheng weishi lun* is a synthesis; the *Western Regions* was compiled with Bianji; letters and memorials survive through edited collections. Bibliographies that treat all seventy-five titles as original books by Xuanzang misstate authorship.

## 8. Heart Sūtra authorship

The Chinese *Heart Sūtra* associated with Xuanzang is traditionally regarded as his 649 translation from Sanskrit. Modern scholarship has debated whether the short text may have been compiled in China from passages in Kumārajīva’s *Large Prajñāpāramitā* translation and subsequently back-translated into Sanskrit.

**Status:** Scholarly dispute. The received Chinese canon attributes the text to Xuanzang. That attribution does not conclusively establish the direction of composition or exclude editorial formation before or within his circle.

## 9. Colonial archaeology and site identification

Alexander Cunningham and other nineteenth-century investigators used Xuanzang’s distances and place descriptions to identify Indian archaeological sites. Modern reassessment argues that circular reasoning sometimes arose: ruins were identified through Xuanzang, then used to “prove” his precision.

**Status:** Documented methodological criticism of later scholarship, not an accusation against Xuanzang.

## 10. Nationalist appropriation

Modern Chinese and Indian governments have represented Xuanzang as a symbol of ancient friendship, peaceful cultural exchange, Silk Road connectivity, and contemporary bilateral cooperation. His life has also been invoked in debates over Nalanda, Buddhism’s Indian decline, Chinese territorial history, and the Belt and Road Initiative.

**Status:** Documented modern political use. Such commemoration may select cooperative themes while minimizing illegal departure, state competition, religious decline, and the imperial intelligence value of his record.

## 11. Relic provenance

Bones identified as Xuanzang’s have been divided among China, Japan, Taiwan, and India. A skull fragment reportedly discovered by Japanese forces in occupied Nanjing in 1942 was distributed through wartime and postwar diplomatic channels.

**Status:** The modern custody history is partly documented; biological identity is unverified. Authentication relies on inscriptions, transmission claims, and temple tradition rather than modern genetic comparison with an established reference.

## 12. Absence of modern criminal-type allegations

A dedicated search combining Xuanzang’s name with scandal, fraud, corruption, lawsuit, accusation, conspiracy, and recent reporting found no credible evidence of personal financial fraud, sexual misconduct, homicide, espionage, money laundering, or judicial conviction.

Results frequently confused him with:

- Emperor Xuanzong of Tang;
- modern monks;
- fictional Tang Sanzang;
- institutions named after Xuanzang.

The historically grounded adverse issues are unauthorized travel, court dependency, source bias, sectarian partisanship, hagiographic inflation, disputed relics, and modern ideological appropriation.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

## Childhood prodigy

**Story:** The boy Xuanzang displayed adult ritual seriousness and extraordinary memory.

**Appearance and circulation:** Disciple biography, later monastic histories.

**Evidence:** Early education is plausible; dramatic anecdotes are not independently attested.

**Effect:** Established innate sanctity and linked Buddhist achievement to Confucian filial and ritual virtue.

## Avalokiteśvara saves him in the desert

**Story:** Near death without water, Xuanzang prayed to Avalokiteśvara, received supernatural relief, and was guided to water.

**Source:** Huili’s biography.

**Status:** Hagiographic miracle.

**Effect:** Made the illegal and dangerous journey a divinely protected mission.

## Śīlabhadra’s prophetic dream

**Story:** Śīlabhadra was told by divine figures not to die because a Chinese monk would come to receive the teaching.

**Status:** Hagiographic prophecy.

**Effect:** Legitimated Xuanzang as the providential heir of Indian Yogācāra.

## Immunity in debate

**Story:** No scholar could refute Xuanzang during eighteen days at Kannauj; jealous opponents resorted to assassination.

**Status:** Court-monastic triumph narrative with a plausible historical assembly at its core.

**Effect:** Established him as a transregional intellectual champion and “Mahāyānadeva.”

## Journey to the West

Between Xuanzang’s death and the Ming dynasty, his journey accumulated miracle stories in oral performance, Buddhist tales, drama, poetry, and prose.

The sixteenth-century novel conventionally attributed to Wu Cheng’en transformed the expedition:

- Xuanzang becomes Tang Sanzang.
- Emperor Taizong personally commissions the journey.
- Guanyin directs the pilgrimage.
- Sun Wukong, Zhu Bajie, Sha Wujing, and the dragon-horse protect him.
- Demons repeatedly attempt to eat him because his flesh supposedly grants immortality.
- The travelers undergo eighty-one ordeals.
- Scriptures are obtained from a mythic Vulture Peak under direct supernatural management.

None of these companions is historical. The real Xuanzang traveled with different attendants at different stages and received extensive political and monastic assistance.

The fictional monk is often timid, credulous, naïve, and dependent on Sun Wukong—the opposite of the forceful scholar, organizer, negotiator, and clandestine traveler found in early sources.

## Emperor Taizong as sponsor from the beginning

**Mythic-literary version:** Taizong sends his “younger brother” west with full imperial authority.

**Historical evidence:** Xuanzang departed without permission. Taizong became his patron after his return.

**Reputational effect:** Converts civil disobedience into a harmonious state mission.

## Perfectly exact geographer

**Modern heroic mythology:** Xuanzang accurately surveyed every place he described.

**Evidence:** Many descriptions are invaluable and correlate with archaeology; others depend on reports, sacred texts, schematic distances, or political-literary construction.

**Effect:** Elevated him into a proto-modern scientific explorer, sometimes obscuring the religious genre of his work.

## Unbroken possession of authentic relics

**Devotional claim:** All distributed bone fragments are physically Xuanzang’s remains.

**Evidence:** Plausible temple transmission exists, but wartime discovery, repeated reburial, division, and lack of scientific authentication leave provenance unresolved.

## Works by or Closely Associated with Xuanzang

### Original, dictated, or compiled works

1. **大唐西域記 — *Da Tang Xiyu ji***  
   *Great Tang Record of the Western Regions*, 646, twelve fascicles.  
   Material supplied by Xuanzang; compiled and written with the monk Bianji under imperial command. Covers more than one hundred named polities and regions. It is not a day-by-day travel diary.

2. **會宗論 — *Huizong lun***  
   “Treatise on the Meeting/Harmonization of the Schools.”  
   Reportedly composed in India to reconcile or adjudicate Yogācāra and Madhyamaka positions. The original is lost or not securely identified.

3. **制惡見論 — *Zhie jian lun***  
   “Treatise for Restraining Evil Views,” or comparable translation.  
   Reportedly a Sanskrit thesis associated with debate in India. Lost; its original title and precise content are uncertain.

4. **Memorials and letters**  
   Petitions to Taizong and Gaozong, acknowledgments of imperial gifts, requests regarding translation and monasteries, and correspondence with Indian associates survive in quotation or edited collections. Their documentary value varies with transmission.

5. **Verses and devotional compositions**  
   A small number are attributed in biographies or catalogues. Attribution and textual independence require case-by-case analysis.

### Translation-compilations

1. **成唯識論 — *Cheng weishi lun***, 659, ten fascicles  
   A synthesis of Indian commentaries on Vasubandhu’s *Triṃśikā*, dominated by Dharmapāla. Xuanzang was translator-compiler; Kuiji was involved in its exegetical transmission.

2. **成唯識寶生論 — *Cheng weishi baosheng lun***  
   A Yogācāra-related translation associated with Dharmapāla.

### Lost or uncertain translations

Medieval catalogues mention projects that may have been begun, planned, reclassified, or lost. The precise equivalence between the 657 imported items and seventy-five translated works cannot be recovered because “text,” manuscript bundle, title, and fascicle were not interchangeable accounting units.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Buddhist translation

Xuanzang established the most ambitious single translation program in medieval Chinese Buddhism. His Chinese versions preserve numerous Indian works whose Sanskrit originals are incomplete or lost. Conversely, some of his translations were less popular liturgically than smoother earlier versions.

### Philosophy

Through Xuanzang and Kuiji, Dharmapāla-lineage Yogācāra became a major East Asian philosophical system. Its vocabulary influenced:

- Buddhist theories of mind;
- epistemology;
- meditation;
- karma;
- cognition;
- Buddha bodies;
- spiritual capacity;
- debates over external objects.

### East Asian transmission

Students and later travelers carried his teachings to Korea and Japan. Japanese Hossō Buddhism treated his translations and Kuiji’s commentaries as foundational. Woncheuk’s interpretations became important in Korea, China, and Tibet.

### Indian and Central Asian history

The *Great Tang Record* preserves descriptions of political geography, roads, monasteries, urban centers, languages, rulers, customs, and sacred sites. It has been used to reconstruct:

- Nālandā;
- Bodh Gayā;
- Lumbinī;
- Gandhāra;
- Bamiyan;
- early medieval Bengal;
- Central Asian oasis kingdoms;
- Harṣa’s empire.

The text’s value survives modern criticism, but it is no longer responsible practice to treat every statement as direct field observation.

### Archaeology

Nineteenth-century archaeologists used Xuanzang’s itineraries to locate sites associated with the Buddha’s life. This led to real discoveries but also encouraged overconfident distance calculations and circular identifications.

### Literature and popular religion

The historical monk became one of the most recognizable religious travelers in world literature. His fictionalized weakness allowed Sun Wukong to become the dramatic hero, while Xuanzang’s sacred mission remained the narrative’s organizing purpose.

### National and diplomatic symbolism

China and India commemorate him as a symbol of long-term cultural exchange. Memorials, diplomatic ceremonies, exhibitions, films, and “New Silk Road” discourse invoke his route. These representations are historically consequential but select from a more complicated life involving border evasion, rival states, imperial intelligence, doctrinal conflict, and court patronage.

### Relic cult

Claimed remains are venerated across several countries. Their division has turned Xuanzang into a transnational relic figure. Historical custody and devotional identity are clearer than scientific authentication.
