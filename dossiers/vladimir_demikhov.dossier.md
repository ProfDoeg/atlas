# Vladimir Petrovich Demikhov: Research Dossier

## Scope and Source Note

This dossier reconstructs the documented life and work of Vladimir Petrovich Demikhov from Russian archival-biographical studies, institutional records, his published work, medical-historical scholarship, contemporary reporting, family recollections, and later cultural representations.

Important limitations:

- Demikhov left no widely available full autobiography or comprehensive published correspondence.
- Many biographical accounts are commemorative and reproduce undocumented anecdotes.
- Several recurrent stories—especially concerning Christiaan Barnard, Demikhov’s alleged persecution, and his “first artificial heart”—change in detail between retellings.
- “First” claims below refer to specific experimental procedures in warm-blooded animals and are stated narrowly. They do not mean the first successful clinical transplantation in a human.
- Dates in Russian sources sometimes use the Julian and Gregorian calendars inconsistently.
- No evidence located in the dedicated adversarial search connected this Vladimir Demikhov with lawsuits, financial fraud, corruption, criminal enterprises, political violence, intelligence work, or human experimentation.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full Russian name:** Владимир Петрович Демихов  
**Standard English transliteration:** Vladimir Petrovich Demikhov  
**Other transliterations:** Vladimir P. Demikhov; Wladimir Petrowitsch Demichow; Vladimir Petrovič Demichov; Vladimir Démijov  
**Patronymic:** Petrovich, “son of Pyotr/Peter”  
**Nationality and state affiliations:** Born a subject of the Russian Empire; later a citizen of the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic and USSR; Russian Federation citizen after 1991  
**Fields:** Experimental biology, physiology, experimental surgery, transplantation, artificial circulation, cardiac surgery  
**Education:** Biology/physiology, not a medical degree  
**Highest degree:** Doctor of Biological Sciences  
**Birth:** 18 July 1916 Old Style, conventionally converted to 31 July 1916 New Style  
**Birthplace:** Khutor Kuliki or Kulikovsky, Yaryzhenskaya stanitsa, Don Host Oblast, Russian Empire; now Kulikovsky, Volgograd Oblast, Russia  
**Death:** 22 November 1998, Moscow  
**Reported cause:** Ruptured aneurysm; the precise anatomical type is not consistently identified in accessible accounts  
**Burial:** Vagankovskoye Cemetery, Moscow  
**Spouse:** Liya Nikolayevna Demikhova  
**Child:** Olga Vladimirovna Demikhova  
**Political affiliation:** No documented major party office or independent political career  
**Religion:** No securely documented religious affiliation  
**Known sobriquets and retrospective titles:**

- “Father of heart and lung transplantation”
- “Father of experimental transplantation”
- “Founder of world transplantology,” especially in Russian commemorative usage
- “The new Michurin,” sometimes used sarcastically by critics
- “Charlatan” and “pseudo-scientist,” labels reportedly used by institutional opponents
- “Mad scientist,” a later popular-cultural stereotype associated with photographs of his two-headed dogs

Demikhov was frequently called a surgeon, including by medical historians. Strictly, however, he graduated as a biologist and did not possess the standard Soviet medical diploma qualifying him as a clinical physician. His surgery was overwhelmingly experimental and performed on animals or, in limited vascular studies, on organs or bodies after death.

## Name, Birth-Date, and Birthplace Problems

The strongest Russian biographical and institutional sources give **18 July 1916** as Demikhov’s birth date. English-language accounts often give **31 July 1916**, treating 18 July as Old Style. Because Imperial Russia was then using the Julian calendar, the two dates describe the same intended birth date, although later Soviet records often retained pre-revolutionary dates without systematically converting them.

The birthplace appears under several forms:

- хутор Кулики — Khutor Kuliki
- хутор Куликовский — Khutor Kulikovsky
- “Kulini,” probably a transcription or typographical corruption
- Yaryzhenskaya stanitsa, Don Host Oblast
- in some popular accounts, “near Kamyshin”

The safest reconstruction is **the Kuliki/Kulikovsky farmstead in the territory now belonging to Volgograd Oblast**.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

Demikhov was born into a Russian peasant family.

His father, **Pyotr Yakovlevich Demikhov**, died or was killed during the Russian Civil War in 1919, when Vladimir was approximately three. Sources use both neutral and martial formulations; detailed service records or the precise circumstances of Pyotr’s death were not located.

His mother is usually named **Domnika Alexandrovna**; spelling variants include Domenika. She raised three children alone after her husband’s death. Accounts emphasize that all three eventually obtained higher education, an unusual social ascent for children from an impoverished rural household in the early Soviet period.

### Siblings

Demikhov was one of three children. Accessible scholarly biographies do not consistently identify the other two by full name, and this dossier does not supply names from unsourced genealogical websites.

### Marriage

Demikhov met **Liya Nikolayevna** shortly after returning from military service. According to their daughter’s recollections, Liya initially refused to give him her telephone number unless he memorized it rather than writing it down. He continued calling and visiting; they married in **August 1946**.

Liya was a **heating engineer**. Family recollections describe her as managing domestic affairs and learning enough about her husband’s experiments to discuss and support them. They remained married for nearly fifty years. Liya reportedly died in 1998, the same year as her husband.

The telephone-number courtship is a **reported family anecdote**, not independently documented contemporaneously.

### Daughter

Their only known child, **Olga Vladimirovna Demikhova**, became a physician, pulmonologist, Doctor of Medical Sciences, professor, Honored Doctor of the Russian Federation, and senior researcher or administrator at the Central Research Institute of Tuberculosis.

Olga later became an important custodian of her father’s papers, photographs, memory, and institutional reputation. A substantial portion of Demikhov’s personal-life record comes through interviews with her.

### Household and Finances

The Demikhovs lived for years in materially restricted circumstances:

- initially in a communal apartment;
- according to Olga, the three-person nuclear family occupied a room of approximately twelve square metres;
- Demikhov’s mother also lived in the apartment;
- later the family received a small one-room apartment;
- only near the end of the 1960s did their housing improve.

Olga recalled that her mother sometimes earned more than her father. No evidence has been found that Demikhov accumulated significant private wealth, owned a business, held commercial investments, or profited substantially from patents. His work belonged to Soviet state institutions.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1916–1934

Demikhov grew up amid the Civil War and postwar deprivation. The loss of his father and his mother’s responsibility for three children shaped the family’s economic situation.

Later popular biographies sometimes invent or embellish a childhood fascination with dissection, injured animals, machinery, or creating life. A detailed historical study published for his centenary reported finding **no documentary evidence connecting him with biology or animal experimentation during childhood**. Such stories should therefore be labeled **retrospective literary reconstruction**, not established biography.

He attended a vocational or trade school and acquired metalworking and mechanical skills. He later worked as a **mechanic or fitter at a factory**. These skills plausibly contributed to his capacity to construct pumps and surgical apparatus, although the precise factory and dates are inconsistently given.

A personnel card completed by Demikhov in 1982 stated that he finished secondary school in **Lipetsk in 1933**. Some popular accounts instead emphasize his vocational schooling and factory employment without mentioning Lipetsk.

In 1934, reportedly through a Komsomol educational placement, he entered higher education in Voronezh.

## Education and Formation

### Voronezh State University, 1934–1938

Demikhov entered the animal-physiology section of the Biology Faculty at **Voronezh State University**. He originally expected to become a zoological engineer or physiologist rather than a surgeon.

Important teachers and institutional influences included:

- **Professor P. M. Nikiforovsky**, head of physiology and a self-described follower of Ivan Pavlov;
- assistant **V. L. Fedder**, who directed student experimental work;
- fellow students **N. P. Vinogradov, G. S. Ivanenko, L. Ya. Ratgauser, and Z. A. Prikhodko**, who assisted with early apparatus experiments;
- the experimental-circulation work of **Sergei Sergeyevich Bryukhonenko**, inventor of the extracorporeal “autojektor.”

Demikhov studied animal physiology at a time when Soviet biology was increasingly shaped by Michurinist and later Lysenkoist doctrine. He specialized partly in animal genetics. His later 1953 article explicitly attempted to align transplantation with “Michurinist theory,” but he did not become an important theorist of Lysenkoism.

### The 1937–1938 Mechanical-Heart Experiment

Demikhov wrote that in 1937 he constructed a compact device made from two adjacent membrane pumps representing the ventricles. It was attached to a dog after the natural heart’s function was stopped.

A surviving report in the Voronezh university newspaper, dated **7 May 1938**, described his presentation at a student scientific conference and quoted an experimental protocol dated **24 March 1938**. The dog reportedly showed respiratory, ocular, and other reflexes after circulation was restored mechanically.

Later descriptions called this “the world’s first implantable artificial heart.” The claim requires qualification:

- **Documented fact:** Demikhov constructed and tested a mechanical circulatory pump as a student.
- **Documented fact:** the experiment and student presentation were reported contemporaneously.
- **Disputed detail:** later sources alternately say the dog lived or showed signs of life for two, two-and-a-half, five, or five-and-a-half hours.
- **Historical interpretation:** researchers S. P. Glyantsev and colleagues argue that the 2.5-hour figure probably belongs to the Voronezh apparatus, while the 5.5-hour figure may refer to later Moscow devices.
- **Disputed originality:** Bryukhonenko’s earlier autojektor was an extracorporeal circulation apparatus and probably an intellectual or mechanical prototype. Demikhov’s innovation was to place his pump within the thoracic cavity, although it remained driven by an external motor.
- **Unsupported legend:** some popular versions say Demikhov sold his only suit to buy silver plates. This anecdote has not been corroborated by the surviving student report.

### Possible Mentorship by Bryukhonenko

Glyantsev reconstructed a probable encounter between Demikhov and Bryukhonenko in Voronezh, followed by Bryukhonenko’s assistance with Demikhov’s transfer to Moscow. Evidence includes:

- Nikiforovsky’s correspondence with Bryukhonenko;
- the presence of Bryukhonenko’s autojektor in Voronezh;
- Bryukhonenko’s presentation concerning Demikhov’s apparatus;
- Demikhov’s subsequent work in Bryukhonenko’s Moscow institute.

However, the researchers explicitly acknowledge that **no direct record of a meeting survives**. Bryukhonenko as a personal mentor is therefore a **strong historical inference**, not a fully documented fact.

### Transfer to Moscow State University

On **31 July 1938**, Demikhov and fellow student Lev Ratgauser petitioned the Soviet higher-education administration for transfer to Moscow State University because Voronezh lacked advanced specialization in animal physiology.

The Biology Faculty dean initially wrote that transfer was impossible because no places were available. Nevertheless, Demikhov was admitted to the fifth year of the MSU Biology Faculty on **1 September 1938**. Intervention by Bryukhonenko is possible but not proven.

At MSU, Demikhov studied physiology and worked on circulatory devices. He graduated with honors in **August 1940**, receiving a biology degree. His first academic paper or graduation research reportedly concerned circulation and heart replacement.

### Medical Status

Demikhov never completed a conventional medical-school curriculum. This became institutionally important:

- he could conduct animal experiments;
- he could develop surgical methods;
- he could not ordinarily operate therapeutically on living human patients;
- opponents could describe him as an unqualified outsider;
- admirers later emphasized the paradox that a non-physician trained many clinically qualified surgeons in vascular technique.

## Military Service, 1941–1945

Demikhov was mobilized into the Red Army.

Institutional records from the Vishnevsky surgical center identify him as:

- physician or specialist in **Pathological-Anatomical Laboratory No. 57** of the 16th Army, 1941–1944;
- later chief or acting chief pathologist associated with the **11th Guards Army**, 1944–1945;
- a guards senior lieutenant in the administrative or medical service.

He served on the Western Front, in the Baltic and East Prussian campaigns, and then in the Soviet Far East during the war against Japan. His work included:

- autopsying soldiers who died in hospitals and, selectively, on battlefields;
- determining causes of death;
- analyzing patterns of wounds and treatment failures;
- identifying defects in medical evacuation and treatment;
- forensic evaluation of gunshot injuries.

### Self-Inflicted-Wound Anecdote

According to a conversation later reported by Olga, soldiers sometimes shot themselves in an attempt to leave combat. Such conduct could bring execution. Demikhov said that, when serving as a forensic expert, he sometimes softened or concealed evidence that wounds were self-inflicted. When his daughter asked whether he had ever lied, he cited these cases.

This is a **reported family recollection**. It is plausible within the historical context but has not been verified against case files.

Demikhov later said that the war had taken years away from his scientific work. Popular biographies sometimes claim that seeing soldiers die with healthy organs directly inspired his transplantation program. The experience probably reinforced his interests, but the core idea clearly predated the war.

## Early Postwar Career, 1946–1955

### Moscow Fur Institute, 1946–1947

After demobilization, Demikhov worked as an assistant in pathological physiology at the veterinary faculty of the **Moscow Fur Institute**. This position gave him access to animals and operating space.

### Institute of Surgery, 1947–1955

From 1947 to 1955 he worked at the USSR Academy of Medical Sciences’ **Institute of Surgery**, later named for A. V. Vishnevsky. He served as junior researcher and then headed an experimental-surgery laboratory.

**Alexander Alexandrovich Vishnevsky** provided crucial institutional protection. Photographs show them together in 1954. Vishnevsky’s authority enabled Demikhov to continue work that other administrators regarded as sensational, premature, or ethically unacceptable.

### Experimental Transplantation Sequence

#### Additional heart and heart–lung transplantation, 1946

Demikhov developed multiple arrangements for implanting a second heart in a dog’s chest or neck and joining it to the recipient’s circulation.

On **30 June 1946**, a dog survived a heterotopic heart–lung transplantation for approximately 9.5 hours. Later accounts credit Demikhov with the first technically successful intrathoracic transplantation of a heart and combined heart–lung complex in a warm-blooded animal.

He eventually described approximately **twenty-four operative configurations** and performed hundreds of additional-heart experiments.

#### Isolated lung transplantation, 1947

In May 1947 he transplanted a lung in a dog. Bronchial breakdown, thrombosis, infection, and rejection limited survival. Later lung-transplant programs cited these experiments as early proof of technical feasibility.

#### Liver transplantation, 1948

Demikhov developed experimental auxiliary liver transplantation, reportedly beginning in September 1948. Later summaries sometimes call this the first liver transplant; the accurate formulation is one of the earliest technically functioning experimental liver allografts in a warm-blooded animal.

#### Orthotopic heart transplantation, 1951

In 1951 Demikhov removed a dog’s native heart and implanted a donor heart in the normal anatomical position. One often-cited experiment maintained survival for approximately eleven hours.

This was a major technical demonstration but not a durable biological success. Long-term survival awaited understanding and control of immune rejection.

#### Coronary revascularization, 1952–1953

Demikhov developed an anastomosis between the internal thoracic or internal mammary artery and a coronary artery. This anticipated a central technique of coronary bypass surgery.

He performed experimental bypasses in dogs and tested methods on human cadavers. Because he lacked a medical degree and clinical authorization, he did not introduce the operation in living patients.

Soviet surgeon **Vasily Kolesov** later performed successful clinical internal-mammary–coronary anastomosis and acknowledged Demikhov’s experimental priority. Claims that Demikhov alone “invented coronary bypass surgery” should nevertheless distinguish experimental conception from Kolesov’s clinical application.

## The Two-Headed Dogs, 1954–1959

### Procedure

Beginning in 1954, Demikhov transplanted the head, neck, shoulders, and forelimbs of a smaller dog or puppy onto the neck and thoracic circulation of a larger recipient dog.

The smaller animal’s isolated upper body retained:

- blood supply from the recipient;
- brain activity;
- visual, auditory, oral, and swallowing reflexes;
- capacity to lap fluid, although swallowed fluid drained from the disconnected esophagus.

The spinal cords were not joined. This was therefore not transplantation of a head to control a new body, nor a restoration of whole-body function. It was a vascularized upper-body or cephalic graft.

### Numbers and Survival

Accounts variously state that Demikhov performed **twenty**, **twenty-three**, or more such procedures. The best-known 1959 pairing involved a smaller dog called **Shavka** and a larger dog called **Brodyaga**.

Individual animals survived from hours to days. The maximum repeatedly reported survival is **twenty-nine days**. The famous Shavka–Brodyaga animal reportedly survived four days, though online captions frequently confuse it with the 29-day case.

Deaths followed surgical complications, circulatory overload, infection, and immune rejection.

### Scientific Purpose

Demikhov presented the experiments as work on:

- rapid vascular anastomosis;
- maintenance of a grafted complex by a recipient circulation;
- physiological interaction between donor and recipient tissues;
- overcoming tissue incompatibility;
- preserving multiple organs as functioning systems.

They did not prove that an isolated head could be transplanted onto and control another body.

### Publicity

The experiments were filmed and photographed. International journalists, including a *Life* photographer in 1959, produced images that became some of the most recognizable artifacts of Cold War science.

A Soviet film commonly catalogued as **“On the Transplantation of a Dog’s Head”** circulated internationally, including at an exhibition in the United States. News coverage brought Demikhov much greater fame than his less visually shocking heart and lung work.

### Contemporary and Later Criticism

The work generated criticism on two different grounds:

1. **Scientific criticism:** limited survival, failure to solve immune rejection, and uncertainty about what the cephalic graft demonstrated beyond vascular-surgical skill.
2. **Ethical criticism:** creation of severely compromised animals with no prospect of recovery, repeated postoperative suffering, and use of sensational public demonstrations.

A Soviet Ministry of Health commission reportedly judged some of his research unethical and ordered it stopped. Institutional protection allowed at least parts of the program to continue.

Modern animal-welfare writing treats the two-headed dog as a symbol of vivisection and “unnatural barbarism.” These are ethical evaluations, not evidence of fraud. No evidence was found that the operation itself was fabricated: photographs, films, surgical descriptions, witnesses, and publications establish that it occurred.

## First Moscow Medical Institute, 1955–1960

Demikhov moved to the **I. M. Sechenov First Moscow Medical Institute**, working in the Department of Operative Surgery and Topographical Anatomy under **Vladimir Vasilyevich Kovanov**.

The relationship deteriorated. Accounts sympathetic to Demikhov say that Kovanov:

- regarded him as a pseudo-scientist or charlatan;
- opposed organ transplantation;
- prevented or delayed submission of his dissertation;
- tried to restrict his demonstrations and foreign contacts.

Later accounts sometimes present Kovanov as a jealous bureaucratic persecutor. The underlying institutional conflict is well attested, but exact motives are harder to establish. Kovanov was a prominent surgeon and administrator with his own scientific and professional interests. No located source establishes a financial rivalry or criminal misuse of authority.

Demikhov’s lack of a medical degree, his rejection of orthodox immunological explanations, his publicity, and his irregular working methods all contributed to resistance.

---

## Foreign Travel and International Demonstrations, 1958–1960

Despite restrictions on Soviet travel, Demikhov was allowed to demonstrate operations abroad.

### East Germany

On **16 December 1958**, he performed or demonstrated heart–lung transplantation in Leipzig. In 1959 the University of Leipzig awarded him an honorary doctorate.

### West Germany

He also visited West Germany, including Munich, in 1958–1959. His interviews and operations attracted extensive press attention. Soviet officials were reportedly displeased that he spoke freely to foreign journalists and described future human transplantation.

### Sweden

On **16 September 1960**, Demikhov was elected or admitted to the **Royal Scientific Society in Uppsala**. The precise membership category varies in translations.

These trips made him much better known abroad than within the Soviet clinical hierarchy.

## The 1960 Monograph

Demikhov’s central book was:

**Пересадка жизненно важных органов в эксперименте: Опыты по пересадке сердца, лёгких, головы, почек и других органов**  
*Transplantation of Vital Organs in Experiment: Experiments in Transplantation of the Heart, Lungs, Head, Kidneys and Other Organs*  
Moscow: Medgiz, 1960, approximately 260 pages.

The book synthesized his operative methods, results, physiological theories, failures, and proposed applications. It became the first major monograph devoted systematically to experimental transplantation of several vital organs.

Translations included:

- **Experimental Transplantation of Vital Organs**, authorized English translation by Basil Haigh, New York: Consultants Bureau, 1962, 285 pages;
- German translation, 1963;
- Spanish translation, **Transplante experimental de órganos vitales**, Madrid: Editorial Atlante, 1967.

The book was important partly because Soviet articles had been linguistically and politically inaccessible. Western transplantation researchers could now examine Demikhov’s operative diagrams and survival data directly.

## Sklifosovsky Institute, 1960–1986

In 1960 Demikhov moved to the **N. V. Sklifosovsky Research Institute for Emergency Medicine** in Moscow. He remained associated with it until retirement in 1986 and headed or directed a small organ-transplantation laboratory for part of that period.

His facilities were reportedly modest—sometimes described as a basement laboratory—and lacked the resources available to major state surgical institutes.

### Dissertation Defense

Demikhov submitted work based on his 1960 monograph for the Candidate of Biological Sciences degree. After prolonged delays, the Biology Faculty of Moscow State University accepted it for defense in 1963.

The defense became contentious. Accounts agree that:

- the hall was crowded;
- opponents challenged his credentials and methods;
- some critics used terms such as “charlatan”;
- supporters argued that the work greatly exceeded an ordinary candidate dissertation.

Surgeon **Georgy Androsov**, serving as an opponent or reviewer, reportedly said that the thesis was worth several doctoral dissertations—often rendered as “six doctorates.”

The academic council first considered or awarded the candidate degree and then voted to recognize the work as a doctorate. Sources differ on whether this happened in a single extraordinary defense or through immediate reconsideration. Demikhov was formally recorded as **Doctor of Biological Sciences in 1964**.

The dramatic claim that he “defended two dissertations in one day” is a **compressed popular version** of this procedure.

### Organ-Preservation Systems

During the 1960s Demikhov developed what he called a “living physiological system” or proposed bank of revived organs. His concept involved keeping connected organ complexes alive by:

- cross-circulation;
- mechanical ventilation;
- warm sterile chambers;
- supplying nutrients through the gastrointestinal tract;
- connecting viable donor or cadaver organs to a living circulation.

Between approximately 1963 and 1965 he worked on maintaining human organs removed after death or preserved in connected complexes for possible later transplantation. Some popular sources call this “the world’s first bank of living human organs.” That phrase is potentially misleading: it was experimental preservation research, not a functioning modern transplant-organ bank supplying routine clinical operations.

### Immunology and Rejection

Demikhov underestimated immune rejection. He often argued that technically perfect vascular sutures, sterility, donor selection, preliminary cross-circulation, and physiological conditioning could overcome incompatibility. He emphasized the toxicity of contemporary immunosuppressive agents.

By the 1960s, clinical kidney transplantation and transplantation immunology had demonstrated that rejection was a specific immune process, not simply the result of surgical imperfection. Demikhov’s resistance to this conclusion was a significant scientific weakness and contributed to his marginalization.

His exceptionally precise surgical technique could prolong graft function, but it could not prevent chronic or acute immune destruction.

## Christiaan Barnard: Documented Connection and Later Mythology

### What Is Documented

South African surgeon **Christiaan Neethling Barnard** visited the USSR in **May 1960** as a delegate to the Twenty-Seventh All-Union Congress of Surgeons.

A letter dated **9 June 1960**, sent by Barnard after returning to Cape Town, thanked “Professor Demikhov” for cooperation and hospitality, mentioned interesting operations or demonstrations at Demikhov’s laboratory, and referred to the Cape Town laboratory’s own transplantation work.

This establishes:

- Barnard met or at least visited Demikhov’s laboratory in 1960;
- he knew Demikhov’s work before the first human heart transplant;
- Demikhov’s experiments formed part of the international technical background to Barnard’s program.

Barnard later described Demikhov as a teacher and as deserving the title “father of heart and lung transplantation.”

### Disputed Second Visit

A widespread account says Barnard visited Demikhov again in **1963**. Archival research by S. P. Glyantsev and B. M. Gorelik found evidence that Barnard visited the USSR only once before 1967, in May 1960. Barnard himself later misremembered the year as 1962.

Accordingly:

- **1960 visit:** documented fact.
- **1963 second visit:** repeated claim, but not supported by the critical archival reconstruction; probably erroneous.
- **Barnard personally studied under Demikhov for a substantial period:** exaggerated.
- **Demikhov influenced Barnard:** supported in a general intellectual and technical sense, but the degree of direct instruction is uncertain.

### The Telephone-Call Legend

Another story says Barnard telephoned Demikhov immediately before or after the 3 December 1967 Cape Town operation. No telephone record, contemporary diary, or corroborating letter was located. It appears in later memoiristic accounts.

Status: **legend or unverified reported anecdote**.

### The Airport-Reception Legend

A related story says that, after his first transplant, Barnard arrived in Moscow, looked at the officials welcoming him, and asked: “Where is my teacher, Mr. Demikhov?” Officials supposedly replied that Demikhov was too busy to attend.

This story circulated in Russian memoirs and commemorative journalism. The critical Barnard-contact study found no adequate contemporary support.

Status: **post-Soviet hagiographic legend**, influential in portraying Soviet bureaucracy as hiding its own genius.

### Historical Balance

Barnard’s operative technique also depended on the American work of Norman Shumway and Richard Lower, cardiopulmonary bypass, immunology, and the Cape Town surgical team. Treating Barnard’s transplant as simply a copy of Demikhov is unsupported. Conversely, omitting Demikhov from the experimental prehistory also distorts the record.

## Scientific Program and Technologies

### Artificial Circulation

- Two-chamber membrane pump constructed in 1937–1938.
- Internally positioned in the thorax but mechanically driven externally.
- Influenced by or developed in the environment created by Bryukhonenko’s autojektor.
- Intended for resuscitation, physiological research, and temporary replacement of cardiac pumping.

### Heart Transplantation

- Heterotopic or additional-heart models.
- Orthotopic heart replacement.
- More than twenty operative schemes.
- Use of direct vascular anastomoses without modern bypass technology in early experiments.
- Survival ranged from hours to approximately one month in selected additional-heart animals.

### Heart–Lung Transplantation

- En-bloc transplantation of donor heart and lungs.
- First technically successful warm-blooded-animal procedures in 1946.
- Demonstrations in Moscow and Leipzig.

### Lung Transplantation

- Isolated lung grafting beginning in 1947.
- Research on bronchial healing and vascular attachment.
- Failure from bronchial complications and rejection.

### Liver and Kidney Transplantation

- Auxiliary liver experiments from 1948.
- Kidney transplantation included in his monograph, though he is not credited with the decisive immunological or clinical kidney-transplant breakthrough.

### Coronary Surgery

- Internal mammary–coronary anastomosis.
- Experimental bypass in dogs.
- Cadaver testing.
- Important predecessor of clinical coronary artery bypass surgery.

### Vascular Suturing

Demikhov became known for speed and accuracy in hand-sewn vascular anastomosis. Visitors observed his operations partly to learn these techniques.

### Biological Valve and Other Procedures

Russian historical reviews attribute to him experiments involving:

- bioprosthetic aortic valves;
- left-ventricular–aortic anastomosis;
- transplantation of abdominal organ complexes;
- union of animal body halves;
- cross-circulation;
- resuscitation of organ complexes after systemic death.

### Patents and Commercialization

No substantial commercial patent portfolio or private licensing operation was located. Soviet research inventions generally belonged to state institutions and were often documented through author’s certificates rather than Western-style private patents. No reliable complete list of Demikhov author’s certificates was found in accessible sources.

## Intellectual and Scientific Development

Demikhov’s guiding idea was that the failing biological organ should be replaced by a functioning biological organ whenever possible.

His thought developed through several stages:

1. **Mechanical substitution:** student work on an artificial pump.
2. **Biological assistance:** grafting an additional heart to support circulation.
3. **Total organ replacement:** orthotopic transplantation.
4. **Complex transplantation:** heart–lung blocks, heads and upper bodies, abdominal organ complexes.
5. **Physiological preservation:** keeping organs functioning outside or across bodies.
6. **Clinical aspiration:** advocating transplantation in humans.

He combined technical audacity with a comparatively weak understanding of transplant immunology. His conceptual model was physiological and surgical: establish blood flow quickly, maintain sterility, and the organ might adapt. As immune rejection became central to the field, Demikhov’s framework became increasingly incomplete.

His 1953 article placing transplantation “in accordance with Michurinist theory” shows accommodation to compulsory Soviet biological ideology. It does not demonstrate that Lysenkoism caused his transplantation program, which originated earlier.

No coherent religious, metaphysical, or political philosophy authored by Demikhov has been identified. He publicly framed his work in Soviet humanitarian and scientific terms: saving lives, replacing organs, rehabilitating wounded people, and overcoming death from organ failure.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### P. M. Nikiforovsky

First major physiology teacher at Voronezh. Arrested after Demikhov’s departure in 1938. Direct influence is documented; detailed later contact is unknown.

### V. L. Fedder

Laboratory assistant who supervised student work and helped with early animal experiments.

### Lev Yakovlevich Ratgauser

Fellow Voronezh student and collaborator who petitioned with Demikhov for transfer to MSU. Their later paths separated.

### Sergei S. Bryukhonenko

Probable formative influence through the autojektor and Moscow institute. A direct mentor–student relationship is a strong inference but not fully documented by surviving correspondence.

### Alexander A. Vishnevsky

Institutional patron and protector at the Institute of Surgery. His authority gave Demikhov laboratory space and credibility.

### Vladimir M. Goryainov

Co-author and experimental collaborator, including work on additional-heart transplantation. He appears in photographs and reports concerning the transplantation program.

### Vladimir V. Kovanov

Department head and institutional opponent at the First Moscow Medical Institute. Their conflict concerned scientific legitimacy, control of the laboratory, dissertation approval, and professional status.

### Georgy M. Androsov

Surgeon and dissertation reviewer who strongly supported recognition of the work as doctoral-level research.

### Aron Yevseyevich Gurvich

Reported friend from Demikhov’s university period and later immunochemist. Later accounts credit him with helping secure the MSU dissertation defense. The friendship is reported in Russian commemorative sources; a complete correspondence has not been published.

### Vasily I. Kolesov

Clinical coronary surgeon who acknowledged Demikhov’s earlier experimental mammary–coronary work.

### Christiaan Barnard

Documented visitor in 1960; later credited Demikhov as a teacher and pioneer. The duration and intimacy of the relationship were amplified in later legend.

### Foreign Visitors

Numerous American, European, and other foreign surgeons attempted to see Demikhov’s laboratory after publication of the English translation. Precise visitor lists are incomplete.

## Political Activity

No evidence was found that Demikhov held major Communist Party office, participated in dissident activity, or exercised political power.

His career was nevertheless conditioned by Soviet politics:

- access to laboratory animals and institutions depended on state administrators;
- foreign travel required official permission;
- his 1953 writing adopted Michurinist vocabulary;
- health-ministry committees could halt research;
- his work was alternately used as evidence of Soviet scientific modernity and restricted as embarrassing sensationalism;
- publicity abroad exposed tensions between state secrecy, professional hierarchy, and scientific prestige.

Stories that the Soviet state completely “banned” or “erased” Demikhov are overstated. He remained employed, published a monograph, traveled abroad, defended a doctorate, and headed a laboratory. The more accurate conclusion is that he received intermittent support while being denied the resources and clinical authority he sought.

## Finances, Property, and Institutional Resources

No private companies, investment vehicles, or major property holdings are documented.

His financial position appears to have consisted of:

- Soviet institutional salary;
- military benefits and decorations;
- later prize money associated with state awards;
- state-provided housing;
- possible publication remuneration.

Family testimony depicts prolonged material austerity. His wife’s salary as an engineer was sometimes greater than his. There is no evidence that foreign surgeons or pharmaceutical companies secretly financed his research.

The contrast between his international reputation and the reported poverty of his laboratory became a central part of the later “unrecognized genius” narrative.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Ethical Questions

### Animal Suffering

**Documented fact:** Demikhov repeatedly performed highly invasive experiments on dogs. Many animals died within hours or days; even the longest-lived graft recipients eventually died or were killed.

**Documented fact:** the two-headed-dog procedures did not reconnect the spinal cord and could not yield an independently mobile second animal.

**Reported fact:** contemporary observers, animal-welfare advocates, and some medical officials condemned the experiments.

**Unresolved question:** surviving publications do not provide enough detail to reconstruct anesthesia, analgesia, humane endpoints, and postoperative care to present-day standards.

**Historical context:** ethical oversight of animal research in the 1940s–1950s differed substantially from modern institutional review. This contextual fact neither proves absence of suffering nor establishes misconduct under rules that did not yet exist.

### Ministry of Health Intervention

A historical review reports that a Soviet Ministry of Health commission considered Demikhov’s work unethical and ordered it stopped. His institutional supporters apparently enabled continuation or resumption.

The exact commission report was not located online; this remains a **reported institutional action**, not a fully reproduced primary document.

### “Charlatanism”

Critics reportedly called Demikhov a charlatan or pseudo-scientist, especially during institutional conflicts and his dissertation defense.

No evidence establishes scientific fraud. His central operations were photographed, filmed, witnessed, described in protocols, and replicated in related forms by other laboratories. The criticism concerned:

- medical qualifications;
- sensationalism;
- short survival;
- theoretical errors concerning rejection;
- ethical acceptability;
- premature claims about clinical application.

Accordingly, “charlatan” is documented as a hostile label, not a demonstrated finding.

### Immunological Error

Demikhov’s underestimation of immune rejection was a genuine scientific error. He sometimes attributed graft death primarily to thrombosis, imperfect suturing, infection, or inadequate physiological preparation.

This error limited the transition from technical feasibility to sustained transplantation. It is not evidence that the observed short-term graft function was fraudulent.

### Human Experimentation

No evidence was found that Demikhov transplanted organs, heads, or body complexes into living human subjects.

He reportedly conducted vascular work on cadavers and maintained organs recovered after death. Claims that he built living “human hybrids” or secretly conducted head transplants on prisoners are **conspiracy narratives without supporting evidence**.

### War-Crime or Intelligence Allegations

No evidence was found of involvement in Soviet security services, prisoner experimentation, political repression, or war crimes. His only ethically ambiguous military anecdote concerns allegedly protecting soldiers with self-inflicted wounds from punishment.

### Lawsuits, Fraud, Corruption, and Financial Crime

The dedicated adversarial search found no relevant lawsuit, indictment, conviction, corruption investigation, fraud allegation, money-laundering connection, or financial scandal involving the scientist.

Recent Russian reports about a businessman named **Vladimir Demikhov**, including extradition and fraud proceedings in 2025–2026, concern a different, much younger person and have no relationship to Vladimir Petrovich Demikhov.

## Dedicated Adversarial Search: Findings Through 2026

Searches in English and Russian combined Demikhov’s name with terms corresponding to controversy, scandal, accusations, lawsuit, fraud, conspiracy, charlatanism, cruelty, criticism, and allegations.

The substantive negative or contested findings were:

- extensive criticism of the suffering caused to experimental dogs;
- a reported Soviet ethical ruling against his work;
- allegations of charlatanism made by professional opponents;
- criticism that the head-grafting program produced publicity disproportionate to scientific value;
- his incorrect or incomplete theory of rejection;
- disputed priority claims for the artificial heart, coronary bypass, and transplantation procedures;
- exaggeration of Barnard’s dependence on him;
- later folklore portraying him either as a persecuted saint of science or a sadistic “mad scientist.”

No sourced allegation of fabricated operations, falsified survival data, criminal experimentation on humans, commercial fraud, or secret police activity was found.

Recent 2024–2026 treatments continue to debate animal ethics but introduce no major new documentary scandal.

## Illness, Accidents, and Later Decline

Demikhov reportedly suffered a **stroke in 1968**, followed by memory problems. The date and clinical severity are reported in popular and family-derived biographies but not supported here by an accessible hospital record.

He nevertheless continued working at the Sklifosovsky Institute until 1986.

Accounts of his later years describe:

- declining health;
- reduced institutional influence;
- modest housing;
- periodic visits by foreign admirers;
- belated Soviet and Russian honors.

No reliable evidence was found of addiction, psychiatric hospitalization, or a diagnosed psychological disorder.

## Awards, Degrees, and Honors

Documented or consistently reported honors include:

- military medals and decorations for Second World War service;
- honorary doctorate, **University of Leipzig**, 1959;
- membership or honorary membership in the **Royal Scientific Society of Uppsala**, 1960;
- Doctor of Biological Sciences, formalized 1964;
- **USSR State Prize**, 1988, apparently shared for work connected with transplantation or cardiovascular surgery;
- **Order “For Merit to the Fatherland,” Third Class**, awarded by Presidential Decree No. 739 of 30 June 1998 for service to the state and major personal contribution to medical science;
- **Prize of the Government of the Russian Federation**, 1998, reported in Russian historical literature.

Some secondary sources incorrectly state that the 1998 Order was Fourth Class. The text of Presidential Decree No. 739 specifies **Third Class**.

## Later Life and Death

Demikhov retired from or ended regular work at the Sklifosovsky Institute in **1986**.

The late Soviet period brought partial rehabilitation of his reputation. The USSR State Prize in 1988 and increased historical interest placed him within the official genealogy of transplantation and cardiac surgery.

On 30 June 1998 President Boris Yeltsin’s decree awarded him the Order “For Merit to the Fatherland,” Third Class. Accounts state that the award came only months before his death.

Demikhov died in his Moscow apartment on **22 November 1998**, aged eighty-two. The generally reported cause is rupture of an aneurysm. He was buried at Vagankovskoye Cemetery.

Liya Nikolayevna also died in 1998. Precise information about whether her death preceded or followed his was not established.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Demikhov invented the first artificial heart”

**Story:** In 1937 Demikhov independently invented and implanted the world’s first artificial heart, keeping a dog alive for five or more hours.

**Evidence:** A 1938 university newspaper proves that he constructed a mechanical device and used it experimentally. His later descriptions establish its intended intrathoracic placement.

**Against or qualifying evidence:** Bryukhonenko’s earlier autojektor was probably an important prototype. Contemporary reporting does not verify every later claim about implantation or duration. Survival figures conflict.

**Effect:** It made Demikhov an origin figure in artificial-heart history.

### “He sold his only suit to buy materials”

**Story:** The impoverished student sold his only suit to purchase silver for the artificial heart.

**Appearance and circulation:** Russian popular biographies and patriotic science journalism.

**Evidence:** No contemporaneous receipt, letter, or student record located.

**Status:** Unverified hagiographic anecdote.

### “He created a true two-headed dog”

**Story:** Demikhov created one dog with two functioning brains controlling a shared body.

**Evidence:** He grafted the vascularized head and forequarters of one dog onto another.

**Qualification:** The second spinal cord was not connected to the recipient body. The graft could perceive and move its own face and forelegs but could not control the host body.

**Status:** The photographs are genuine; “two-headed dog” is visually accurate but physiologically simplified.

### “The dogs fought each other”

Online captions sometimes say the two heads barked at or bit one another. Some filmed behavior may be interpreted as irritation or defensive reaction, but no systematic behavioral record establishes a continuing “fight.”

**Status:** Possible observed behavior transformed into folklore.

### “The smaller dog could drink normally”

The grafted head could lap and swallow, but its esophagus was disconnected; liquid drained externally.

**Status:** Genuine reflex often misleadingly presented as normal feeding.

### “The longest-lived animal was the famous Shavka–Brodyaga pair”

The photographed 1959 pair is commonly said to have lived twenty-nine days. Better accounts distinguish the famous pair, which survived about four days, from another animal that reached twenty-nine days.

**Status:** Recurrent caption error.

### “The Soviet government totally banned and erased him”

Demikhov faced real opposition and resource restrictions, but he remained employed, published, traveled, defended a doctorate, and received state honors.

**Status:** Exaggerated persecution narrative.

### “Barnard was Demikhov’s direct pupil”

Barnard visited the laboratory and later honored Demikhov as a teacher. Evidence does not show a sustained formal apprenticeship.

**Status:** Symbolically true in Barnard’s retrospective language; institutionally and biographically exaggerated.

### “Barnard visited in both 1960 and 1963”

Critical archival research supports only May 1960.

**Status:** Repeated but probably false.

### “Barnard phoned immediately after the first transplant”

No contemporary record located.

**Status:** Legend.

### “Barnard demanded Demikhov at a Moscow reception”

No adequate contemporary evidence located.

**Status:** Post-Soviet commemorative legend.

### “Demikhov secretly experimented on human prisoners”

No documentary evidence was found in institutional histories, medical literature, memoirs, or the adversarial search.

**Status:** Unsupported conspiracy narrative.

### “His two-headed dogs directly made modern transplantation possible”

The experiments contributed to vascular technique and demonstrated that a large composite graft could be perfused. Modern transplantation depended more directly on immunology, organ preservation, anesthesia, intensive care, cardiopulmonary bypass, antibiotics, and many laboratories.

**Status:** Overcompressed causal claim.

### “He was merely a cruel showman”

The two-headed dogs attracted sensational publicity, but Demikhov’s heart, lung, coronary, and preservation work preceded and exceeded those demonstrations.

**Status:** Hostile mythology based on a real and ethically controversial program.

## Successes and Failures

### Principal Successes

- Demonstrated repeatable intrathoracic additional-heart transplantation.
- Performed pioneering experimental heart–lung, isolated-lung, auxiliary-liver, and orthotopic-heart procedures.
- Developed numerous vascular-connection configurations.
- Anticipated internal-mammary–coronary bypass.
- Produced the first comprehensive multiorgan experimental-transplantation monograph.
- Influenced international transplantation and cardiac-surgery research.
- Demonstrated the importance of speed, vascular patency, and immediate graft perfusion.
- Helped establish transplantation as a technically conceivable field.

### Principal Failures and Limitations

- Did not solve immune rejection.
- Often treated technical perfection as sufficient for biological acceptance.
- Did not translate his work into a durable clinical human transplant program.
- Lacked a medical degree and broad clinical team.
- Generated extreme animal suffering by present standards and controversy even in his own period.
- Allowed the visually sensational head experiments to overshadow other work.
- Had incomplete institutional and financial support.
- Some priority claims were later inflated by admirers.
- His organ-bank concepts did not become a practical clinical service in his lifetime.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

After his death, Demikhov’s reputation expanded markedly in Russia.

Institutional commemoration has included:

- monuments and memorial plaques;
- anniversary conferences;
- museum exhibits;
- lectures and prizes bearing his name;
- republication and translation of his papers;
- inclusion in histories of transplantation, coronary surgery, and artificial organs.

His legacy divides into several partly independent reputations:

1. **Technical pioneer:** experimental proof that complex thoracic-organ transplantation was surgically possible.
2. **Precursor of coronary bypass:** developer of mammary–coronary anastomosis in animals.
3. **Artificial-heart pioneer:** constructor of an early implantable mechanical pump.
4. **Victim of bureaucracy:** Russian commemorative narratives portray him as blocked by credentialism and jealousy.
5. **Animal-experimentation symbol:** the two-headed dog represents the ethical dangers of unconstrained experimental medicine.
6. **Mad-scientist icon:** photographs circulate detached from medical context in horror, meme, conspiracy, and “strange history” culture.
7. **Head-transplant ancestor:** advocates and critics of proposed human head or body transplantation cite him, often without distinguishing vascular grafting from spinal-cord reconnection.

Demikhov’s name has also been adopted by cultural projects, including a European noise-core band and recordings titled after *Experimental Transplantation of Vital Organs*. This is symbolic appropriation, not biographical connection.
