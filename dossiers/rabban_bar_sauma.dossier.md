# Rabban Bar Sauma: Research Dossier

## Research Scope and Evidentiary Framework

Rabban Bar Sauma’s life is preserved principally in the anonymous Syriac *History of Mar Yahballaha and Rabban Sauma*. The work was composed after Yahballaha III’s death in 1317 and probably completed by 1319. Its compiler says explicitly that he used and heavily abridged—and sometimes amplified—a Persian narrative written by Bar Sauma. That Persian original is lost.

The principal modern critical edition is Pier Giorgio Borbone’s *History of Mar Yahballaha and Rabban Sauma* (2020/2021), based on the three surviving Syriac manuscript witnesses. Earlier editions and translations by Paul Bedjan, Jean-Baptiste Chabot, James A. Montgomery, and E. A. Wallis Budge remain historically important but contain obsolete identifications and occasional mistranslations.

Throughout this dossier:

- **Documented fact** means directly stated in the Syriac history and, where possible, corroborated by independent Latin, Mongol, Persian, Armenian, or institutional evidence.
- **Reported fact** means stated in the principal narrative but not independently verified.
- **Disputed reconstruction** identifies competing modern interpretations.
- **Hagiographic claim, legend, or reported relic tradition** denotes material presented as sacred history or miracle rather than independently demonstrable fact.
- Dates before 1287 are frequently approximate because the narrative supplies few absolute dates.

## Basic Identifying Information

### Names and titles

- **Syriac:** ܪܒܢ ܒܪ ܨܘܡܐ, *Rabban Bar Ṣawma* or *Rabban Ṣawma*.
- **Common English forms:** Rabban Bar Sauma, Rabban Bar Sawma, Rabban Sauma, Rabban Sawma.
- **Older French form:** Rabban Çauma.
- **Chinese transcriptions:** 拉班·扫马 and variants such as 拉賓掃務瑪.
- **Name meaning:** *Bar Ṣawma* is conventionally translated “son of the fast” or “son of fasting.” The narrative connects the name to the fasting and prayers by which his parents sought a child.
- **Rabban:** Syriac honorific meaning “our master” or “teacher,” commonly given to distinguished monks and ecclesiastical scholars. It was not his birth name.
- **Ecclesiastical titles:** monk, priest or cleric, church keeper, teacher of disciples, patriarchal steward or governor, and **Visitor-General** (*periodeutes*) of the eastern Christians or “Eastern Turks.”
- **Diplomatic capacity:** plenipotentiary or ambassador of the Ilkhan Arghun and Catholicos Yahballaha III to the Byzantine emperor, Roman curia, king of France, and king of England.

The Syriac text also refers to his father Shiban as a *saʿora* or *periodeutes*. Older translations render this uncertain office as “inspector” or “visitor.”

### Birth

- **Date:** not securely known.
- **Strongest scholarly range:** approximately **1220–1230**.
- Modern reference works commonly print **c. 1220** or **c. 1225**.
- The narrative says he entered an explicitly ascetic vocation at twenty, delayed three years for his parents, and was considered old by the late 1280s. It does not give his birth year.
- A birth around 1220–1225 fits his description as elderly during the embassy of 1287–1288. The occasionally encountered date “c. 1260” is incompatible with that chronology and appears to be an error.

### Birthplace

- **Khan Baligh/Khanbaliq**, the Mongol capital corresponding to modern Beijing.
- Depending on the proposed birth year, the city was still historically associated with the former Jin capital Zhongdu before its reconstruction as the Yuan capital Dadu/Khanbaliq. Calling his exact birthplace “Beijing” is geographically convenient but administratively anachronistic in some reconstructions.

### Ethnic and political identity

- The earliest narrative calls Bar Sauma and Markos “Eastern Turks” and places Bar Sauma in a prominent Christian family at Khanbaliq.
- Later medieval and modern writers variously classify him as **Önggüd/Ongut**, **Uyghur**, Turkic, Mongol, Chinese-born, or combinations of these.
- **Best-supported formulation:** a Chinese-born Turkic Christian operating within the Mongol imperial world, probably connected to the Önggüd Christian population.
- **Disputed reconstruction:** Gregory Bar Hebraeus has been cited for a Uyghur identification, while Chinese and Mongol contextual evidence has encouraged an Önggüd identification. “Uyghur” and “Önggüd” should not be treated as interchangeable without qualification.
- He was not ethnically Han Chinese, as far as the surviving evidence shows.
- Modern nationality labels such as “Chinese diplomat,” “Mongolian traveler,” or “Persian ambassador” describe birthplace, imperial setting, or employment rather than an established medieval nationality.

### Religion

- Christian of the **Church of the East**, traditionally called “Nestorian” in older Western literature.
- The modern Church of the East does not simply identify its theology with every proposition attributed to Nestorius; “Nestorian” is therefore retained here only when describing medieval Western classification or older scholarship.
- Bar Sauma’s preserved confession expresses East-Syriac dyophysite Christology and rejects the Latin *filioque* formulation that the Holy Spirit proceeds from both Father and Son.

### Death

- **Date:** 10 January 1294, according to the internal liturgical dating in the Syriac history.
- **Place of final illness:** his fever began around Shahrazur; he traveled through Erbil and died after reaching Baghdad or while attached to the patriarchal establishment there.
- **Burial:** at the *Darath Rhomaye* in Baghdad, north of the altar and outside the inner court, on the south side of the house of prayer.
- Some modern summaries give Maragha or Erbil as the place of death. The detailed narrative instead places his final sequence at Shahrazur, Erbil, and Baghdad and describes his burial at Baghdad. Baghdad is consequently the strongest conclusion.

## Family and Ancestry

### Father: Shiban

The Syriac biography describes **Shiban** as:

- a believing Christian;
- noble and God-fearing;
- wealthy;
- a member of a famous family and well-known tribe;
- a *saʿora* or ecclesiastical inspector/visitor;
- resident in Khanbaliq.

The laudatory adjectives are characteristic of hagiographic biography, but the combination of wealth, ecclesiastical standing, and access to education plausibly places the family among Khanbaliq’s established Christian elite.

### Mother: Keyamta

Bar Sauma’s mother is named **Keyamta**. The text says Shiban and Keyamta remained childless for a long time and prayed repeatedly for a son. His conception and birth are framed as an answer to prayer.

### Siblings

The biography presents him as the couple’s only son and heir. No siblings are named. His parents’ objections to his monastic vocation center on the extinction of their family line and the destination of their property.

### Betrothal, spouse, and descendants

The narrative says his parents **betrothed him to a maiden** after his ecclesiastical education. It does not name her or say that a marriage was completed.

No spouse, partner, or child is securely recorded. The parents’ fear that their “seed and name” would disappear indicates that he had no recognized heir when he embraced monasticism. The opening theological preface refers generally to renouncing “wife and children,” but it should not be treated as proof that Bar Sauma personally had either.

### Ancestry

No genealogical line beyond his parents survives. Claims that he descended from a specifically named Uyghur or Önggüd house are modern reconstructions rather than documented genealogy.

## Childhood and Early Life

The narrative contains no anecdotes securely attributable to his childhood. It says that, when old enough to learn, his parents placed him with an unnamed “worthy teacher.” He was trained diligently in ecclesiastical learning.

His education probably included:

- Church-of-the-East scripture, doctrine, liturgy, and hymnody;
- reading or recitation in Syriac;
- practical clerical administration;
- one or more spoken Turkic languages;
- languages necessary for life in Yuan Khanbaliq.

His later embassy required communication through interpreters in the Latin West. The claim that he was fluent in Chinese, Turkic, Persian, and Syriac is plausible in varying degrees but cannot be demonstrated equally for each language. The surviving narrative confirms that his own travel report was in Persian, although whether he personally wrote it or dictated it is uncertain.

The biography says he was ordained or admitted to a priestly grade, entered the clergy, and became keeper of a church in Khanbaliq. Modern writers sometimes call this “court employment,” but the primary account does not identify the church as a palace church or Bar Sauma as a direct officer of Kublai Khan.

## Education and Formation

### Unnamed childhood teacher

Bar Sauma’s first teacher is described only as worthy and responsible for his ecclesiastical education. No school or monastery is named.

### Mar Giwargis/George

After Bar Sauma resolved to leave secular life, **Mar Giwargis (George), metropolitan**, gave him the monastic tonsure. This is the only named senior ecclesiastic directly attached to his own profession as a monk.

### Ascetic formation

At approximately twenty, according to the narrative’s relative chronology, Bar Sauma adopted strict abstinence:

- rejection of luxurious food;
- rejection of intoxicating drink;
- chastity;
- humility;
- sustained prayer and ecclesiastical service.

His parents persuaded him to remain with them for another three years. At approximately twenty-three he distributed his personal clothing and possessions to the poor, received the monastic habit and tonsure, and lived for seven years in a monastic cell.

He then sought deeper isolation. He found a cave near a spring in mountainous country approximately a day’s journey from Khanbaliq and lived there as an anchorite. His reputation attracted visitors seeking instruction.

### Intellectual profile

Bar Sauma’s recorded questioning by Roman cardinals shows command of East-Syriac Trinitarian and Christological language. He reasoned through analogies involving soul, reason, life, sun, light, heat, Adam, Seth, and Eve. The narrative may polish the dialogue, but his selection as ambassador because he was “wise,” capable, and linguistically qualified supports a reputation for theological and administrative competence.

## Markos/Yahballaha III: Disciple, Companion, and Institutional Partner

A young Christian named **Markos**, born in 1245, came from Kawshang/Koshang. His father, Bayniel, was an archdeacon with four sons. Markos sought Bar Sauma after hearing of the anchorite’s reputation.

Bar Sauma initially warned him that monastic life was difficult even for old men. When Markos persisted, he accepted him, taught him, clothed him in a hair garment, and trained him in ascetic labor. After three years, Markos received the monastic schema from Metropolitan Mar Nestorios.

The relationship subsequently passed through several forms:

1. teacher and disciple;
2. fellow monks;
3. pilgrimage companions;
4. metropolitan and Visitor-General;
5. patriarch and senior patriarchal administrator;
6. close personal friends whose careers remained intertwined.

When Markos became Catholicos-Patriarch **Yahballaha III**, Bar Sauma formally became subordinate to his former pupil. The biography nevertheless presents him as Yahballaha’s counselor, interpreter of dreams, household governor, diplomatic representative, “strong arm,” and emotional support.

No evidence supports a familial or sexual relationship. Their intimacy is documented in the institutional and spiritual language of medieval monastic companionship.

## Chronological Life History

## Monastic Life near Khanbaliq, approximately 1240s–1270s

After seven years in a conventional cell, Bar Sauma withdrew to a mountain cave beside a spring. He acquired a reputation as a holy teacher. Visitors came to hear him, and this reputation brought Markos to him.

The narrative does not identify the exact mountain or monastery. Proposed locations around medieval Beijing remain conjectural.

Bar Sauma and Markos eventually concluded that spiritual benefit required a pilgrimage westward. Their goals were:

- to visit the tombs and shrines of martyrs and Church fathers;
- to receive blessings from western monasteries and bishops;
- ultimately to reach Jerusalem;
- to obtain remission of sins associated with pilgrimage to the holy places.

Their decision was initially opposed by Markos’s relatives and by local Christian notables, who emphasized that western clergy were being brought east and questioned why two respected monks should leave.

### Relationship to Kublai Khan

The principal narrative says that two local lords—**Konbogha** and **Ifogha/Ibogha**, sons-in-law of Kublai Khan—summoned the monks, attempted to dissuade them, and then supplied animals, gold, silver, clothing, and rugs. The monks accepted these as a trust to be spent if necessary or distributed to western monasteries if left over.

Many modern accounts say Kublai Khan personally authorized or financed the pilgrimage. That is possible within the imperial system, but the surviving narrative specifically documents the intervention of his sons-in-law, not a personal audience with Kublai. Claims that Bar Sauma was “sent west by Kublai” conflate this pilgrimage with his later embassy for Arghun.

---

## First Transcontinental Journey, approximately 1275–1280

The departure is usually dated **1275 or 1276**, though dates as late as 1278 appear in secondary literature. Borbone’s critical reconstruction favors approximately 1274–1275.

### Khanbaliq to Tangut

The pilgrims traveled west from Khanbaliq to **Tangut**, probably through territory associated with the former Western Xia realm. Local Christians welcomed them in large numbers, gave gifts, requested blessings, and escorted them onward.

### Tangut to Khotan

They spent approximately two months crossing a sparsely inhabited region to **Khotan/Loton**. The narrative emphasizes:

- bitter water;
- lack of cultivation;
- physical exhaustion;
- the dangers of desert travel.

Later popular accounts embellish this with specific dune heights and sandstorm episodes not stated in the surviving medieval text.

### Kashgar and war

At or near **Kashgar**, they encountered insecurity associated with warfare among Mongol rulers. The source says the inhabitants had fled and the region was disturbed.

### Talas and Kaidu

They reached the encampment of **Kaidu**, a grandson of Ögedei and political rival of Kublai, near the Talas region. They obtained a written order protecting their passage through his territory.

This audience illustrates the value of Mongol imperial documentation and elite sponsorship, but it does not mean Kaidu joined or endorsed their religious project.

### Khorasan and Tus

After severe difficulty, fear, exhaustion, and the loss of much of their property, they reached **Khorasan**. Near **Tus**, they rested at the monastery of Mar Sehyon/Zion. The resident bishop and monks received them. The account compares their relief to being born anew.

### Azerbaijan and Maragha

They traveled toward Azerbaijan, intending to reach Baghdad and the Catholicos **Mar Denha I**. Denha was then at **Maragha**, the former Ilkhanid capital, and received them there.

Bar Sauma and Markos identified themselves as Christians from Khanbaliq and expressed their intention to visit Baghdad, the monasteries of Mesopotamia, and—if a road opened—Jerusalem.

### Mesopotamian pilgrimage

With Denha’s authorization, they visited:

- Baghdad;
- the great Church of Kokhe, associated with Seleucia-Ctesiphon;
- the traditional tomb or shrine of the apostle Mar Mari;
- monasteries and ecclesiastical centers in Beth Garmai;
- Nisibis;
- other holy places in northern Mesopotamia.

The source describes these visits in devotional rather than logistical detail.

### Armenia, Ani, and the blocked route

The monks proceeded through Armenia and visited the ruined but still monumental city of **Ani**, where they saw churches and monasteries. They then attempted to travel through **Georgia** toward Jerusalem.

Local reports warned that the road had been cut by killing and robbery. Modern Georgian scholarship associates this insecurity with resistance to Ilkhanid power and the disruptions following the earthquake of 1275, but that causal reconstruction is not explicit in the Syriac text.

They turned back. The narrative treats this not as abandonment of faith but as an unavoidable failure caused by war and unsafe roads.

### Jerusalem: never reached

Bar Sauma never entered Jerusalem. Repeated popular descriptions of him as a pilgrim “to Jerusalem” refer to his intention, not successful arrival.

Denha advised that sincere visits to the regional shrines were spiritually no less valuable than reaching Jerusalem under impossible conditions.

---

## Appointment as Visitor-General, approximately 1280

Denha decided to consecrate Markos as metropolitan of **Cathay and Ong**, giving him the name **Yahballaha**, and appointed Bar Sauma **Visitor-General**.

The precise responsibilities of the office are imperfectly known. They probably included:

- inspection of churches and monasteries;
- doctrinal and disciplinary supervision;
- representation of the patriarch;
- teaching;
- liaison among distant eastern communities.

Denha intended to send both men back to China. They resisted, citing the hardships already endured and expressing a wish to remain in a monastery until death. They eventually accepted the appointments in obedience.

The eastern road then became unusable because of warfare across the Oxus frontier. Bar Sauma and Yahballaha stayed approximately two years at the monastery of **Mar Michael of Tarʿil**.

### Dream interpretations

The narrative gives Bar Sauma a prophetic role. Yahballaha dreamed:

- of reaching for a cross that rose above him;
- of eating fruit and distributing it to a crowd;
- of sitting on a high throne while his tongue divided into three fiery parts.

Bar Sauma interpreted these dreams as signs that Yahballaha would attain episcopal or patriarchal authority and distribute spiritual gifts.

**Evidentiary label: hagiographic claim.** These dream narratives appear in the early-fourteenth-century ecclesiastical biography. They cannot be independently verified and function literarily to establish divine election.

---

## Election of Yahballaha III, 1281

Catholicos Denha I died in 1281. Yahballaha was selected as patriarch despite his limited Syriac and his foreign origin. His Mongol-world connections were politically useful to the Church.

Bar Sauma accompanied him to Baghdad and participated in the institutional transition. Yahballaha received consecration at the Church of Kokhe and became **Yahballaha III, Catholicos-Patriarch of the Church of the East**.

Bar Sauma became a leading administrator of the patriarchal household. The later biography calls him governor or steward of Yahballaha’s cell and says his departure would throw its affairs into confusion.

---

## Accusation of Treason under Ahmad Tegüder, 1282–1284

Following Abaqa’s death, **Tegüder**, who had converted to Islam and took the name Ahmad, became Ilkhan.

The Syriac narrative reports that two hostile bishops and Muslim officials accused Yahballaha and Bar Sauma of:

- supporting Arghun;
- sending treasonable communications to Kublai Khan;
- participating in a conspiracy involving the emir of Mosul.

They were summoned to judgment. An investigation followed.

### Evidentiary assessment

- **Documented accusation:** the accusation is preserved in the near-contemporary Syriac history.
- **No conviction:** the investigation reportedly found them innocent; they were released and restored.
- **Biographer’s judgment:** the source calls the charges calumnies motivated by envy.
- **Modern qualification:** Borbone observes that the same author’s representation of Ahmad as a persecutor of Christians does not fit all other evidence and is internally inconsistent with the fair inquiry and acquittal that the narrative itself reports.
- **Unresolved element:** it cannot now be proved whether the accused had informal sympathies for Arghun. Their later close relationship with him makes the allegation politically intelligible, but later patronage does not prove earlier treason.
- No imprisonment of defined length, torture, formal conviction, or punishment is recorded for Bar Sauma.

Arghun defeated and replaced Ahmad in 1284.

---

## Service under Arghun, 1284–1287

Yahballaha and Bar Sauma enjoyed increased standing at Arghun’s court. Arghun sought cooperation with Latin Christian powers against the Mamluk Sultanate, which controlled Syria and Palestine.

Arghun had already sent an embassy to Pope Honorius IV in 1285. When he sought another envoy, he asked Yahballaha for a wise and capable representative. The Syriac history says Bar Sauma was chosen partly because no one else available knew the required language.

This statement probably refers to Persian and the language needed for diplomatic interpretation, not Latin. Bar Sauma relied on interpreters in Europe.

Bar Sauma welcomed the mission. Arghun supplied:

- letters and royal orders;
- gifts for rulers;
- a Mongol *paiza*, giving him authority and access to the relay system;
- thirty riding animals;
- 2,000 *mathqals* of gold.

The modern monetary equivalents printed by older translators are speculative and should not be treated as reliable conversions.

Yahballaha provided separate ecclesiastical letters and gifts for the pope.

---

## Embassy to Europe, 1287–1288

### Composition and purpose

Bar Sauma traveled with clergy from the patriarchal household and diplomatic assistants. Western or later sources name interpreters and companions variously, including **Thomas of Anfus**, **Ugueto/Ugeto**, and **Sabadin**, but the identification and functions of every member are not equally certain.

The embassy had overlapping purposes:

- communicate Arghun’s proposal for military cooperation against the Mamluks;
- coordinate action concerning Syria and Jerusalem;
- establish direct relations between the Church of the East and Rome;
- exchange letters, gifts, blessings, and intelligence;
- survey western Christian institutions and holy sites.

Calling it purely a “Christian alliance” obscures Arghun’s religious position. Arghun was not a baptized Christian; his court accommodated Buddhists, Christians, Muslims, and traditional Mongol practices. His diplomacy was strategic even when expressed through Christian intermediaries.

---

### Constantinople

Bar Sauma traveled through Byzantine territory and crossed the Black Sea or adjoining maritime route with more than 300 people aboard the ship. He preached or discoursed daily to the largely Greek-speaking passengers, presumably through a shared language or interpreters.

At **Constantinople**, Emperor **Andronikos II Palaiologos** received him ceremonially, provided lodging, and exchanged gifts.

Bar Sauma visited **Hagia Sophia** and other shrines. The account attributes to Constantinople:

- an image of Mary reportedly painted by Luke;
- the hand of John the Baptist;
- relics of Lazarus and Mary Magdalene;
- the stone associated with Christ’s tomb and Mary’s ever-renewed tears;
- a vessel from the wedding at Cana;
- the relics of John Chrysostom;
- the stone on which Peter sat when the cock crowed;
- porphyry and green-stone tombs attributed to Constantine and Justinian;
- incorrupt bodies of the 318 bishops of Nicaea;
- icons, statues, and talismans.

These are **reported medieval relic traditions**, not modern identifications. Several items were duplicates of relics claimed elsewhere, a normal feature of medieval sacred geography.

After returning to Andronikos, Bar Sauma requested permission to continue to the Franks. The emperor gave him gold and silver.

---

### Mediterranean crossing and volcanic marvel

During the voyage toward Italy, Bar Sauma saw a smoking, fiery mountain surrounded by sulfurous fumes. The narrative records a local claim that a giant serpent or dragon lived there.

The mountain is often identified as **Etna**, whose eruption of June 1287 helps date the passage. This identification is plausible but not explicitly named in the Syriac text.

- **Documented observation:** smoke, nighttime fire, and sulfur.
- **Reported natural explanation:** volcanic activity.
- **Legend:** the giant serpent.
- The dragon story is important as an example of local Mediterranean folklore recorded by an Asian Christian traveler, not evidence that Bar Sauma personally asserted the creature’s existence.

---

### Naples and the naval battle, June 1287

After an exceptionally difficult passage described as lasting two months, the embassy landed at **Naples**.

Bar Sauma met the ruler identified in the text as “King Charles,” probably **Charles II of Anjou**, then entangled in the War of the Sicilian Vespers.

From a rooftop, Bar Sauma and his companions watched a naval engagement between Angevin and Aragonese forces. The narrative says the Aragonese defeated Charles’s force, killed 12,000 men, and sank ships.

Modern editors connect the event with the battle off Naples on **23–24 June 1287**, in which the Aragonese admiral Roger of Lauria defeated the Angevin fleet.

- The occurrence of the battle is independently historical.
- The figure of **12,000 dead** is probably inflated or derived from report rather than direct counting.
- The narrative praises the Franks for harming only combatants. That observation should not be generalized into a reliable description of medieval European warfare; it records what the party believed it saw in one naval action.

---

### First visit to Rome, summer 1287

Pope **Honorius IV** died on 3 April 1287. Bar Sauma arrived during the resulting conclave and therefore initially met a group of cardinals rather than a pope.

The narrative calls them “twelve,” though the exact composition of the conclave varied with absences and deaths.

The cardinals questioned him about:

- his homeland;
- the seat and apostolic origins of the Church of the East;
- his office;
- why a Christian cleric represented a Mongol ruler;
- his Christology and Trinitarian doctrine.

Bar Sauma said that Thomas, Addai, and Mari had evangelized the East, and that eastern Christians had preserved their teaching without instruction from the contemporary papacy.

### Theological examination and the *filioque*

Bar Sauma confessed:

- one God;
- Father, Son, and Holy Spirit as three coequal persons;
- Christ as perfect God and perfect man;
- two natures and associated East-Syriac Christological terminology.

When the cardinals said the Son was a cause of the Holy Spirit, he challenged them. His reasoning maintained the Father as cause of Son and Spirit and rejected assigning multiple causes within the Trinity.

The cardinals eventually said they had been testing him. The narrative says they admired his argumentative ability.

This dialogue is important but textually mediated. The surviving form is:

1. Bar Sauma’s experience;
2. possibly recorded in his lost Persian report;
3. translated and abridged into Syriac;
4. preserved in later manuscripts;
5. translated again by modern editors.

It should not be treated as a verbatim transcript.

Bar Sauma ended the debate by saying that he had not come to dispute doctrine but to receive the pope’s blessing, visit the shrines, and communicate Arghun’s message.

### Roman sacred sites

The delegation visited:

- old St. Peter’s;
- St. Paul Outside the Walls;
- the Lateran complex;
- Santa Maria Maggiore and other Marian churches;
- apostolic tombs;
- relic collections and sites of martyrdom.

The narrative records Roman traditions about:

- Peter’s tomb and chair;
- the Mandylion or image sent to King Abgar;
- Paul’s chain and staff;
- Paul’s severed head striking the ground three times and producing healing springs;
- the Last Supper table;
- Christ’s seamless tunic;
- Constantine’s baptismal vessel;
- relics of Stephen, Ananias, Matthew, Philip, James, and Mary.

These are **reported relic traditions and hagiographic legends**. Their inclusion shows Bar Sauma’s primary devotional interest and the Roman institutions’ presentation of themselves to pilgrims.

The cardinals would not give a definitive diplomatic answer before a new pope was elected.

---

### Tuscany and Genoa

Bar Sauma traveled through **Tuscany** and reached **Genoa**, whose republican political structure attracted his attention. He observed that Genoa had no king but selected a leading man to govern.

At the cathedral of **San Lorenzo**, he saw:

- relics presented as the body or remains of John the Baptist;
- a hexagonal green dish identified as the vessel used by Christ at the Last Supper.

The latter is the Genoese *Sacro Catino*. Medieval tradition sometimes described it as emerald; it is now recognized as glass.

- **Documented object:** the vessel existed and was shown as a relic.
- **Legend:** its identification with the Last Supper and its supposed emerald composition.

---

### Paris and Philip IV

Bar Sauma entered the territory of France and was ceremonially received at **Paris** by **Philip IV “the Fair.”**

He delivered Arghun’s and Yahballaha’s letters and gifts and explained the proposed campaign concerning Jerusalem.

Philip reportedly answered that, if non-Christian Mongols fought for Jerusalem, Christians should be still more willing to fight. This was an expression of sympathy, not a binding military treaty.

Bar Sauma toured Parisian churches and royal relic collections. The narrative describes:

- a crown of thorns;
- a fragment of the cross;
- other Passion relics;
- a great church containing the tombs of kings, usually identified as Saint-Denis;
- a large community of teachers and students studying theology, philosophy, logic, medicine, geometry, arithmetic, astronomy, and related disciplines.

The source gives a famously large student number, conventionally read as **30,000**. It cannot be verified and likely represents rhetorical magnitude or inaccurate local information.

Philip supplied gifts and costly clothing and promised to send a noble representative back with the embassy. Western records identify the principal French envoy as **Gobert de Helleville**, accompanied by clerics and attendants.

### Notre-Dame silence

Popular writers sometimes speculate that Bar Sauma omitted Notre-Dame because East-Syriac Christians did not call Mary *Theotokos*. This is unsupported. The abridged narrative omits numerous places, and the compiler expressly says he condensed the report. Silence about a named cathedral cannot establish aversion.

---

### Bordeaux and Edward I

Bar Sauma traveled approximately twenty days from Paris to **Bordeaux**, then under English royal rule, where **Edward I of England** was residing.

He delivered Arghun’s documents and gifts and Yahballaha’s letter. Edward expressed enthusiasm concerning Jerusalem and reportedly said that the Mongols’ intention matched his own crusading commitment.

The narrative says:

- Bar Sauma celebrated the Eucharist in Edward’s presence;
- Edward received communion;
- the court held a feast;
- Edward provided gifts and travel money.

The statement that Edward personally received communion from Bar Sauma is striking and has generated discussion because of ecclesiastical division between Latin and East-Syriac Christians. The account is plausible within the unusually accommodating setting, but independent English documentation is limited. It should be described as a **reported fact in the Syriac narrative**, not an independently proved sacramental event.

Edward made no immediate military commitment. His later envoy **Geoffrey de Langley** traveled to the Ilkhanate in 1291, demonstrating continued Anglo-Mongol contact, though not the successful alliance Bar Sauma sought.

---

### Winter in Genoa, 1287–1288

The delegation returned to Genoa and wintered there.

Bar Sauma described Genoa as garden-like, with:

- moderate winters and summers;
- evergreen vegetation;
- fruit-bearing trees;
- a grapevine said to yield seven times yearly.

The seven harvests are likely exaggeration, misunderstanding, or conventional “paradise” imagery. Margaret Kim interprets such descriptions as part of a literary geography in which the western Mediterranean becomes a mirror of orderly and benevolent rule for an Ilkhanid readership.

Bar Sauma complained that he had spent nearly a year in the West without obtaining a papal audience or decisive answer. A papal official traveling from “Almadan”—possibly Germany—promised to transmit his concerns.

There is no evidence that Bar Sauma caused the election of a pope. The biography’s suggestion that the official would “urge” the cardinals belongs to its protagonist-centered narrative.

---

### Nicholas IV and Holy Week in Rome, 1288

**Nicholas IV** was elected pope on 22 February 1288 and crowned shortly afterward. Bar Sauma returned to Rome.

Nicholas received him ceremonially. Bar Sauma attended several major liturgies around Lent, Palm Sunday, Holy Week, Easter, and the Sunday after Easter.

The pope:

- permitted Bar Sauma to celebrate according to the East-Syriac rite;
- gave him communion;
- exchanged professions of faith and ecclesiastical courtesies;
- issued letters to Arghun and Yahballaha;
- provided gifts and relics;
- sent Yahballaha vestments, a ring, a jeweled crown or headdress, and a document concerning patriarchal authority;
- gave Bar Sauma a document recognizing or authorizing his Visitor-General role;
- supplied 1,500 *mathqals* of gold for return expenses.

Nicholas’s recognition should not be overstated as juridical incorporation of the Church of the East into the Roman Church. The papal replies pressed eastern rulers and clergy toward Catholic profession and baptism while extending unusually warm personal and liturgical hospitality.

Modern liturgical comparison has found many of Bar Sauma’s observations consistent with contemporary papal *ordines*. Some details cannot be reconciled with those sources, and his ignorance of Latin limited his account of spoken liturgical texts.

### Diplomatic result

The embassy obtained:

- personal audiences;
- letters and gifts;
- public honor;
- renewed diplomatic channels;
- a French return embassy;
- greater knowledge of the Church of the East in western courts.

It did **not** obtain:

- a signed, operational military alliance;
- a coordinated invasion date;
- a committed crusading army;
- recovery of Jerusalem.

Calling the mission a total failure ignores its diplomatic consequences. Calling it a successful alliance is equally inaccurate.

---

## Return to the Ilkhanate, 1288

Bar Sauma returned by the maritime route and reached Arghun’s court safely. He delivered the letters and gifts and reported on the western kingdoms.

Arghun acknowledged that the journey had imposed great hardship on an old man and said he would not send him away again. He ordered a church erected beside the royal camp or throne enclosure and placed Bar Sauma in charge.

### French return delegation

Philip IV’s representative **Gobert de Helleville** and companions returned east with or near Bar Sauma’s party. Their presence constitutes independent evidence that the French court’s response went beyond ceremonial words, although the mission still produced no campaign.

### Subsequent Mongol diplomacy

In 1289 Arghun sent another embassy led by the Genoese **Buscarello de Ghizolfi**. Arghun’s surviving Mongol letter to Philip IV refers to the message brought through Bar Sauma’s embassy and discusses coordinated operations against Egypt.

This independently confirms:

- Bar Sauma’s diplomatic mission;
- transmission of Philip’s reply;
- continuation of negotiations after his return.

---

## Royal Camp Church, 1288–1291

Yahballaha consecrated the movable church beside Arghun’s camp. The narrative says the ropes of its tent intermingled with those of the royal residence.

Bar Sauma served as:

- director;
- chief;
- steward;
- distributor of food and supplies;
- supervisor of clergy, deacons, visitors, and caretakers.

Arghun ordered continual Eucharistic services and prayers for himself. This was Christian patronage by a religiously plural Mongol ruler, not proof of Arghun’s conversion.

In September 1289, Arghun visited Yahballaha at Maragha. The narrative says Arghun’s son Khudabanda/Öljeitü, born in 1281, had been baptized and received Christian mysteries. Öljeitü later ruled as a Muslim. His childhood passage through Christian, Buddhist, and Muslim identities reflects the pluralism of the Mongol court.

Arghun died in 1291. The Church-of-the-East biography idealizes him as a benefactor and passes lightly over the coercive and military dimensions of his rule.

---

## Retirement and Building at Maragha, 1291–1293

Under Ilkhan **Gaykhatu**, Bar Sauma found the mobile life of the Mongol camp intolerable because of his age and the hardships of desert residence.

He obtained permission to transfer the vessels and vestments of Arghun’s camp church to **Maragha**.

There he built a permanent church dedicated to:

- Mar Mari;
- Mar George.

The church contained relics attributed to:

- the Forty Martyrs;
- Stephen the protomartyr;
- James the Mutilated;
- Demetrius.

Bar Sauma:

- constructed a cell beside it;
- supplied costly liturgical vessels and vestments;
- established endowments or *waqf* for continuing maintenance;
- organized regular offices and Eucharistic services.

The narrative gives the combined construction and endowment cost as approximately **105,000 zuze**. This is the only substantial personal financial figure connected with him late in life.

### Financial interpretation

It is unclear what portion came from:

- Bar Sauma’s own retained resources;
- papal gifts;
- Ilkhanid patronage;
- Yahballaha’s assistance;
- other donors.

The word *waqf* shows the adaptation of an Islamic legal-financial institution for Christian property maintenance. No business enterprise, private investment portfolio, commercial company, tax farm, or personally owned landed estate is documented.

---

## Final Illness and Death, 1293–1294

After completing the church, Bar Sauma went to serve Yahballaha in Baghdad in October 1293.

At a banquet held by the prince **Baidu** at **Shahrazur**, he rose while physically unwell, collapsed, and developed a fever.

The following day he took leave of Baidu and traveled to **Erbil** to settle urgent church affairs. His illness worsened and caused severe pain. He survived until Yahballaha reached Baghdad.

Bar Sauma died during the night associated with the first Sunday after Epiphany, on **10 January 1294**.

### Cause of death

- **Documented symptoms:** fever, collapse, bodily disorder, severe pain, progressive worsening.
- **Medical diagnosis:** unknown.
- No evidence identifies plague, poisoning, violence, addiction, psychiatric illness, or a chronic named disease.
- He was probably in his late sixties or seventies, depending on the birth date adopted.

Yahballaha reportedly mourned intensely for three days. The biographer calls Bar Sauma the courageous “strong arm and support” of the patriarchal household and of Christians who sought its assistance.

## Employment, Offices, Institutions, and Projects

### Church of the East

Bar Sauma’s institutional roles included:

- church keeper at Khanbaliq;
- priest or cleric;
- monk and anchorite;
- teacher of Markos and other disciples;
- Visitor-General;
- governor or steward of the patriarchal residence;
- counselor to Yahballaha III;
- administrator of a royal camp church;
- founder and endower of a church at Maragha.

### Yuan and Ilkhanid political institutions

His pilgrimage benefited from Mongol elite patronage and the imperial travel-document system. His European embassy was formally conducted for Arghun under a *paiza* and royal orders.

He was not a permanent secular minister or military commander. Describing him as a “Mongol statesman” is defensible only in the broad sense of high diplomacy.

### Technologies and infrastructure

No invention, patent, or technical treatise is attributed to him. Relevant technologies and institutions encountered in his career included:

- the Mongol relay and passport system;
- manuscript correspondence in multiple scripts and languages;
- caravan travel;
- Mediterranean shipping;
- portable royal-camp churches;
- church endowment through *waqf*;
- diplomatic gift exchange.

## Religious and Intellectual Development

### East-Syriac Christianity in China

Bar Sauma belonged to a Christian tradition established along Central Asian trade networks and in China centuries before his birth. His career demonstrates that Church-of-the-East institutions in Yuan China could produce:

- trained clergy;
- monastic ascetics;
- multilingual administrators;
- figures recognized by the Mesopotamian patriarchate.

### Asceticism

His early life follows a recognizable hagiographic pattern:

1. miraculous or prayer-mediated birth;
2. religious precocity;
3. conflict between family inheritance and renunciation;
4. distribution of possessions;
5. monastic profession;
6. cave dwelling;
7. reputation for holiness;
8. attraction of a disciple.

The pattern does not make every event fictitious, but it means the biography was organized to show sanctity rather than modern psychological development.

### Pilgrimage theology

Bar Sauma conceived geography through sacred presence. His descriptions prioritize:

- apostolic tombs;
- relics;
- miracle-working objects;
- liturgy;
- churches;
- royal protection of Christianity.

Political and economic observations appear within this religious itinerary.

### View of Latin Christianity

He recognized Latin Christians as Christians while maintaining East-Syriac doctrine. His exchanges in Rome show:

- theological disagreement without rupture;
- curiosity about ritual;
- willingness to receive communion from Nicholas IV;
- willingness to celebrate his own liturgy before Latin rulers and clergy;
- a practical ecclesiology broader than later confessional stereotypes sometimes suggest.

### Political theology

The surviving work idealizes rulers who:

- protect multiple religious communities;
- show generosity;
- support churches;
- maintain order;
- receive holy men respectfully.

Margaret Kim interprets the western journey as a “mirror for princes”: western courts and their orderly landscapes are deployed as an implicit lesson for the unstable Ilkhanate.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Shiban and Keyamta

Parents who financed his education and initially resisted his monastic renunciation because he was their only heir.

### Mar Giwargis

Metropolitan who gave Bar Sauma the monastic tonsure.

### Markos/Yahballaha III

Disciple, pilgrimage companion, patriarch, patron, friend, and principal institutional partner. This is the central relationship of Bar Sauma’s adult life.

### Mar Nestorios

Metropolitan who tonsured Markos after Bar Sauma’s period of instruction.

### Denha I

Catholicos who received the pilgrims, authorized their regional pilgrimage, ordained Markos metropolitan, and appointed Bar Sauma Visitor-General.

### Konbogha and Ifogha/Ibogha

Kublai Khan’s sons-in-law who attempted to retain the monks in China and then provided animals, money, clothes, and travel supplies.

### Kaidu

Mongol ruler who supplied protective written authorization at Talas.

### Abaqa

Ilkhan who received or facilitated the pilgrims’ petitions and protected passage. Direct intimacy with Bar Sauma is not established.

### Ahmad Tegüder

Ruler before whom Bar Sauma and Yahballaha were accused of treason. His government investigated and reportedly acquitted them.

### Arghun

Principal secular patron and sender of the European embassy. Provided money, animals, letters, gifts, and authority; later established the camp church under Bar Sauma’s management.

### Andronikos II Palaiologos

Byzantine emperor who formally received him and facilitated visits to Constantinople’s shrines.

### Charles II of Anjou

Probably the “King Charles” who received him at Naples amid the War of the Sicilian Vespers.

### Roman cardinals

Examined his theology and facilitated visits to Roman churches during the papal vacancy.

### Philip IV of France

Received the embassy at Paris, exchanged gifts, expressed qualified crusading support, and sent Gobert de Helleville east.

### Gobert de Helleville

French noble and return envoy whose travel provides evidence of continuing diplomatic engagement.

### Edward I of England

Received Bar Sauma at Bordeaux, expressed support concerning Jerusalem, and reportedly participated in Bar Sauma’s liturgy.

### Nicholas IV

Pope who received him in 1288, shared communion with him, allowed an East-Syriac Eucharistic celebration, and sent documents and gifts east.

### Buscarello de Ghizolfi

Genoese Ilkhanid agent who carried Arghun’s 1289 follow-up correspondence. He was part of the diplomatic chain created or renewed by Bar Sauma’s mission, though not necessarily Bar Sauma’s personal companion.

### Gaykhatu

Approved the transfer of the camp church’s contents to Maragha.

### Baidu

Hosted the final banquet at which Bar Sauma collapsed with fever.

## Wealth, Property, and Financial Relationships

### Family wealth

The biography says Bar Sauma was born into a wealthy household and expected to inherit. His parents explicitly asked who would possess their accumulated property if he renounced the world.

The ultimate disposition of their estate is unknown.

### Personal renunciation

At monastic profession he distributed his clothing and personal possessions to the poor. This should not automatically be read as a legally documented transfer of the entire family estate.

### Pilgrimage financing

Konbogha and Ifogha supplied:

- animals;
- gold and silver;
- clothing;
- rugs.

The gifts were framed as a conditional trust: use what necessity required and distribute any surplus to western church institutions.

### Embassy financing

Arghun provided:

- 2,000 *mathqals* of gold;
- thirty riding animals;
- diplomatic gifts;
- travel authority.

Nicholas IV later reportedly supplied:

- 1,500 *mathqals* of gold;
- gifts and ecclesiastical objects.

Philip IV, Edward I, Andronikos II, and other hosts also provided gifts or expenses.

These transfers were diplomatic and institutional resources, not evidence of personal enrichment.

### Maragha foundation

The Maragha church and endowment reportedly cost approximately 105,000 *zuze*. No audit survives. The biographer presents the expenditure as pious institutional investment.

### No evidence of commercial enterprise

There is no documented evidence of:

- mercantile ownership;
- banking;
- tax farming;
- private lending;
- profit-seeking investment;
- hidden assets;
- money laundering;
- misappropriation of diplomatic funds.

## Political Activity

Bar Sauma’s political significance lies in diplomacy rather than domestic governance.

### Objectives

- strengthen Arghun’s relations with Christian rulers;
- obtain military cooperation against the Mamluks;
- encourage coordinated action toward Jerusalem;
- secure papal recognition and support for the Church of the East;
- convey knowledge between courts.

### Constraints

- papal vacancy in 1287;
- European dynastic warfare;
- differing Latin and East-Syriac ecclesiologies;
- western doubts about Mongol intentions and religion;
- absence of an agreed campaign mechanism;
- Arghun’s death in 1291;
- the fall of Acre in 1291 and changing crusading conditions.

### Outcome

The embassy contributed to continued Franco-Mongol and Anglo-Mongol exchanges but did not create the operational coalition proposed by Arghun.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Contested Questions

## Treason allegation under Ahmad

**Status: documented accusation; reported acquittal; no conviction.**

Bar Sauma and Yahballaha were accused of corresponding treasonably with Kublai and supporting Arghun. The narrative says an investigation cleared them.

Modern historians caution that the Church source’s anti-Ahmad framing is partisan. No surviving trial transcript independently establishes the evidence presented.

## Participation in an anti-Muslim war project

**Status: documented diplomatic activity.**

Bar Sauma knowingly represented Arghun’s proposal for joint warfare against the Mamluk state and the conquest of Syria and Jerusalem. The embassy therefore had a military-strategic purpose as well as a religious one.

The sources do not show Bar Sauma personally commanding troops, advocating violence against Muslim civilians, or participating in combat.

## Representation of Muslims and “Arabs”

The Syriac narrative sometimes frames the proposed conflict as Mongols and Christians against “Arabs” or Muslim rulers. This reflects Ilkhanid-crusading diplomatic language and the confessional interests of the compiler. It should not be expanded into an undocumented general theory of Bar Sauma’s personal attitudes toward all Muslims.

## Arghun’s Christianity

**Status: common exaggeration.**

Arghun favored Christians and employed Christian envoys but was not demonstrably a Christian convert. Presenting Bar Sauma as ambassador of a “Christian Mongol king” is inaccurate.

## Papal submission or church union

**Status: disputed interpretation.**

Papal documents and gifts have sometimes been described as recognizing Yahballaha’s authority or bringing the Church of the East into Roman obedience. The encounter produced warm relations, but no durable institutional union followed. Nicholas IV’s documents were written within papal claims to universal jurisdiction; their acceptance did not erase East-Syriac autonomy.

## Edward I’s communion

**Status: reported fact, not independently conclusive.**

The Syriac history says Edward received the Eucharist during Bar Sauma’s celebration. This may record an extraordinary act of intercommunion, but historians should account for translation, redaction, and the absence of a surviving English sacramental record.

## “First Chinese traveler to Europe”

**Status: useful but overbroad modern label.**

Bar Sauma is often called the first known traveler born in China to leave a written description of western Europe. Earlier individuals may have crossed comparable distances without surviving narratives. He was Turkic and Christian, not Han Chinese, and his account survives only through later redaction.

## “Reverse Marco Polo”

**Status: modern publicity label.**

The phrase effectively describes opposite travel directions but can mislead:

- there is no evidence Bar Sauma and Marco Polo met;
- Bar Sauma’s project was pilgrimage and official diplomacy, not a merchant-family expedition;
- his Persian account is lost;
- Marco Polo’s and Bar Sauma’s surviving narratives have different textual histories.

## Accuracy of the narrative

The European chronology aligns substantially with independent events: Honorius IV’s death, the papal vacancy, Nicholas IV’s election, Philip IV’s and Edward I’s locations, the 1287 naval battle, and Arghun’s follow-up diplomacy.

Points requiring caution include:

- inflated numbers;
- compressed geography;
- confused ruler names;
- duplicated or legendary relics;
- polished speeches;
- theological dialogue presented decades later;
- the compiler’s express admission that he abridged and amplified;
- moralized portraits of favored and disfavored rulers.

## Adversarial search findings, including 2024–2026

Dedicated searches combined Bar Sauma’s name with “controversy,” “scandal,” “allegations,” “lawsuit,” “fraud,” “accusations,” “conspiracy,” and “criticism,” including 2024–2026 material.

No credible evidence was located for:

- financial fraud;
- sexual scandal;
- lawsuit;
- criminal conviction;
- corruption investigation;
- espionage network in the modern sense;
- forged identity;
- personal participation in massacre or political assassination.

The meaningful negative or contested material consists instead of:

- the medieval treason accusation;
- his participation in planning an anti-Mamluk military coalition;
- the hagiographic and partisan character of the main source;
- uncertainty over ethnicity, dates, route, language ability, and death place;
- exaggeration of the mission’s success;
- romanticized “reverse Marco Polo” portrayals;
- overstatement that Kublai personally sent him;
- disputed interpretation of papal relations and intercommunion.

A 2025 article calling him the “Uyghur Marco Polo” repeats several popular identifications, including a death at Erbil, that are less secure than the critical Syriac reconstruction. No new documentary scandal or allegation surfaced in the most recent two years.

## Myths, Legends, Hagiography, Rumors, and Propaganda

## Prayer-mediated birth

- **Story:** Shiban and Keyamta remained childless, fasted and prayed, and received Bar Sauma as a divinely granted son.
- **Appearance:** anonymous Syriac biography, composed by c. 1319.
- **Circulator:** Church-of-the-East biographer.
- **Evidence:** no independent family record.
- **Function:** establishes providential election and explains the name “son of fasting.”
- **Label:** hagiographic claim.

## Prophetic dreams about Yahballaha

- **Story:** Yahballaha’s cross, fruit, throne, and fiery-tongue dreams foretold his patriarchate.
- **Appearance:** Syriac biography.
- **Evidence:** no independent record.
- **Function:** legitimizes a foreign-born patriarch whose Syriac was reportedly limited.
- **Label:** hagiographic claim.

## Miraculous relics

The text records, without modern verification:

- a perpetually moist tear-stone;
- healing reliquaries;
- incorrupt Nicene bishops;
- Luke-painted images;
- Cana vessels;
- the Last Supper table and dish;
- Paul’s severed head crying “Christ” and producing springs;
- numerous body parts of apostles and saints.

These stories are evidence for thirteenth-century relic culture and what custodians told the embassy, not necessarily Bar Sauma’s independent authentication.

## The volcanic dragon

- **Story:** a great serpent lived near the smoking mountain in the Mediterranean.
- **Source:** local report embedded in Bar Sauma’s travel narrative.
- **Evidence against literal reading:** the smoke, fire, and sulfur are characteristic of Etna or another volcano.
- **Reputational effect:** frequently cited as picturesque evidence of medieval wonder-writing.
- **Label:** local legend attached to a plausible volcanic observation.

## Genoa as earthly paradise

- **Story:** Genoa had perpetual greenery, ideal temperatures, and vines producing seven crops yearly.
- **Evidence:** climate description is broadly understandable; seven grape harvests are implausible as a literal annual norm.
- **Interpretation:** exaggeration, misunderstanding, or deliberate paradise imagery.
- **Label:** literary or reported marvel.

## Bar Sauma as the forgotten “Chinese Marco Polo”

This modern myth-system presents:

- two mirror-image travelers;
- Marco Polo moving east as Bar Sauma moves west;
- Bar Sauma as unjustly erased from world history;
- a providential meeting of East and West.

The comparison arose in modern popular history and publishing. It has increased recognition but obscures different motives, evidence, identities, and textual histories.

## Personal meeting with Marco Polo

- **Story:** the two great travelers crossed paths.
- **Evidence:** none.
- Marco Polo remained in the Yuan world during much of Bar Sauma’s western movement, and their paths may have overlapped geographically at different times.
- Mere contemporaneity is insufficient.
- **Label:** unsupported romantic possibility, not documented fact.

## Bar Sauma as a bishop

Some modern pages call him a bishop. The primary narrative clearly makes Markos a metropolitan and Bar Sauma Visitor-General. Although his offices were senior and quasi-episcopal in reach, a distinct episcopal consecration for Bar Sauma is not securely documented.

## Bar Sauma as saint

The biography uses saintly language and invokes prayers for him, but no universally documented formal canonization process survives. Modern Church-of-the-East communities may commemorate him as an exemplary monk and traveler. Claims to a formal feast or universal cult require denominational and local qualification.

## Death, Burial, and Present Status

Bar Sauma died of an unidentified febrile illness on 10 January 1294 and was buried at the Church-of-the-East patriarchal establishment in Baghdad.

No reliably identified tomb monument, body, or authenticated personal artifact is known to survive. The subsequent destruction and transformation of medieval Christian sites in Baghdad complicates archaeological identification.

There is no evidence for posthumous transfer of his remains.

---

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Within the Church of the East

The anonymous biography preserves him as:

- exemplary monk;
- teacher;
- loyal companion of Yahballaha III;
- defender of East-Syriac theology;
- benefactor and builder;
- effective intermediary with Mongol rulers;
- courageous institutional administrator.

His reputation was partly inseparable from Yahballaha’s patriarchate.

### In European memory

Despite his audiences with several rulers, he did not become a major figure in medieval Latin historical memory. The failure to produce a crusading alliance, the disappearance of his Persian account, and limited circulation of Syriac literature in Europe contributed to obscurity.

### Rediscovery

Late-nineteenth-century manuscript discovery and publication transformed him into a significant figure in scholarship on:

- the Mongol Empire;
- East-West diplomacy;
- Syriac Christianity;
- medieval travel writing;
- comparative liturgy;
- global and connected history.

### Comparison with Marco Polo

The “reverse Marco Polo” framework remains widespread because:

- both traveled across Mongol Eurasia;
- their journeys overlapped chronologically;
- their directions were opposite;
- each narrative describes a culturally unfamiliar world.

The comparison has promoted Bar Sauma’s fame but risks treating European recognition as the measure of his importance.

### Diplomatic legacy

His embassy was one link in a sustained sequence of contacts among:

- the Ilkhans;
- the papacy;
- France;
- England;
- Italian maritime networks;
- the Church of the East.

It did not achieve an alliance, but Arghun’s 1289 letter and later European envoys show that the exchange was not diplomatically empty.

### Historiographical reinterpretation

Older scholarship tended to present:

- a heroic Christian pilgrim;
- a proto-ecumenical figure;
- an Asian Marco Polo;
- a missed opportunity for a Christian-Mongol crusade.

Recent scholarship emphasizes:

- textual redaction;
- Ilkhanid politics;
- Mongol religious pluralism;
- the distinction between Bar Sauma’s lost Persian voice and the surviving Syriac compiler;
- literary construction of western political order;
- the inadequacy of simple East/West or Christian/Muslim binaries.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are listed. Mere contemporaneity, shared routes, or later comparison is excluded.

### Kublai Khan

- **Nature:** imperial background and indirect patronage.
- **Date/place:** before the monks’ departure, Khanbaliq, approximately 1274–1276.
- **Evidence:** Kublai’s sons-in-law Konbogha and Ifogha summoned the monks, tried to dissuade them, and financed their journey. Bar Sauma later held documents and offices operating across the Mongol imperial system.
- **Qualification:** the principal source does not securely document a personal meeting between Bar Sauma and Kublai or prove that Kublai personally commissioned the pilgrimage.
- **Label:** documented indirect connection; personal audience disputed.

### Marco Polo

- **Nature:** no documented personal connection.
- Their Eurasian journeys overlapped in era and partly in geography, and modern historians repeatedly compare them.
- Arghun’s 1289 correspondence and the wider Ilkhanid-Yuan network belong to the same political world in which Marco Polo traveled.
- No meeting, correspondence, influence, collaboration, or acknowledged knowledge of one another is documented.
- **Label:** absence is the finding; later comparative association only.

### Genghis Khan

- **Nature:** no personal connection; dynastic and institutional background.
- Bar Sauma traveled through an empire created by Genghis’s conquests and dealt with descendants including Kublai, Kaidu, Abaqa, Ahmad, and Arghun.
- He was born years after Genghis’s death.
- **Label:** documented historical-institutional context, not a personal connection.

### Giovanni da Pian del Carpine

- **Nature:** no documented connection.
- Both were clerical travelers within the Mongol world, but Carpine’s embassy occurred decades earlier and no evidence shows Bar Sauma read, cited, or was influenced by him.
- **Label:** absence is the finding.

### Ramon Llull

- **Nature:** no documented meeting.
- Llull addressed crusade and mission projects to Nicholas IV during the same broad period, but no consulted source establishes contact with Bar Sauma.
- **Label:** absence is the finding.

### Odoric of Pordenone

- **Nature:** no documented connection.
- Odoric’s Asian travels belong to a later generation.
- **Label:** absence is the finding.

### Abu Abdallah Muhammad ibn Battuta

- **Nature:** no connection.
- Ibn Battuta was born after Bar Sauma’s embassy and was a child when Bar Sauma died, depending on the accepted birth date.
- **Label:** absence is the finding.

### Rabbi Benjamin son of Jonah of Tudela

- **Nature:** no documented connection.
- Both described long-distance religious travel, but Benjamin belonged to the preceding century.
- **Label:** absence is the finding.

### Xuanzang

- **Nature:** no documented connection.
- Modern comparative histories sometimes group them as trans-Eurasian pilgrims, but no textual influence has been demonstrated.
- **Label:** later comparative association only.

No other person in the supplied atlas roster could be connected to Bar Sauma by a documented meeting, correspondence, collaboration, patronage, rivalry, family relationship, or explicitly acknowledged influence on the evidence consulted.
