# Aimé Félix Tschiffely: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Note

Aimé Félix Tschiffely left a substantial published record of his travels but no modern, fully documented scholarly biography. The principal surviving evidence consists of:

- his books and magazine articles;
- contemporary press reports and obituaries;
- correspondence and manuscripts preserved at Dartmouth College and the National Library of Scotland;
- civil and genealogical indexes;
- records and publications of Argentine equestrian institutions;
- later testimony from the Solanet family;
- the Aimé Tschiffely Literary Estate website;
- commemorative journalism and local historical publications.

Some familiar biographical details originated in Tschiffely’s own travel writing or later commemorative literature and have not been independently confirmed. Such material is identified below as reported fact, family recollection, authorial claim, promotional claim, or legend as appropriate.

## Names, Titles, and Public Identities

Tschiffely normally signed and published as **A. F. Tschiffely**. “Aimé” is sometimes rendered without its accent, and English-language sources sometimes reverse or misspell his given names.

No genuine pseudonym has been identified. “The Long Rider” and variants such as “the most famous Long Rider” are posthumous promotional descriptions associated particularly with the Long Riders’ Guild and the literary estate. Argentine commemoration frequently represents him as an adopted national son, a “criollo by adoption,” or the rider of *Gato y Mancha*.

The principal public identities attached to him were:

1. Swiss schoolmaster abroad.
2. Argentine-associated exponent of the Criollo horse.
3. Transcontinental equestrian traveler.
4. Travel writer and lecturer.
5. Friend and biographer of Robert Bontine Cunninghame Graham.
6. Later, an emblem of long-distance horse travel and Argentine horse culture.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and siblings

A FamilySearch profile, based partly on indexed civil and genealogical records, identifies his parents as:

- **Georg Friedrich Alfred Tschiffely**, born 1834, died 1918.
- **Maria Amalie Sutermeister**, born 1867, died 1933.

The same profile lists four children in the family:

- Oswald Georg Roger Tschiffely, born 1891.
- Désirée Irène Béatrice Tschiffely, born 1893.
- Aimé Félix Tschiffely, born 1895.
- Maria Anna Fleurette Corina Tschiffely, born 1900.

This reconstruction is plausible but should be checked against the relevant Swiss birth, marriage, and household registers. The accessible profile gives the father’s age at Aimé’s birth as sixty-one and the mother’s as twenty-seven.

Several biographical accounts call the Tschiffelys an “old” or “traditional” Swiss family. These descriptions are not accompanied by a documented pedigree in the accessible sources.

### Earlier family migration to Argentina

The German-language biographical tradition states that Tschiffely’s grandfather and elder brother had already established themselves in Argentina before Aimé emigrated in 1917. This helps explain his selection of Argentina but was not corroborated by an accessible passenger record, census, or commercial directory.

### Marriage

Tschiffely married **Violette Theodora Hume** on **21 December 1933**, apparently in London. Her name appears in variant forms:

- Violet Hume;
- Violeta Hume;
- Violette Theodora Hume Mundt;
- Violet Marquesita.

Later accounts describe her as Buenos Aires-born, of Scottish and French ancestry, and as a musician, singer, opera singer, or radio performer. These occupational descriptions may refer to different stages of the same career, but a detailed performance record has not been reconstructed.

Tschiffely reportedly wrote that Robert Bontine Cunninghame Graham introduced them at a gathering. The National Library of Scotland preserves six letters from Cunninghame Graham to Violet Tschiffely, dated 1932–1933, showing that she had an independent relationship with him before or around the marriage.

No children are identified in genealogical summaries, estate histories, obituaries, or archival descriptions. The evidence presently supports a childless marriage, though it does not absolutely exclude an unrecorded child.

### Literary estates

After Tschiffely’s death, Violeta administered both his literary estate and aspects of Cunninghame Graham’s literary legacy. In 1975, reportedly because of her advancing age, she transferred responsibility for the Graham material to Jean, Lady Polwarth, Cunninghame Graham’s great-niece. The later Tschiffely estate became associated with Jean Cunninghame Graham and subsequently with literary executor Basha O’Reilly.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1895–c. 1915

### Birthplace dispute

The birth date **7 May 1895** is consistent across the strongest available records. The location is not.

- **Zofingen version:** The literary estate, FamilySearch profile, and German-language accounts identify Zofingen, Canton Aargau.
- **Bern version:** Dartmouth’s archival authority record and numerous English-, French-, and Spanish-language summaries identify Bern.
- **Possible reconciliation:** He may have been born in Zofingen and subsequently raised or educated in Bern. No accessible Swiss birth certificate was located, so the precise municipality should remain formally unresolved.

One low-quality biographical site gives 7 March rather than 7 May. This is contradicted by the broader record and appears to be an error.

### Education

Biographical accounts state that Tschiffely received his primary and secondary education in Bern and trained as a teacher. No school, teacher-training college, certificate, or named mentor has been established from an accessible primary record.

He was described later as an ardent reader and serious student. His subsequent books reveal interests in:

- history;
- geography;
- horses and rural society;
- indigenous cultures;
- the Spanish conquest of the Americas;
- biography;
- exploration literature;
- the transformation of rural life by industrial modernity.

No evidence was found of a university degree.

### England

Tschiffely left Switzerland as a young man, probably during the middle 1910s. Sources place him in England before his migration to Argentina. He reportedly:

- taught;
- played football professionally or semi-professionally;
- boxed or worked briefly as a professional prizefighter;
- undertook assorted occupations.

The boxing and professional-football claims recur in estate-derived biographies but were not corroborated by a fight record, club roster, or contemporary sporting report. They should be classified as **reported biographical claims**, not firmly documented professional careers.

## Migration to Argentina and Teaching Career, 1917–1925

### Arrival

The literary estate dates Tschiffely’s move to Buenos Aires to **1917**. The move was connected with an offer to teach at **St. George’s College**, a British-style boys’ school in Quilmes, south of Buenos Aires.

His employment there is consistently reported. He is usually described as an English or mathematics teacher; the estate specifically calls him a mathematics teacher.

He later joined the **Buenos Aires English High School**, associated with the English-speaking educational community of Buenos Aires. Sources variously call him:

- teacher;
- director of studies;
- temporary headmaster.

The exact dates of these appointments and their official titles were not located in accessible school records.

### Life in the pampas

During school holidays, Tschiffely traveled and rode through the pampas. These excursions brought him into contact with:

- ranch owners;
- horse breeders;
- gauchos;
- rural workers;
- the Criollo horse tradition.

His later public persona was formed in this environment. He was not born into Argentine horse culture and apparently did not begin as an expert horseman. The estate’s later assertion that he had “no previous equestrian experience” is probably rhetorical: by 1925 he had spent years riding in Argentina, even if he lacked the childhood formation of a gaucho or cavalryman.

### Octavio Peró and Emilio Solanet

A 2025 account based on Solanet family recollections states that **Octavio Peró** connected Tschiffely with veterinarian, rancher, politician, and horse breeder **Emilio Solanet**.

Solanet was a leading promoter of the Argentine Criollo horse and a founder of the breed association or studbook movement. He had acquired horses in Patagonia in 1911 from an indigenous chief identified in different accounts as:

- Liempichún;
- Juan Schakmatr or Schakmatr;
- occasionally by other spellings.

The discrepancy may reflect confusion between different indigenous owners, intermediaries, or separate transactions. Later simplified versions frequently state that both Mancha and Gato came directly from the same Tehuelche chief. Solanet family testimony published in 2025 instead says:

- Mancha came as a foal at his mother’s side from the Patagonian herd;
- Gato was born at El Cardal.

That recent family account is more specific and should replace the blanket claim that both horses themselves were purchased from the chief, unless contrary primary records emerge.

### Selection of Mancha and Gato

Tschiffely initially asked to buy suitable horses for a ride to the United States. Solanet reportedly doubted the inexperienced foreigner but believed in the horses. After Tschiffely appeared at his estate and persisted, Solanet gave him two older geldings:

- **Mancha**, an overo or pink-and-white spotted Criollo, approximately fifteen years old;
- **Gato**, a gateado or dun-colored Criollo, approximately sixteen.

Family recollection says Solanet deliberately withheld younger animals because he expected Tschiffely to mishandle or ruin them. The gift therefore functioned both as practical support and as a test or demonstration of the breed.

## The Buenos Aires–Washington–New York Ride, 1925–1928

## Purpose

Tschiffely’s declared purpose was to ride from Argentina to the United States and demonstrate the endurance of the Criollo horse. The journey also promised personal adventure and publicity.

Later celebrations sometimes frame the expedition as a formally designed national mission. The evidence instead suggests a private undertaking enabled by Solanet and subsequently embraced by Argentine breeders, newspapers, institutions, and officials.

### Departure-date discrepancies

Reported departure dates include:

- **20 April 1925** — used by Argentina’s Archivo General and a 2025 *La Nación* centennial article.
- **23 April 1925** — found in a 1929 contemporary almanac and German-language accounts.
- **24 April 1925** — common in later histories.
- **25 April 1925** — sometimes used for the actual departure from his lodging or a ceremonial stage.

The 1929 *Almanaque del Mensajero*, being close to the event, states that he left Buenos Aires on **23 April 1925**. The apparent explanation is that different sources date the ceremonial farewell, the removal of the horses from the Sociedad Rural grounds, and the effective departure from the city differently. “Late April 1925, conventionally 23 April” is the most defensible formulation.

### Starting point

The public departure took place at or from the grounds of the **Sociedad Rural Argentina** in Palermo, Buenos Aires. Some later accounts loosely state that he began in Ayacucho because the horses came from Estancia El Cardal. The internationally recognized expedition began in Buenos Aires.

### Equipment and method

Tschiffely traveled without a large support caravan. He alternated the two horses, rode one and used the other as a pack or remount animal, and relied on local hospitality, diplomatic or institutional contacts, ranches, military posts, and improvised camps.

A later account says a Belgian police dog accompanied him at the beginning but did not continue on the full journey. This is a minor, poorly documented episode.

### Route

The broad route is well established:

1. Buenos Aires and the Argentine interior.
2. Northwestern Argentina.
3. Bolivia, including the high Andes and La Paz.
4. Peru, including Cuzco, Lima, and the northern coastal desert.
5. Ecuador, including Quito.
6. Colombia, including Bogotá and the Caribbean approach.
7. Sea crossing or maritime transport around the impassable Darién section to Panama.
8. Panama and the Central American republics.
9. Mexico.
10. Entry into the United States at Laredo, Texas.
11. San Antonio, Dallas, St. Louis, Columbus, and Washington, D.C.
12. New York City.

Specific summaries list La Paz, Cuzco, Lima, Quito, Bogotá, Panama City, San José, San Salvador, Mexico City, San Luis Potosí, Monterrey, Laredo, San Antonio, Dallas, St. Louis, Columbus, Washington, and New York.

Claims that he literally rode every mile overland from Buenos Aires to New York are inaccurate. The Darién Gap could not be crossed by the same continuous land route, and maritime transport formed part of the expedition. The accomplishment remains a transcontinental horse journey but was not an uninterrupted hoof journey across every geographic barrier.

### Distance

Reported totals vary substantially:

- approximately **10,000 miles** in English-language publicity and Tschiffely estate accounts;
- approximately **21,000–21,500 kilometers** in Argentine commemorative sources;
- sometimes **13,400 miles**, apparently produced by converting 21,500 kilometers;
- 504 riding stages and an average of approximately 46 kilometers per stage in later Argentine summaries.

The difference may reflect:

- planned versus actual distance;
- ridden miles versus total itinerary;
- indirect routes and detours;
- rounding for publicity;
- inclusion or exclusion of maritime sections.

No complete audited stage ledger was located. The safest range is roughly **16,000–21,500 kilometers**, with 10,000 miles being the traditional contemporary figure.

### Hardships

Tschiffely’s published narrative describes:

- mountain cold and altitude;
- jungle heat and disease environments;
- swollen rivers;
- quicksand and mud;
- deserts, including the Peruvian Matacaballo or “horse-killer” desert;
- poor trails;
- scarcity of food and water;
- bureaucratic border crossings;
- physical exhaustion;
- animal injuries;
- hostile or mocking press;
- increasing danger from motor traffic in the United States.

Later promotional retellings often pile these hazards together without dates or corroboration. They broadly match the geography and his own account, but individual dramatic episodes depend mainly on his narration.

### High-altitude claim

Argentine horse literature credits Mancha and Gato with a world altitude record of approximately **5,900 meters** at the El Cóndor pass in Bolivia. Locations are variously given as between Potosí and Challapata or between Potosí and Chuquisaca.

No independent surveying or record-certification document was found. It is best labeled a **traditional expedition claim**, widely repeated by breed organizations but insufficiently documented to qualify as a formally verified world record.

### Treatment and condition of the horses

No contemporary evidence was located that Tschiffely was formally accused of animal cruelty. Both horses survived the expedition and lived for many years afterward, an important fact in evaluating the journey’s severity.

Gato was left behind temporarily during part of the later route. Sources disagree whether this occurred in Mexico or farther north and whether the reason was:

- injury from a mule’s kick;
- fatigue;
- risk from automobile traffic;
- a combination of these considerations.

Tschiffely continued some stages with Mancha alone. Both horses were subsequently reunited and returned to Argentina.

### Washington, D.C.

A contemporary 1929 almanac dates his completion of the “raid” at Washington to **29 August 1928**. President **Calvin Coolidge** received him at the White House.

Later language saying he was “hailed as a hero by the President” is promotional. The documented core is the presidential reception, an important act of official recognition.

### New York

Tschiffely continued to New York, where **20 September 1928** became the standard commemorative arrival date.

He rode Mancha in a police-escorted procession associated with Broadway, Fifth Avenue, and Central Park. Mayor **James J. “Jimmy” Walker** greeted him and reportedly presented a New York City medal. Mancha and Gato were exhibited for approximately ten days at the International Horse Show at Madison Square Garden.

The expedition thereby moved from practical travel into organized spectacle, public relations, and international promotion of the Criollo breed.

### Return to Argentina

Tschiffely and the horses returned by ship to Buenos Aires in **December 1928**. The horses retired to Solanet’s Estancia El Cardal rather than being permanently displayed at the Buenos Aires zoo, an option reportedly proposed and rejected by Tschiffely.

## Immediate Fame and Publicity, 1928–1933

Tschiffely became a public figure in Argentina and the United States. He lectured, wrote articles, and appeared in illustrated media.

### National Geographic

He contributed an illustrated account usually identified as **“Buenos Aires to Washington by Horse”** or a closely equivalent title to *The National Geographic Magazine*. Bibliographic references disagree over whether the relevant number was February or August 1929. A modern French collector’s article identifies February 1929; another scholarly bibliographic reference gives August 1929, volume 56, pages 178–211.

The article should be verified against a complete National Geographic index before assigning a definitive issue. The familiar claim that he “appeared on the cover” also requires qualification: National Geographic covers of that period normally used the magazine’s standard typographic design rather than modern subject photography. He was prominently featured in the magazine, but “cover star” may be a later simplification.

### Literary turn

After the journey Tschiffely spent time in Argentina, the United States, and Europe. The estate places his return to England in **1932**, when he began writing full-time.

His first major book appeared in **1933** under titles including:

- *Southern Cross to Pole Star*;
- *The Ride*;
- *Tschiffely’s Ride*.

The various titles reflect different editions and markets, not three separately written expedition books. Contemporary and later reports describe it as a bestseller. Precise sales, royalty income, contract terms, and estate value were not located.

A *Time* obituary said the ride brought him “fame and fortune.” That establishes contemporary perception of financial success, not a documented net worth.

## Marriage and London Literary Life, 1933–1937

Tschiffely married Violeta Hume in December 1933 and established a London-based writing life.

### Robert Bontine Cunninghame Graham

The most consequential literary relationship of Tschiffely’s later life was with **Robert Bontine Cunninghame Graham** (1852–1936), Scottish-Argentine traveler, rancher, writer, socialist politician, and Scottish nationalist.

Cunninghame Graham:

- shared Tschiffely’s interest in Argentina, horses, and travel;
- introduced him to Violeta, according to Tschiffely;
- helped feed or care for Mancha and Gato in Argentina;
- encouraged his literary career;
- became the subject of Tschiffely’s biography.

The National Library of Scotland preserves fourteen letters, some containing Spanish verse, from Tschiffely to Graham, dated 1932–1936. It also preserves letters to Tschiffely from Graham’s friends collected during research for the biography.

After Graham died in Buenos Aires in March 1936, Mancha and Gato reportedly took part in his funeral procession. This episode reinforced the shared mythology surrounding Graham, Tschiffely, Argentina, and the horses.

Tschiffely published *Don Roberto: Being the Account of the Life and Works of R. B. Cunninghame Graham* in 1937. He subsequently helped administer Graham’s literary affairs.

The archival record also presents a caution: Tschiffely reported that Graham destroyed much notable correspondence, while later scholarship argues that Graham helped shape the archive according to how he wished posterity to see him. *Don Roberto* is therefore both a vital biography and part of a consciously managed posthumous image.

## Later Journeys and Writing, 1935–1953

### Ride through Britain

Tschiffely undertook a horseback journey through rural England, generally described as running from Salisbury toward Scotland. This produced:

- *Bridle Paths: The Story of a Ride Through Rural England* (1936).

The book records a British countryside before extensive postwar suburbanization and road development. It belongs to travel writing and social observation rather than sport alone.

### Southern South America by motorcar, 1937–1938

In 1937 or 1938, Tschiffely returned to South America and traveled by automobile around or toward the southern end of the continent. Ford reportedly supplied the vehicle, making the trip partly a sponsored expedition.

He revisited Argentina and traveled into Patagonia and Tierra del Fuego, recording:

- modernization;
- roads and motor vehicles;
- ranching;
- indigenous communities;
- remote settlements;
- the contrast between remembered and contemporary South America.

The resulting book was *This Way Southward*. Sources variously date it to 1940 or 1945; 1940 appears to be the original British publication date, while 1945 may represent another edition.

Financial details of the Ford arrangement are unavailable. It should be described as a documented or reported provision of a vehicle, not necessarily as a salaried commercial endorsement.

### Ireland, 1939

A German-language account states that in 1939 Tschiffely traveled through Ireland with a horse, wagon, and dog. The outbreak of the Second World War forced him to abandon or curtail the journey. No resulting full-length book has been identified.

### War-period return to Argentina

In **1943**, Tschiffely traveled to Argentina for a lecture tour expected to last a few months. Wartime circumstances extended the visit to roughly two and a half years. He was separated from Violeta until after the war.

During this period he visited Mancha and Gato at El Cardal. Solanet family memory supplies the best-known recognition story: after many years apart, Tschiffely whistled, and the horses raised their ears and came to him. The story was later told by family members who said they remembered the reunion.

The broad reunion is credible and consistent with his travel. The precise choreography—long whistle, immediate recognition, and gallop—is a **family recollection that became legend**, not a contemporaneous independently recorded observation.

### Gato and Mancha’s deaths

Reported dates are:

- **Gato:** died in 1944, approximately thirty-two years old.
- **Mancha:** died in 1947, approximately thirty-seven years old.

Both were buried at El Cardal. Their exceptional ages are plausible but depend on estimated birth dates and ranch records not independently examined.

### Historical and children’s writing

Tschiffely expanded beyond travel narrative:

- *Coricancha: Garden of Gold* reconstructed the discovery and conquest of Peru and the Inca Empire.
- *Ming and Ping* was a children’s work.
- *The Tale of Two Horses* retold the great ride from the horses’ perspective.
- *Bohemia Junction* drew on autobiographical or fictionalized travel material.
- *Matt Cass* was a northern or frontier-themed novel.

His writings retained a recurring concern with mobility, rural life, conquest, changing technology, and the moral or emotional relationship between people and animals.

### Spain by motorcycle

In the early 1950s Tschiffely spent approximately four months traveling through Spain by motorcycle. A British motorcycle manufacturer reportedly supplied a machine after another plan or means of travel failed.

The trip produced *Round and About Spain* (1952). It reflects post–Civil War, early Franco-era Spain as seen by an itinerant foreign observer. No evidence was found that Tschiffely held an official political role in Spain or was employed by the Franco government.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Employment and institutions

Documented or consistently reported associations include:

- St. George’s College, Quilmes.
- Buenos Aires English High School.
- Sociedad Rural Argentina, as the ceremonial starting point of the ride.
- Emilio Solanet and Estancia El Cardal.
- Argentine Criollo horse-breeding organizations.
- National Geographic Society.
- British and American publishing houses, especially William Heinemann and Hodder & Stoughton.
- Ford, as vehicle provider for the southern South American journey.
- Robert Bontine Cunninghame Graham’s literary estate.
- Dartmouth College, posthumously, as repository for manuscripts and correspondence.
- National Library of Scotland, as repository for Graham–Tschiffely correspondence.

### Businesses and investments

No evidence was found that Tschiffely founded a company, maintained a substantial investment organization, held patents, or developed a proprietary technology.

His economic activities appear to have consisted principally of:

- teaching salary;
- lectures;
- magazine fees;
- book advances and royalties;
- possible expedition sponsorship or in-kind support;
- literary-estate income.

No tax record, probate inventory, publishing ledger, bank account, landholding record, or quantified net worth was located.

### Property

No confirmed personal ranch or major real-estate holding has been identified. Estancia El Cardal belonged to the Solanet family, not Tschiffely. Later references to “his” resting place or ranch are commemorative, not evidence of ownership.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Politics

No evidence establishes Tschiffely as a party official, candidate, propagandist, revolutionary, intelligence operative, or government employee.

His friendship with Cunninghame Graham brought him into close contact with a socialist and Scottish nationalist political tradition, but it does not prove that Tschiffely adopted Graham’s program. Tschiffely’s own books generally foreground travel, history, rural cultures, and character rather than systematic political doctrine.

He interacted ceremonially with:

- Calvin Coolidge;
- Argentine officials and legislators;
- municipal authorities in New York;
- horse-breeding and rural elites.

These encounters were connected to the ride and its commemoration, not evidence of partisan alignment.

### Argentina under Perón

Later estate literature says plans for a permanent Buenos Aires monument stalled because of political turmoil under Juan Domingo Perón and after his overthrow in 1955. The urn did remain at Recoleta for decades, and the proposed monument was not built. The estate’s characterization of Perón as a dictator and its causal attribution to political unrest are later interpretive claims. No budget file or official cancellation record was located.

### Religion

No denomination, conversion, religious office, or sustained theological position has been documented. Funeral and cremation arrangements do not establish a particular belief.

### Intellectual outlook

Recurring themes in his writing include:

- admiration for endurance and self-reliance;
- preservation of rural knowledge;
- concern over the displacement of horses and older lifeways by motorization;
- fascination with conquest and exploration;
- attention to landscape and cultural difference;
- a strong literary identification with Argentina despite Swiss origins and London residence.

His historical writing on the conquest of Peru should be read as mid-twentieth-century popular history. It was not based on modern archaeological or ethnohistorical methods and may reproduce assumptions and terminology characteristic of its period.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Emilio Solanet — patron, collaborator, and horse breeder

**Documented fact.** Solanet supplied Mancha and Gato, promoted the Criollo horse, and provided the institutional and material foundation for the ride. The relationship joined Tschiffely’s appetite for adventure with Solanet’s breed-improvement and publicity objectives.

### Mancha and Gato — working animals and central public partners

**Documented fact.** Tschiffely consistently described the ride as the achievement of “we,” meaning himself and the horses. He resisted treating them as disposable equipment and opposed permanent zoo display after their return.

### Robert Bontine Cunninghame Graham — friend, mentor, patron, and biographical subject

**Documented fact.** Their correspondence survives; Graham helped introduce Tschiffely to literary and personal networks; Tschiffely wrote Graham’s biography and administered aspects of his literary legacy.

### Violeta Tschiffely — spouse and literary executor

**Documented fact.** Married in 1933; maintained the literary estates after his death. The surviving record presents her chiefly through her husband’s and Graham’s estates, leaving her own musical career underdocumented.

### Herbert Faulkner West — correspondent and fellow Graham biographer

**Documented fact.** Dartmouth holds Tschiffely’s letters to West concerning Graham research and literary matters. West’s collecting helped preserve the Tschiffely manuscript and related materials.

### Jean, Lady Polwarth — posthumous estate custodian

**Documented fact.** Graham’s great-niece later assumed responsibility for his literary estate and helped preserve Tschiffely’s legacy.

### Solanet family

**Documented and reported fact.** Later generations safeguarded the horses’ graves, traced Tschiffely’s urn, facilitated its transfer to El Cardal, and maintained the family’s oral history.

### Gauchos and indigenous informants

Tschiffely wrote about gauchos, ranch workers, and indigenous communities throughout the Americas. Individual participants are often unnamed in general biographies. This creates an evidentiary imbalance: local people supplied food, directions, shelter, animal knowledge, and transport assistance, while the traveler remained the named hero.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Adversarial-search result

A dedicated search combined Tschiffely’s full name and initials with terms including controversy, scandal, allegations, lawsuit, fraud, accusations, conspiracy, criticism, animal cruelty, racism, and disputed record, including searches restricted to 2024–2026.

**Finding:** No credible record was located of:

- arrest;
- criminal investigation;
- indictment or prosecution;
- civil lawsuit;
- fraud allegation;
- corruption;
- money laundering;
- espionage or intelligence work;
- political violence;
- sexual scandal;
- professional dismissal;
- plagiarism proceeding;
- animal-cruelty charge;
- authenticated claim that the expedition itself was fabricated.

Searches produced numerous irrelevant results involving people named Schiff or similarly spelled names. These have no connection to Tschiffely.

Absence of an identified allegation is not proof that none ever appeared in local or inaccessible press. It is nevertheless the result of the requested adversarial pass.

### Promotional inflation of the ride

**Documented discrepancy, not fraud.** Later retellings sometimes:

- say he rode every mile continuously from Buenos Aires to New York;
- give a single exact distance despite incompatible totals;
- call the altitude claim a verified world record;
- call him a National Geographic “cover” personality in the modern photographic sense;
- describe the horses as direct individual descendants, through a demonstrable pedigree, of horses brought by Pedro de Mendoza;
- imply both horses were personally obtained from the same indigenous chief;
- collapse Washington and New York arrival dates.

These are best understood as commemorative simplifications unless evidence appears that Tschiffely knowingly falsified them for financial gain.

### Colonial and indigenous framing

**Modern critical question.** Tschiffely’s travel and historical books use the categories and vocabulary of their era. Later celebratory accounts sometimes describe Solanet as having “rescued” pure Criollo horses from an indigenous herd, a formulation that centers the breeder and minimizes indigenous ownership and breeding knowledge.

Available evidence instead indicates that indigenous Patagonian horse keepers preserved or developed the animals from which Solanet drew breeding stock. The precise terms of the transaction and the identities of the indigenous sellers remain inconsistently reported.

No substantial peer-reviewed postcolonial study focused specifically on Tschiffely was located. This is a valid field for further research, not an established personal accusation against him.

### Authorship and archival shaping

**Documented concern.** *Don Roberto* was written by a close friend who later helped manage Graham’s reputation and literary estate. Graham destroyed correspondence and helped shape the surviving archive. The biography is genuine Tschiffely authorship but cannot be treated as detached, comprehensive documentation.

### Financial transparency

No suspicious financial structure has been found. At the same time, claims that he became wealthy or “made his fortune” cannot presently be quantified. Publisher records, royalty accounts, probate files, and sponsor agreements remain research targets.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “The man must be mad”

**Story:** Buenos Aires newspapers supposedly greeted the proposed journey with headlines such as “Impossible!”, “Absurd!”, and “The man must be mad!”

**Appearance and circulation:** Repeated in estate biographies and later popular histories.

**Evidence:** No digitized original clipping carrying the complete quoted formula was located.

**Assessment:** **Reported contemporary reaction; exact quotation unverified.** Skepticism is entirely plausible, but the three-part wording may have become standardized through repetition.

**Effect:** It provides the classic heroic structure of ridicule followed by vindication.

### Direct descent from Pedro de Mendoza’s horses

**Story:** Mancha and Gato were “direct descendants” of horses brought to the Río de la Plata by Pedro de Mendoza in 1535.

**Evidence for:** Criollo horses descend broadly from Iberian horses introduced during Spanish colonization; Mendoza’s expedition is symbolically important in Argentine horse history.

**Evidence against literal form:** No individual pedigree spanning four centuries exists. Other Spanish expeditions also introduced horses into the Americas. The biological population history is broader than a direct, documented Mendoza-to-Mancha line.

**Assessment:** **Breed-history tradition and symbolic genealogy, not a verified individual pedigree.**

**Effect:** It binds the ride to the conquest, foundation mythology of Buenos Aires, and the endurance of a “native” Argentine breed.

### The horses’ immediate recognition after eighteen years

**Story:** On returning to El Cardal, Tschiffely whistled; Mancha and Gato recognized him immediately and galloped to him.

**Circulation:** Solanet family recollections, Argentine journalism, estate literature.

**Evidence:** Family members later recalled the reunion; no contemporaneous film or independent diary entry has been located.

**Assessment:** **Reported family recollection that developed into a durable legend.**

**Effect:** It transformed an endurance story into one about fidelity, memory, and emotional partnership.

### The unsupported novice

**Story:** Tschiffely began the ride with no equestrian experience.

**Evidence:** He was Swiss-born and not raised as a gaucho, but he had lived in Argentina from 1917 and rode through the pampas during holidays.

**Assessment:** **Promotional exaggeration.** He may have lacked expert expedition credentials, but he was not literally encountering horses for the first time.

### The unbroken overland crossing

**Story:** He rode continuously overland from Buenos Aires to New York.

**Evidence:** The route involved maritime transport, particularly around the Darién barrier, and periods when one horse was rested or transported.

**Assessment:** **Simplified legend.** The expedition was real; the “every inch in the saddle” version is not.

### “World records”

**Story:** The ride established world records for distance and altitude.

**Evidence:** Argentine equestrian sources repeatedly use the record language; the scale of the journey is undisputed.

**Counterevidence:** No contemporaneous international certifying authority or independently audited record file was found.

**Assessment:** **Traditional record claim, not independently certified in the material reviewed.**

### Forgotten ashes and unrealized monument

**Story:** Tschiffely’s urn lay forgotten in an obscure Recoleta corner for almost four decades because political turmoil prevented construction of his monument.

**Evidence:** His ashes were deposited at Recoleta in 1954 and moved to El Cardal in 1998; the planned monument was not built.

**Unresolved elements:** How literally the urn was “lost,” which authority controlled it, and whether politics, finances, bureaucracy, or all three prevented the memorial.

**Assessment:** **Documented broad sequence with interpretive and dramatic embellishment.**

## Awards, Honors, and Commemoration

Documented or consistently reported honors include:

- reception by President Calvin Coolidge at the White House, 1928;
- welcome by New York Mayor Jimmy Walker;
- New York City medal, reportedly presented by Walker;
- police-escorted New York procession;
- exhibition of Mancha and Gato at Madison Square Garden;
- official and popular receptions in Argentina;
- memorials at El Cardal and Ayacucho;
- a museum display in the transportation or great-journeys collection at the Enrique Udaondo museum complex in Luján;
- designation of **20 September as Argentina’s Día Nacional del Caballo** under National Law No. 25,125, adopted in 1999;
- later memorial rides and centennial observances.

The national horse day commemorates the 20 September 1928 New York arrival and the horse’s broader place in Argentine history. It is not formally named “Aimé Tschiffely Day.”

## Illness, Injury, and Death

No chronic addiction, psychiatric diagnosis, major psychological crisis, or disabling injury has been documented.

The physical hazards of his expeditions were considerable, but accessible accounts do not provide a reliable medical history.

In December 1953, approximately ten days before Christmas, Tschiffely entered **Mile End Hospital**, London, for what later estate sources called a minor or routine operation.

A contemporary *Swiss Observer* obituary stated that he died in a London hospital on **5 January 1954**, aged fifty-eight, “after an operation for kidney trouble.” *Time* similarly reported death after an operation.

The most defensible cause formulation is:

> **Postoperative complications following surgery for kidney disease or a kidney condition.**

The exact diagnosis, procedure, and immediate physiological cause have not been located in a hospital or death-certificate record.

He was cremated in London.

---

## Funeral, Ashes, and Reburial

Tschiffely’s ashes were sent to Argentina. On **13 November 1954**, a large public funeral procession reportedly accompanied the urn to Recoleta Cemetery. Participants included representatives of government, parliament, equestrian institutions, mounted riders, and members of the public.

A permanent monument at or near the 1925 departure point was proposed but never realized. The urn remained at Recoleta until the 1990s.

Oscar Emilio Solanet and associates located it and obtained permission for transfer. In **February 1998**, in accordance with Tschiffely’s reported wishes, the ashes were placed at Estancia El Cardal near the resting places of Mancha and Gato.

Some sources speak of “remains” or “bones,” but the more precise evidence identifies cremated ashes.

## Works by Aimé Félix Tschiffely

Publication dates vary among editions. The following separates original works from reissues and alternate titles as far as the accessible catalog record permits.

### Books

1. **Southern Cross to Pole Star**. London: William Heinemann, 1933.  
   First major book-length account of the 1925–1928 ride. Published or reissued under the titles *The Ride* and *Tschiffely’s Ride*. Genuine authorship.

2. **Tschiffely’s Ride**. 1933 onward.  
   Alternate-market title or reissue of *Southern Cross to Pole Star*, not an independent expedition narrative. Later editions include a 1952 postscript in which Tschiffely reflected on the journey and the deaths of the horses.

3. **The Tale of Two Horses**.  
   A children’s retelling of the journey from Mancha and Gato’s viewpoint. Sources give conflicting first-publication dates, including 1935 and 1949; a revised Hodder & Stoughton edition is cataloged in 1951. The first edition requires examination in a national-library catalog.

4. **Bridle Paths: The Story of a Ride Through Rural England**. London, 1936.  
   Travel narrative of a horseback journey through Britain.

5. **Don Roberto: Being the Account of the Life and Works of R. B. Cunninghame Graham**. London and Toronto: William Heinemann, 1937.  
   Authorized or intimate biography based on friendship, correspondence, interviews, and Graham’s circle. Genuine authorship, but shaped by proximity to its subject and management of Graham’s posthumous reputation.

6. **This Way Southward**. London: Hodder & Stoughton, probably 1940.  
   Account of a motor journey through southern South America and observations of Patagonia and Tierra del Fuego. Some bibliographies give 1945, probably reflecting a later edition.

7. **Coricancha: Garden of Gold: Discovery of Peru and Conquest of the Inca Empire**. London: Hodder & Stoughton, 1943.  
   Popular historical reconstruction. A Spanish edition, *Coricancha: jardín de oro: narración de la conquista del imperio de los incas*, was published in Madrid by Ediciones La Nave in 1945.

8. **Ming and Ping**. 1948.  
   Children’s book. Further bibliographical verification is required.

9. **Bohemia Junction**. 1951.  
   Travel-related or autobiographical narrative, sometimes treated as fiction or memoir. Edition-level research is needed.

10. **Round and About Spain**. London: Hodder & Stoughton, 1952.  
    Account of travel through Spain by motorcycle.

11. **Matt Cass: A Tale of a Man from the North**. 1953.  
    Novel or fictionalized frontier narrative.

### Articles and periodical writing

- **“Buenos Aires to Washington by Horse”**, or equivalent title, *The National Geographic Magazine*, 1929. The precise month requires final verification; modern references conflict between February and August.
- Additional articles, lectures, interviews, and extracts were published in equestrian, travel, and newspaper venues. No complete periodical bibliography has yet been compiled.

### Correspondence and manuscripts

- Fourteen surviving letters from Tschiffely to R. B. Cunninghame Graham, 1932–1936, National Library of Scotland.
- Letters to Herbert Faulkner West, 1933–1954, Dartmouth College.
- Manuscript of *Don Roberto*, Dartmouth College.
- Letters received from Graham’s friends during preparation of *Don Roberto*, National Library of Scotland.
- Scattered correspondence associated with the Tschiffely and Cunninghame Graham literary estates.

No diary covering the entire ride has been identified in the archival descriptions reviewed, although working notes may survive in private estate material.

### Authorship assessment

No major work listed above is known to be ghostwritten. Alternate titles and rewritten children’s versions have sometimes been mistakenly counted as separate journeys or wholly separate books. Posthumous editions issued by the Long Riders’ Guild or specialist publishers are reprints, occasionally with new introductions, not new works by Tschiffely.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Argentina

Tschiffely’s strongest continuing public reputation is in Argentina, where his ride became inseparable from the symbolic history of the Criollo horse.

The principal commemorative structure joins three figures:

- Tschiffely, the rider and narrator;
- Emilio Solanet, breeder and organizer;
- Mancha and Gato, increasingly treated as co-heroes rather than equipment.

Día Nacional del Caballo institutionalized that memory at the national level in 1999.

### Equestrian travel

The Long Riders’ Guild presents Tschiffely as the most influential equestrian travel writer of the twentieth century. That is an evaluative, promotional claim, but his ride and book demonstrably influenced later long-distance riders.

South African rider **Marianne du Toit** completed a later South America–North America journey inspired by him. Numerous reenactments and memorial rides explicitly invoke Tschiffely, Mancha, and Gato.

### Literary standing

*Tschiffely’s Ride* has remained in print through specialist reissues and is his most durable work. His other travel books retain value as observations of:

- rural England before large-scale postwar development;
- southern South America during modernization;
- wartime and postwar cultural transition;
- early Franco-era Spain.

His popular history and fiction are less widely remembered.

### Historical reinterpretation

Earlier narratives centered the lone white male adventurer. More recent family and institutional accounts give greater space to:

- the horses as agents;
- Solanet’s breeding program;
- gauchos and rural assistance;
- indigenous preservation of Patagonian horse stocks;
- the public-relations function of the expedition.

A fully modern treatment would need to analyze both the achievement and the social infrastructure that made it possible.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the reviewed sources are included. Mere presence in the same country or era has not been treated as a relationship.

### Pedro de Mendoza

**Evidentiary label: documented symbolic and authorial association; literal genealogical claim unverified.**

Tschiffely and promoters of the Criollo horse repeatedly connected Mancha and Gato to Iberian horses brought to the Río de la Plata by Pedro de Mendoza’s expedition in 1535–1536. This was not a personal connection—the men lived four centuries apart—but an explicit historical genealogy central to Tschiffely’s project and its mythology.

The broad population descent of Criollo horses from colonial Iberian stock is credible. A direct individual pedigree from Mendoza’s horses to Mancha and Gato is not documented.

### Francisco Pizarro González

**Evidentiary label: documented intellectual and authorial connection.**

Tschiffely made Pizarro and the Spanish conquest of Peru central subjects of *Coricancha: Garden of Gold: Discovery of Peru and Conquest of the Inca Empire* (1943). This is an explicit author-to-historical-subject relationship, not a personal connection.

### Atahualpa

**Evidentiary label: documented intellectual and authorial connection.**

Atahualpa and the destruction of the Inca imperial order form part of the historical narrative of *Coricancha*. As with Pizarro, this is a documented engagement through historical writing, not personal contact.

### Juan Domingo Perón

**Evidentiary label: reported contextual connection; no personal meeting established.**

Tschiffely’s ashes were returned to Argentina in November 1954 during Perón’s presidency. Later estate literature attributes the failure to construct a planned permanent memorial partly to political unrest under Perón and the upheaval surrounding his 1955 overthrow.

No evidence reviewed establishes a meeting, correspondence, patronage relationship, or political alliance between Tschiffely and Perón. The connection is contextual and posthumous.

### Other roster members

No documentary evidence was found of a meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, family tie, acknowledged influence, or institutional relationship between Tschiffely and the other named atlas figures.

Searches specifically tested possible Argentine and travel-world overlaps, including Jorge Luis Borges, Victoria Ocampo, Antoine de Saint-Exupéry, Carlos Gardel, Vito Dumas, George Orwell, H. G. Wells, and Ernest Shackleton. No supported connection was located. Absence is therefore the present finding.
