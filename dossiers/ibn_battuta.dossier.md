# Abu Abdallah Muhammad ibn Battuta: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary limits

Abu ʿAbd Allāh Muḥammad ibn Baṭṭūṭa is known principally through one work: the travel narrative conventionally called the *Riḥla*. It was dictated after most of the journeys, arranged and stylistically revised by Ibn Juzayy, and completed at the Marinid court. It is not a surviving day-by-day diary.

Consequently, this dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a surviving manuscript, contemporary external testimony, or unusually strong agreement among primary and specialist sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated in the *Riḥla* and broadly accepted, but not independently documented.
- **Disputed claim:** challenged on chronological, geographical, textual, or comparative grounds.
- **Literary borrowing:** wording or material demonstrably or plausibly taken from an earlier author; this does not by itself prove that Ibn Battuta never visited the place.
- **Legend or miracle story:** narrated as supernatural, folkloric, hagiographic, or marvelous rather than independently verifiable history.
- **Unresolved:** evidence does not permit a secure conclusion.

Calendar conversions vary by one or occasionally more days. His birth is therefore given as 24 or 25 February 1304. Many journey dates after Delhi are reconstructed rather than explicit.

## Basic Identifying Information

### Full name

**Shams al-Dīn Abū ʿAbd Allāh Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Muḥammad ibn Ibrāhīm ibn Muḥammad ibn Yūsuf al-Lawātī al-Ṭanjī ibn Baṭṭūṭa**

Arabic:

**شمس الدين أبو عبد الله محمد بن عبد الله بن محمد بن إبراهيم بن محمد بن يوسف اللواتي الطنجي ابن بطوطة**

Elements of the name:

- **Muḥammad:** personal name.
- **Abū ʿAbd Allāh:** *kunya*, “father of ʿAbd Allāh”; it need not prove that he had a son of that name.
- **Shams al-Dīn:** honorific, “Sun of the Faith.”
- **al-Lawātī:** association with the Lawāta, a Berber/Amazigh tribal grouping.
- **al-Ṭanjī:** “of Tangier.”
- **Ibn Baṭṭūṭa:** hereditary family designation by which he became known.

Common renderings include Ibn Battuta, Ibn Baṭṭūṭa, Ibn Batuta, Ibn Battutah, Ibn Batouta, Ibn Batoutah, Ebn Battuta, and ابن بطوطة.

### Titles and occupational descriptions

- **Shaykh:** learned or honorific title.
- **Faqīh:** jurist or person trained in Islamic jurisprudence.
- **Qāḍī:** judge; he held or claimed judicial office in Delhi, the Maldives, and probably Morocco.
- **Traveller / *jawwāla*:** Ibn Juzayy’s conclusion calls him the traveller of his age.
- “Prince of travellers,” “greatest Muslim traveller,” and “the Muslim Marco Polo” are modern or post-medieval epithets, not personal titles demonstrably used by Ibn Battuta himself.

### Birth

- **Date:** 17 Rajab 703 AH, usually converted as **24 or 25 February 1304**.
- **Place:** **Tangier**, then within the Marinid Sultanate, now Morocco.
- **Status:** broadly accepted, but ultimately derived from the biographical information incorporated into the *Riḥla*.

### Death

Competing versions:

- **770 AH / 1368–1369:** now the most commonly accepted scholarly date.
- **779 AH / 1377–1378:** found in older references and some biographical traditions.
- **Place:** somewhere in Morocco; Tangier and Marrakesh have both been asserted, but neither is securely established by contemporary documentation.
- **Cause:** unknown. Claims that he died of plague or the same disease that killed his mother are unsupported.
- **Tomb:** a small mausoleum in Tangier is popularly identified as his burial place. The attribution is traditional and lacks decisive medieval archaeological or documentary authentication.

[Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ebn-battuta/) gives 770/1368–69 while acknowledging the broader textual problems. The relevant *Encyclopaedia of Islam* material also records 770/1368–69 or 779/1377.

## Family and Ancestry

### Ethnic and regional background

**Reported fact:** Ibn Battuta identified with the Lawāta, a widespread Berber/Amazigh tribal confederation. The nisba *al-Lawātī* is the principal evidence. “Arab explorer,” common in popular descriptions, describes the Arabic language and Islamic literary culture more accurately than his ancestry.

### Family profession

**Reported fact:** he came from a family associated with Mālikī legal learning and the office of *qāḍī*. This background helps explain his legal education, his immediate recognition as a jurist abroad, and his repeated pursuit of salaried judicial appointments.

No reliable names survive for his parents in the commonly accessible sources beyond his patronymic—his father was ʿAbd Allāh—and the longer male lineage preserved in his formal name.

### Parents

- **Father, ʿAbd Allāh:** Ibn Battuta learned on his return through Syria that his father had died about fifteen years earlier, probably around 1333–1334. Exact date and circumstances are unknown.
- **Mother:** name unknown. Ibn Battuta returned to Morocco in 1349 to find that she had died only months before his arrival, apparently during the Black Death era. The precise disease is not documented.
- His opening account says that in 1325 he left both parents behind and departed alone, without a travelling companion.

### Siblings

No securely identified siblings are recoverable from the *Riḥla* or the principal biographical sources.

### Marriages, concubines, and children

The exact total cannot be reconstructed. Ibn Battuta entered numerous marriages and owned enslaved women, some of whom were his sexual partners. His mobility, repeated divorce, and the conventions of the *riḥla* genre leave many women unnamed.

Documented within his own narrative, though generally not independently corroborated:

1. **North African caravan marriage, 1325:** he contracted a marriage while travelling through the central Maghreb. A dispute with the prospective father-in-law or wife’s family led to divorce or cancellation.
2. **A second caravan marriage:** he soon married the daughter of another Maghrebi traveller or learned man and celebrated the marriage while continuing east.
3. **Damascus connection:** on his later return he learned that a son he had left in Damascus had died years before. Details about the child’s mother are unclear.
4. **Delhi:** he formed marital and household relationships while enjoying court patronage. The identities and sequence are incompletely preserved.
5. **Maldives:** he married repeatedly, including women connected to the ruling and ministerial elite. He says local marriage was easy to contract and dissolve because women ordinarily remained on their home islands while visiting men departed.
6. **Maldivian child:** he left at least one child in the islands. Sources differ in summaries over whether this was a son or daughter and over the child’s mother.
7. **Slave-concubines:** he bought, received, travelled with, sold, exchanged, or lost several enslaved women. He records emotional preference for some and conflict with others. Their names and origins are only intermittently supplied.

Marina Tolmacheva characterizes him as a “serial slave owner” whose enslaved companions bore the burdens of his mobility without the legal agency available to free wives. The evidence is Ibn Battuta’s own narrative, not a later accusation. In modern terminology these relationships involved sexual slavery; in his Mālikī legal environment they were treated as licit concubinage.

No complete list of descendants is possible, and modern claims of descent should be treated as genealogical tradition unless supported by a continuous pedigree.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1304–1325

Almost nothing is securely known about Ibn Battuta’s first twenty-one years beyond birthplace, family profession, and legal formation.

Tangier was a port linking the western Mediterranean, the Strait of Gibraltar, the Maghreb, and maritime routes into the Atlantic. It belonged to the Marinid state, whose political center lay at Fez. Ibn Battuta therefore grew up at a geographical edge of the Arabic-speaking Islamic scholarly world but within an established network of ports, pilgrimage routes, madrasas, hospices, judges, and Sufi lodges.

There is no contemporary evidence for:

- a specific childhood home;
- named childhood companions;
- siblings;
- formal enrollment in a particular Tangier madrasa;
- youthful business activity;
- military training;
- a pre-1325 journey;
- inherited wealth of any known amount.

His later ease in quoting legal concepts, identifying scholars, seeking certificates of learning, and obtaining judicial employment indicates substantial Mālikī instruction.

## Education and Formation

### Mālikī legal education

**Reported fact:** Ibn Battuta was trained in the Mālikī school of Sunni jurisprudence, dominant in the Maghreb. He was not presented as a major original legal theorist, but his western Mālikī formation was scarce and valuable in regions such as the Maldives.

His education probably included:

- Qurʾanic study;
- Arabic grammar and rhetoric;
- Mālikī jurisprudence;
- traditions concerning pilgrimage;
- elementary hadith study;
- the social practices expected of a jurist.

### Travelling education

Travel extended his formation. He frequented:

- madrasas;
- mosques;
- Sufi hospices or *zāwiyas*;
- tombs and shrines;
- scholarly houses;
- rulers’ courts.

He received hospitality, robes, money, mounts, and certificates from scholars and Sufi figures. The *Riḥla* repeatedly presents dreams, saints’ predictions, and blessings as guidance toward India and China.

A modern report that manuscripts of legal texts in Cairo can be associated with copying he undertook in Damascus has been offered as corroboration of his Syrian presence, though this is not yet a substitute for a full critical documentary study.

### Intellectual profile

Ibn Battuta’s intellectual orientation was:

- Sunni and Mālikī in law;
- strongly receptive to Sufi saints, dreams, blessings, and miracle traditions;
- attached to the unity and prestige of the *dār al-Islām*;
- socially conservative concerning public dress and gender relations;
- nevertheless dependent on the great cultural variety of Islamic trade and scholarly networks.

He sometimes condemned local Muslim customs—unveiled women, women’s social independence, mixed-gender friendship, alcohol use, or non-Mālikī ritual—as departures from the standards he preferred.

## Chronological Life History

## 1. Departure from Tangier and the first pilgrimage, 1325–1326

### Tangier to Tunis

On **2 Rajab 725 AH / 14 June 1325**—some conversions give 13 June—Ibn Battuta left Tangier at age twenty-one.

His famous account says he departed alone, leaving male and female loved ones and abandoning home “as birds forsake their nests.” The wording survives through Ibn Juzayy’s literary redaction and should not automatically be treated as an unedited diary entry.

Route, broadly accepted:

- Tangier;
- coastal and inland North Africa;
- Tlemcen;
- Miliana;
- Algiers;
- Bijaya;
- Constantine;
- Bône/Annaba;
- Tunis.

He travelled during outbreaks of illness, joined caravans for safety, and briefly became seriously sick. At Tunis he received recognition among scholars but also described loneliness when other travellers’ families greeted them.

### Tripolitania and Egypt

He proceeded through:

- Sfax;
- Gabès;
- Tripoli;
- Alexandria;
- the Nile Delta;
- Cairo.

In Alexandria he visited learned and Sufi figures. One holy man, Burhān al-Dīn, reportedly predicted that he would meet the saint’s spiritual brothers in India, Sind, and China. This is a **reported visionary or hagiographic episode**, not independent proof that the later route had already been planned.

Ibn Battuta admired Cairo’s size, population, institutions, markets, and Nile system. Some descriptive language in these western portions reflects the conventions of Arabic geographical literature.

### Failed Red Sea route

He first intended to approach Mecca through Upper Egypt and the Red Sea port of ʿAydhab. Conflict involving the Beja and Mamluk authorities blocked the route, forcing him back toward Cairo.

### Palestine and Syria

He travelled through parts of Palestine and Syria, including places associated with:

- Gaza;
- Hebron;
- Jerusalem;
- Acre and other coastal sites;
- Damascus.

The Palestine and Syria descriptions contain material closely resembling the earlier traveller Ibn Jubayr and possibly al-ʿAbdarī. This is **documented or strongly supported literary borrowing**. It remains possible that Ibn Battuta visited many of the locations while Ibn Juzayy supplemented his account with prestigious earlier prose.

In Damascus he studied, lodged in religious institutions, and joined the pilgrimage caravan.

### First hajj

He reached Medina and then Mecca in **1326**, completing his first pilgrimage. What began as a hajj became a long career of movement through Islamic scholarly and political networks.

---

## 2. Iraq, Persia, and repeated residence in Mecca, 1326–1330

After the 1326 pilgrimage he joined the Iraqi caravan and travelled through or claimed to travel through:

- Najaf;
- Basra;
- al-Ubulla;
- Isfahan;
- Shiraz;
- Baghdad;
- Mosul and northern Mesopotamia.

He visited ʿAlid shrines, Sufi establishments, scholars, and rulers. His reporting preserves observations about the fragmented post-Ilkhanid political landscape.

### Chronological and geographical problems

The Persian and Iraqi itinerary cannot always be made internally consistent. The narrative sometimes:

- places later information under an earlier visit;
- confuses historical rulers;
- compresses implausible distances;
- incorporates conventional shrine descriptions.

His account confuses the Khwarazmshah Muḥammad with his son Jalāl al-Dīn, one example of why it cannot be read as a uniformly reliable political chronicle.

### Meccan years

He returned to Mecca and spent prolonged periods there, probably participating in additional pilgrimages in 1327 and 1330. These years strengthened his identity as a pilgrim-jurist and connected him with scholars from across Africa and Eurasia.

He did not possess a fixed salary in Mecca so far as is known. Hospitality, gifts, religious endowments, and patronage helped sustain him.

---

## 3. Yemen, East Africa, Oman, and the Persian Gulf, approximately 1330–1332

Ibn Battuta crossed the Red Sea to Jidda and travelled through Yemen, including:

- Zabid;
- Taʿizz;
- Aden.

The Sanaʿa portion is among passages questioned by specialists because of chronology and possible dependence on received descriptions.

### East African coast

He sailed along the Swahili coast and reported visiting:

- Zeila;
- Mogadishu;
- Mombasa;
- Kilwa;
- other ports between the Horn of Africa and present-day Tanzania.

The broad voyage is generally accepted. His account is an important early source for the Indian Ocean commercial world, Muslim urban elites, maritime trade, and the prosperity of Kilwa.

At Mogadishu he described a powerful sultan, woven cloth, food customs, interpreters, and ceremonial hospitality. At Kilwa he observed a predominantly African population and a wealthy Muslim court linked to the gold trade.

Potential problems include conventional descriptions and uncertain identification of some ports, but the East African section is generally judged more valuable than the most disputed eastern passages.

### Arabia and Hormuz

He returned toward southern Arabia and claimed an overland or coastal journey through Oman and the Gulf, including Hormuz. Parts of the Oman itinerary are geographically difficult and may combine more than one route, hearsay, or editorial rearrangement.

---

## 4. Anatolia, the Black Sea, the Golden Horde, and Constantinople, approximately 1332–1333

After another passage through Egypt and Syria, Ibn Battuta travelled—or claimed to travel—through much of Anatolia.

### Anatolia

He described the **Akhī** fraternities: urban associations combining hospitality, craft or merchant organization, religious solidarity, and local authority. He received elaborate hospitality from them.

The full itinerary is disputed because successive towns cannot always be reconciled with distance and time. Specialists differ between:

- accepting the broad Anatolian journey while treating its order as corrupt;
- proposing several separate excursions later combined;
- regarding some places as acquired from informants.

### Golden Horde

He crossed the Black Sea to the Crimean region and entered the realm of **Öz Beg Khan**, ruler of the Golden Horde. He visited or described:

- Kaffa;
- the steppe;
- Azov;
- Majar;
- New Sarai;
- Astrakhan.

He joined the entourage of Bayalun, a wife of Öz Beg traditionally identified as a Byzantine princess.

### Constantinople

Ibn Battuta says he accompanied Bayalun to Constantinople, met Emperor Andronikos III Palaiologos, and saw major churches and urban monuments.

**Disputed claim:** some scholars accept the visit; others question it because:

- the itinerary is vague;
- names and court details are problematic;
- portions resemble stock descriptions;
- the chronology is compressed.

The passage nevertheless contains details that defenders regard as compatible with a genuine visit mediated by interpreters.

### Bulghar and the “Land of Darkness”

His claimed excursion north from the Volga region to Bulghar has long been considered one of the least credible episodes. Stephen Janicsek called it a fabrication. Travel times are implausible, and the “Land of Darkness” material resembles inherited geographical lore.

Best assessment:

- Golden Horde residence: **broadly accepted reported fact**.
- Journey to Bulghar exactly as narrated: **strongly disputed**.
- Far-northern “Land of Darkness”: **hearsay, literary geography, or legend**, not established eyewitness travel.

---

## 5. Central Asia, Afghanistan, and arrival in India, 1333 or 1334

From the Golden Horde world he travelled through territories associated with:

- Khwarazm;
- Bukhara;
- Samarkand;
- Balkh;
- the Hindu Kush;
- Afghanistan;
- the Indus frontier.

The text places his arrival at the Indus on **12 September 1333**, but reconstructed chronology has led some historians to prefer **1334**. The narrative’s exact sequence through Khurasan and eastern Anatolia is not reliable enough to eliminate the discrepancy.

His goal had become the Delhi court of Sultan **Muḥammad ibn Tughluq**, reputed for lavish recruitment of foreign scholars and officials.

---

## 6. Delhi and service to Muḥammad ibn Tughluq, approximately 1334–1341

### Appointment as judge

Muḥammad ibn Tughluq welcomed foreign jurists and appointed Ibn Battuta a *qāḍī*. The exact jurisdiction and practical reach of the office are unclear. Ibn Battuta lacked strong command of the local languages and depended on assistants.

### Court wealth and indebtedness

He received:

- money;
- robes;
- horses;
- housing or landed revenues;
- slaves;
- ceremonial gifts.

He also incurred heavy debts, apparently anticipating that royal generosity would cover them. His wealth was therefore court-dependent rather than a stable private fortune. He did not create a known commercial company, investment partnership, or durable business organization.

The sultan could be extraordinarily generous and violently punitive. Ibn Battuta’s position alternated between privilege and fear.

### Political danger

He associated with or visited the Sufi shaykh Shihāb al-Dīn, who fell under royal suspicion. Ibn Battuta feared that the relationship would implicate him. He withdrew into ascetic practices and contemplated permanent religious seclusion.

This was not a formal criminal prosecution in the modern sense, but it was a genuine danger within an autocratic court in which associates of suspected opponents could be punished.

### Embassy to China

The sultan eventually appointed him to an embassy intended for the Yuan emperor. The delegation carried rich gifts and was expected to travel by sea.

The appointment gave Ibn Battuta a prestigious exit from Delhi, but the mission became a major failure.

---

## 7. The failed embassy and shipwreck, approximately 1341–1342

The delegation travelled toward the west coast of India. It encountered attacks and disorder; Ibn Battuta was separated from his party, robbed, and nearly killed.

At Calicut, ships prepared for the China mission. A storm or maritime accident destroyed one vessel and drove another away. Ibn Battuta’s baggage, enslaved persons, and official gifts were lost or carried off. He was left ashore.

He feared returning to Delhi because he could not complete the embassy and might be punished. Instead he attached himself to the ruler Jamāl al-Dīn at Honavar on the Malabar coast.

The embassy was therefore a **documented narrative failure in his own account**, not a completed diplomatic mission.

---

## 8. Maldives: judicial office, marriage, coercive reform, and political intrigue, approximately 1343–1344

Ibn Battuta reached the Maldives unintentionally or as a refuge from the failed mission. His western Mālikī credentials were unusual and politically valuable.

### Chief judicial authority

He became *qāḍī* and married into prominent local families. He received wealth, servants, and influence.

### Legal and social program

He attempted to enforce his understanding of Islamic law, including:

- regular congregational prayer;
- punishments for religious noncompliance;
- changes to women’s dress;
- stricter judicial procedures.

He complained that Maldivian women went bare-breasted and says he could not persuade them to adopt the covering he demanded.

These statements are not later hostile allegations; they are his own presentation. Whether every punishment was actually implemented is not independently documented.

### Marriages

He married several women, including elite women connected to ministers or the ruling house. He divorced and remarried as political relationships shifted.

### Political intrigue

Ibn Battuta became involved in factional conflict and discussed bringing the islands under the influence or control of a South Indian ruler related to him by marriage. A specialist assessment has described him as an opportunist whose Maldivian activity may have included designs for intervention or regime change.

Evidentiary label: **reported political plotting**, because the principal evidence is his own narrative. There is no surviving indictment or judicial finding against him.

He left after roughly nine months amid hostility with ministers and the likelihood that he would otherwise be dismissed.

---

## 9. Sri Lanka, Maʿbar, Bengal, Southeast Asia, and China, approximately 1344–1347

### Sri Lanka

Ibn Battuta sailed to Sri Lanka and sought the ruler’s assistance to visit Adam’s Peak, revered in Muslim tradition as the place where Adam descended to earth.

The mountain journey mixes recognizable geography with devotional tradition. The enormous sacred footprint is a religious object interpreted differently by Muslims, Buddhists, and Hindus; it is not physical evidence for the historical Adam.

His ship later encountered maritime danger. He returned toward southern India.

### Maʿbar and southern India

He became entangled in the politics of the Maʿbar Sultanate, whose ruler was connected to him by marriage. He witnessed or reported military violence and severe punishments. Disease and political upheaval disrupted his plans.

### Return to Maldives

He returned briefly to the Maldives, where he saw or attempted to see a child he had left behind. The surviving narrative does not support a complete reconstruction of the child’s later life.

### Bengal

He travelled to Bengal and sought the Sufi Jalāl al-Dīn Tabrīzī or, more probably, a later regional saint confused in transmission. Chronology and saintly identities are problematic.

He met Fakhr al-Dīn Mubārak Shāh’s political world and described the abundance and low prices of Bengal, while remarking on its oppressive climate.

### Sumatra

He visited the Muslim kingdom of Samudra-Pasai in northern Sumatra and described Sultan al-Malik al-Ẓāhir as a pious ruler. This passage is generally considered plausible and valuable evidence for an early Southeast Asian Muslim court.

### Tawālisī

The *Riḥla* describes a place called Tawālisī ruled or defended by a warrior princess who spoke Turkish or a related language.

Identification proposals have included:

- parts of the Philippines;
- Champa;
- Java;
- a composite literary location;
- a misunderstood Southeast Asian port.

No identification commands consensus. The warrior-princess episode is **unresolved and possibly literary or hearsay-based**.

### China

Ibn Battuta says he reached Chinese ports conventionally identified as Quanzhou and Guangzhou, travelled through the interior, and reached Khān Bāligh/Beijing or another major northern city.

#### Evidence supporting some Chinese travel

- descriptions of paper money, porcelain, shipping, urban controls, and portrait registration fit Yuan China in general;
- Muslim maritime networks connected India, Southeast Asia, and southern Chinese ports;
- some toponyms have plausible identifications;
- defenders argue that errors can result from dictation years later and Ibn Juzayy’s editing.

#### Evidence against the full itinerary

- few places are securely identified;
- travel times and sequence are unclear;
- the Yuan emperor’s name is wrong or confused;
- the account contains stock marvels and stories available in earlier literature;
- some passages resemble al-ʿUmarī, Sulaymān al-Tājir, Rashīd al-Dīn, al-Juwaynī, Marco Polo traditions, or Alexander-romance material;
- no Chinese record securely identifies Ibn Battuta or the alleged Delhi embassy;
- he provides less precise Chinese information than expected from the length of the claimed journey.

#### Assessment

- Travel from India to Sumatra: **broadly accepted**.
- Arrival at one or more southern Chinese ports: **plausible but disputed**.
- Extensive interior journey to Beijing exactly as narrated: **strongly disputed**.
- Claim that the entire travel career was fabricated in Morocco: a minority theory associated especially with Ralf Elger; it has not become the scholarly consensus.

---

## 10. Return west, Black Death, and Morocco, approximately 1346–1349

Ibn Battuta returned by stages through:

- Southeast Asia;
- India;
- the Persian Gulf;
- Iraq;
- Syria;
- Egypt;
- the Hijaz;
- North Africa.

### Black Death

In Syria in 1348 he encountered the Black Death. He described mass mortality and communal prayers at Damascus. The broad epidemic context is independently documented, although his exact numerical death totals should be treated cautiously.

He made another pilgrimage to Mecca and then travelled west.

### Sardinia episode

From Tunis he joined a vessel that reached or approached Sardinia, then Christian territory. He soon withdrew, reportedly fearing capture or danger. This was a short maritime excursion rather than a sustained European journey.

### Return to Fez and Tangier

He reached the Marinid court at Fez in 1349 and returned to Tangier.

He learned:

- his father had died about fifteen years before;
- his mother had died only a few months before his arrival.

After approximately twenty-four years away, neither parent survived to hear his account directly.

---

## 11. al-Andalus, approximately 1350

A Castilian threat to Gibraltar prompted Ibn Battuta to cross into Nasrid Granada. Alfonso XI of Castile died of plague during the siege in 1350, reducing the immediate danger.

Ibn Battuta visited:

- Gibraltar;
- Málaga;
- Granada;
- other Andalusi locations.

At Granada he met scholars and officials, including the young writer **Ibn Juzayy**, who would later edit the *Riḥla*. This meeting is one of the most consequential relationships of his later life.

He did not conduct a major military campaign and is not documented as having fought in battle, despite framing the journey partly in terms of frontier defense.

---

## 12. Trans-Saharan journey to Mali, 1351–1354

After returning to Morocco, Ibn Battuta set out for the western Sudan.

Route included:

- Sijilmasa;
- the salt-mining settlement of Taghaza;
- Walata;
- the Mali imperial heartland;
- the Niger region;
- Timbuktu;
- Gao;
- Takadda;
- the central Sahara and Tuat;
- return to Morocco.

### Taghaza

He described buildings made from salt slabs and the harsh conditions endured by enslaved laborers. Salt, gold, textiles, slaves, and foodstuffs sustained the trans-Saharan economy.

### Mali court

He visited the court of **Mansa Sulayman**, brother and successor of Mansa Musa.

Ibn Battuta expected lavish patronage and was disappointed by the ruler’s initial gift of food. He complained openly and later received gold or other support. His reaction shows the expectations of an itinerant court scholar accustomed to rulers rewarding foreign prestige.

### Observations

He praised:

- public security;
- low incidence of theft;
- regular prayer;
- Qurʾanic education;
- the ruler’s enforcement of order.

He criticized:

- women’s lack of veiling;
- friendship between unrelated men and women;
- public nudity or partial nudity in certain settings;
- customs he considered inconsistent with his Mālikī norms.

His account is a central eyewitness source for fourteenth-century Mali, but it is an outsider’s selective court-and-clerical view. He misunderstood the Niger’s drainage, associating it with the Nile system.

### Recall

The Marinid ruler Abū ʿInān ordered him back. He reached Fez in late 1353 or early 1354.

---

## 13. Dictation of the *Riḥla*, 1354–1355/1356

Sultan **Abū ʿInān Fāris** instructed Ibn Battuta to recount his travels. The Andalusi scholar **Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad Ibn Juzayy al-Kalbī** served as editor and literary craftsman.

The work’s full title is:

**Tuḥfat al-nuẓẓār fī gharāʾib al-amṣār wa-ʿajāʾib al-asfār**

**تحفة النظار في غرائب الأمصار وعجائب الأسفار**

Translations include:

- *A Gift to Those Who Contemplate the Wonders of Cities and the Marvels of Travelling*;
- *A Masterpiece for Observers concerning the Curiosities of Cities and the Wonders of Journeys*.

Ibn Juzayy:

- organized Ibn Battuta’s dictation;
- supplied elevated prose and poetry;
- inserted or adapted earlier literary descriptions;
- occasionally signaled that a passage came from another author;
- framed Ibn Battuta as the supreme traveller of the Islamic world.

A colophon dates completion to **3 Dhu al-Hijja 756 AH**, normally **9 December 1355**, although references sometimes give 1356 or 1357 for completion or circulation.

The surviving BnF manuscript tradition includes an incomplete manuscript widely regarded as Ibn Juzayy’s autograph or close to it. Its exact shelfmark history is complicated by renumbering; modern discussions identify the relevant manuscripts among BnF Arabic 2287–2291 and the former Supplément arabe 907–911.

## Later Life and Final Years

After completion of the *Riḥla*, the evidence becomes sparse.

A later biographical report states that Ibn Battuta served as a judge in Morocco, possibly at Tamasna. The duration, jurisdiction, and rulings of this office are unknown.

There is no reliable evidence that he:

- undertook another major international expedition;
- founded a school or commercial enterprise;
- produced another book;
- accumulated a documented estate;
- held high ministerial office;
- suffered imprisonment;
- faced a formal lawsuit or criminal charge.

He probably died in **1368–1369**, though 1377–1378 persisted in older literature.

## Employment, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Occupations

- Mālikī jurist.
- Court-supported traveller and scholar.
- Judge in Delhi.
- Chief or senior judge in the Maldives.
- Probable judge in Morocco.
- Diplomatic envoy-designate from Delhi to Yuan China.
- Narrator or co-producer of the *Riḥla*.

### Patronage network

Important patrons included:

- Muḥammad ibn Tughluq of Delhi;
- the Maldivian ruling and ministerial elite;
- regional rulers and governors in India;
- Muslim sultans and merchants around the Indian Ocean;
- Mansa Sulayman of Mali;
- Abū ʿInān Fāris of Morocco.

### Financial pattern

Ibn Battuta’s livelihood depended on:

- rulers’ gifts;
- judicial salary;
- robes and mounts;
- hospitality foundations;
- marriage connections;
- religious prestige;
- enslavement and ownership of human beings.

He sometimes became heavily indebted. Gifts could make him wealthy temporarily, but storms, robbery, political changes, and travel repeatedly destroyed or dispersed his property.

No patents, inventions, companies, formal investments, or independent commercial ledgers are associated with him.

---

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Political conduct

Ibn Battuta was not an ideological revolutionary. He operated within monarchical court systems and sought patronage from rulers. His political conduct was pragmatic:

- praising rulers who protected scholars and travellers;
- criticizing those whose gifts he found inadequate;
- avoiding return to Delhi after diplomatic failure;
- considering political intervention in the Maldives;
- responding to the Marinid sultan’s recall from Mali.

### Religious outlook

His worldview combined:

- Mālikī legalism;
- Sunni piety;
- pilgrimage;
- veneration of saints;
- Sufi hospitality;
- dreams and prognostication;
- fascination with miracles.

He showed no modern separation between observation, sacred geography, miracle report, and political description.

### Attitudes toward difference

He often evaluated Muslim societies against Maghrebi legal and gender norms. He could admire non-Maghrebi institutions while condemning:

- unveiled women;
- matrilocal or easily dissolved marriage;
- women’s independent social life;
- intoxicant use;
- unfamiliar prayer customs;
- non-Muslim ritual.

He also depended heavily on multilingual, multiethnic maritime communities and repeatedly formed relationships across the Islamic world.

---

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Ibn Juzayy

**Documented relationship:** editor and literary collaborator, first encountered in Granada and later assigned to shape the travel narrative at Fez.

His role is central to every authorship and reliability question. The *Riḥla* is best treated as the product of Ibn Battuta’s testimony plus Ibn Juzayy’s compilation and literary intervention.

### Abū ʿInān Fāris

**Documented patron:** Marinid sultan who recalled or received Ibn Battuta and commissioned the travel book. The project converted an itinerant court career into a durable literary reputation.

### Muḥammad ibn Tughluq

**Reported employer and patron:** Delhi sultan who appointed Ibn Battuta as judge and envoy. Their relationship combined extraordinary largesse, debt, dependence, and fear.

### Öz Beg Khan and Bayalun

**Reported court relationship:** Ibn Battuta stayed within the Golden Horde court network and says he accompanied Bayalun to Constantinople.

### Mansa Sulayman

**Reported host:** ruler of Mali whom Ibn Battuta criticized for insufficient initial generosity but whose empire he described at length.

### Scholars and Sufis

The *Riḥla* names hundreds of jurists, ascetics, saints, teachers, hosts, officials, and merchants. Many encounters were brief. Some names are independently known; others survive only in his account.

### Adversaries

No single lifelong enemy is known. Situational antagonists included:

- robbers and attackers;
- Delhi courtiers and officials;
- Maldivian ministers who opposed his judicial and political activity;
- maritime rivals and unreliable ship captains;
- rulers whose displeasure he feared.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Evidentiary Findings

## Authorship and plagiarism

### Established evidence

Ibn Juzayy incorporated descriptions from earlier travel writers, especially **Ibn Jubayr**, and likely from al-ʿAbdarī and geographical literature. Parts of Damascus, Mecca, Medina, Palestine, and other regions closely track older wording.

Label: **documented literary borrowing**.

This practice must be evaluated within medieval compilation conventions, but those conventions do not erase the fact that some ostensibly first-person geographical prose was inherited rather than freshly observed.

### Strong accusation

Ralf Elger has called Ibn Battuta one of literary history’s great liars, forgers, or plagiarists and proposed that much of the text was constructed in the Maghreb with Ibn Juzayy.

Label: **scholarly accusation/minority theory**, not consensus.

Counterevidence includes:

- plausible and sometimes highly specific regional observations;
- information not known from the obvious literary models;
- compatibility of much of the route with contemporary Islamic mobility;
- manuscript and scholarly evidence consistent with at least substantial genuine travel.

## Internal chronology

The post-Delhi chronology cannot be harmonized exactly. Problems include:

- inconsistent dates;
- impossible or compressed travel times;
- events placed under the wrong visit;
- confused rulers;
- editorial combination of separate journeys.

Label: **documented textual problem**.

## China

The greatest geographical controversy. Possible southern-port travel remains plausible; the full inland route and imperial audience are unverified and widely doubted.

Label: **unresolved/disputed claim**.

## Bulghar

Travel time and inherited northern lore render the journey as narrated improbable.

Label: **strongly disputed; likely fabrication, hearsay, or itinerary corruption**.

## Constantinople

Accepted by some specialists and rejected by others.

Label: **disputed but plausible**.

## Maldives political activity

Ibn Battuta acknowledges factional involvement and contemplated external intervention. This may be described as political plotting, but not as a legally proven coup attempt.

Label: **self-reported political intrigue**.

## Coercive legal conduct

His own narrative describes attempts to compel prayer, impose punishments, and alter women’s dress.

Label: **self-reported conduct**, with the scale of implementation uncertain.

## Slavery and sexual exploitation

His ownership, transfer, and sexual use of enslaved women are explicit in the *Riḥla*. These are not rumors. Individual women’s perspectives do not survive.

Label: **documented by self-report; independently contextualized by scholarship**.

## Modern criminal or financial allegations

The dedicated adversarial search found no meaningful modern-style lawsuit, fraud prosecution, money-laundering claim, intelligence connection, patent dispute, or corruption proceeding. Applying those categories to a fourteenth-century itinerant jurist without evidence would be anachronistic.

Searches from 2024–2026 chiefly returned renewed debate over whether he reached China, discussions of plagiarism, and nationalist or religious appropriation—not new documentary criminal findings.

## Myths, Legends, Miracles, and Disputed Narratives

## The sea-spirit and conversion of the Maldives

### Story

A sea demon, often called **Rannamaari**, supposedly demanded a virgin sacrifice each month. A visiting Muslim, Abū al-Barakāt al-Barbarī, recited the Qurʾan through the night, defeated or drove away the being, and induced the ruler to convert to Islam.

### Appearance and circulation

Ibn Battuta says Maldivian informants told him this story in the fourteenth century. Later Maldivian chronicles and folklore preserve related but different conversion accounts.

### Competing identities

- Ibn Battuta’s version makes the converter a Maghrebi or Berber.
- Later official history identifies a Tabrizi or Persian-associated holy man.
- Modern Somali, Moroccan, Persian, and local narratives have advanced competing ethnic identifications.

### Evidence

The Maldives converted in the twelfth century, before Ibn Battuta’s visit. No contemporary evidence verifies the demon or virgin sacrifice. The story resembles wider Indian Ocean and Islamic saint-versus-demon conversion motifs.

### Reputation

It made Ibn Battuta’s narrative central to Maldivian national and religious memory and generated modern disputes over who brought Islam to the islands.

Label: **conversion legend containing a historical memory of Islamization**.

## Adam’s footprint

The footprint on Sri Lanka’s sacred mountain was identified by Muslims with Adam, by Buddhists with the Buddha, and by Hindus with Śiva.

Label: **sacred legend and shared pilgrimage tradition**, not a verifiable bodily footprint.

## The roc

At sea Ibn Battuta describes or reports an enormous bird-like form associated with the legendary **rukh/roc**.

Possible explanations include:

- cloud or island;
- atmospheric refraction;
- sailor folklore;
- literary inheritance from marvel literature.

Label: **maritime marvel/legend**.

## Chinese magician dismemberment story

Ibn Battuta reports a performance in which a magician apparently dismembered and restored a boy or produced a comparable illusion. He presents it as trickery or astonishing performance rather than reliable anatomy.

Label: **traveller’s marvel, illusion report, or literary tale**.

## Prophecy of worldwide travel

The Alexandrian saint Burhān al-Dīn allegedly predicted Ibn Battuta’s future meetings in India and China.

Evidence exists only in a narrative composed after those supposed events.

Label: **retrospective hagiographic prophecy**.

## “Greatest traveller before steam”

Modern maps often assign him approximately 117,000 kilometres or 73,000 miles, making him the most widely travelled known premodern individual.

The figure is a reconstruction dependent on accepting disputed segments and selecting routes between named places. It is not an odometer reading.

Label: **reasonable modern estimate, not an exact documented total**.

## Rivalry with Marco Polo

Popular culture frequently stages Ibn Battuta and Marco Polo as rival claimants to the title of greatest traveller. They never met; Marco Polo died around the time Ibn Battuta left Tangier.

Label: **modern symbolic rivalry**, not a historical relationship.

## Works by Ibn Battuta

## Genuine or substantially attributable work

### *Tuḥfat al-nuẓẓār fī gharāʾib al-amṣār wa-ʿajāʾib al-asfār*

- Original language: Arabic.
- Dictated: approximately 1354–1355.
- Completed by Ibn Juzayy: 3 Dhu al-Hijja 756 AH / approximately 9 December 1355.
- Genre: *riḥla*, combining itinerary, pilgrimage narrative, biography, geography, court ethnography, saints’ lives, marvels, and literary compilation.
- Authorship: Ibn Battuta supplied the travel testimony; Ibn Juzayy edited, structured, embellished, and incorporated borrowed material.
- Status: only securely attributable major work.

### Poetry

The *Riḥla* contains or attributes occasional verses to Ibn Battuta, but he is not known to have produced an independent surviving *dīwān*. Attribution of individual verses can be complicated by Ibn Juzayy’s editorial role.

## Major manuscript and print history

- Surviving BnF manuscripts acquired principally through nineteenth-century French activity in Algeria.
- An incomplete copy is widely regarded as Ibn Juzayy’s autograph or near-autograph.
- Baron de Slane published a French translation of the western Sudan section in 1843.
- Charles Defrémery and Beniamino Raffaello Sanguinetti published the foundational Arabic/French edition, *Voyages d’Ibn Batoutah*, four volumes, 1853–1859.
- H. A. R. Gibb began the standard full English translation for the Hakluyt Society; C. F. Beckingham completed the final volume. Publication extended from 1958 into the 1990s, with later collected publication.
- ʿAbd al-Hādī al-Tāzī edited a major five-volume Arabic edition, Rabat, 1997.
- Tim Mackintosh-Smith edited an accessible abridged English version in 2002/2003.

## Death, Burial, and Present Status

Ibn Battuta was dead by the late fourteenth century. The strongest modern preference is:

- **Death:** 770 AH / 1368–1369.
- **Location:** Morocco, exact place unknown.
- **Cause:** not recorded.
- **Burial:** uncertain.

The Tangier mausoleum bearing his name is a site of civic memory. It should be described as a **traditional tomb attribution**, not an archaeologically verified grave.

---

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Medieval reception

The *Riḥla* did not disappear, but Ibn Battuta was not immediately transformed into a globally canonical author. Ibn Khaldun knew of him and reportedly met or heard him at the Marinid court. Some contemporaries or near-contemporaries doubted extraordinary portions of his stories.

Ibn Juzayy’s conclusion called him the traveller of the age and suggested that calling him the traveller of the entire Islamic community would not exceed the truth. This was courtly praise and a foundational act of reputation-building.

### Nineteenth-century recovery

European awareness expanded after abbreviated manuscripts reached scholars such as Ulrich Jasper Seetzen. French colonial acquisition of Algerian manuscripts supplied the textual basis for the Defrémery–Sanguinetti edition.

The transfer of manuscripts to Paris is now also studied as part of the colonial history of confiscated or displaced Arabic archives. The modern scholarly “recovery” of Ibn Battuta was therefore inseparable from French expansion in North Africa.

### Modern national and global memory

He has become:

- a Moroccan national figure;
- an emblem of Tangier;
- a symbol of Islamic-world connectivity;
- a comparative figure beside Marco Polo;
- a source for histories of globalization, pilgrimage, and the Indian Ocean.

Institutions and objects named for him include:

- Tangier Ibn Battouta Airport;
- Ibn Battuta Stadium in Tangier;
- Ibn Battuta Mall in Dubai;
- hotels, schools, streets, museums, travel prizes, and cultural programs;
- the lunar crater **Ibn Battuta**.

### Reinterpretation

Older celebratory accounts often treated the *Riḥla* as transparent eyewitness reporting. Later scholarship has emphasized:

- Ibn Juzayy’s co-production;
- copying from earlier travel literature;
- impossible chronology;
- courtly self-fashioning;
- dependence on patronage;
- enslavement and concubinage;
- gender and legal coercion;
- the colonial history of the manuscripts.

At the same time, the work remains indispensable because it preserves unique or exceptionally early information about regions for which surviving narrative sources are scarce.

## Atlas Connections

The atlas roster is extremely large, but only a small number of defensible connections appear.

### Abu Abdallah Muhammad ibn Battuta

- **Nature:** subject himself.
- **Date/place:** 1304–1368/69, principally Tangier, Fez, and the territories described above.
- **Label:** documented identity.

### Mansa Musa

- **Nature:** Ibn Battuta did not meet Mansa Musa, who died before the traveller reached Mali. He visited Musa’s empire under Musa’s brother and successor, Mansa Sulayman, and moved through a political and pilgrimage network transformed by Musa’s reign.
- **Date/place:** Mali, 1352–1353.
- **Label:** documented historical influence/context; **no personal meeting**.

### Marco Polo

- **Nature:** no meeting, correspondence, or direct influence is documented. Their narratives are compared because both described Eurasian travel and Yuan China. Similar China motifs may reflect shared literary traditions or later textual comparison, not personal contact.
- **Dates/places:** Marco Polo died circa 1324, shortly before Ibn Battuta departed Tangier in 1325.
- **Label:** modern comparative association; no biographical connection.

### Muhammad ibn Abdullah

- **Nature:** Ibn Battuta’s religious life was explicitly organized around Islam, the hajj, Medina, prophetic traditions, and Islamic law. He visited the Prophet’s mosque and tomb precinct in Medina.
- **Dates/places:** first visit in 1326, with later pilgrimages.
- **Label:** documented religious and intellectual influence; no contemporaneous personal relationship.

### Abu Bakr as-Siddiq

- **Nature:** encountered through Islamic sacred history, traditions, and sites, not personally.
- **Dates/places:** references arise within pilgrimage and shrine geography.
- **Label:** documented religious-historical influence; no personal meeting.

### Ibn Arabi

- **Nature:** Ibn Battuta moved through Sufi networks in regions where Ibn ʿArabi’s reputation circulated, but the sources consulted do not establish an explicit personal intellectual debt to Ibn ʿArabi. Ibn ʿArabi died in 1240.
- **Label:** no securely documented direct connection found.

### Nizam ad-Din Auliya and Amir Khusrow Dehlavi

- **Nature:** both belonged to the Delhi religious-literary world immediately preceding Ibn Battuta’s residence. Nizam al-Din died in 1325 and Amir Khusrow in 1325, years before Ibn Battuta reached Delhi. No personal meeting was possible, and no explicit direct influence sufficient for an atlas connection was established in the consulted sources.
- **Label:** absence of documented connection.

### Kublai Khan

- **Nature:** Ibn Battuta travelled in the later Yuan period, after Kublai Khan’s death. He did not meet him. Popular comparisons sometimes merge Mongol-era China across generations.
- **Label:** shared dynastic setting only; no direct connection.

### Genghis Khan

- **Nature:** Ibn Battuta travelled through successor states created by the Mongol conquests and met courts descended from the Mongol imperial order. Genghis Khan had died a century earlier.
- **Label:** documented structural historical influence; no direct relationship.

### Odoric of Pordenone

- **Nature:** near-contemporary traveller in Asia whose route and reports are sometimes compared with Ibn Battuta’s, especially in debates about China. No meeting, correspondence, or acknowledged borrowing was established.
- **Label:** scholarly comparison only.

### Rabban Bar Sauma

- **Nature:** earlier Eurasian traveller through Mongol networks. No direct meeting or acknowledged textual influence was found.
- **Label:** comparative association only.

### Rabbi Benjamin son of Jonah of Tudela, Xuanzang, Egeria, Pytheas, Herodotus, and other atlas travellers

- **Nature:** later historiography places them in comparative histories of travel. No direct transmission or explicit acknowledgment by Ibn Battuta was established in the sources used.
- **Label:** modern typological comparison, not a biographical connection.

### General finding

Apart from Mansa Musa’s institutional legacy and the religious influence of Muhammad, the roster contains few genuine intersections. Ibn Battuta’s documented personal network—Muḥammad ibn Tughluq, Öz Beg Khan, Mansa Sulayman, Abū ʿInān, Ibn Juzayy, Bayalun, and numerous jurists and Sufis—is largely absent from the supplied atlas list. Absence of further connections is therefore a substantive finding.
