# Daniel Gastón Schávelzon: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Daniel Gastón Schávelzon is living. This dossier reconstructs his life through September 2026 from university and research-institution records, publications, interviews, archival notices, contemporaneous journalism, and academic studies. Public documentation is unusually rich concerning his education, exile, institutional career, fieldwork, and bibliography, but sparse concerning his private adult family life, finances, health, and intimate relationships.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by an institutional record, publication, archival collection, or direct contemporary evidence.
- **Reported fact:** supplied by Schávelzon or a secondary source but not independently documented here.
- **Interpretation:** an analytical conclusion advanced by Schávelzon or another scholar.
- **Allegation or theory:** asserted without sufficient evidence for treatment as fact.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story circulating socially but contradicted, unsupported, or intrinsically legendary.
- **Unresolved:** the available evidence does not establish a firm conclusion.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal ancestry

**Documented family history:** Schávelzon comes from a Jewish immigrant medical family whose earlier generations migrated to Argentina from territories then within the Russian Empire.

His paternal great-grandparents are identified in family records as:

- **Aisik Schavelzon**
- **Adela Kantarovik**

They reached Argentina with their children in 1905. The family’s places of origin are given as Minsk—now Belarus—and Kherson/Jerson—now Ukraine. Daniel’s surviving family photographs reportedly extend back to Russia and Poland in the 1890s.

Two brothers of the immigrant generation, **Simón Schavelzon** and **Salomón Schavelzon**, founded the Instituto de Clínica Médica y Cirugía in Buenos Aires in 1929. The institute has been described as an early—possibly the first—prepaid medical-care organization in the Americas. Daniel later wrote about its history.

### Parents

- **Father:** Dr. **José “Coco” Schavelzon** (Buenos Aires, 5 February 1918–Buenos Aires, 9 July 2014), surgeon, oncologist, professor of surgery, psychoanalyst, and pioneer of Argentine psycho-oncology. José directed the family medical institute with his cousins Eduardo and Alberto Schavelzon; later he developed a clinical and psychoanalytic approach to cancer patients and corresponded with Anna Freud about Sigmund Freud’s illness.
- **Mother:** **Rosa Esther Chavín**, born in Buenos Aires on 9 August 1916. No reliable death date was located.

The family’s medical, psychoanalytic, and documentary interests supplied part of Daniel’s formative environment, but no source located says that his father directed him toward architecture or archaeology.

### Siblings

- **Guillermo Schavelzon**, editor and literary agent.
- **Diego Schavelzon**, plastic surgeon.

Daniel was one of José and Rosa’s three sons. Publicly accessible family summaries say José had six grandchildren and six great-grandchildren, but they do not reliably allocate those descendants among the three sons.

### Spouse, partners, and children

No sufficiently reliable public source located in this research identifies Daniel Schávelzon’s spouse, partner, or children. Search-engine people-data fragments are unsuitable evidence and are not used here. This is therefore **unresolved**, not evidence that he never married or had children.

### Family archives

From 2017, Schávelzon’s professional manuscripts, correspondence, personal papers, and family photographs were deposited at the University of New Mexico’s Center for Southwest Research and Special Collections. UNM publicized the processing of the “Daniel Schavelzon, Archeology Research Manuscript Collection.” A related José Schavelzon family collection covers approximately 1890–2017.

In December 2017 the Biblioteca Nacional Mariano Moreno in Buenos Aires announced a donation from Schávelzon of material relating particularly to Mesoamerican archaeology. These deposits make UNM and the Argentine National Library the main repositories for future research into his correspondence, family background, unpublished work, and professional networks.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1950–1967

Schávelzon was born and grew up in Buenos Aires. Detailed evidence about his schools, childhood homes, religious upbringing, and early domestic life is not publicly available.

A later academic reconstruction based partly on an interview with him says that he initially wanted to study anthropology. By the late 1960s, however, anthropology at the Universidad de Buenos Aires had been disrupted by the interventions associated with Juan Carlos Onganía’s dictatorship. He therefore entered architecture while retaining a historical and anthropological orientation.

His subsequent work suggests an early interest in the physical traces of the past—buildings, plans, excavated objects, altered landscapes, and the ways institutions construct historical narratives—but no source establishes a single childhood event as the origin of that interest.

## Education and Formation, 1968–1984

### Universidad de Buenos Aires, 1968–1975

Schávelzon entered the Facultad de Arquitectura y Urbanismo of the Universidad de Buenos Aires in 1968 and graduated as an architect in 1975.

His student years coincided with intense political conflict, successive university interventions, the rise of revolutionary organizations, state and para-state violence, and the disappearance or exile of students and teachers.

He worked or collaborated in two markedly different intellectual settings:

1. The **Centro de Estudios Urbanos y Regionales**, then associated with the Instituto Torcuato Di Tella, under urban historian and planner **Jorge Enrique Hardoy**.
2. The UBA’s **Instituto de Arte Americano e Investigaciones Estéticas**, the principal local center for Latin American architectural history.

Hardoy became a consequential mentor and patron. He not only exposed Schávelzon to continental urban history but later supplied recommendation letters that enabled him to find work in Ecuador and Mexico.

### Student politics and institutional conflict

**Reported fact, based on Schávelzon’s 2022 recollection:** He collaborated with and participated in the architecture student center and was linked to a sector of the Partido Comunista Revolucionario, but denied having been a committed or organizational militant.

He later distinguished his case from those of architects expelled or pursued principally for clandestine militancy. He attributed his immediate institutional vulnerability partly to a personal and professional confrontation with **Jorge Gazaneo** and **Mabel Scarone**, then important figures at the Instituto de Arte Americano.

The precise substance of that confrontation has not been recovered from institutional minutes or personnel records. What is documented through his testimony is that he regarded it as an important reason for leaving.

### Flight before the 1976 coup

Schávelzon left Argentina approximately one week before the military coup of 24 March 1976. He recalled disappearances around the university, intimidation affecting members of his family, warnings about the impending coup, and his belief that even if he was not first on a repression list, he would eventually be reached.

This is appropriately described as **political exile**, while recognizing his own distinction: he did not claim a high-level militant role or a formal expulsion order. The threat environment and timing are documented; his exact status on any security-service list remains **unresolved**.

### Ecuador, approximately 1976–1978

Schávelzon initially moved to Quito, Ecuador, using letters from Hardoy addressed to the dean of the architecture faculty and the director of the Museo del Banco Central.

He quickly obtained university teaching and private museum work. He also conducted archaeological research on Ecuador’s coast, including a large Manteño cultural site, and began publishing on pre-Hispanic architecture and representations of buildings in material culture.

He later explained that an extended university strike left him without salary for approximately eight months. Once the arrears were paid, he and his household decided to leave. The identity of the companion implied by his use of “we” in the interview has not been established.

### Mexico, approximately 1978–February 1984

Schávelzon then relocated to Mexico, again carrying Hardoy’s recommendations. Mexico became the decisive stage of his professional formation.

He taught:

- History of architecture in the self-governed or **Autogobierno** sector of the Facultad de Arquitectura, UNAM, alongside **Jorge Ramos de Dios**.
- History and theory of restoration at the Escuela Nacional de Conservación, Restauración y Museografía “Manuel del Castillo Negrete,” commonly called the Escuela de Churubusco, within Mexico’s Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia.

He completed:

- A master’s degree in the restoration of archaeological monuments and sites, generally dated 1981.
- A doctorate in architecture at the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México, generally dated 1984.

His doctoral research treated the theory and history of restoration of Mesoamerican pre-Hispanic monuments from approximately 1380 to 1980. The work placed conservation practice within the history of ideas rather than treating restoration as a neutral technical procedure. Later scholarship on Mesoamerican architecture has singled out his thesis as an early architectural contribution to a field often controlled administratively by archaeologists or engineers.

His Mexican years established four lasting lines of work:

- Mesoamerican architectural history.
- Archaeological conservation and restoration.
- The study of architectural representations and models in pre-Hispanic artifacts.
- A critique of apparently objective heritage practices as historically conditioned reconstructions.

Among his early publications were bibliographical and synthetic studies of Olmec culture, Mixteca-Puebla archaeology, Ecuadorian architecture, Cuicuilco, and architectural representations in the archaeology of the Americas.

## Return to Argentina and the Creation of Urban Archaeology, 1984–1991

Schávelzon returned to Buenos Aires in February 1984, shortly after the restoration of constitutional government under Raúl Alfonsín. Accounts describe the return as taking place at the democratic government’s invitation or with institutional encouragement, although no appointment decree has been located here.

He returned to a country where archaeology was institutionally associated mainly with remote pre-Hispanic sites and where modern cities were not generally conceptualized as unified archaeological objects. Drawing on international historical archaeology and his Mexican experience, he proposed investigating Buenos Aires through its entire material sequence.

### Programa de Arqueología Urbana

In 1985 he established the **Programa de Arqueología Urbana** inside the Instituto de Arte Americano at FADU-UBA. Early work included an excavation at Defensa 755, where the team uncovered what was presented as the first archaeologically excavated cistern or *aljibe* in Buenos Aires.

The program developed:

- Stratigraphic excavation of standing urban properties.
- Typologies and chronologies for ceramics, glass, building materials, pipes, bottles, tiles, food remains, and domestic refuse.
- Integration of archaeology with architectural history, cadastral plans, probate records, photographs, and oral testimony.
- The treatment of an entire city, rather than an isolated monument, as a temporally layered archaeological unit.
- Salvage or rescue archaeology at construction and restoration sites.

The UBA stated in 2022 that the center’s collections had grown to more than 400,000 pieces.

### Centro de Arqueología Urbana

In 1991 the program received formal recognition from UBA and CONICET as the **Centro de Arqueología Urbana**. Schávelzon directed it for decades. The institution became a training and publication center for historical archaeology in Argentina and a reference point elsewhere in Latin America.

He entered CONICET in 1985 and advanced through its research ranks. He also became active in ICOMOS Argentina and retained close working relations with Mexico’s INAH.

## Chronological Life History and Major Projects

### 1985–1988: Establishing method and collections

Initial Buenos Aires excavations tested whether the dense nineteenth- and twentieth-century city had preserved earlier domestic deposits. The results contradicted the assumption that construction had erased the archaeological record.

Schávelzon and colleagues issued reports through the journal *Arqueología Urbana* and began establishing artifact sequences suitable for Buenos Aires. Unlike monumental archaeology, the new program emphasized latrines, wells, cisterns, refuse, tableware, animal bone, ordinary houses, printing works, and commercial buildings.

### 1989–1998: Buenos Aires and Mendoza

In April 1989 work at the former Cabildo of Mendoza exposed historical flooring. Schávelzon collaborated with **Roberto Bárcena** and local institutions on Mendoza’s foundational area and the Ruinas de San Francisco.

Projects during this period included:

- The Cabildo of Buenos Aires.
- The Cabildo and foundational area of Mendoza.
- The Imprenta Coni.
- Michelángelo, a historic San Telmo property.
- San Francisco in Mendoza.
- Houses and urban deposits in San Telmo.

These projects were accompanied by a sequence of books rather than only technical reports. He framed archaeology as evidence about consumption, sanitation, technological change, class distinction, demolition, and forgetting.

### The unsuccessful search for the first Buenos Aires

For years Schávelzon investigated the possible location of **Pedro de Mendoza’s 1536 settlement**. This was a significant failure in the narrow sense: the expedition did not identify an indisputable footprint of the first Spanish foundation at Parque Lezama or another expected location.

The broader research nevertheless recovered evidence of indigenous occupation on the Riachuelo predating the Spanish city by centuries. The work supported his argument that Buenos Aires did not simply “begin” with a single European foundational act. Later reporting described an indigenous settlement near the Autódromo as among the city’s oldest known archaeological occupations.

The interpretation and dating of individual Riachuelo sites belong to an evolving research field; statements that the work definitively moved Buenos Aires’s history back to the eleventh century should be read as archaeological interpretation, not a universally settled civic chronology.

### Afro-Argentine history

*Buenos Aires negra* (2003) was his most visible contribution to the archaeological recovery of Afro-Porteño history. It assembled objects, locations, documentary references, visual evidence, and urban contexts to contest the conventional narrative that Argentina’s African-descended population had simply vanished.

The book’s core contribution was methodological: it asked what material objects and spatial forms might be attributable to communities that official histories had minimized. A scholarly review in the Universidade de São Paulo’s *Revista de História* recognized it as an important synthesis while situating it within the difficulties of assigning ethnicity to archaeological material.

His later projects extended this interest to Paraná’s historic “Barrio del Tambor” and the chapel of San Miguel Arcángel.

### Everyday consumption and “the archaeology of garbage”

Projects on tableware, food remains, cesspits, wells, and household refuse culminated in *Historias del comer y del beber*, ceramic catalogs, and public interviews describing archaeology as a way to compare family self-representation with what discarded objects reveal.

Schávelzon argued that garbage can contradict inventories, memoirs, and class narratives: families may describe themselves as traditional or austere while their waste indicates imported consumption, rapid fashion changes, alcohol use, or economic instability. This became part of his public image as the archaeologist of the hidden or discarded city.

### Tunnels and Buenos Aires’s underground mythology

His books on subterranean construction distinguished among:

- Documented drains, cisterns, wells, sewers, utility conduits, and basements.
- Genuine connecting passages.
- Structures later interpreted as tunnels.
- Literary, touristic, political, or conspiratorial stories about secret networks beneath the city.

He treated the mythology itself as historical evidence. In interview he specifically mentioned including **Julio Cortázar’s** fiction about the Buenos Aires subway as part of the underground city’s imagined history.

### Cultural-property theft and art forgery

Beginning with *El expolio del arte en la Argentina* (1993), Schávelzon investigated theft, illegal circulation, false attribution, and forgery in the Argentine and Latin American art markets.

He has repeatedly said that objects of doubtful authenticity continue to appear at auctions. His work treats forgery as a relationship linking maker, expert, dealer, collector, institution, and market demand—not simply as an isolated criminal act.

No evidence located accuses Schávelzon himself of participating in theft or forgery. His role has been as researcher, commentator, and critic of weak provenance and authentication practices.

### *Ejercicio Plástico* and David Alfaro Siqueiros

Schávelzon researched the history, abandonment, fragmentation, recovery, and conservation of **David Alfaro Siqueiros’s** mural *Ejercicio Plástico*, painted in 1933 with Lino Enea Spilimbergo, Antonio Berni, Juan Carlos Castagnino, and Enrique Lázaro.

He and Héctor Mendizábal published a major history in 2003; Schávelzon continued publishing on it in 2011 and 2013. Institutional accounts credit his research and advocacy with helping publicize the mural and establish the case for its rescue and exhibition.

The mural’s legal ownership and removal generated larger political and commercial disputes, but no source reviewed identifies Schávelzon as a party accused of wrongdoing.

### Caserón de Rosas, Parque 3 de Febrero, and Café de Hansen

Schávelzon studied the material transformations of Palermo:

- The site of Juan Manuel de Rosas’s former residence.
- Its later appropriation by state and military institutions.
- The creation of Parque 3 de Febrero.
- Structures identified with the Café de Hansen, prominent in tango mythology.
- Nineteenth-century waterworks and tunnels.

The discovery of remains associated with Café de Hansen was widely reported in 2008. His treatment again combined documentary history with archaeology of a place heavily overlaid by popular legend.

### House of Juan Domingo Perón

With Ana Igareta he investigated a house in Roque Pérez associated with Perón’s childhood. Their 2009 publication addressed the material setting rather than functioning as a political biography.

The project documents a research connection to Perón as a historical subject. It does not establish a personal relationship; Schávelzon was born in 1950 and no meeting between them has been documented.

### Semana Trágica and Talleres Vasena

*Arqueología de un conflicto social* (2011) applied archaeological and architectural analysis to the Talleres Vasena and the 1919 Semana Trágica. The project linked industrial space, labor conflict, state violence, and the later erasure of contested sites.

### Casa Liniers, Casa del Naranjo, Casa Rosada, and Aduana Taylor

During the 2010s and early 2020s he directed or published salvage work involving:

- The Casa del Virrey Liniers.
- The Casa del Naranjo.
- Casa Rosada and the former viceregal/government complex.
- The ex-Aduana Taylor beneath Plaza Colón.
- The former Museo Etnográfico building.
- Numerous private properties affected by subsidence, pipe failure, or construction.

A 2023 archaeological assessment of Balcarce 1050, later published by the CAU, addressed cracks, subsidence, broken pipes, and heritage fabric.

### Teyú Cuaré, 2015–2022

The most controversial project of Schávelzon’s career concerned stone ruins in Parque Provincial Teyú Cuaré near San Ignacio, Misiones.

Schávelzon, **Ana Igareta**, and colleagues examined a complex previously known locally but not systematically excavated. Excavated materials included:

- Five German Reichspfennig coins.
- English, Slovak, Yugoslav, Bohemian, and Argentine coins, principally from 1935–1945.
- German-manufactured ceramics.
- Food tins and household containers.
- Architectural features the team considered atypical for regional vernacular or Jesuit-Guaraní construction.
- Papers or images associated with the Third Reich, reported in the team’s later scholarship.

#### Initial media theory

**Theory, not established fact:** In March 2015 Schávelzon suggested that the complex might have formed part of a Nazi plan to prepare remote refuges for senior officials in the event of German defeat.

International coverage frequently hardened “might be” into “archaeologists discover secret Nazi lair.” Some reports connected the site specifically to Martin Bormann.

#### Immediate criticism

A detailed Página/12 article criticized the announcement as methodologically premature. It argued that German coins and tableware did not prove a centrally organized Luftwaffe or Nazi refuge program, particularly in a region with German settlement predating Hitler. The critic also noted that the buildings were not genuinely “discovered” in 2015 and that no documentary source for an international refuge-building program had been produced.

The Times of Israel summarized Schávelzon as acknowledging that the high-level-hideout theory was speculation magnified by the press. This accurately captures the epistemic status of the initial claim: hypothesis, not proof.

#### Later archaeological position

The team’s 2017 book and 2020 article gave a more qualified argument:

- The “house of Bormann” legend could not be sustained.
- There was no documentary or forensic evidence placing Bormann at Teyú Cuaré.
- DNA and forensic evidence established that Bormann died in Berlin in 1945.
- The structures did not constitute a defensive “hideout” in the sensationalized sense.
- Some material and architectural evidence nevertheless suggested occupation or construction by people arriving from Nazi-era Europe or connected to fascist networks.
- The identity of those people could not be established.
- Only part of the roughly thirty identified structures had been systematically studied, leaving the interpretation open to revision.

The researchers later complained that journalists, television producers, and advocates of alternative stories selectively assembled quotations and evidence, attributing claims to the team that it had explicitly rejected.

Thus the present evidentiary conclusion is:

- **Bormann lived there:** myth, contradicted by forensic evidence.
- **The buildings were a secret refuge built for top Nazi fugitives:** unresolved theory; no confirming program documents or identified intended resident.
- **The complex had twentieth-century occupants possessing Central European and Nazi-period material:** documented archaeological finding.
- **Those occupants had some relationship to Nazi-era Europe or local fascist activity:** plausible archaeological interpretation, not a proven individual identification.
- **The site was newly discovered in 2015:** false in the ordinary sense; the ruins were locally known, though the project undertook new systematic study.

### Patagonia Trágica

With Patricia Frazzi and Matías Hernández, Schávelzon published archaeological research on the police station at Puerto San Julián connected with the 1921 repression of rural workers. This continued his later focus on spaces of political violence and on events whose physical settings had been neglected or altered.

### Retirement and later work

Schávelzon retired from CONICET in 2018 after reaching Investigador Superior. Retirement did not end his research or teaching.

Subsequent publications included:

- A manual of urban archaeology.
- Studies of Casa Rosada and the Aduana Taylor.
- The Barrio del Tambor.
- Teyú Cuaré and Second World War archaeology.
- The Patagonia Trágica police station.
- A book about a German-born art forger under the fictitious name “Kurth.”
- *Las cabezas olmecas en el imaginario universal* (2024).
- A 2025 article on a Buenos Aires table service from approximately 1815–1820.
- A 2025 FADU doctoral seminar, “Arqueología en proyectos de arquitectura moderna.”

## Political, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### University-era politics

His political environment was the radicalized UBA of 1968–1975. His own later formulation is precise: participation in student-center activity and links to a PCR sector, but not sustained militant membership.

### Exile and democratic reconstruction

Exile converted an intended anthropological and architectural career into a continental one. Ecuador furnished early excavation experience; Mexico provided the institutional stability, postgraduate education, and conservation history that shaped his mature work.

His 1984 return placed his urban-archaeology project within Argentina’s democratic reconstruction. The recovery of buried or suppressed histories—Afro-Argentine communities, industrial conflict, state violence, indigenous settlement, looted art—became a recurring concern.

### Intellectual orientation

His work consistently advances several propositions:

- Cities are archaeological entities, not merely containers for isolated “sites.”
- Heritage is selected and constructed; destruction and forgetting are themselves historical processes.
- Material evidence can contradict elite texts and family memory.
- Myth is not interchangeable with fact, but its circulation and physical consequences are historical evidence.
- Restoration embodies changing ideologies and cannot simply return an artifact to an uncontested original state.
- Everyday objects may reveal structures of race, class, technology, trade, and consumption excluded from monumental history.

These positions are compatible with historical archaeology and social-history approaches, but no reliable source found a formal adherence by Schávelzon to a named philosophical school.

## Successes and Failures

### Significant successes

- Established urban archaeology as an organized university research program in Buenos Aires.
- Built a large comparative artifact collection and publication infrastructure.
- Helped create practical chronologies for historical ceramics and construction materials.
- Expanded archaeological attention to ordinary domestic life, Afro-Argentine history, labor conflict, and urban infrastructure.
- Sustained research in Buenos Aires, Mendoza, Patagonia, Misiones, Ecuador, and Mesoamerica.
- Contributed to public recognition and conservation of *Ejercicio Plástico*.
- Produced an unusually large body of books accessible to both specialists and general readers.
- Reached CONICET’s highest research rank and received major international fellowships.

### Limitations and unsuccessful projects

- Did not locate conclusive remains of Pedro de Mendoza’s first Buenos Aires settlement.
- Some ethnic identifications in historical archaeology remain interpretive because objects rarely encode a unique identity.
- The 2015 public presentation of Teyú Cuaré allowed a preliminary hypothesis to be transformed into a global “Nazi lair” claim.
- Heritage advocacy often documented destruction after the fact rather than preventing it; Schávelzon himself has repeatedly described Argentine heritage as heavily depleted.
- The huge CAU collection creates continuing cataloging, storage, conservation, and publication burdens.

### Teyú Cuaré scientific and media controversy

This is the principal substantive negative or contested episode.

**Documented fact:** Schávelzon publicly advanced a speculative Nazi-refuge interpretation before a final scholarly synthesis appeared.

**Criticism:** Journalists and skeptics argued that five Nazi-period coins and imported goods were insufficient to establish a planned sanctuary for top Nazi officials. They noted the region’s older German immigration and the absence of a documented Luftwaffe refuge program.

**Schávelzon’s response:** He acknowledged the speculative nature of the initial reconstruction and later differentiated between the unsupported Bormann legend, a sensational “secret lair,” and the narrower archaeological inference of a Central European or fascist-associated occupation.

**Later team position:** The Bormann story was rejected; the exact occupants remained unidentified; a relationship to Nazi-era European arrivals remained a revisable interpretation.

**Finding:** No fraud is demonstrated. The controversy concerns evidentiary overreach, preliminary public communication, and subsequent media amplification.

### Heritage criticism and institutional disputes

Schávelzon has accused Argentine governments, owners, dealers, museums, and development practices of failing to protect archaeological and artistic heritage. Such statements sometimes generated institutional or political friction. They are policy criticism, not proof of corruption by named actors unless accompanied by a documented case.

### Art-forgery research

His 2023 book about “Kurth” relies on interviews with an admitted forger conducted between 2005 and 2010. “Kurth” is explicitly a fictitious name intended to conceal the subject’s identity.

This creates unavoidable verification limits:

- The narrator was by profession a maker of false objects.
- Some claims cannot be checked without revealing the source.
- Schávelzon himself asked rhetorically who would believe a forger.
- The reported 2017 murder in Paris of the subject’s son “Hans” was not causally linked to the father’s activities; Schávelzon expressly declined to assert such a connection.

The book is therefore partly oral history and partly a study of deception, rather than a court-tested exposé.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Martin Bormann’s house” at Teyú Cuaré

**Story:** Martin Bormann escaped Berlin, reached Argentina, and lived in the stone ruins at Teyú Cuaré.

**Circulation:** Local stories were established by the 1970s, shaped by Argentina’s real reception of Nazi fugitives, international Bormann sightings, wanted posters, popular books, tourism, and later television.

**Evidence for:** Central European artifacts, Nazi-period coins, unusual twentieth-century construction, and the documented presence of fascist or Nazi-linked migrants elsewhere in the region.

**Evidence against:** No document or witness securely places Bormann at the site. Bormann’s remains, discovered in Berlin in 1972, were identified through dental and mitochondrial-DNA evidence. The archaeological team itself rejected his residence there.

**Classification:** Myth built around a real archaeological complex and a historically credible regional background, but false as a biographical claim about Bormann.

**Effect on Schávelzon:** It brought global attention, generated allegations of sensationalism, and permanently associated his public reputation with “Nazi archaeology.”

### “A newly discovered secret Nazi lair”

**Story:** In 2015 Schávelzon’s team discovered a previously unknown fortified refuge built by the Luftwaffe for senior Nazis.

**Evidence:** The ruins were locally known; the site lacks the clear defensive properties attributed to it in some reports; no construction order or intended resident has been identified.

**Classification:** Media mythology developed from a genuine investigation and an overly expansive preliminary hypothesis.

### Buenos Aires’s infinite secret-tunnel network

**Story:** Buenos Aires possesses a vast clandestine network connecting churches, convents, government buildings, Rosas’s residence, and escape routes.

**Schávelzon’s treatment:** Documented underground structures exist, but many “tunnels” are drains, sewers, foundations, cisterns, or later utility works. He cataloged both physical findings and stories.

**Classification:** Mixed corpus—some factual structures, many misidentifications, and a durable urban legend.

**Historical importance:** The myth influences tourism, building interpretation, excavation expectations, and Buenos Aires’s self-image as a city with a concealed double beneath the streets.

### The disappearance of Black Buenos Aires

**Story:** Argentina’s Black population vanished through war, disease, intermarriage, or demographic replacement, leaving little or no continuing presence.

**Schávelzon’s work:** Archaeology and documentation show a substantial Afro-Porteño material and spatial history that conventional narratives marginalized.

**Classification:** National historical myth or oversimplification, not a reliable demographic description.

### The “birth” of Buenos Aires at Parque Lezama

**Story:** The exact site of Pedro de Mendoza’s 1536 foundation is known and centered on Parque Lezama.

**Schávelzon’s position:** Archaeological evidence has not confirmed that conventional location. Earlier indigenous settlement also makes a single European “birth” concept inadequate.

**Classification:** Civic tradition without conclusive archaeological confirmation.

### The glamorous Café de Hansen

**Story:** Café de Hansen was a singular, almost mythical birthplace or temple of tango populated by compadritos and legendary dancers.

**Schávelzon’s contribution:** Excavation and documentation established material traces of the site while distinguishing them from literary and touristic embellishment.

**Classification:** Historically grounded place surrounded by accumulated tango mythology.

## Reputation and Legacy

### Disciplinary legacy

Schávelzon’s clearest institutional legacy is the establishment of urban archaeology as a sustained field in Buenos Aires. The CAU’s survival, artifact collections, field manuals, electronic publications, and graduates transformed what had been occasional antiquarian discovery into an organized research program.

His work helped normalize the archaeological study of:

- Recent centuries.
- Standing buildings.
- Domestic refuse.
- Infrastructure.
- Ethnicity and racial erasure.
- Industry and labor repression.
- Dictatorship- and fascism-related material.
- Heritage destruction itself.

### Historiographical influence

He contributed to a view of Buenos Aires as a layered indigenous, colonial, African, immigrant, industrial, and modern landscape rather than as a city with a purely European identity.

His Mesoamerican studies remain distinct from his Argentine reputation but were central to his formation and supplied significant histories of restoration, representations of architecture, and Maya urban theory.

### Public reputation

Popular media often characterize him as Argentina’s leading urban archaeologist. His accessible books and interviews made wells, ceramics, tunnels, food remains, and demolished buildings part of public historical discussion.

The same visibility produced the principal reputational controversy: the Teyú Cuaré hypothesis circulated globally in more categorical form than the archaeological evidence justified. His later publications corrected important elements, especially the Bormann story, but did not eliminate the original media association.

### Archival legacy

Depositing family and research papers at UNM and books at the Biblioteca Nacional indicates deliberate preparation of a documentary legacy while still living. These archives may eventually resolve unanswered questions about advisers, marriage, field diaries, political contacts, correspondence, finances of particular projects, and the internal history of the CAU.
