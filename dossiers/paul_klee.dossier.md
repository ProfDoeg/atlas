# Paul Klee: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

Paul Klee left an unusually dense documentary record: four retrospectively revised diaries covering 1898–1918, correspondence, teaching manuscripts, photographs, a personal library, studio materials, and his own handwritten catalogue of works. These are preserved principally at the Zentrum Paul Klee in Bern. Its archive also documents roughly 600 exhibitions in which Klee participated during his lifetime.

The nine-volume *Catalogue raisonné Paul Klee* records approximately 9,600 drawings, prints, watercolors, paintings, and related works from 1883–1940. Consequently, the “Works” section below supplies the complete authoritative catalogue reference, all principal writings and posthumous textual collections, major portfolios, and a representative chronological selection of artworks; reproducing 9,600 individual catalogue entries is not practicable in one dossier.

Labels used for potentially uncertain material:

- **Documented fact:** supported by primary documentation or convergent institutional scholarship.
- **Reported fact:** found in reputable secondary literature but not independently verified here from the underlying document.
- **Retrospective diagnosis:** a medical inference made after Klee’s death.
- **Nazi accusation/propaganda:** an allegation generated by National Socialist organs, not a substantiated fact.
- **Disputed interpretation:** a scholarly reading without factual consensus.
- **Myth or legend:** a culturally influential story whose literal accuracy is doubtful or overstated.

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Paul Klee. Some references give **Ernst Paul Klee**, but “Paul Klee” is the name he consistently used professionally.
- **Pronunciation:** approximately “Clay” in German.
- **Pseudonyms or professional aliases:** none established.
- **Birth:** 18 December 1879, Münchenbuchsee, Canton of Bern, Switzerland.
- **Death:** 29 June 1940, Clinica Sant’Agnese, Muralto, adjoining Locarno, Canton Ticino, Switzerland.
- **Burial:** Schosshalden Cemetery, Bern.
- **Religion:** baptized and recorded as Swiss Reformed/Protestant.
- **Nationality:** German throughout his life, through his German father, despite Swiss birth and upbringing. He applied for Swiss naturalization in 1939; the Bern municipal decision came only after his death.
- **Occupations:** painter, draftsman, printmaker, watercolorist, art theorist, teacher, violinist, music and art critic.
- **Associated movements:** Expressionism, abstraction, Cubism, Surrealism, *Der Blaue Reiter*, and the Bauhaus. Klee participated in these networks but maintained that his art should not be confined to a single movement.
- **Recorded oeuvre:** 9,146 works in Klee’s own catalogue according to Felix Klee; the completed modern catalogue raisonné contains approximately 9,600 entries.
- **Common descriptions:** Swiss-born German artist; German artist born in Switzerland; Swiss-German artist. Calling him legally Swiss during his lifetime is inaccurate.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents and grandparents

Klee’s father, **Hans Wilhelm Klee** (1849–1940), came from Tann in the Rhön region of Germany. He trained in singing, piano, organ, and violin at the Stuttgart Conservatory and became a music teacher at the Bern cantonal teachers’ seminary at Hofwil. His parents were Johann Peter Klee, an administrator/accountant, and Elise Göbel.

Klee’s mother, **Ida Maria Frick** (1855–1921), was Swiss and trained as a singer at Stuttgart, where she met Hans. Her parents were Heinrich Franz Frick, identified in genealogical sources as an absinthe manufacturer, and Anna Catharina Rosina Riedtmann. Ida became partly paralyzed around 1899 and ceased professional activity.

Klee later wrote that his mother’s family might have had southern-French ancestry, but admitted that this was not clearly established. He also recalled a supposedly successful maternal great-uncle who painted portraits in London and then disappeared from family knowledge. These are **family traditions**, not securely reconstructed genealogical facts.

His maternal grandmother drew, painted, and embroidered and gave him his earliest instruction in drawing and coloring.

### Sibling

His only known sibling was **Mathilde Klee** (28 January 1876–1953). She remained unmarried, lived near or with the family, and cared for their disabled mother.

### Wife

**Karoline Sophie Elisabeth “Lily” Stumpf** (10 October 1876–22 September 1946) was a German pianist and piano teacher, daughter of Munich physician and medical official Ludwig Stumpf and Marie-Anna Pohle. She studied, among others, with composer Ludwig Thuille.

Paul met Lily at a Munich chamber-music gathering in 1899. They became engaged in 1901 and married in Bern on 15 September 1906 despite opposition from her father. Their Munich household did not follow the era’s conventional division of labor: Lily’s teaching and performances supplied most family income during Klee’s early career, while Paul painted, cooked, kept house, and cared for their child.

Their surviving letters show an unusually music-centered partnership but also separations, career pressures, and Klee’s earlier sexual relationships. Lily became the principal custodian of his estate after his death.

### Children and sexual relationships

- **Unnamed son by “Tini”:** **documented in Klee’s diaries and biographical records.** Klee had a relationship with a shop assistant identified only as Tini. Their son was born on 5 November 1900 and died on 29 November. Klee and Lily were not yet married.
- **Cenzi Resthmayer:** a Munich artist’s model with whom Klee had a brief relationship around 1900–01.
- **Felix Paul Klee** (30 November 1907–1990): only surviving child of Paul and Lily; later an opera and theater director, curator of his father’s legacy, and editor of the diaries, poetry, and family correspondence.

Klee’s candid accounts of sexual encounters, models, prostitution, pregnancy, and erotic fantasy are important primary evidence but were edited by Klee himself and later by Felix. They therefore cannot be treated as an unmediated day-by-day record.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1879–1898

The family moved from Münchenbuchsee to Bern in 1880 and occupied several addresses: Aarbergergasse, Hallerstrasse 32 and 26, Marienstrasse 8, and, from 1897, a family-owned row house at Obstbergweg 6.

Klee attended primary school from 1886 to 1890, then the municipal Progymnasium and the literary/humanities division of the Bern Gymnasium. He began violin lessons at seven with **Karl Jahn**, a pupil of Joseph Joachim. At eleven he was admitted as an extraordinary member of the Bern municipal orchestra/music society.

Drawing, caricature, imaginative storytelling, poetry, natural history, and music developed in parallel. Some childhood drawings from 1883 onward survive because Klee later incorporated them into his oeuvre catalogue. This self-cataloguing helped construct a continuous artistic biography reaching back to childhood.

He was not an exemplary school pupil. He completed the Matura in 1898 with marginal results and joked that attaining the exact minimum was difficult and risky. With school friend and later writer **Hans Bloesch**, he produced a satirical graduation paper, *Die Wanze* (“The Bug”).

Klee faced a genuine choice between professional music, literature, and visual art. He considered modern musical performance less creatively open than painting: his preferred musical canon—especially Bach and Mozart—belonged to the past, whereas modern visual art seemed to offer territory still to be invented.

## Education and Formation, 1898–1906

### Munich

In October 1898 Klee went to Munich. Initially rejected by the Academy because his figure drawing was insufficiently developed, he entered **Heinrich Knirr’s private drawing school**. His teachers and formative contacts included:

- Heinrich Knirr: academic drawing.
- Franz von Stuck: symbolist painter whose Academy class Klee entered in October 1900.
- Walter Ziegler: introduced Klee to etching at Burghausen in July 1899.
- Lily Stumpf: musical partner, correspondent, critic, and eventual spouse.

Klee attended von Stuck’s class for only about five months, leaving in March 1901. He considered himself technically strong in line but deficient in color. His student years included tavern life, sexual relationships, study of anatomy and the nude, caricature, and experiments in satire.

### Italy, 1901–02

From October 1901 to May 1902 he traveled with sculptor **Hermann Haller** through Italy, including Milan, Genoa, Livorno, Pisa, Rome, Naples, Florence, and other centers. Classical, Renaissance, and Baroque art impressed but also inhibited him. Rather than confirming an academic vocation, Italy produced a creative crisis: he believed the authority of historical art could not simply be continued.

### Bern self-education

From 1902 to 1906 he lived mainly at his parents’ house. He:

- played violin professionally or semi-professionally;
- wrote music and art criticism for the *Berner Fremdenblatt* and later *Die Alpen*;
- studied anatomy, literature, nature, and graphic technique;
- made reverse-glass paintings;
- developed the etched series *Inventionen*;
- visited Paris in 1905 with Louis Moilliet and Hans Bloesch.

The eleven *Inventions* of 1903–05 joined grotesque satire, sexuality, bodily distortion, and technical precision. Klee described the project as an attempt at “satire in the grand style.” Its hostile or comic treatment of beauty anticipated his continuing mixture of attraction and mockery.

## Early Career and Family Economy, 1906–1914

After the September 1906 marriage, Paul and Lily settled in Munich. Felix was born in 1907. Until roughly 1913, Lily’s piano teaching and concert work underwrote the household. Paul’s domestic work and childcare—including a meticulous “Felix calendar” during the child’s 1909 illness—are exceptionally well documented for a male artist of his generation.

Klee’s first solo exhibition opened at the Kunstmuseum Bern in 1910 and traveled to Zürich, Winterthur, and Basel. In 1911 he began systematically numbering and cataloguing his works and helped form the artists’ association **Sema**.

### Blue Rider network

In 1911–12 Klee met or associated with:

- Wassily and Nina Kandinsky;
- Franz Marc;
- August Macke;
- Gabriele Münter;
- Alexej Jawlensky;
- Marianne von Werefkin;
- Hans Arp;
- Robert and Sonia Delaunay;
- dealer/editor Herwarth Walden.

Klee was close to the *Blaue Reiter* circle and exhibited seventeen works in its second exhibition in 1912. It is safer to call him an associate than a founder or doctrinaire member.

In Paris in April 1912 he visited Robert and Sonia Delaunay and saw Cubist works by Picasso and Braque. He translated Delaunay’s essay *La Lumière* into German for *Der Sturm*. Delaunay’s translucent color planes and simultaneous contrasts were central to Klee’s developing color practice.

He exhibited in Walden’s **First German Autumn Salon** in Berlin in 1913.

## Tunisia and the First World War, 1914–1918

### Tunisia, April 1914

Klee traveled with **August Macke** and **Louis Moilliet** from 6 to 22 April 1914. Their route included Tunis, St. Germain/Ezzahra, Hammamet, and Kairouan. Moilliet had contacts with the Swiss couple Ernst and Rosa Jäggi-Müller near Tunis, which facilitated the trip.

Klee worked in watercolor and recorded the celebrated diary statement at Kairouan that color possessed him and that “color and I are one.” The trip consolidated—not created from nothing—his move toward autonomous color and abstraction. Delaunay, Macke, Parisian Cubism, earlier watercolor experiments, and sustained study had already prepared it.

The journey’s later status as a revelation partly derives from Klee’s own retrospective editing of his diaries and from biographies and exhibitions that treated North Africa as an exotic source of European modernism.

### War

Germany mobilized after August 1914. Klee’s friends August Macke and Franz Marc were killed in 1914 and 1916 respectively. Their deaths affected him personally and removed two central peers.

Klee was conscripted into the German army in March 1916. After infantry training, he served with aviation units at Schleißheim and Gersthofen, chiefly in rear-area duties. Tasks reportedly included painting camouflage and aircraft markings and clerical work. He was never a combat aviator and did not serve at the front. His superiors allowed sufficient time for continued artistic work.

His art responded obliquely and sometimes satirically to mechanized war rather than through documentary battlefield scenes. Works such as *Der Tod für die Idee* (“Death for the Idea,” 1915) complicate any depiction of him as apolitical, but Klee did not become a party activist.

From 1916 onward, regular exhibitions at Herwarth Walden’s **Der Sturm** gallery made him a prominent figure among younger German collectors and artists. Commercial success increased during wartime despite his military status.

## Consolidation, Dealers, and Bauhaus Appointment, 1919–1921

In the revolutionary aftermath of the war, Klee briefly participated in Munich artistic politics. He joined the **Action Committee of Revolutionary Artists** in 1919 and expressed sympathy for an artists’ council, but there is no evidence that he held governmental authority, participated in political violence, or played a substantial role in the Bavarian Soviet Republic.

Dealer **Hans Goltz** arranged a 1920 Munich retrospective of 362 works and obtained contractual exclusive sales rights. Three early monographs or substantial publications—by Leopold Zahn, Hermann von Wedderkop, and Wilhelm Hausenstein—helped define Klee publicly.

Walter Gropius invited him to the Staatliches Bauhaus in October 1920. Klee formally began teaching in Weimar in 1921, initially commuting from Munich. Lily and Felix followed.

Walter Benjamin bought *Angelus Novus* from Goltz in 1921 for 1,000 marks. No evidence indicates that Klee and Benjamin met.

## Bauhaus Years, 1921–1931

### Weimar

Klee served as a **Formmeister** and taught elementary design, bookbinding-related form work, stained-glass-related instruction, and increasingly independent courses in pictorial form. His teaching analyzed movement, growth, rhythm, proportion, equilibrium, color interaction, nature, and the conversion of observed processes into visual structure.

His classroom work survives in thousands of manuscript pages. The frequently repeated formula “taking a line for a walk” summarizes only a small part of a systematic and often demanding pedagogy.

Notable Bauhaus colleagues included:

- Walter Gropius;
- Wassily Kandinsky;
- Lyonel Feininger;
- Johannes Itten;
- Oskar Schlemmer;
- László Moholy-Nagy;
- Josef Albers;
- Georg Muche;
- Gunta Stölzl.

Klee was respected but could appear aloof, ironic, or enigmatic. Students sometimes treated him as an oracle or magician, an image encouraged by his compressed aphoristic language and the fantastic character of his pictures.

The Berlin Nationalgalerie gave him his first German museum exhibition in 1923. He delivered the lecture later published as *Über die moderne Kunst* at Jena in January 1924.

### Die Blaue Vier

In 1924 dealer **Galka Scheyer** organized Klee, Kandinsky, Feininger, and Jawlensky as **Die Blaue Vier** (“The Blue Four”), principally as an American promotional and exhibition vehicle. Scheyer lectured, arranged exhibitions, cultivated collectors, and materially expanded their United States reception.

### Dessau

Political pressure forced the Weimar Bauhaus’s closure; it reopened in Dessau in 1925. The Klee family moved there in 1926. Paul and Lily occupied half of a Gropius-designed Masters’ House; Wassily and Nina Kandinsky occupied the other half.

Klee and Kandinsky’s proximity was both personal and institutional. They exchanged works and correspondence, visited, and taught related but distinct theories. The Klees held musical evenings attended by Feininger, Kandinsky, Gropius, Schlemmer, and others.

Klee published *Pädagogisches Skizzenbuch* as Bauhaus Book 2 in 1925. He participated that year in the Paris exhibition *La peinture surréaliste* with Picasso, Miró, Max Ernst, and others. Surrealists admired his automatism and hybrid creatures, though Klee never formally joined their movement.

He traveled to Egypt in December 1928–January 1929. Nile agriculture, field divisions, monumental proportion, desert light, architecture, and hieroglyphic signs entered works such as *Hauptweg und Nebenwege*.

For his fiftieth birthday in 1929, major exhibitions occurred in Berlin, Dresden, Paris, and elsewhere; MoMA included and exhibited his work in New York. Will Grohmann’s substantial monograph strengthened his international standing.

### Departure

Klee left the Bauhaus in 1931. Reasons included a better-paid, more autonomous post at the Düsseldorf Academy and exhaustion with institutional conflicts. Describing his departure simply as a Nazi dismissal is incorrect; that came two years later at Düsseldorf.

## Düsseldorf, National Socialism, and Exile, 1931–1933

Klee became professor at the Düsseldorf Academy on 1 July 1931. He initially retained the Dessau residence and rented a Düsseldorf room. He taught painting technique and maintained substantial freedom.

After Hitler’s appointment as chancellor in January 1933:

- a Nazi newspaper attacked Klee as a supposed “Galician Jew” and foreign cultural influence;
- police searched his Dessau home;
- he was suspended and then dismissed under National Socialist civil-service and cultural policy;
- authorities demanded proof of “Aryan” ancestry;
- the Academy sealed or interfered with access to his studio;
- he entered an agency relationship with Daniel-Henry Kahnweiler’s Galerie Simon in Paris.

The claim that Klee was Jewish was **Nazi propaganda and factually false**. Its importance lies in how racialized cultural denunciation functioned, not in genealogy.

Klee visited Picasso’s Paris studio in 1933. In December, Lily left Germany first and Paul followed on 24 December. They returned initially to the family house at Obstbergweg 6, Bern. Felix and his wife remained in Germany.

## Bern Exile, 1934–1940

The Klees moved to an apartment at Kistlerweg 6 in Bern in 1934. Switzerland provided physical safety but not immediate citizenship or an easy professional position. Klee remained legally German and faced restrictions affecting employment and public activity.

His German market collapsed under the Nazi regime. A 1934 Potsdam book of his drawings was confiscated in 1935. In 1937 German museums lost 102 Klee works to the state confiscation campaign; seventeen appeared in the propagandistic *Entartete Kunst* exhibition.

International recognition continued. A 1935 Bern retrospective and exhibitions in Basel and abroad helped maintain his reputation. Picasso made a return visit to Klee in Bern in 1937. Accounts describe mutual reserve; later anecdotes about Picasso rotating a Klee work and Klee remarking that the whole world was upside down are **reported recollections**, not securely established verbatim dialogue.

### Illness

In summer 1935 Klee developed fatigue, fever, bronchopulmonary symptoms, skin changes, and severe systemic illness. He produced only about twenty-five works in 1936. Later symptoms included:

- tightening or alteration of facial skin;
- shortness of breath;
- anemia;
- myocarditis;
- gastric bleeding or ulceration;
- esophageal involvement and painful swallowing;
- reliance on liquid or puréed food;
- diarrhea and progressive weakness.

No surviving medical record supplies a definitive contemporary diagnosis. Dermatologist Oscar Naegeli may have recognized scleroderma but apparently did not clearly disclose it to the patient.

Physician Hans Suter later reconstructed the case from Felix Klee’s testimony, Lily’s letters, postcards, photographs, and other accounts. His conclusion—**retrospective diagnosis, highly probable but not documentary certainty**—was diffuse systemic sclerosis, an aggressive form of scleroderma affecting internal organs. A medical reanalysis using later diagnostic criteria supported that conclusion. Claims that emotional shock from Nazi persecution “caused” the disease are speculative and medically unproved.

### Final productivity

Klee partially recovered in 1937 and changed his working method. Large supports, broad dark signs, simplified color, angels, masks, war imagery, death, and transformation characterize the late work.

Recorded annual output rose dramatically:

- 1936: approximately 25 works;
- 1937: 264;
- 1938: 489;
- 1939: 1,253, his highest total;
- 1940: 366 before death.

The numbers document productivity but do not establish that every late symbol directly encodes illness or foreknowledge of death.

Among the late works are *Insula dulcamara*, *Revolution des Viadukts*, *Die Engel-Serie*, *Tod und Feuer*, and the posthumously titled *Stilleben*. The 1939 angels range from comic and bureaucratic to wounded, doubtful, and unfinished.

## Death, Citizenship, and Estate

Klee applied for Swiss citizenship on 24 April 1939. The application underwent police and municipal scrutiny. His avant-garde art and foreign status complicated the process. He entered a sanatorium or spa in Ticino in May 1940, deteriorated, and was transferred to the Clinica Sant’Agnese in Muralto.

He died on 29 June 1940, aged sixty. The official immediate cause was given as myocarditis. The strongest medical reconstruction attributes the fatal systemic process to diffuse systemic sclerosis. The Bern municipal naturalization decision was taken on 5 July 1940, six days after his death; it could not confer citizenship on a deceased applicant.

Klee’s epitaph, adapted from his own words, presents him as ungraspable in this world and dwelling as much among the dead and unborn as among the living. It became part of the artist’s posthumous mystical image.

Lily inherited and administered the estate. Because Paul remained German and Lily was German, Swiss authorities treated the estate after the Second World War as German property subject to custodial control. Before Lily died in September 1946, Swiss collectors and associates—including Hans Meyer-Benteli, Hermann Rupf, Rolf Bürgi, and Werner Allenbach—formed a purchasing consortium or Klee Society. The transaction and ensuing institutional arrangements prevented dispersal or foreign control of much of the estate.

The **Paul Klee Foundation** was established in 1947 at the Kunstmuseum Bern. Felix Klee devoted much of his later life to editing and interpreting the archive.

The Zentrum Paul Klee opened in Bern in 2005 in a building by Renzo Piano. Its collection brings together roughly 4,000 works—about 40 percent of the oeuvre—along with manuscripts, correspondence, photographs, tools, library, and natural-history objects.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

Klee did not establish industrial companies, patent inventions, or accumulate documented corporate investments. His significant financial structures were those of an artist, teacher, and estate owner:

- **Lily Klee’s piano studio and performance income:** principal household support during the early Munich years.
- **Der Sturm/Herwarth Walden:** exhibitions and early market formation from 1916.
- **Hans Goltz/Galerie Neue Kunst Hans Goltz:** exclusive sales contract and major 1920 retrospective.
- **Klee-Gesellschaft:** collector-supported purchasing arrangement founded in 1925 by Otto Ralfs; members acquired works collectively and supported the artist.
- **Galka Scheyer/Die Blaue Vier:** American representation, lectures, exhibitions, and collector cultivation.
- **Daniel-Henry Kahnweiler/Galerie Simon:** international agency agreement in 1933.
- **Bauhaus and Düsseldorf Academy:** salaried academic employment.
- **Personal oeuvre catalogue:** a practical authentication, inventory, pricing, and legacy instrument.
- **Posthumous Klee Society and Paul Klee Foundation:** mechanisms for purchasing, consolidating, and administering the estate.

Exact lifetime wealth is not firmly reconstructed in the sources consulted. The record indicates early dependence on Lily, increasing sales from the First World War onward, secure professorial income in the 1920s, and financial/professional contraction after exile.

## Artistic and Intellectual Development

### Line, satire, and grotesque

The early *Inventions* combine academic draftsmanship with caricature, erotic grotesque, and attacks on ideal beauty. Klee drew from Dürer, Goya, Daumier, Symbolism, Jugendstil, and popular graphic culture.

### Color and abstraction

Paris, Delaunay, Macke, and Tunisia were cumulative stages. Klee did not abandon observable nature so much as translate its processes into constructed signs and color relations. His maxim that art does not reproduce the visible but makes visible became a central statement of modernist aesthetics.

### Nature and genesis

Plants, embryos, crystals, water, stars, animals, and anatomical diagrams supplied models of growth and transformation. He conceived the artist less as a copier than as a mediator through whom natural forces became pictorial form.

### Music

Klee continued playing violin throughout his life, alone, with Lily, and in chamber ensembles. Bach and Mozart formed his primary canon; he also studied or admired Beethoven, Wagner, Debussy, Schoenberg, Stravinsky, Bartók, and Hindemith.

Musical concepts—polyphony, rhythm, counterpoint, variation, notation, temporal movement, and structural harmony—entered his titles, images, and teaching. More than 500 catalogued works reportedly reference music, theater, or masks.

### Signs, language, and titles

Letters, arrows, numbers, pseudo-writing, diagrams, and hieroglyph-like marks frequently operate between image and language. Titles are not neutral descriptions: they redirect perception, create jokes, or set up semantic contradictions.

### Children, “primitive” art, and psychiatry

Klee valued children’s drawings and nonacademic image-making as evidence of direct formal invention. He also knew reproductions of art by psychiatric patients. Modern criticism disputes the old vocabulary of “primitive” and “childlike,” which can erase the sophistication of children, colonized cultures, and institutionalized creators. Klee’s appropriation belongs to the broader modernist search for alternatives to European academic naturalism.

### Politics

Klee was not consistently apolitical, but neither was he a party intellectual. His political record consists of:

- limited participation in Munich artists’ revolutionary organization in 1919;
- satirical or allegorical responses to war, nationalism, bureaucracy, and authoritarianism;
- refusal to accommodate Nazi cultural doctrine;
- late pictures often interpreted in relation to fascism, exile, and impending European war.

Assigning one stable partisan ideology to the entire oeuvre exceeds the evidence.

### Religion and metaphysics

Biblical figures, angels, demons, creation, death, resurrection, and cosmic order recur without establishing doctrinal orthodoxy. His thinking joined Protestant cultural background, Romantic natural philosophy, Goethean morphology, modern science, music theory, and personal cosmology. Claims that he belonged to an occult order, Theosophical society, or esoteric conspiracy are unsupported.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

### Nazi racial accusation

**Label: Nazi accusation/propaganda; disproved genealogically.**

A Nazi newspaper called Klee a “Galician Jew,” and authorities required proof of ancestry. Klee’s documented parents were a German Protestant music teacher and a Swiss singer. The accusation was a device for racial-cultural exclusion.

### Participation in war

**Label: documented fact with frequent exaggeration.**

Klee served in the German army from 1916 to 1918 but held rear-area aviation-unit assignments. Statements that he was a combat pilot are false. Conversely, presenting him as wholly detached from German military service also suppresses documented fact.

### Munich revolution

**Label: documented limited association; inflated in some retellings.**

He joined an artists’ revolutionary committee in 1919. Evidence does not make him a major revolutionary official, armed participant, or party organizer.

### Diaries, sexuality, and class

**Label: documented conduct; interpretation disputed.**

Klee recorded premarital affairs, an illegitimate child who died in infancy, encounters with models, and sexualized observations of working-class women. Scholarship has examined eroticism, prostitution, gender hierarchy, and the male gaze in his writing and art. These records complicate the harmless “poetic child” persona. No evidence found in this research establishes sexual crime or coercion, but the texts carry the class and gender assumptions of their milieu.

### Gender criticism

**Label: disputed scholarly interpretation.**

Klee’s domestic parenting and economic dependence on Lily challenge conventional masculine artist mythology. At the same time, some female figures in his works and diaries are eroticized, grotesquely caricatured, or treated as types. Feminist scholarship has argued that critical celebration of his tiny, textile-like works sometimes “feminized” Klee while still marginalizing actual women’s labor. These are interpretive controversies, not legal allegations.

### Tunisia and colonial modernism

**Label: documented journey; disputed postcolonial interpretation.**

Tunisia was under French colonial rule in 1914. The standard story frames its landscape and people as raw visual revelation for European modernists. Recent criticism asks how this narrative turns colonized North Africa into color, light, ornament, and timeless “otherness” while minimizing Tunisian modernity and colonial power.

No evidence found shows Klee working for colonial authorities or advocating colonial violence. The issue is the structure of representation and subsequent art-historical mythmaking.

### “Childlike” and psychiatric art

**Label: documented influence; terminology contested.**

Klee valued children’s art and images by psychiatric patients. Claims that his work is literally childlike can obscure deliberate technique. Older comparisons to supposedly “primitive” minds reflected hierarchical early-twentieth-century categories that contemporary scholarship treats critically.

### Nazi-confiscated and looted works

**Label: documented confiscations; individual ownership cases vary.**

The Nazi state removed 102 Klee works from German museums in 1937 and displayed seventeen as “degenerate.” That state seizure differs legally from persecution-driven dispossession of privately owned works, although some pieces on museum loan actually belonged to private collectors.

The most prominent dispute concerns *Sumpflegende* (*Swamp Legend*, 1919), owned by **Sophie Lissitzky-Küppers** and left on loan in Hanover when she moved to the Soviet Union. Nazis confiscated it as “degenerate art”; it later entered the Lenbachhaus through private-market transactions. Her heirs sought recovery beginning in the early 1990s. Litigation encountered limitation defenses. After roughly twenty-five years, Munich and the heirs reached a 2017 settlement under which the museum retained the painting while compensating the family. This was a documented ownership/restitution dispute, not wrongdoing by Klee.

Other Klee works have been returned or compensated after provenance investigations, including works formerly belonging to Lissitzky-Küppers and collectors persecuted by the Nazis. Claims must be assessed work by work.

### Flechtheim litigation

**Label: allegation in a civil complaint, not a finding quoted here.**

In litigation against the Bavarian State Painting Collections, heirs of dealer Alfred Flechtheim alleged that Klee’s *Grenzen des Verstandes* changed hands because of Nazi persecution and that later provenance accounts were false. A complaint states plaintiffs’ allegations; it is not by itself a judicial finding.

### Forgeries

**Label: criminal conduct by later dealers/forgers; no involvement by Klee.**

Klee’s high market value and small paper works attract false attributions. In the Ely Sakhai case, prosecutors alleged that a forged copy of Klee’s *Paläste* was marketed while the authentic painting was later offered separately. Sakhai pleaded guilty in the broader art-forgery scheme. The episode concerns posthumous fraud using Klee’s name, not fraud by Klee.

Klee’s meticulous oeuvre catalogue and the nine-volume modern catalogue raisonné make unsupported “discoveries” especially suspect, though absence alone does not automatically prove forgery.

### Recent adversarial-search finding, 2024–September 2026

Searches pairing Klee’s name with “controversy,” “scandal,” “allegations,” “lawsuit,” “fraud,” “accusations,” and “conspiracy” found no credible new evidence in the most recent two years that Klee personally committed fraud, violent crime, corruption, abuse, or intelligence activity. Recent questionable claims largely concern marketplace authenticity, modern restitution/provenance research, or reinterpretations of his political and colonial context.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

### “Color and I are one”

**Story:** Tunisia instantaneously transformed Klee from a monochrome draftsman into a master of color.

**Origin and circulation:** Klee’s edited diary entry from Kairouan, later amplified by monographs, museum exhibitions, and tourism-oriented narratives.

**Evidence:** The diary records a genuine breakthrough experience. Earlier Paris visits, Delaunay’s theory, Macke’s work, and Klee’s pre-1914 experiments prove that the development was gradual.

**Reputational effect:** Tunisia became his canonical conversion scene and Kairouan a birthplace of European abstraction.

### The innocent child-artist

**Story:** Klee painted spontaneously like a gifted child.

**Evidence against literal form:** His manuscripts reveal highly systematic studies of geometry, color, movement, music, and natural process. His “naïveté” was constructed through sophisticated means.

**Effect:** The myth increased accessibility but concealed disciplined labor, erotic grotesque, political satire, and formal theory.

### The magician or Bauhaus oracle

**Story:** Klee was a remote, mystical professor who transmitted secret laws of creation.

**Origin:** student recollections, his compressed lectures, studio persona, and images of cosmic growth.

**Evidence:** Students did experience him as enigmatic, but surviving lesson plans are analytical and methodical rather than occult revelations.

**Effect:** Produced the enduring image of “Klee the magician.”

### Switzerland’s national painter

**Story:** Klee was straightforwardly a Swiss national artist.

**Evidence:** Swiss birth, upbringing, dialect, residence, and cultural formation support a strong Swiss identity. Legally, however, he remained German and died before naturalization.

**Effect:** Posthumous Swiss commemoration often softened the exclusion and delay he experienced as a refugee applicant.

### Jewish identity

**Story:** Klee was a Galician Jew concealing his origins.

**Origin:** Nazi press in 1933.

**Evidence:** contradicted by documented genealogy; no supporting evidence.

**Effect:** helped justify dismissal and became an example of Nazi cultural racialization.

### *Angelus Novus* as Klee’s “angel of history”

**Story:** Klee painted an angel specifically representing historical catastrophe.

**Origin:** Walter Benjamin’s ninth thesis “On the Concept of History” in 1940.

**Evidence:** Klee made and titled the drawing *Angelus Novus* in 1920. Benjamin bought it in 1921 and supplied the historical-theological interpretation. Klee and Benjamin never met, and no evidence shows Klee intended Benjamin’s meaning.

**Effect:** transformed a small Klee drawing into an icon of catastrophe, exile, memory, and Critical Theory.

### Premonitions of death

**Story:** late angels and *Tod und Feuer* are prophetic records of Klee consciously approaching death and the Holocaust.

**Evidence:** illness, war, persecution, and mortality clearly surrounded the late work. Exact one-to-one prophetic interpretations are retrospective.

**Effect:** encouraged hagiographic reading of the late oeuvre as a visual testament.

### Picasso visit anecdote

**Story:** Picasso turned a Klee picture repeatedly because he could not find its orientation, and Klee replied that everything in the world was upside down.

**Evidence:** the artists’ 1933 and 1937 visits are documented. Precise dialogue survives as recollection and may have been polished in transmission.

**Effect:** symbolizes their contrasting temperaments and the disordered politics of 1937.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Klee became one of the most reproduced and pedagogically influential artists of modernism. His reception developed along several partly competing lines:

- **Formal theorist:** his Bauhaus diagrams became standard texts in art, architecture, and design education.
- **Poetic miniaturist:** small-scale works and fanciful titles encouraged an image of intimate, gentle imagination.
- **Musical painter:** composers translated his polyphony, rhythm, and titles into sound. The Zentrum Paul Klee’s music archive holds more than 450 compositions responding to him, including works by Pierre Boulez, Francis Poulenc, Krzysztof Penderecki, and Tan Dun.
- **Artist of childhood:** educators often use his work to introduce line, color, and visual invention, sometimes at the cost of its intellectual complexity.
- **Exile and anti-totalitarian symbol:** his dismissal and “degenerate art” classification made him emblematic of modern art persecuted by Nazism.
- **Philosophical icon:** Benjamin’s interpretation of *Angelus Novus* gave one Klee image a life largely independent of Klee’s intention.
- **Medical case:** the transformation of his late style has been studied in relation to systemic sclerosis, though deterministic claims that disease “explains” the art are unwarranted.
- **Global-modernist problem:** the Tunisia story now receives scrutiny for its colonial setting and for the art-historical conversion of North Africa into a catalyst for European innovation.
- **Market and authentication:** high prices, easily reproduced works on paper, and a distinctive signature have generated persistent forgeries and false attributions.
- **Provenance debates:** Klee’s presence in German museums and Jewish private collections before 1933 has made his works recurrent subjects of Nazi-era restitution research.

His influence crosses painting, graphic design, architecture, animation, poetry, children’s education, music, information visualization, and theories of creative process.

## Atlas Connections

Only relationships supported by consulted sources are included.

- **Achille-Claude Debussy — documented influence, not a personal meeting.** Klee listened closely to Debussy and admired works including *Prélude à l’après-midi d’un faune*. Musicological museum sources place Debussy among the modern composers whose procedures Klee studied in relation to visual composition.
- **Johannes Itten — documented fact.** Bauhaus colleague in Weimar during the early 1920s. Their courses occupied overlapping areas of preliminary/form education, although their theories and institutional trajectories differed.
- **Oskar Alfred Victor Schlemmer — documented fact.** Bauhaus colleague in Weimar and Dessau; member of the masters’ social and musical circle around the Klees.
- **Pablo Ruiz Picasso — documented fact.** Klee studied Picasso’s Cubism from 1912, visited Picasso’s Paris studio in 1933, and received Picasso at Kistlerweg 6 in Bern in 1937. Their relationship was respectful but not intimate.
- **Walter Benjamin — documented ownership and intellectual reception; no meeting.** Benjamin bought *Angelus Novus* in 1921 and transformed it into the “angel of history” in 1940. The Zentrum Paul Klee explicitly states that the two men never met.
- **Wassily Kandinsky — documented fact.** Friend from the Blue Rider milieu, Bauhaus colleague, Dessau neighbor, correspondent, artistic comparator, and fellow member of Die Blaue Vier.
- **Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart — documented artistic influence; chronologically no personal contact.** Mozart was among Klee’s most revered composers and a mainstay of his violin and chamber-music practice.
- **Walter Gropius — documented fact, although the roster gives no middle name and lists him only implicitly through the Bauhaus context?** Gropius appointed Klee to the Bauhaus in 1920 and designed the Dessau Masters’ House occupied by Klee and Kandinsky. Because “Walter Gropius” does not appear as a standalone roster entry, this is noted for context rather than counted as an atlas-roster match.
- **Albert Einstein — no documented personal connection established in the consulted sources.** Both had Bern associations and Klee’s work is sometimes analogized to modern physics, but proximity and analogy do not prove contact.
- **Carl Gustav Jung — no documented personal relationship established.** Later Jungian readings of Klee do not demonstrate contact or acknowledged influence.
- **Theodor W. Adorno — documented posthumous chain of custody, not a personal relationship.** Benjamin’s papers and *Angelus Novus* passed after the war through Adorno before reaching Gershom Scholem. No evidence consulted establishes that Klee met Adorno.
- **Friedrich Nietzsche, Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, William Blake, and other rostered thinkers sometimes invoked in Klee criticism — no sufficiently direct, source-supported personal or explicitly acknowledged connection established in this research.**

## Compact Chronology

- **1879, 18 December:** born at Münchenbuchsee.
- **1880:** family moves to Bern.
- **1883:** earliest catalogued childhood drawings.
- **1886:** begins school and violin instruction.
- **c. 1890:** extraordinary member of Bern musical ensemble.
- **1897:** family moves to Obstbergweg 6; Klee begins his diary.
- **1898:** completes Matura; moves to Munich; enters Knirr’s school.
- **1899:** learns etching from Walter Ziegler; meets Lily Stumpf.
- **1900:** enters Franz von Stuck’s Academy class; son by Tini born 5 November and dies 29 November.
- **1901:** leaves Academy; becomes engaged to Lily; returns to Bern.
- **1901–02:** Italian study journey with Hermann Haller.
- **1902–05:** self-education in Bern; produces *Inventionen*.
- **1905:** Paris trip with Louis Moilliet and Hans Bloesch.
- **1906, 15 September:** marries Lily; settles in Munich.
- **1907, 30 November:** Felix Klee born.
- **1910:** first traveling solo exhibition in Switzerland.
- **1911:** begins systematic oeuvre catalogue; enters Blue Rider network.
- **1912:** exhibits with the Blue Rider; visits Delaunays in Paris; meets Arp.
- **1913:** translates Delaunay; participates in First German Autumn Salon.
- **1914, April:** Tunisia with Macke and Moilliet.
- **1914, September:** Macke killed in war.
- **1916:** Marc killed; Klee conscripted into German army.
- **1916–18:** rear-area aviation-unit service; growing Der Sturm success.
- **1919:** joins Munich Action Committee of Revolutionary Artists.
- **1920:** Goltz retrospective; *Schöpferische Konfession*; Bauhaus appointment.
- **1921:** begins teaching in Weimar; Benjamin buys *Angelus Novus*.
- **1923:** Nationalgalerie museum exhibition; publishes “Wege des Naturstudiums.”
- **1924:** Jena lecture; Scheyer begins organizing Die Blaue Vier.
- **1925:** publishes *Pädagogisches Skizzenbuch*; Bauhaus relocates to Dessau; appears in first Surrealist group exhibition.
- **1926:** Klee family moves into Dessau Masters’ House beside Kandinskys.
- **1928–29:** Egypt journey.
- **1929:** fiftieth-birthday exhibitions; Grohmann monograph.
- **1931, 1 July:** begins Düsseldorf Academy professorship.
- **1933:** Nazi attacks, search, suspension, and dismissal; visits Picasso; emigrates to Bern in December.
- **1934:** moves to Kistlerweg 6.
- **1935:** major illness begins.
- **1936:** produces only about twenty-five works.
- **1937:** 102 works confiscated from German museums; seventeen shown as “degenerate”; Picasso visits Bern; productivity revives.
- **1938:** creates *Insula dulcamara* and *Park bei Lu*.
- **1939:** applies for Swiss citizenship; creates 1,253 works.
- **1940, May:** travels to Ticino for treatment.
- **1940, 29 June:** dies in Muralto.
- **1940, 5 July:** Bern municipal citizenship decision occurs after his death.
- **1946:** Lily dies; Klee estate placed under Swiss custodial controls applicable to German property.
- **1947:** Paul Klee Foundation established at Kunstmuseum Bern.
- **1957:** Felix publishes edited diaries.
- **1983:** *Angelus Novus* enters the Israel Museum after Benjamin-Scholem custody.
- **1998–2004:** nine-volume catalogue raisonné published.
- **2005:** Zentrum Paul Klee opens in Bern.
- **2017:** *Sumpflegende* restitution dispute settled with compensation and museum retention.
- **2024:** major scholarly edition of Klee–Kandinsky correspondence published.
- **2026:** continuing exhibitions and reinterpretation focus on the politically unsettled final decade.
