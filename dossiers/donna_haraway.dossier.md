# Donna Haraway: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Donna Jeanne Haraway  
**Special focus:** *A Manifesto for Cyborgs*; the relation between Haraway's cyborg and Norbert Wiener's cybernetics; companion animals and companion species; feminist science and technology studies  
**Status date:** 31 August 2026  
**Method:** documentary chronology assembled from Haraway's autobiographical interviews, the UCSC oral history and archive finding aid, institutional records, original publications, publisher records, contemporaneous profiles, and scholarly criticism. First-person recollections are identified as such. Critical claims are attributed. Where the public record does not establish a fact, the gap is stated rather than filled by inference.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

- **Frank Outten Haraway** (father) was a Denver sportswriter. Haraway recalled that childhood tuberculosis left his hips and knees rigid; he used crutches and at times a wheelchair and had spent long periods in a body cast. Despite that disability he became a competitive table-tennis player. He worked for *The Denver Post* for approximately forty-four years, retiring in January 1980. The [National Sports Media Association's Colorado award list](https://nationalsportsmedia.org/awards/state-awards/colorado) records him as the state's 1959 sportswriter award recipient. Haraway's brother's obituary confirms the parents as Frank Outten and Dorothy Maguire Haraway ([Stauffer Funeral Home](https://www.staufferfuneralhome.com/obituaries/william-haraway)).
- **Dorothy Maguire Haraway** (1917–1960) (mother) came from a working-class Irish Catholic family. She died when Donna was sixteen. Haraway dedicated *Simians, Cyborgs, and Women* to Dorothy and Frank, giving Dorothy's dates. In the autobiographical interview *How Like a Leaf*, Haraway said the death initially produced emotional numbness; she did not fully work through it until middle age, near the age at which her mother had died. Haraway also said that she and her mother had medically related conditions “in some respects,” but did not specify a diagnosis; no diagnosis should be inferred from the statement ([Haraway and Goodeve, interview PDF](https://attachments.are.na/632919/706257a5039d513c87d42c531c69c4de.pdf?1465734125=); [*Simians* PDF](https://caringlabor.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/12/3254-simians_cyborgs_and_women-1-1.pdf)).

### Grandparents and class background

Haraway described her paternal grandparents as Tennesseans who moved to Colorado Springs because her grandfather had tuberculosis. He later operated Piggly Wiggly grocery stores, entered unsuccessful business deals, and died in debt during the late 1930s. Her account depicts downward mobility rather than inherited wealth. Her maternal ancestry was Irish Catholic and working-class. No reliable public genealogy extending farther back, and no evidence of aristocratic, political-dynastic, or inherited corporate wealth, was found.

### Siblings

- **William Neil Haraway** (18 August 1942–31 January 2014), elder brother, worked in finance and business. His obituary names Donna, her husband Rusten Hogness, and their surviving brother.
- **Rick Miller-Haraway**, brother, married to Roberta. The exact reason and date for the compound surname were not established in the sources reviewed.

No other siblings were established.

### Spouses, partners, children, and chosen family

- **Jaye Miller**: Haraway met Miller in the Yale milieu. She described him as gay rather than bisexual. Their intimate relationship began amid the post-Stonewall gay-liberation world; they married in 1970. When he took a University of Hawaiʻi position, she followed, describing her initial institutional position as that of a “faculty wife.” They separated in 1973 and divorced in Hawaiʻi without lawyers. Haraway recalled that the court initially rejected the papers because they had been typed on the wrong kind of typewriter, and she believed they were the first Hawaiian couple to obtain such a lawyer-free divorce. This last detail is **first-person recollection**, not independently verified court history.
- **Rusten Hogness**: Haraway met Hogness at Johns Hopkins when he audited one of her courses. She said he was not formally her student. They became a couple in February 1975 and moved in together about a week later. Later official records and her 2025 Erasmus acceptance speech call Hogness her husband. He had been a conscientious objector, had taught physics, mathematics, and philosophy in the southern Philippines with Volunteers in Asia, and left graduate study without pursuing an academic career. He became a frequent photographer and practical collaborator in Haraway's companion-animal life.
- **Bob Filomeno**: Filipino-Chicano partner of Jaye Miller, from a Watsonville family. From roughly 1980 to 1986, Miller, Filomeno, Haraway, and Hogness formed an extended queer kin network. Filomeno developed AIDS and died in 1986.
- **Jaye Miller's death**: Miller later had a T-cell count below 200 and died of AIDS-related illness in 1991. Haraway's experience of Filomeno's and Miller's illnesses and deaths shaped her writing about immune systems, boundary-making, and the biomedical politics of self/nonself.
- **Children**: No biological or adopted children are documented. Haraway said that as a teenager she expected to have ten children; later she sometimes called her students her children. That is metaphorical kinship, not evidence of legal parenthood. Yale reports that she advised more than sixty doctoral students.

The detailed relationship history above derives principally from Haraway's own retrospective account in [*How Like a Leaf*](https://attachments.are.na/632919/706257a5039d513c87d42c531c69c4de.pdf?1465734125=). It should be read as unusually rich primary testimony, but not as a substitute for private records or testimony from every participant.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1944–1962

Haraway was born and raised in Denver. Her father took her to large numbers of baseball games—she recalled roughly seventy a year—and taught her to keep score. She played basketball. The sportswriter household supplied an early language of bodies, technique, disability, competition, and observation that later biographers have connected to her attention to embodied practices. That connection is interpretive; the games themselves are her documented recollection.

She grew up in an intensely Catholic environment associated with Christ the King parish and attended **St. Mary's Academy**, where nuns were important intellectual and disciplinary presences. She recalled saying Mass for dolls, hearing their confessions in a closet, and at one time considering a convent vocation. A Jesuit seminarian uncle and an intellectually serious friend of his encouraged her questions. She read Thomas Aquinas at about thirteen. Her doubts began by age ten or eleven, but ritual, sacramental realism, and Catholic habits of thought endured long after belief and church membership fell away.

Her 1950s political formation was conventionally anti-Communist. A Belgian missionary priest recently returned from China frightened parish children with stories of communism. Haraway recalled writing a high-school letter opposing abortion rights. These are documented autobiographical admissions, not positions she retained. By college and the 1960s she had moved toward the Catholic Left, antiwar activism, feminism, and reproductive rights.

Dorothy Haraway's death in 1960 was the decisive family rupture of adolescence. Haraway later connected her delayed grief to work on *Primate Visions* and to the deaths of Filomeno and Miller. There is no evidence of childhood exile, imprisonment, juvenile criminality, addiction, or inherited fortune.

## Education and Formation

### Colorado College, 1962–1966

Haraway attended Colorado College on a full **Boettcher Foundation scholarship** covering tuition, room, board, and books. The usual institutional description is a B.A. in zoology with minors in philosophy and English; in interview she described her course of study as effectively a triple major in zoology, philosophy, and literature. She graduated in 1966.

Important teachers included:

- **Jane Cauvel**, a philosophy professor whom Haraway remembered as an unusually serious teacher and formative model.
- **Glenn Gray**, who taught Heidegger; computer scientist **Terry Winograd** was among Haraway's contemporaries in that intellectual setting.
- Catholic and Teilhardian thinkers, Thomas Aquinas, evolutionary biology, literature, and the political crises of civil rights and the Vietnam War.

During college she moved away from both anti-Communism and orthodox Catholicism. Colorado College later awarded her an honorary **Doctor of Science in 1986**, one of its first honorary degrees to alumni ([Colorado College recipients](https://www.coloradocollege.edu/other/academic-events-committee/honorary-degrees/recipients.html)).

### France: Fulbright and Teilhard de Chardin, 1966–1967/68

After graduation Haraway went to Paris on a Fulbright fellowship, studying evolutionary philosophy and theology at the Fondation Teilhard de Chardin and the University of Paris. Sources compress the stay as “a year”; Haraway's recollections place it in the academic period after 1966 and before Yale. She encountered the Vietnam/Indochina war through French politics shortly after Algerian independence. Teilhard's attempt to join evolution, theology, matter, and spirit gave her an early model of crossing scientific and philosophical registers, even as she moved away from his Catholic synthesis.

### Yale University and Woods Hole, approximately 1967/68–1972

Haraway entered Yale biology and took the 1968 Marine Biological Laboratory course at Woods Hole. She worked with tunicates or “sea grapes,” studying regeneration and developmental potency. She remembered the course's round-the-clock experimental intensity, but by summer 1969 laboratory work left her depressed and frequently tearful. She concluded that she loved biology as a way of knowing worlds but disliked, and believed herself poorly suited to, experimental bench practice.

Her principal doctoral mentor was the ecologist and limnologist **G. Evelyn Hutchinson**. Haraway described him as an old-school feminist in the sense of supporting women scientists. His laboratory and intellectual network were central to systems ecology and cybernetics; she later told Nicholas Gane that many people from the Hutchinson world became deeply interested in cybernetics, “including me” ([Gane interview](https://www.academia.edu/4724092/When_We_Have_Never_Been_Human_What_Is_to_Be_Done_Interview_with_Donna_Haraway)). **Thomas Kuhn** supplied a model for histories of scientific paradigms, and historian **Frederic Lawrence Holmes** served on her committee and supported her job search.

Her dissertation, completed in 1972, was **“The Search for Organizing Relations: An Organismic Paradigm in Twentieth-Century Developmental Biology.”** It crossed biology, philosophy, and the history of science and medicine, analyzing how metaphors such as crystals, fabrics, and fields structured experimental programs. A revised version became *Crystals, Fabrics, and Fields* (1976). The [UCSC archive description](https://oac.cdlib.org/findaid/ark:/13030/c8b283b2/) and [Yale's Wilbur Cross page](https://wgss.yale.edu/wgss-welcomes-donna-haraway) independently confirm the 1972 biology Ph.D.

### Political and communal education at Yale

From approximately 1968 to 1970 Haraway lived in a mixed Black-and-white commune. Its members and associates included a Black Panther and a welfare-rights organizer; the New Haven Black Panther trials, civil-rights work, gay liberation, and opposition to the Vietnam War formed its political environment. Biology students organized around the military use of chemical herbicides. Haraway taught in a “Biology and Society” course and recalled **Henry Louis Gates Jr.** as a student who wrote on Arthur Jensen's race-and-IQ controversy.

This period joined four domains that later remained inseparable in her work: biological practice; the politics of race and war; feminism and sexual politics; and technologies of classification and control.

## Early Career

### University of Hawaiʻi, 1971–1974

Haraway moved to Hawaiʻi because Jaye Miller obtained a university position. She wrote much of the dissertation in absentia and described herself as depressed in the initial “faculty wife” position. She taught General Science—combining biology and history of science—and worked in the experimental New College. She helped establish women's studies at the university.

Miller was publicly involved in gay liberation. Haraway said his 1974 tenure denial was closely connected to explicit homophobia and his activism. This is a **documented allegation in Haraway's retrospective account**, not a court finding; no tenure file or judicial adjudication was located. They separated in 1973 and divorced. In 1975, after the divorce, Haraway and Miller made a trip involving Mexico City and Honolulu that she called a “divorce trip,” an example of the friendship and chosen kinship that outlasted their marriage.

### Johns Hopkins University, 1974–1980

Johns Hopkins hired Haraway as an assistant professor in the History of Science in 1974, despite her limited formal coursework in history. Yale University Press had accepted the dissertation-derived book, and Holmes supplied a strong recommendation. At Hopkins she began research into primatology, race, sex, colonialism, museums, and scientific representation that eventually produced *Primate Visions*.

She worked closely with political theorist **Nancy Hartsock**. They built women's-studies and feminist-theory teaching, read Marxist feminism, science fiction, and theories of labor, and participated in a Feminist Union concerned with violence against women. Haraway met Rusten Hogness in this period.

When a feminist-theory position opened at UC Santa Cruz, Haraway and Hartsock proposed sharing the job. Haraway recalled that some colleagues presumed they were lovers; they were not. The History of Consciousness program rejected the job-share arrangement, Hartsock withdrew, and Haraway accepted the appointment. This episode belongs to the documentary history of feminist academic labor, not to a rivalry between the two women.

## Chronological Life History

### 1976–1980: first book, Sonoma land, move to Santa Cruz

Yale University Press published *Crystals, Fabrics, and Fields* in 1976. The book treated scientific metaphors not as decorative language but as material organizers of research programs. In 1977 Haraway, Hogness, Miller, and Nick Paulina jointly bought about thirty acres outside **Healdsburg, Sonoma County**, containing a collapsed house that they rebuilt. The purchase is known through Haraway's testimony; purchase price, deed shares, later assessed value, financing, and present title structure were not established.

Her two-part “Animal Sociology and a Natural Economy of the Body Politic” appeared in *Signs* in 1978, and “The Biological Enterprise: Sex, Mind, and Profit from Human Engineering to Sociobiology” followed in 1979. These essays joined histories of animal behavior research to political economy, sex/gender, race, and the management of populations.

Haraway joined UC Santa Cruz in 1980. The [UCSC oral-history page](https://library.ucsc.edu/reg-hist/haraway) calls her feminist-theory position in History of Consciousness probably the first of its kind in the United States; formulations that call her simply “the first tenured professor of feminist theory” should be treated as shorthand for that more qualified institutional claim.

### 1980–1985: UCSC, Socialist Review, and the cyborg

Santa Cruz placed Haraway in a deliberately interdisciplinary program alongside political theory, cultural studies, history, psychoanalysis, and critical theory. She retained affiliations with women's/feminist studies and other departments rather than settling into a conventional biology department.

After Ronald Reagan's election, the West Coast collective of *Socialist Review* asked Haraway for a short statement on the future of socialist feminism. A meeting of left movements in **Cavtat, Yugoslavia** (now Croatia), where she represented the collective, supplied a transnational context. The requested five pages grew into “A Manifesto for Cyborgs: Science, Technology, and Socialist Feminism in the 1980s,” published in *Socialist Review* no. 80 (1985), pp. 65–108.

The essay argued through a deliberately ironic political myth: late-twentieth-century people were already hybrids of organism and machine, nature and culture, labor and information. It rejected a return to an innocent organic whole and challenged feminisms that treated “woman” as a natural, universal identity. Coalition by political affinity, not biological essence, was its proposed strategy. It also mapped an “informatics of domination”: the conversion of work, war, reproduction, and communication into coded systems.

Haraway recalled writing the manifesto on a newly acquired computer. Publication itself was disputed within *Socialist Review*: retrospective accounts say members of the East Coast collective considered the piece a naïve embrace of technology, while the Berkeley/West Coast collective supported publication. This was an editorial-political dispute, not a ban or government censorship.

### Wiener’s cybernetics “made flesh and feminist”: the exact genealogy

The slogan is useful only if carefully qualified.

1. **Norbert Wiener's role was conceptual, not personal.** Haraway was not Wiener's student or collaborator. She adopted the cybernetic problem-field that Wiener named in *Cybernetics* (1948): communication and control across animals and machines, feedback, information, and the permeability of organism/machine boundaries.
2. **Hutchinson's ecology was a proximate route.** Haraway's Yale mentor and his network worked in systems ecology, population theory, feedback, and information. Haraway explicitly said that many in Hutchinson's orbit, herself included, were deeply interested in cybernetics.
3. **Her transformation was political and embodied.** Wiener treated communication/control as a general science and warned about automation and military power. Haraway recoded the cybernetic organism through socialist feminism, antiracism, labor, militarism, reproductive technologies, and the embodied positions of women working in electronics and biotechnology. The cyborg was therefore not merely a prosthetic human; it was the material-semiotic subject of information capitalism.
4. **The famous 1960 cyborg rat was not the direct trigger.** Manfred Clynes and Nathan Kline coined “cyborg” in “Cyborgs and Space” for self-regulating human-machine adaptation in space; their demonstration involved a rat with an implanted osmotic pump. Haraway told Goodeve that she **did not know that paper or image while writing the 1985 manifesto**. Chris Hables Gray showed it to her only in the middle or later 1980s. The rat became a retrospective genealogy, not a source she had consulted before composition.
5. **The manifesto opposed both technophobia and technological salvation.** The 1997 [*Wired* profile](https://www.wired.com/1997/02/ffharaway/) accurately summarized her view that technology is not neutral because people inhabit what they make and are made by it. She neither proposed escape from technology nor guaranteed that hybridization was emancipatory.

Thus “Wiener's cybernetics made flesh and feminist” captures Haraway's conversion of abstract communication/control into situated bodies and power relations. It becomes false if it implies a direct Wiener–Haraway relationship or a simple line from the 1960 rat to the manifesto.

### 1986–1991: AIDS, situated knowledge, and primate visions

Bob Filomeno died in 1986. Jaye Miller died in 1991. The AIDS epidemic was not an abstract backdrop: Haraway experienced caregiving, medical classification, stigma, secrecy, and the politics of immune status inside her intimate network. Her 1989 essay “The Biopolitics of Postmodern Bodies” examined immune-system discourse as a historically specific language of self, invasion, communication, and defense.

“Situated Knowledges: The Science Question in Feminism and the Privilege of Partial Perspective” appeared in *Feminist Studies* 14, no. 3 (1988), pp. 575–599 ([JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3178066)). Written partly in response to debates around Sandra Harding's *The Science Question in Feminism*, it rejected both the disembodied “god trick” of seeing everything from nowhere and the idea that all accounts are equally arbitrary. Objectivity was reconceived as accountable, partial, embodied positioning.

*Primate Visions: Gender, Race, and Nature in the World of Modern Science* appeared in 1989. It reconstructed primatology through laboratories, field sites, colonial travel, museum dioramas, popular culture, gender roles, racial imaginaries, and the careers of women primatologists. Its method treated scientific facts as real achievements made inside institutions, stories, instruments, and power relations—not as mere fictions.

*Simians, Cyborgs, and Women: The Reinvention of Nature* (1991) collected major essays and reprinted the manifesto under the now-standard title “A Cyborg Manifesto: Science, Technology, and Socialist-Feminism in the Late Twentieth Century.” This version made the work much more widely available.

### 1992–2000: recognition, OncoMouse, and collaborative art

The American Sociological Association's Science, Knowledge and Technology section gave *Primate Visions* its **1992 Robert K. Merton Award** ([ASA recipient history](https://www.asanet.org/communities-and-sections/sections/current-sections/science-knowledge-and-technology-award-recipient-history/)).

From approximately 1990 to 1996 Haraway collaborated with Houston painter **Lynn Randolph**. Randolph's images—among them hybrid, pregnant, vampiric, and biomedical figures—entered lectures and the visual argument of *Modest_Witness@Second_Millennium.FemaleMan©_Meets_OncoMouse™* (1997). The book traced technoscience through databases, biotechnology, immune systems, race, gender, intellectual property, and the patented cancer-research mouse.

**OncoMouse was not Haraway's invention or property.** It was a transgenic research mouse developed in a Harvard/DuPont setting and patented through those institutional and corporate structures. Haraway made the animal a political relative—sometimes a “sister”—to ask who bears suffering, who receives patent ownership, and how organisms become technologies. No Haraway patent, biotechnology company, equity interest, or licensing income was found.

The Society for Social Studies of Science awarded *Modest_Witness* the **1999 Ludwik Fleck Prize** ([4S prize history](https://4sonline.org/1994_earlier_fleck_prize_winners.php)). Her extended interview with Thyrza Nichols Goodeve, *How Like a Leaf*, was copyrighted in 1999 and published by Routledge around 1999–2000; catalogs differ by market and edition. In September 2000 the Society gave Haraway the **J. D. Bernal Prize** for lifetime contributions ([4S record](https://4sonline.org/27_september_2000.php)).

### 2001–2010: from cyborgs to companion species

*The Companion Species Manifesto: Dogs, People, and Significant Otherness* (2003) shifted the foreground from machine-organism hybrids to coevolution, breeding, training, work, love, and unequal histories shared by humans and dogs. *The Haraway Reader* appeared in 2004.

Her companion animals included:

- **Cayenne Pepper**, an Australian shepherd with whom she trained and competed in dog agility. Training became a case study in bodily communication, discipline, pleasure, error, and mutual response.
- **Roland**, a mixed-breed dog discussed in the companion-species work.
- Later dogs and chickens, including formerly stray animals reported in 2026.

In Haraway's use, “companion species” did not mean sentimental pets alone. It included microbes, laboratory animals, livestock, working animals, crops, and the biological and colonial histories that compose species together. The dog-human relation was a “contact zone,” borrowing and extending Mary Louise Pratt's term for encounters structured by asymmetrical power.

*When Species Meet* was first issued in late 2007 in paperback and is commonly dated 2008, creating a harmless catalog discrepancy ([University of Minnesota Press](https://www.upress.umn.edu/9780816650460/when-species-meet/)). The cyborg and companion species were, she told Gane, “bookends”: neither replaced the other. Both refused purified nature and isolated individualism, but the companion-species work emphasized reciprocal becoming, work, and obligation among living kinds.

The 2007 UCSC oral history, **Irene Reti, *Edges and Ecotones: The Worlds of Donna Haraway at UCSC***, documented her institutional life and teaching ([eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9h09r84h)). Public sources call her emerita by the 2010s, but the exact effective retirement date was not established. Retirement did not end supervision, speaking, or center participation.

### 2011–2018: Anthropocene debates, Chthulucene, and making kin

Haraway's ecological vocabulary expanded through collaborations and conversations with **Isabelle Stengers, Bruno Latour, Anna Tsing, Marilyn Strathern, Vinciane Despret, Scott Gilbert**, and others. She resisted “Anthropocene” when it made an undifferentiated humanity the causal agent and used alternatives—**Capitalocene**, **Plantationocene**, and especially **Chthulucene**—to track capitalist extraction, plantation systems, colonial rearrangements of life, and multispecies entanglement.

*Manifestly Haraway* (2016) joined the cyborg and companion-species manifestos with a conversation with Cary Wolfe ([University of Minnesota Press](https://www.upress.umn.edu/9780816650484/manifestly-haraway/)). *Staying with the Trouble: Making Kin in the Chthulucene* (2016) developed “sympoiesis” or making-with, string figures, science fiction/speculative fabulation, and the “Camille Stories,” fictional multi-generational communities bound to endangered monarch butterflies ([Duke University Press](https://www.dukeupress.edu/staying-with-the-trouble)).

The book's injunction “Make kin, not babies” became the most disputed slogan of her later career. In context it advocated expanding kinship beyond genealogy and reducing coercive pronatalism, not harming existing children. *Making Kin Not Population* (2018), coedited with **Adele E. Clarke**, deliberately included disagreement about whether “population” can be used without reproducing racial, colonial, eugenic, and coercive histories.

### 2019–2024: public elder, ecological politics, and papal citation

Haraway continued long-form interviews and lectures rather than withdrawing from public work. In a 2020 [*Science for the People* interview](https://magazine.scienceforthepeople.org/vol23-3-bio-politics/beyond-critique-an-interview-with-donna-haraway/) she emphasized collective practices and the need to build more livable worlds rather than make critique an end in itself.

In October 2023 Pope Francis's apostolic exhortation *Laudate Deum* cited *When Species Meet* for the phrase **“contact zone.”** Haraway expressed surprise because she had been in “deep apostasy” for decades, but explained that Catholic sacramental formation still informed her insistence that meaning and flesh are not separable. She praised elements of Francis's ecological teaching while criticizing his church's misogyny, population analysis, and the phrase “technocratic paradigm,” which she thought displaced responsibility from specific capitalist formations ([NCR interview](https://www.ncronline.org/news/feminist-scholar-donna-haraway-reacts-inclusion-pope-francis-climate-letter); [Vatican text](https://www.vatican.va/content/francesco/en/apost_exhortations/documents/20231004-laudate-deum.html)).

### 2025–present: archive, international honors, injury, and continued work

In 2025 UCSC completed major processing of the **Donna Jeanne Haraway Papers, 1966–2026**, 52 boxes plus 70.2 GB comprising 161,959 digital files. Haraway donated materials in 2005, 2014, 2018, 2021, 2023, and 2024. The archive contains teaching files, writings, research, conferences, correspondence, biographical materials, and digital files; it is the indispensable basis for future biography ([finding aid](https://oac.cdlib.org/findaid/ark:/13030/c8b283b2/)).

The Venice Architecture Biennale awarded her the **Golden Lion for Lifetime Achievement** on 10 May 2025 ([La Biennale](https://www.labiennale.org/en/news/2025-lion-awards-architecture)). The Praemium Erasmianum Foundation awarded the **2025 Erasmus Prize**, worth €150,000, for work on kinship among people, technology, and other species ([laureate page](https://erasmusprijs.org/en/laureates/donna-haraway/); [prize description](https://erasmusprijs.org/en/about-erasmus-prize/)).

In her acceptance speech Haraway reported that in summer 2025 she tripped while picking lemons, fell into the street on her head, and then experienced months of imbalance and tinnitus; doctors limited sustained thinking during recovery. This is a documented self-report of an accident and symptoms, not a disclosed neurological diagnosis ([acceptance speech](https://erasmusprijs.org/en/laureates/donna-haraway/acceptance-speech-2/)).

In July 2026 she remained intellectually and politically active. Addressing population controversy, she called herself **pro-child and non-natalist, not anti-natalist**, arguing that babies should be “rare and precious” in a world she described as pronatalist but hostile to children. She also discussed artificial intelligence through a new Lynn Randolph painting that joined a Harawayan cyborg named Claudette to Anthropic's Claude. These are current positions, not settled consensus ([*El País* interview](https://english.elpais.com/culture/2026-07-24/donna-haraway-philosopher-we-live-in-a-pro-natalist-anti-child-world-babies-should-be-rare-and-precious.html)).

### Technologies studied rather than owned

## Political / Religious / Intellectual / Artistic Development

### Political development

1. **Catholic anti-Communist childhood:** parish and school formation; youthful opposition to abortion rights.
2. **Catholic Left and New Left:** civil-rights work, antiwar biology, communal living, welfare-rights and Black liberation contacts, gay liberation, women's liberation.
3. **Marxist and socialist feminism:** Hopkins work with Nancy Hartsock; analysis of labor, capital, reproduction, race, and science; *Socialist Review* collective.
4. **Coalition and affinity:** the cyborg manifesto rejected a naturally unified category of women while seeking historically specific coalitions.
5. **Technoscience politics:** refusal of both technological determinism and technological innocence; attention to military, industrial, and biomedical systems.
6. **Multispecies and climate politics:** critique of human exceptionalism, extractive capitalism, plantations, extinction, and coercive pronatalism; advocacy of response-ability and making kin.

No elected office, formal party leadership, armed organization membership, political imprisonment, state-security employment, or participation in political violence was found.

### Religion

Haraway left Catholic belief and institutional membership in her twenties, judging the church's patriarchal structure morally insupportable before belief itself disappeared. Yet Catholic materialism remained: Eucharistic “real presence,” ritual, incarnation, preference for the poor, and the inseparability of signs from bodies. She is therefore neither a returned orthodox Catholic nor simply a secular rationalist with no religious residue. Her own phrase in 2023 was “deep apostasy,” coupled with refusal to disavow Catholic formation.

### Biology, metaphor, and situated knowledge

The dissertation established the lifelong thesis that metaphors are not outside material science; they organize questions, apparatuses, organisms, and institutions. *Situated Knowledges* extended that position into epistemology: knowledge is embodied and partial, yet claims can be more or less accountable and objective. Haraway's position explicitly differs from “anything goes” relativism.

### Cyborg feminism

The cyborg condenses three historical boundary breakdowns: human/animal, organism/machine, and physical/nonphysical. It is simultaneously a real configuration of late-capitalist labor and biomedicine, a science-fiction figure, and a political myth. Its feminism is anti-essentialist and coalition-oriented. It does not abolish material sex, deny oppression, or predict that machines automatically end patriarchy.

### Companion species and sympoiesis

Dogs did not mark a retreat from politics into pet sentiment. Breeding, colonial movement, labor, sport, veterinary care, food, and coevolution made companion relations political-economic. “Becoming with” and “response-ability” named the capacity to answer within unequal relations. “Sympoiesis” later extended making-with to ecological systems that have no self-contained maker.

### Science fiction and art

Haraway repeatedly worked with science fiction as theory and method. Important literary companions include **Ursula K. Le Guin, Octavia E. Butler, Samuel R. Delany, Joanna Russ, Vonda N. McIntyre**, and feminist speculative traditions. “SF” in her later writing simultaneously means science fiction, speculative feminism, speculative fabulation, science fact, and string figures. Lynn Randolph's paintings are not illustrations appended to prose; they are co-produced arguments in color, body, and iconography.

## Significant Successes, Failures, and Constraints

### Documented successes

- Created a durable bridge among biology, history of science, feminist theory, cultural studies, and political economy.
- Helped institutionalize women's studies in Hawaiʻi and feminist technoscience at UCSC.
- Produced three especially field-defining interventions: the cyborg, situated knowledge, and companion species.
- Advised more than sixty doctoral students across disciplines.
- Reached audiences in architecture, art, design, ecology, religious debate, and science fiction beyond academic STS.
- Received major disciplinary and international honors listed below.

### Failures, abandoned paths, and limits in her own account

- She left experimental laboratory biology after concluding she was not good at it and finding the work emotionally unsustainable.
- The proposed Hartsock–Haraway job share at UCSC failed.
- Haraway said *Primate Visions* had no direct transforming effect on the daily practice of primatology, although it mattered in STS and broader debates. Some women primatologists felt misread or exposed; Sarah Blaffer Hrdy was a notable point of tension in Haraway's recollection.
- Dense prose, proliferating puns, typographic experiments, and mythic figures expanded the work's reach but also limited accessibility and generated recurring claims of obscurity.
- Her later reproductive slogan produced criticism that the book itself anticipated but did not resolve.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, Fraud, Corruption, or Financial Questions

### Evidentiary register

No lawsuit, criminal investigation, indictment, charge, conviction, fraud finding, corruption case, money-laundering allegation supported by evidence, intelligence-service relationship, or act of political violence involving Haraway was found in the consulted archive descriptions, institutional records, press profiles, or scholarship. No accusation of plagiarism or fabricated scientific data was located. Because public absence cannot prove absolute absence, the finding is stated narrowly.

The controversies surrounding Haraway are overwhelmingly **intellectual and political**, not judicial.

### 1. Technology: naïve embrace or critical inhabitation?

**Claim:** Critics within *Socialist Review* and later readers treated the manifesto as a celebration of high technology and the disappearance of material bodies.

**Evidence:** The text calls the cyborg a product of militarism, patriarchal capitalism, and state socialism as well as a possible oppositional myth. Its “informatics of domination” catalogs exploitation rather than promising automatic liberation. Haraway has repeatedly rejected technological neutrality and utopian rescue. The fairest documentary description is a politics of inhabiting compromised systems, not technophilia.

**Status:** interpretive dispute; no factual adjudication.

### 2. Gender, sex, and “postgender” readings

**Claim:** Haraway is often called a prophet of postgender humanity or the abolition of sex.

**Evidence:** The manifesto attacks naturalized identities, and its cyborg has no origin in the heterosexual nuclear family. Yet Haraway told Gane that she never liked “postgender” and considered the term misleading. Her work analyzes the material production of sexed and racialized bodies rather than declaring them unreal.

**Status:** widespread reception claim contradicted by the author's explicit clarification.

### 3. Race and analogy

Malini Johar Schueller's peer-reviewed “Analogy and (White) Feminist Theory: Thinking Race and the Color of the Cyborg Body” argued that white feminist uses of women-of-color positions as analogies can appropriate racial difference and reproduce whiteness ([University of Chicago Press](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/431372)). The critique applies to Haraway's deployment of figures such as the “women of color” cyborg and to broader theory.

Haraway's defenders and intellectual descendants, including Chela Sandoval, have read the manifesto as allied with oppositional consciousness rather than substituting for it. The documentary conclusion is not that either reading has been formally proven, but that race is internal to the work and that the authority to figuratively use racialized positions remains disputed.

### 4. Disability and the “we are all cyborgs” metaphor

In “Common Cyborg,” disabled writer Jillian Weise argued that universal cyborg rhetoric appropriates an identity materially lived by disabled, Deaf, and neurodivergent people using prostheses, implants, or assistance technologies. If everyone is metaphorically a cyborg, the specific access politics and discrimination facing “actual” cyborg users can disappear ([*Granta*](https://granta.com/common-cyborg/)).

Haraway's own cyborg was always broader than prosthetics, including labor, war, databases, and biomedicine. That breadth explains the figure's power but does not dissolve Weise's criticism. **Status:** critical argument about consequences of a metaphor, not an allegation of personal abuse.

### 5. *Primate Visions*: science history or partisan distortion?

Some primatologists and reviewers charged that *Primate Visions* selectively interpreted scientific work through feminist, racial, and political narratives, or that it risked relativism. Haraway acknowledged that the book did not directly change primatological practice and that some scientists, including Sarah Blaffer Hrdy, objected to aspects of her presentation. She also recounted a productive exchange in which Alison Richard revised a chapter after Haraway pointed to omitted histories.

The controversy concerns method: whether scientific stories, museum displays, colonial institutions, and gendered careers are constitutive of knowledge or external context. No evidence of falsified data or research misconduct was found. **Status:** continuing scholarly dispute.

### 6. Science wars and relativism

Haraway was grouped with postmodern or social-constructionist critics of science during the 1990s “science wars.” Her difficult style and language of constructed facts made her a convenient symbol. Yet “Situated Knowledges” explicitly opposes unaccountable relativism, and “enlightenment@science_wars.com” defended factual and ethical accountability while criticizing claims to disembodied universality.

The label “anti-science” is therefore a hostile shorthand, not a neutral description of a Ph.D. biologist who repeatedly analyzes how better scientific accounts are made. The degree to which her epistemology secures realism remains philosophically disputed.

### 7. Purebred dogs, sport, and privilege

Companion-species theory grows from an affluent North American practice: owning and training an Australian shepherd, participating in agility, and paying for veterinary and bodily care. Critics have asked how readily that contact zone travels to stray dogs, slaughterhouses, laboratory animals, subsistence relations, and people without such resources. Haraway herself foregrounded the commodity, breeding, colonial, and class contradictions of dog love rather than presenting her household as innocent.

**Status:** material limitation and scope controversy; no financial impropriety alleged.

### 8. Population, reproduction, and neo-Malthusian danger

“Make kin, not babies” generated the most politically sensitive criticism of Haraway's later work.

- Banu Subramaniam argued that “overpopulation” discourse has historically displaced responsibility from colonialism and consumption onto racialized and poor people's fertility, while noting that the Clarke–Haraway volume includes internal debate ([*Public Books*](https://www.publicbooks.org/overpopulation-is-not-the-problem/)).
- Nayantara Sheoran Appleton and coauthors stressed the eugenic, coercive, and colonial burden of population language and highlighted Michelle Murphy's argument that the category should be abandoned ([*American Anthropologist*](https://anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/aman.13780)).
- Nikolas Mattheis argued that the slogan's claimed pro-child politics remained underdeveloped and could reproduce adultism or sit too close to neo-Malthusian discourse ([*Childhood*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09075682221098155)).
- Haraway's response is that coercive population control is unacceptable, that wealthy high-consumption formations bear particular responsibility, and that she is pro-child but non-natalist—not anti-child or committed to eliminating births.

**Status:** documented ideological controversy. No evidence was found that Haraway administered a population-control program, advocated forced sterilization, or held governmental power over reproduction.

### 9. Chthulucene and H. P. Lovecraft

Haraway says “Chthulucene” derives from chthonic powers and the spider **Pimoa cthulhu**, and she explicitly says Lovecraft's Cthulhu “plays no role.” Critics note that the zoologists named the spider after Lovecraft's creature, making complete disconnection etymologically strained. A [*Viewpoint* critique](https://viewpointmag.com/2017/05/08/cthulhu-plays-no-role-for-me/) traced the racial politics of Lovecraft and challenged the disavowal. The Venice Biennale's own 2025 notice incorrectly or simplistically described Haraway's Chthulucene as named “after H. P. Lovecraft's fictional Cthulhu.”

**Status:** disputed etymology and symbolic association. Haraway's intended conceptual source is documented; the intermediate Lovecraft link in the spider's name also exists.

### 10. Abortion and Catholic formation

Haraway's admission that she wrote against abortion rights in high school can be detached from chronology and circulated as evidence of a continuing position. Her adult work supports reproductive freedom while treating reproduction, death, and obligation as morally serious rather than rhetorically simple. No evidence supports describing the adolescent letter as her current policy.

### Translation and edition note

The manifesto and major books have appeared in Spanish, French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Japanese, and many other languages. Translated titles vary—e.g., *Manifiesto cyborg*, *Manifeste cyborg*, *Manifesto cyborg*—and some national editions assemble essays not grouped together in the English originals. These are authorized translations/compilations, not separate original books. No disputed or forged Haraway corpus comparable to a pseudepigraphic tradition was identified.

### Review history and hostile/skeptical reception

Reviews of *Primate Visions* in primatology and history of science ranged from admiration for its archive and political reach to charges of partisanship, selective evidence, and relativism. Reviews of the cyborg work likewise range from treating it as a foundational feminist intervention to dismissing its style as jargon or its politics as incoherent. There is no full independent cradle-to-present biography as of the status date. Consequently, *How Like a Leaf*, the UCSC oral history, the 2025 archive, and long profiles remain the principal life-history sources.

## Death / Later Life / Present Status

Haraway is **alive** as of 31 August 2026. She lives between Santa Cruz and rural Sonoma County with Rusten Hogness and companion animals. She remains an emerita scholar, lecturer, interview subject, archival donor, and participant in political and intellectual discussions. No terminal illness or cause-of-death question applies.

The last well-documented health event is the summer 2025 fall and subsequent imbalance/tinnitus. The most recent substantive interview located was published 24 July 2026. Her archive's inclusive date extends through 2026, reflecting continuing additions or processing.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Because Haraway is living, “posthumous” reception cannot yet be assessed. Her present legacy has several distinct, sometimes contradictory forms:

1. **Feminist science and technology studies:** *Situated Knowledges* provides a standard vocabulary for partial perspective, embodied objectivity, and accountability.
2. **Cyborg and digital culture:** the manifesto helped make the cyborg a feminist, artistic, and political figure rather than a military-medical engineering term. Its impact reaches cyberfeminism, media art, design, disability debate, HCI, AI discourse, and popular music.
3. **Histories of biology and primatology:** *Crystals* and *Primate Visions* made metaphors, museums, race, gender, colonial travel, and institutional practice part of the internal history of biological knowledge.
4. **Animal and multispecies studies:** companion species, becoming-with, and response-ability reorganized humanities and social-science approaches to animals without assuming pristine nature.
5. **Environmental humanities:** Chthulucene, Plantationocene, sympoiesis, and “staying with the trouble” provide alternatives to apocalypse, technological rescue, and undifferentiated Anthropocene narratives.
6. **Pedagogy and institutional lineage:** more than sixty doctoral advisees and UCSC's cross-departmental culture transmitted Harawayan methods into anthropology, feminist studies, geography, art, environmental studies, and STS.
7. **Cult and slogan:** “I'd rather be a cyborg than a goddess,” “make kin, not babies,” “stay with the trouble,” and “we are compost, not posthuman” circulate beyond their arguments. This visibility produces both hagiography and hostile caricature.
8. **Critical counter-legacy:** scholars of race, disability, childhood, reproduction, and population have used Haraway's own demand for situated accountability to expose limits in Haraway's figurations. Those criticisms are part of the legacy, not external noise.
9. **Archival reinterpretation:** the opening of 161,959 digital files and extensive paper records will allow future scholars to test the polished autobiographical narrative against drafts, correspondence, teaching, and collaborative networks. A definitive biography remains unwritten.
