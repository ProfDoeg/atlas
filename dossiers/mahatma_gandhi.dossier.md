# Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary method

This dossier reconstructs Gandhi’s life from contemporary records, his own writings, archival collections, government publications, court records, correspondence, and historical scholarship. Gandhi’s autobiography is indispensable but retrospective, selective, and incomplete after 1921. It is treated as testimony rather than an infallible record.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by primary records, contemporary documents, or a strong convergence of evidence.
- **Reported fact:** credible secondary account whose complete underlying documentation was not examined here.
- **Allegation or interpretation:** a claim advanced by a participant, critic, scholar, or political movement that remains contested.
- **Rumor or myth:** a story lacking adequate evidence, whether favorable or hostile.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

Gandhi’s paternal family came from the Modh Bania trading caste. The family had entered administrative service in Kathiawar’s princely states.

His grandfather, Uttamchand Gandhi, commonly called Ota Gandhi, served as diwan or chief minister of Porbandar. Family tradition celebrated his political loyalty and independence; some anecdotes about his confrontations with rulers are preserved principally through family memory.

Gandhi’s father, **Karamchand Uttamchand Gandhi**, commonly called **Kaba Gandhi**, served as diwan of Porbandar and in administrative positions in Rajkot and Wankaner. Mohandas characterized him as practically experienced, loyal, courageous, impartial, and incorruptible, but with little formal education.

Karamchand married four times, his earlier wives having died. His final wife was Putlibai.

### Mother

**Putlibai Gandhi**, from a Pranami Vaishnava family, was Karamchand’s fourth wife. Gandhi remembered her as intensely observant, regularly fasting and fulfilling religious vows. Her combination of Vaishnava devotion, ritual fasting, and service strongly influenced his later religious vocabulary and bodily disciplines.

### Siblings

Karamchand and Putlibai’s children included:

- Laxmidas Gandhi, elder son.
- Raliatbehn Gandhi, daughter.
- Karsandas Gandhi, elder son.
- Mohandas, the youngest.

There were also half-siblings from Karamchand’s earlier marriages. Exact genealogical presentations vary among family trees.

### Marriage

Gandhi and **Kasturba Makhanji Kapadia**—later Kasturba Gandhi—were betrothed as children and married in 1883, when both were approximately thirteen. Some chronologies give 1882 because of differences in traditional dating and recollection; 1883 is now more commonly accepted.

Gandhi later criticized child marriage. His autobiography candidly describes adolescent jealousy, sexual possessiveness, efforts to control Kasturba, and guilt that he was with her sexually near the time his father died. This “double shame” became part of his autobiographical explanation for treating sexual desire as a form of bondage.

Kasturba did not remain merely a dependent spouse. She resisted his attempts to dictate her movements, participated in South African satyagraha, endured imprisonment, managed communal households, and became an independent public symbol. Their marriage included conflict, separation caused by Gandhi’s travels and imprisonment, and increasingly renunciatory domestic arrangements.

### Children

They had four sons who survived infancy:

1. **Harilal Gandhi** (1888–1948).
2. **Manilal Gandhi** (1892–1956), later associated with *Indian Opinion* in South Africa.
3. **Ramdas Gandhi** (1897–1969).
4. **Devdas Gandhi** (1900–1957), later editor of the *Hindustan Times*.

An earlier child born in 1885 died shortly after birth.

Harilal’s relationship with his father became deeply troubled. He believed Gandhi obstructed his desire for advanced education and subordinated the family to public experiments. Harilal experienced financial instability, alcoholism, estrangement, and religious conversions—most publicly a conversion to Islam under the name Abdullah Gandhi in 1936, followed by a return to Hinduism. Gandhi’s expectations, refusal to grant family privilege, and moral interventions in Harilal’s life remain central to critical assessments of his conduct as a father.

### Extended household

Gandhi increasingly replaced the conventional nuclear household with the ashram. Relatives, secretaries, nurses, political workers, and younger women lived within a community regulated by vows. Important household associates included Maganlal Gandhi, Chhaganlal Gandhi, Mahadev Desai, Pyarelal Nayar, Sushila Nayar, Mirabehn, and Manu Gandhi.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1869–1888

Gandhi was born in Porbandar on 2 October 1869. His family moved to Rajkot during his childhood as Karamchand entered service there.

He attended local schools at Porbandar and Rajkot, including Alfred High School. He described himself as shy, academically unexceptional, truthful but fearful, and uncomfortable with public speaking.

Two popular religious dramas became formative childhood stories:

- **Shravana’s devotion to his parents**, which strengthened Gandhi’s ideal of filial service.
- **King Harishchandra’s uncompromising truthfulness**, which provided a narrative model for his later identification of God with truth.

During adolescence, a friend, Sheikh Mehtab, encouraged him to eat meat secretly on the theory that British strength came from meat and that Indians needed physical power. Gandhi also experimented briefly with smoking and once stole gold from his brother’s armlet to pay a debt. He confessed the theft to his father in writing. His father’s tearful, non-punitive response was later reconstructed by Gandhi as an early lesson in nonviolent moral force.

His account of these episodes is **documented autobiographical testimony**, not independently verifiable in every detail. Their polished place within his later moral narrative makes them part history and part self-created exemplary literature.

Karamchand died in 1885. Putlibai and senior family members subsequently oversaw the young Gandhi’s education and proposed legal career.

## Education and Formation

### Samaldas College

In 1888 Gandhi briefly attended Samaldas College at Bhavnagar. He struggled with English-medium lectures and did not complete the course.

### Decision to study law in London

Family adviser Mavji Dave recommended legal study in England as a route to administrative advancement. Gandhi faced opposition from relatives and from caste authorities, who regarded ocean travel as religiously polluting. He promised his mother—through a vow administered or witnessed by the Jain-associated family adviser Becharji Swami—to abstain from meat, alcohol, and sexual relations.

The Modh Bania caste council reportedly excommunicated him when he refused to abandon the journey. He sailed from Bombay on 4 September 1888.

### London, 1888–1891

Gandhi enrolled at the Inner Temple. He also took classes connected with University College London, though descriptions that imply he earned a UCL degree are incorrect. He passed the London matriculation examination after initial difficulty, studied Roman law and English law, and was called to the Bar on 10 June 1891. He enrolled in the High Court in London and left for India shortly afterward.

His London years included an abortive effort to become an English gentleman through fashionable dress, dancing, elocution, and violin lessons. Financial pressure and ethical commitments redirected him toward simplicity.

Vegetarianism became intellectual rather than merely vow-bound. He joined the London Vegetarian Society, served on its executive committee, wrote for *The Vegetarian*, and developed friendships with Henry Salt and vegetarian, theosophical, Christian, and freethinking circles.

Two Theosophist acquaintances asked him to read the Bhagavad Gita with them. Edwin Arnold’s English rendering, *The Song Celestial*, became important. He read the Bible, finding portions of the Old Testament difficult but responding strongly to the Sermon on the Mount. He read Arnold’s *The Light of Asia*, Thomas Carlyle on Muhammad, and other comparative religious literature.

### Principal mentors and intellectual influences

- **Raychandbhai/Shri Rajchandra**, Jain poet, merchant, and religious thinker: Gandhi’s most important early Indian spiritual interlocutor.
- **Leo Tolstoy**, especially *The Kingdom of God Is Within You*: nonresistance, critique of state violence, simplicity, and moral Christianity.
- **John Ruskin**, especially *Unto This Last*: dignity of labor, moral equality of occupations, and communal economy.
- **Henry David Thoreau**, especially “Civil Disobedience”: terminology and precedents for resistance, although Gandhi maintained that satyagraha developed from Indian experience rather than being copied from Thoreau.
- **Bhagavad Gita**: disciplined action without attachment to results.
- **Jesus’ Sermon on the Mount**: love of enemies and non-retaliation.
- Jain doctrines of **ahimsa**, self-restraint, and many-sided truth.
- Vaishnava and Pranami devotional culture.
- Gopal Krishna Gokhale, his principal political mentor after the South African period.

## Early Legal Career, 1891–1893

Gandhi returned to India in July 1891 and learned that Putlibai had died while he was abroad; his family had withheld the news.

His initial legal career was unsuccessful. In Bombay, shyness reportedly prevented him from effectively cross-examining a witness in his first case. He returned the client’s fee. In Rajkot he earned money drafting petitions but became involved in a dispute with a British political agent, reinforcing his dissatisfaction with local legal patronage.

In 1893 Dada Abdulla & Co., a Muslim Indian mercantile firm with interests in Natal and the Transvaal, hired him for a one-year assignment involving commercial litigation. He sailed for South Africa in April 1893.

## Chronological Life History

## South Africa, 1893–1914

### Pietermaritzburg and racial discrimination

In June 1893, while traveling to Pretoria, Gandhi was expelled from a first-class railway compartment at Pietermaritzburg after a white passenger objected to his presence. He spent the cold night in the station waiting room. He also described being assaulted by a stagecoach conductor and ordered to remove his turban in court.

The railway incident is supported by Gandhi’s near-contemporary and later testimony and became the canonical origin story of his political awakening. The simplified legend—that one moment instantly transformed an apolitical lawyer into the Mahatma—compresses a longer development involving class position, legal work, imperial citizenship, Indian merchant networks, and repeated discrimination.

### Legal work and Natal Indian Congress

Gandhi assisted Dada Abdulla’s commercial dispute with a relative, Tayob Haji Khan Muhammad, helping secure arbitration rather than ruinous litigation.

Preparing to return to India in 1894, he learned of legislation threatening Indian voting rights in Natal. Indian merchants persuaded him to stay. He founded or helped organize the **Natal Indian Congress** in August 1894, drafted petitions, cultivated newspapers and officials, and coordinated merchant financing.

The organization initially represented chiefly merchants and educated Indians. Indentured and former-indentured laborers were not equally incorporated. Gandhi’s political vocabulary in the 1890s sought to distinguish respectable Indians from Black Africans and sometimes used the racial slur then rendered as “kaffir.” These are **documented facts in his writings**.

### India journey and Durban attack, 1896–1897

In 1896 Gandhi returned to India to bring Kasturba and their children to South Africa and publicize Indian grievances. His *Green Pamphlet* summarized discrimination. White Natal newspapers and agitators portrayed him as importing Indians and slandering colonists.

When he returned to Durban in January 1897, a white mob assaulted him. He declined to prosecute identified attackers, helping establish his public reputation for non-retaliation.

### Boer War

During the South African War of 1899–1902, Gandhi organized an Indian Ambulance Corps in support of the British. He hoped service would demonstrate Indians’ claim to imperial citizenship. He received the Queen’s South Africa Medal.

This was not pacifist opposition to war. Gandhi supported the imperial war effort in a noncombatant role. His position evolved from loyalist claims within the Empire toward rejection of modern militarism and eventually colonial rule.

### Return to India and renewed South African work

Gandhi visited India in 1901, attended the Indian National Congress, worked briefly with Gokhale and other leaders, and attempted a Bombay legal practice. South African Indian clients recalled him in 1902.

### *Indian Opinion* and Phoenix Settlement

In 1903 Gandhi began legal practice in Johannesburg. In 1904 he took a leading role in *Indian Opinion*, a multilingual newspaper founded at Durban. It became the communications infrastructure of the South African campaign.

After reading Ruskin’s *Unto This Last* on a train journey, he established **Phoenix Settlement** near Durban. Residents combined agricultural and printing work. Ownership, labor discipline, family hierarchy, and Gandhi’s authority within such communities later generated both admiration and criticism.

### Zulu rebellion, 1906

During the British suppression of the 1906 Bambatha or Zulu rebellion, Gandhi organized an Indian stretcher-bearer corps. Members tended wounded Africans as well as serving the colonial apparatus.

Gandhi’s participation is a **documented fact**. Critics characterize it as support for imperial repression. Defenders emphasize his noncombatant role and later condemnation of the flogging and suffering he witnessed. Both readings arise from evidence; it is inaccurate either to portray him as leading violence against Zulus or to erase his cooperation with the colonial campaign.

### Brahmacharya

In 1906, aged thirty-seven, Gandhi took a vow of **brahmacharya**, interpreted by him as complete sexual celibacy. Kasturba’s precise participation in the decision is less fully documented than Gandhi’s account. Celibacy became linked to control of diet, anger, possessions, and political energy.

### Birth of satyagraha

The Transvaal’s Asiatic Registration legislation required Indian and other Asian residents to register, provide identifying information, and carry certificates. At a Johannesburg meeting in September 1906, participants pledged resistance.

Gandhi initially used “passive resistance” but considered it inadequate. A naming competition in *Indian Opinion* produced “sadagraha,” refined to **satyagraha**: holding firmly to truth or “truth-force.”

He negotiated with Jan Smuts, encouraged voluntary registration after Smuts allegedly promised repeal, and was assaulted in February 1908 by Mir Alam Khan and other Pathans who regarded the compromise as betrayal. When the legislation remained, certificates were publicly burned.

### Imprisonments

South African imprisonment records on the Gandhi Heritage chronology include terms in:

- Johannesburg, January 1908.
- Johannesburg and Volksrust, October–December 1908.
- Volksrust and Pretoria, February–May 1909.
- Volksrust and Bloemfontein, November–December 1913.

Exact institutional and transfer dates vary in summary chronologies.

### London mission and *Hind Swaraj*

In 1909 Gandhi traveled to London with Haji Habib to lobby imperial authorities. On the return voyage aboard the *Kildonan Castle*, between 13 and 22 November, he wrote *Hind Swaraj* in Gujarati, reportedly using his left hand when the right tired.

The book attacks modern industrial civilization, parliamentary politics, lawyers, doctors, railways, machinery, and violent nationalism. It defines genuine self-rule as ethical self-mastery rather than merely replacing British officials with Indian rulers. The Gujarati text appeared in *Indian Opinion* in December 1909 and was prohibited in India; Gandhi supplied an English translation, *Indian Home Rule*, in 1910.

### Tolstoy Farm

Hermann Kallenbach, a German-Jewish architect and Gandhi’s intimate friend and supporter, made land near Johannesburg available for **Tolstoy Farm** in 1910. The settlement housed satyagrahis and families and practiced manual labor, simplified education, and communal discipline.

Gandhi and Kallenbach’s emotionally intense correspondence prompted later speculation about a sexual relationship. **Documented fact:** their relationship was intimate, affectionate, financially and practically important, and sometimes expressed in language modern readers may interpret romantically. **Unresolved interpretation:** no conclusive evidence establishes a sexual relationship. Joseph Lelyveld’s *Great Soul* did not straightforwardly claim that Gandhi was homosexual, despite publicity surrounding the book.

### 1913 campaign and settlement

A court ruling invalidating non-Christian marriages, the £3 tax on former indentured laborers, and immigration restrictions helped produce the 1913 struggle. Women, including Kasturba, crossed provincial boundaries and courted arrest. Gandhi led thousands of mine workers from Natal toward the Transvaal.

Strikers experienced arrests, deprivation, coercion, and violence. Desai and Vahed argue that worker militancy and episodes of violence, rather than philosophical nonviolence alone, helped force negotiation.

The Indian Relief Act of 1914 recognized Indian marriages, abolished the £3 tax, and provided limited relief. It did not dismantle South Africa’s racial order. Gandhi left South Africa in July 1914, traveled through England, and returned to India in January 1915.

## Return to India and Local Campaigns, 1915–1919

Gandhi reached Bombay on 9 January 1915. He received the **Kaiser-i-Hind Medal**, awarded earlier for humanitarian service, but returned it in 1920 in protest against British rule and repression.

Following Gokhale’s advice, he spent approximately a year traveling and observing India before taking national leadership. He founded an ashram at Kochrab near Ahmedabad in May 1915. Admission of an “untouchable” family caused donors to withdraw support; assistance commonly attributed to industrialist Ambalal Sarabhai helped the community survive.

The ashram moved to the banks of the Sabarmati in 1917.

### Champaran, 1917

Raj Kumar Shukla repeatedly persuaded Gandhi to investigate indigo cultivators’ grievances in Champaran, Bihar. British officials ordered Gandhi to leave; he declined and appeared in court, offering to accept punishment. The order was withdrawn.

Gandhi and associates—including Rajendra Prasad, J. B. Kripalani, Brajkishore Prasad, and Mahadev Desai—collected thousands of cultivator statements. A government committee recommended reform of the tinkathia system. Gandhi accepted a 25-percent refund of illegal exactions, valuing acknowledgment of planter power’s limits over the amount.

### Ahmedabad mill strike, 1918

Gandhi mediated between textile workers and mill owners, including the Sarabhai family, over a plague bonus and wages. Anasuya Sarabhai organized workers; her brother Ambalal represented employers. Gandhi undertook a fast to sustain worker solidarity. Arbitration produced a 35-percent increase.

This fast illustrates a persistent controversy: supporters regarded fasting as self-suffering that awakened conscience; critics regarded a revered leader’s threat of self-harm as coercive moral pressure.

### Kheda, 1918

Crop failure led cultivators in Kheda district to seek suspension of revenue collection. Gandhi, Vallabhbhai Patel, Indulal Yagnik, and others organized refusal by cultivators deemed unable to pay. The government eventually relaxed collection, though the settlement was less categorical than nationalist memory sometimes suggests.

### First World War recruitment

Despite his increasingly anti-imperial politics, Gandhi campaigned in 1918 for Indian recruitment into the British army. He argued that Indians seeking political rights should share imperial responsibilities and that courage was preferable to cowardice.

This is a **documented contradiction** in his development. Recruitment largely failed in Kheda; Gandhi became seriously ill during the campaign.

## Rowlatt Acts, Jallianwala Bagh, and Khilafat, 1919–1922

### Rowlatt satyagraha

The Rowlatt legislation extended wartime emergency powers. Gandhi called for a nationwide hartal on 6 April 1919. Disturbances and repression followed. Authorities prevented him from entering Punjab and temporarily detained him.

At Amritsar on 13 April, Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer’s troops fired on an unarmed gathering at Jallianwala Bagh. Gandhi initially suspended civil disobedience after violence, calling his premature launch a “Himalayan miscalculation.”

### Journalism

From 1919 Gandhi led or edited:

- *Young India* in English.
- *Navajivan* in Gujarati and later Hindi.
- From 1933, *Harijan*, *Harijanbandhu*, and *Harijan Sevak*.

These journals functioned as political command networks, ethical laboratories, fundraising instruments, and records of changing positions.

### Khilafat and Non-Cooperation

Gandhi allied with Muslim leaders including the Ali brothers to oppose British treatment of the Ottoman caliphate and to build Hindu-Muslim unity. Congress adopted non-cooperation: surrender of titles, boycotts of schools, courts, councils, and foreign cloth, promotion of spinning, and eventual civil disobedience.

The alliance expanded mass mobilization. Critics then and later argued that supporting the caliphate inserted religious authority into nationalist politics. Gandhi opposed Moplah violence in Malabar in 1921 but continued to seek Hindu-Muslim solidarity.

### Chauri Chaura and suspension

On 5 February 1922 a crowd at Chauri Chaura killed twenty-two policemen after police firing. Gandhi suspended mass civil disobedience, believing the movement lacked nonviolent discipline.

Many nationalists considered this a major strategic failure and an abandonment of mass momentum. For Gandhi, means were constitutive of ends.

### Sedition trial and imprisonment

Arrested in March 1922 for articles in *Young India*, Gandhi pleaded guilty to sedition before Judge C. N. Broomfield and received six years’ imprisonment. He served at Yeravda Central Prison from March 1922 until an appendicitis operation and release in February 1924.

## Communal Politics, Social Reform, and the Salt Campaign, 1924–1931

Gandhi fasted for twenty-one days in 1924 following Hindu-Muslim violence. He served as president of the Indian National Congress at Belgaum in December 1924—the only year he formally held that office.

During the later 1920s, Congress politics increasingly involved Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel, Subhas Chandra Bose, C. Rajagopalachari, Maulana Azad, and others whose aims did not always coincide with Gandhi’s.

He promoted the spinning wheel, khadi, village sanitation, Hindu-Muslim unity, and removal of untouchability. The charkha became both economic program and political sacrament. Critics argued that hand-spinning romanticized poverty and could not meet the requirements of a modern economy. Gandhi considered it decentralized supplementary employment and a discipline linking elites to rural labor.

### Independence resolution

At Lahore in December 1929, Congress adopted **purna swaraj**, complete independence, and marked 26 January 1930 as Independence Day.

### Salt March

On 12 March 1930 Gandhi left Sabarmati Ashram with seventy-eight selected marchers. After approximately 240 miles, he reached Dandi and broke the colonial salt law on 6 April.

The campaign spread through salt manufacture, boycott, tax resistance, and raids on salt facilities. Gandhi was arrested on 5 May and imprisoned at Yeravda until January 1931. At Dharasana, police beat nonviolent volunteers; Webb Miller’s reporting generated international attention.

### Gandhi-Irwin Pact

In March 1931 Gandhi and Viceroy Lord Irwin agreed to suspend civil disobedience. The government released many political prisoners and allowed limited salt manufacture; Congress entered the Second Round Table Conference.

Critics argued that the pact conceded too little and failed to secure commutation for Bhagat Singh, Shivaram Rajguru, and Sukhdev Thapar. **Documented fact:** Gandhi raised clemency with Irwin and sent a written appeal on 23 March 1931. **Documented fact:** he did not make commutation a condition for concluding the pact, and the executions proceeded. Claims that he affirmatively demanded their execution are unsupported; claims that he did everything institutionally possible are also interpretive rather than demonstrable.

### London and Europe

Gandhi attended the Second Round Table Conference in London as Congress’s principal representative. He stayed at Kingsley Hall with Muriel Lester, met workers in Lancashire, spoke with politicians, clergy, students, and intellectuals, and visited Romain Rolland in Switzerland.

In Rome on 12 December 1931, he met **Benito Mussolini**. Gandhi’s diary recorded the meeting only briefly. He expressed some admiration for Mussolini’s energy and physical-culture program while criticizing coercion and militarization. Later efforts to present him as either a fascist supporter or a prescient anti-fascist oversimplify a meeting marked by curiosity and political misjudgment.

## Civil Disobedience, Poona Pact, and Anti-Untouchability Work, 1932–1939

### Communal Award and fast

The British Communal Award proposed separate electorates for the “Depressed Classes.” Gandhi, imprisoned at Yeravda, began a fast unto death on 20 September 1932. He accepted reserved seats but opposed treating untouchable Hindus as a separate political community.

B. R. Ambedkar regarded separate electorates as protection against caste-Hindu domination. Under extraordinary pressure, negotiations produced the **Poona Pact** on 24 September: increased reserved seats for the Depressed Classes but election through a joint electorate, with a primary-election mechanism.

Evidence supports multiple simultaneous descriptions:

- **Documented fact:** Gandhi risked death to oppose separate electorates.
- **Documented fact:** Ambedkar negotiated under severe public and moral pressure.
- **Interpretation:** Gandhi saw the fast as defense of Hindu social unity.
- **Ambedkarite criticism:** it amounted to coercion that deprived Dalits of autonomous political representation.
- **Later political finding:** Ambedkar remained dissatisfied with joint electorates and continued to criticize Gandhi and Congress.

### Language and caste position

Gandhi popularized **Harijan**, “children of God,” for those then labeled untouchable. Many Dalit activists later rejected the term as paternalistic; “Dalit” or constitutional “Scheduled Castes” is generally preferred today.

His position on caste evolved but was inconsistent:

- In earlier writings he defended an idealized hereditary **varna** division while rejecting untouchability.
- He distinguished ideal varna from caste hierarchy and exclusion.
- By the 1930s–1940s he increasingly endorsed inter-caste dining and marriage and said caste as practiced should disappear.
- Ambedkar argued that untouchability could not be destroyed without annihilating the caste and scriptural structures supporting it.

Descriptions of Gandhi as simply a defender of caste or simply an anti-caste revolutionary each omit phases and contradictions in the record.

### Harijan campaign

After imprisonment and fasts in 1933, Gandhi toured India raising funds for work against untouchability and temple exclusion. He supported the Harijan Sevak Sangh. His campaign encountered orthodox-Hindu opposition and criticism from Dalit leaders who objected to caste-Hindu control of reform.

### Withdrawal and Sevagram

Gandhi formally withdrew from ordinary Congress membership in 1934, although his influence remained decisive. He transferred emphasis toward village industries, sanitation, basic education, and constructive work.

In 1936 he settled at Segaon near Wardha, renamed **Sevagram**. Jamnalal Bajaj provided land and substantial institutional support in the Wardha region.

### Bose conflict

Subhas Chandra Bose won the Congress presidency in 1938 and again in 1939 against Gandhi’s preferred candidate, Pattabhi Sitaramayya. Gandhi said Sitaramayya’s defeat was his own. The Working Committee’s resistance made Bose’s position untenable; he resigned and founded the Forward Bloc.

This was not a legal dismissal by Gandhi, but Gandhi’s moral and organizational power materially contributed to Bose’s isolation. Their conflict concerned strategy, socialism, leadership authority, and preparation to exploit a coming European war.

## Second World War and Quit India, 1939–1944

Gandhi opposed India’s involuntary inclusion in the Second World War. His position toward fascism and war was radical and controversial. He urged nonviolent resistance and sometimes advised populations facing invasion or persecution to refuse cooperation even at risk of death.

### Letters to Hitler

Gandhi wrote to Adolf Hitler in July 1939 and December 1940, addressing him as “Dear Friend” and appealing for peace. British authorities apparently intercepted at least one letter. The salutation reflected Gandhi’s customary universalist language, not evidence of friendship or collaboration.

### Jews and Nazi persecution

In 1938 Gandhi wrote that Jews had the strongest historical claim to employ satyagraha and suggested nonviolent resistance even against Nazi persecution. He opposed a Jewish national project imposed on Arab Palestine and wrote that Palestine belonged to the Arabs in the same sense that England belonged to the English.

Martin Buber and Judah Magnes challenged him, arguing that the scale and nature of Nazi violence made his South African analogy untenable. Gandhi’s advice is a **documented position**, widely criticized as impractical and morally inadequate; claims that it constituted support for Nazism are unsupported.

### Individual satyagraha

After failed negotiation with the Raj, Gandhi launched limited individual satyagraha in 1940. Vinoba Bhave was the first selected resister; Jawaharlal Nehru followed.

### Quit India

Following the failure of the Cripps Mission, Congress adopted the Quit India resolution in Bombay on 8 August 1942. Gandhi called on Indians to “Do or Die.”

British authorities arrested Gandhi and the Congress leadership on 9 August. Gandhi, Kasturba, Mahadev Desai, and associates were detained at the Aga Khan Palace near Poona.

Mahadev Desai died there on 15 August 1942. Kasturba died in detention on 22 February 1944 after chronic bronchial and cardiac illness. Gandhi was released on 6 May 1944 because of ill health and fear that his death in custody would have political consequences.

Quit India included strikes, sabotage, attacks on communications, parallel governments, police repression, shootings, imprisonment, and deaths. Gandhi denied directing violence but would not condemn participants in terms demanded by the Raj while he remained isolated from events.

## Partition, Independence, and Final Peace Missions, 1944–1948

### Gandhi-Jinnah talks

In September 1944 Gandhi met Muhammad Ali Jinnah in Bombay to seek agreement on Congress–Muslim League relations and possible self-determination. The talks failed. Gandhi rejected the two-nation theory; Jinnah argued that Congress could not represent Muslim nationhood.

### Cabinet Mission and partition

Gandhi preferred a united, decentralized India. Congress and League disputes over the 1946 Cabinet Mission Plan, interim government, and constitutional power-sharing contributed to collapse.

He did not possess unilateral authority to prevent partition. By June 1947, Patel, Nehru, and other Congress leaders accepted partition as the least disastrous available settlement. Gandhi did not lead a mass rebellion against their decision.

### Noakhali, Bihar, and Calcutta

Following communal killings in Noakhali in eastern Bengal in October 1946, Gandhi traveled village to village, beginning in November, preaching reconciliation and attempting to restore confidence among Hindu survivors. Critics charged that his remedies relied too heavily on moral conversion and insufficiently on security.

He intervened after anti-Muslim massacres in Bihar and threatened fasting unless violence ceased.

In August–September 1947, while independence ceremonies took place in Delhi, Gandhi stayed in Calcutta. After renewed violence, he began a fast on 1 September. Political and community leaders pledged peace, and he ended it on 4 September. The “Calcutta miracle” became a celebrated story; Gandhi’s presence and fast mattered, but peace also depended on local political networks, exhaustion, policing, and negotiations.

### Delhi fast and Pakistan payment

Partition produced perhaps 10–15 million displaced people and several hundred thousand to more than a million deaths; exact totals remain disputed. Delhi filled with refugees amid attacks on Muslims and seizure of mosques and homes.

Gandhi began his final fast on 13 January 1948, demanding restoration of communal peace, protection for Muslims, return of occupied religious sites, and freedom of movement. The Indian government also released ₹550 million—55 crore rupees—owed to Pakistan under partition arrangements after previously delaying payment amid the Kashmir war.

**Documented fact:** Gandhi supported payment because it was contractually due.  
**Contested causal claim:** Hindu-nationalist critics alleged the fast forced India to hand money to an enemy. The fast’s demands and the cabinet’s decision overlapped, but the money was not a personal gift or arbitrary concession invented by Gandhi. Evidence examined by the later Kapur Commission indicated that the assassination conspiracy predated this final fast and payment controversy.

Leaders signed a peace pledge, and Gandhi ended the fast on 18 January.

## Companies, Institutions, Projects, and Financial Interests

### Legal practice

Gandhi earned a comfortable professional income in South Africa, chiefly from Indian merchant clients. He gradually redirected income, gifts, and legal labor toward political and communal projects.

### Principal institutions

- Natal Indian Congress, founded 1894.
- *Indian Opinion*, launched 1903; Gandhi was its principal strategist, writer, fundraiser, and later financial supporter.
- Phoenix Settlement, founded 1904.
- Tolstoy Farm, established 1910 on land supplied by Hermann Kallenbach.
- Satyagraha/Kochrab Ashram, founded 1915.
- Sabarmati Ashram, occupied from 1917.
- Gujarat Vidyapith, founded in 1920 as part of national education.
- All India Spinners’ Association, established 1925.
- Harijan Sevak Sangh, established after the 1932 campaign.
- All India Village Industries Association, established 1934.
- Sevagram Ashram, from 1936.
- Navajivan Publishing House/Trust, custodian and publisher of many writings.

### Patrons and financial relationships

Gandhi’s austere personal image coexisted with reliance on wealthy supporters and business families, among them:

- Ambalal and Anasuya Sarabhai.
- Jamnalal Bajaj.
- Ghanshyamdas Birla.
- Pranjivan Mehta.
- Hermann Kallenbach.
- Ratanji and other Indian merchants in South Africa.

Industrialists funded ashrams, travel, publications, welfare programs, and Congress activities. Critics argued that this compromised his critique of capital. Gandhi answered with his doctrine of **trusteeship**: the wealthy should hold property for social benefit rather than be violently dispossessed.

He did not found a conventional profit-seeking business empire, hold public-company office, or accumulate a large documented personal fortune. Gifts received for public work were generally assigned to trusts or organizations. His symbolic possessions at death were few. That personal austerity does not mean his institutions were costless or financially autonomous.

### Property and copyright

Gandhi assigned rights in much of his writing to Navajivan Trust. The later *Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi* became a government-backed, 100-volume editorial project. The original standard English edition contains roughly 48,000 pages and tens of thousands of letters, articles, speeches, and interviews.

### Technologies and economic program

Gandhi invented no patented technology. His major technological symbol was the spinning wheel. He encouraged improvements in portable spinning devices and offered prizes for efficient designs. His larger program favored labor-intensive, decentralized tools over capital-intensive industrial systems.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Truth

Gandhi initially wrote “God is Truth” and later preferred “Truth is God,” allowing persons without conventional theism to share the ethical search. Truth was experimental: no person possessed it completely.

### Ahimsa

Ahimsa meant more than refraining from killing. It required reducing hatred, exploitation, humiliation, and retaliation. Gandhi nevertheless permitted exceptions in practice—destruction of dangerous animals, euthanizing a suffering calf, and participation in wartime medical corps—showing that his application was not absolute in a simple rule-based sense.

### Satyagraha

Satyagraha combined refusal to cooperate with injustice, willingness to accept punishment, and rejection of deliberate injury. It differed from passive resistance by claiming an active moral force. Mass satyagraha repeatedly encountered violence, indiscipline, strategic retreat, and disputes about who bore the burden of suffering.

### Swaraj

Swaraj meant national independence, local self-government, and mastery over appetite. Gandhi’s ideal polity emphasized village republics and limited centralized state power.

### Economics and trusteeship

He criticized consumerism, industrial concentration, exploitative machinery, and economic growth detached from human need. Trusteeship attempted to convert property owners morally rather than abolish private wealth through state coercion. Socialist critics regarded it as unrealistic and protective of capital.

### Religion and pluralism

Gandhi described himself as Hindu while saying Christianity, Islam, Jainism, Buddhism, and other religions contained truth. His prayer meetings included texts from several traditions. He rejected conversion pursued through material inducement or denigration of another faith.

His use of explicitly Hindu language—*Ram Rajya*, vows, fasting, bhajans, cow protection—could unite some audiences and estrange others. He defined Ram Rajya as just rule, not a Hindu theocracy.

### Gender

Gandhi recruited women into mass politics and argued against purdah, dowry, child marriage, and enforced widowhood. He often depicted women as especially capable of sacrifice and nonviolence.

His language could also be essentialist, idealizing chastity, endurance, and motherhood. His bodily experiments with women raised profound questions of authority and consent.

### Modern civilization

*Hind Swaraj* rejected the moral assumptions of industrial modernity, parliamentary representation, professional monopolies, and endless material desire. Gandhi later qualified some literal attacks on railways, doctors, and machinery but retained the civilizational critique.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Kasturba Gandhi

Spouse, opponent within the household, satyagrahi, political prisoner, and manager of communal life. Their relationship moved from adolescent patriarchy toward collaboration but never escaped Gandhi’s demanding moral authority.

### Gopal Krishna Gokhale

Political mentor. Gandhi called him his guide and followed his advice to tour India before taking leadership.

### Rabindranath Tagore

Friend and public interlocutor. Their debates concerned nationalism, non-cooperation, education, the spinning wheel, earthquake theology, and reason. Tagore helped establish “Mahatma” in public usage; Gandhi called him “Gurudev.”

### Jawaharlal Nehru

Close associate and political heir, but not a disciple in every respect. They differed over industrialization, socialism, the state, secular language, and village economy. Gandhi ultimately treated Nehru as central to postcolonial leadership.

### Vallabhbhai Patel

Organizer in Kheda and Bardoli, Congress strategist, fundraiser, and later deputy prime minister. Patel was often closer to Gandhi’s organizational methods than Nehru but differed over security and partition-era statecraft.

### Muhammad Ali Jinnah

Colleague in Indian public life who became Gandhi’s chief constitutional and political antagonist over Muslim representation and Pakistan. Their relationship involved negotiation, mutual formality, and strategic incompatibility rather than personal enmity alone.

### B. R. Ambedkar

Principal opponent on caste, untouchability, representation, and Hindu social structure. The Poona Pact joined them in a negotiated settlement but did not resolve their disagreement.

### Hermann Kallenbach

Friend, benefactor, communal-living partner, and correspondent. Their relationship was central to the South African period.

### Henry and Millie Polak

Friends and collaborators in South Africa. Henry Polak introduced Gandhi to Ruskin’s *Unto This Last*.

### Sonja Schlesin

Secretary and highly capable organizer in South Africa, especially during imprisonments.

### C. F. Andrews

Anglican priest, anticolonial ally, intermediary, and affectionate friend who could also criticize Gandhi.

### Mahadev Desai

Secretary, translator, editor, diarist, and administrative center of Gandhi’s Indian work from 1917 until his death in 1942.

### Pyarelal Nayar and Sushila Nayar

Secretary/biographer and physician/associate respectively. Both became important custodians of the documentary record.

### Mirabehn

Madeleine Slade, British disciple who joined the ashram, adopted the name Mirabehn, worked closely with Gandhi, and later pursued rural and ecological work.

### Sarojini Naidu

Congress leader, orator, correspondent, and satyagrahi who affectionately mocked Gandhi’s austerity and assumed leadership after his arrest during the salt campaign.

### Industrial patrons

Jamnalal Bajaj, G. D. Birla, Ambalal Sarabhai, and others supplied money, property, contacts, and logistical support while sometimes disagreeing with labor or economic campaigns.

### Political rivals and critics

Subhas Chandra Bose, Ambedkar, Jinnah, Winston Churchill, revolutionary nationalists, orthodox Hindu organizations, Hindu Mahasabha activists, imperial officials, and some socialists each opposed different aspects of Gandhi’s program.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Adverse Findings

## Racism and Black Africans

**Documented facts:**

- Gandhi’s early South African writings sometimes used “kaffir,” now recognized as a severe racial slur.
- He objected to Indians being classified or imprisoned with Black Africans.
- His early campaigns emphasized Indians’ civilizational status and imperial citizenship rather than universal racial equality.
- He supported British causes during the Boer War and Zulu rebellion in noncombatant capacities.

**Development and counterevidence:**

- His rhetoric changed over two decades.
- He later recognized the overriding significance of the African land question and predicted African resistance.
- His political program in South Africa remained centered on Indians; he did not become a leader of African liberation.

**Historical dispute:** Ashwin Desai and Goolam Vahed interpret Gandhi as an imperial loyalist who worked within and sometimes reproduced the racial and class order. Other scholars emphasize changes in his thought and later influence on African resistance. The offensive early record cannot be erased by later development, while projecting his mature universalist image backward is equally misleading.

Statues in Ghana, South Africa, Britain, and the United States have prompted removal campaigns. The University of Ghana statue was removed in 2018 after protests centered on these writings.

## Caste and the Poona Pact

**Documented fact:** Gandhi fought untouchability yet for years defended an idealized varna scheme and opposed separate electorates for Dalits.

**Ambedkar’s documented criticism:** Gandhi’s social reform preserved Hindu paternal authority and the Poona Pact weakened autonomous Dalit representation.

**Disputed interpretation:** Whether Gandhi’s views underwent a fundamental anti-caste transformation or remained bounded by a reformed Hindu social order. The answer varies by date and by whether statements or institutional consequences receive greater weight.

## Celibacy experiments and women

After taking a celibacy vow in 1906, Gandhi progressively tested control over sexual desire. Practices included close physical proximity to women and, in the 1940s, sleeping naked or nearly naked beside young female associates, most controversially his grandniece **Manu Gandhi**, then in her late teens.

**Documented fact:** The experiments occurred and were discussed in correspondence, diaries, and testimony.

**Documented fact:** Several close associates—including Nirmal Kumar Bose, Vallabhbhai Patel, Devdas Gandhi, and others—objected or distanced themselves.

**Reported fact:** Manu expressed willingness and interpreted service to Gandhi within the devotional ashram relationship.

**Unresolved ethical issue:** Consent cannot be assessed as though the participants were social equals. Gandhi was an elderly world leader, guardian, religious authority, and controller of the ashram environment. No documentary evidence establishes intercourse, but absence of intercourse does not resolve questions of pressure, power, privacy, or psychological effect.

Descriptions of these acts as proven rape or a proven sexual affair exceed the available evidence. Describing them merely as innocent sleeping arrangements minimizes the documented purpose and unequal authority.

## Treatment of family

Gandhi required relatives to accept sacrifices that served his public ethics. Harilal’s education dispute, Kasturba’s early domestic resistance, and strict regulation of sons and ashram residents have supported accusations of emotional authoritarianism.

The evidence establishes painful conflict and Gandhi’s prioritization of public vocation over conventional family responsibility. Claims assigning all of Harilal’s later illness and alcoholism to Gandhi are causal interpretations, not established findings.

## Fasting as coercion

Gandhi described fasting as self-purification and appeal to conscience. Critics argued that when performed by a leader of immense authority, fasting threatened followers or opponents with responsibility for his death. The 1932 fast against separate electorates is the strongest case for this criticism because of the pressure placed on Ambedkar and Dalit negotiators.

## Bhagat Singh

Gandhi opposed the revolutionaries’ use of violence but appealed to Irwin for commutation. He did not make clemency a condition of the Gandhi-Irwin Pact.

- “Gandhi ordered or desired the execution” — **unsupported allegation**.
- “Gandhi made an appeal” — **documented fact**.
- “Gandhi exhausted every possible means” — **political interpretation**, disputed by critics.

## War, cowardice, and nonviolence

Gandhi supported ambulance corps in two South African conflicts and recruitment in 1918. He sometimes said violence was preferable to cowardice, though nonviolence was superior to both. These positions complicate a mythology of invariant pacifism.

His advice that Jews and invaded peoples adopt nonviolent resistance was criticized for underestimating exterminatory regimes. No evidence establishes sympathy with Nazi racial ideology.

## Partition

Competing accusations hold that Gandhi caused partition by conceding too much to the Muslim League, or enabled Hindu domination by conceding too little.

**Documented record:** he opposed the two-nation theory and partition, negotiated unsuccessfully with Jinnah, proposed extraordinary compromises, and eventually declined to rebel against the Congress leadership’s acceptance. No single individual controlled the British government, Congress, Muslim League, provincial politics, wartime transformation, or communal violence.

## Sexual-orientation claims

Publicity surrounding Lelyveld’s *Great Soul* produced claims that Gandhi had a homosexual affair with Kallenbach.

**Documented fact:** the surviving language is emotionally intimate and their partnership unusually close.  
**Unverified claim:** a sexual relationship.  
**Historical significance:** the controversy reveals changing readings of male intimacy and efforts by both admirers and detractors to police Gandhi’s symbolic identity.

## Finances and industrial patrons

No credible evidence examined establishes personal embezzlement, fraud, money laundering, or secret enrichment. Criticism focuses instead on structural dependence on industrialists and whether trusteeship insulated wealth from redistribution.

The popular joke that it cost a great deal of money to keep Gandhi in poverty, often attributed to Sarojini Naidu, captures the logistical cost of travel, publication, staff, and public security. Exact wording and occasion vary; it should not be treated as audited financial evidence.

## Assassination and conspiracy

Nathuram Godse shot Gandhi on 30 January 1948. Godse and Narayan Apte were convicted and executed; other conspirators received sentences. Vinayak Damodar Savarkar was prosecuted but acquitted because the trial court found the evidence legally insufficient, particularly without adequate corroboration of the approver.

The later **Kapur Commission**, using testimony unavailable at trial, concluded that the accumulated evidence was destructive of theories other than a conspiracy involving Savarkar and his group.

Correct labels therefore are:

- Savarkar’s acquittal — **court finding at the criminal trial**.
- Kapur Commission conclusion — **later official inquiry finding**, not a criminal conviction.
- Claim that the RSS as an organization was judicially convicted of ordering Gandhi’s murder — **false**.
- Claim that Godse acted wholly alone — contradicted by the conspiracy convictions and evidence.
- Claim that the ₹55-crore issue originated the plot — inconsistent with evidence of earlier planning.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

### “An eye for an eye makes the whole world blind”

The aphorism is widely attributed to Gandhi. No secure primary Gandhi source has been established for the familiar wording. Its association was strengthened by later biographical and dramatic treatments, including the 1982 film.

**Label:** disputed attribution.

### “Be the change you wish to see in the world”

This exact formulation is not securely found in Gandhi’s writings. It paraphrases a broader ethical argument about self-reform and social change.

**Label:** modern paraphrase falsely presented as a verbatim quotation.

### “First they ignore you…”

The statement ending “then you win” is frequently attributed to Gandhi but lacks a reliable source in his corpus and resembles language from American labor politics.

**Label:** unsupported attribution.

### Gandhi gave India independence single-handedly

This hagiographic narrative excludes mass movements, workers, peasants, revolutionaries, constitutional politics, the Muslim League, princely-state politics, Subhas Bose and the INA, wartime exhaustion, international change, and military unrest.

**Label:** national founding myth based on a real but non-exclusive leadership role.

### The Pietermaritzburg epiphany

The expulsion is historically supported. The claim that Gandhi instantly conceived his complete doctrine that night is retrospective compression.

**Label:** documented event; mythologized transformation narrative.

### Tagore uniquely bestowed “Mahatma”

Tagore unquestionably popularized the title and used it in their relationship. Evidence of an earlier January 1915 use associated with Nautamlal Mehta complicates the exclusive claim.

**Label:** partially true conventional story.

### Gandhi and Hitler were friends

Gandhi addressed Hitler as “Dear Friend” in appeals against war. They did not meet, and no reciprocal friendship is documented.

**Label:** hostile internet myth built from authentic letters.

### Gandhi told Hindus simply to let Muslims kill them

Gandhi made extreme statements about refusing retaliatory killing and accepting death. Extracts circulate without the contexts of communal peace missions, reciprocal appeals, and his efforts to protect both Hindus and Muslims.

**Label:** authentic underlying rhetoric often converted into misleading propaganda by selective quotation.

### Gandhi forced the ₹55-crore payment to Pakistan solely through his fast

The money was part of the agreed partition settlement. Gandhi supported payment, and the cabinet released it during his fast, but the fast addressed a broader communal settlement.

**Label:** contested causal simplification.

### Gandhi had a secret sexual affair with Kallenbach

Affectionate correspondence and intense companionship are documented; sexual conduct is not.

**Label:** unresolved speculation, not established fact.

### Churchill called Gandhi a “half-naked fakir”

Churchill used the phrase “seditious Middle Temple lawyer, now posing as a fakir of a type well known in the East, striding half-naked…” in a hostile 1931 speech. Popular versions shorten it.

**Label:** substantially authentic hostile rhetoric, often paraphrased.

### “Father of the Nation” is Gandhi’s official constitutional title

The phrase is widely used, and Subhas Bose addressed Gandhi as “Father of our Nation” in a 1944 broadcast. India has no constitutional office or formally conferred state title by that name.

**Label:** established honorific; myth when described as a legal title.

### Nobel Peace Prize denial due to British pressure

Gandhi was nominated repeatedly, including in 1937, 1938, 1939, 1947, and 1948. Nobel archival discussion reveals reservations about his nationalism, consistency, and the India-Pakistan conflict. There is no archival proof that fear of Britain determined the decisions. The committee awarded no prize in 1948, stating that no suitable living candidate existed, though its precise reasoning remains debated.

**Label:** repeated nomination documented; British-veto narrative unverified.

## Health, Illnesses, Injuries, and Fasts

Gandhi’s health history included:

- Pleurisy while in England during the First World War.
- Severe illness during the 1918 recruitment campaign, with dysentery-like symptoms.
- Appendicitis and surgery in January 1924.
- Recurrent malaria; he was seriously ill during his final detention.
- High blood pressure and declining weight in later life.
- Injuries from the 1897 Durban mob assault and 1908 attack by Mir Alam Khan.
- Numerous political and penitential fasts, ranging from several days to twenty-one days.

His dietary experiments included vegetarianism, fruit and nut regimens, periods without milk, later use of goat’s milk after illness, salt and grain restrictions, and enemas and nature-cure practices. He opposed aspects of conventional medicine but accepted surgery and medical treatment when necessary.

There is no persuasive evidence of alcohol or narcotic addiction. His youthful smoking experiment was brief. His own writings repeatedly describe anxiety over sexuality, food, anger, and moral failure but do not support a retrospective clinical diagnosis.

## Awards, Honors, and Offices

- Called to the Bar, Inner Temple, 1891.
- Queen’s South Africa Medal for Boer War ambulance service.
- Kaiser-i-Hind Medal for public service; returned in 1920.
- President of the Indian National Congress, Belgaum session, 1924.
- *Time* magazine Man of the Year for 1930.
- Repeated Nobel Peace Prize nominee; never laureate.
- “Mahatma” and “Bapu,” popular honorifics rather than government decorations.
- India’s annual national observance **Gandhi Jayanti**, 2 October.
- The United Nations recognizes 2 October as the International Day of Non-Violence.
- His portrait appears on Indian currency, and roads, institutions, statues, and public programs worldwide bear his name.

He held no ministerial office in independent India and did not participate in its government.

## Death

On 20 January 1948, Madanlal Pahwa and fellow conspirators attempted an attack at Gandhi’s Birla House prayer meeting; a bomb exploded without injuring him. Gandhi resisted intrusive security measures.

On 30 January, delayed by conversation with Vallabhbhai Patel, Gandhi walked toward the evening prayer ground supported by Manu and Abha Gandhi. At approximately 5:17 p.m., Nathuram Godse approached, bowed or pushed through the crowd, and fired three shots at close range from a Beretta pistol.

Accounts differ over whether Gandhi audibly said “Hey Ram.” Manu and Abha supplied recollections, but the immediate evidence is not conclusive.

He was carried inside Birla House and pronounced dead. A vast funeral procession accompanied his body to the cremation ground on 31 January. Ramdas Gandhi lit the pyre.

Godse and Apte were hanged on 15 November 1949. Gandhi’s sons appealed against execution in keeping with their father’s opposition to vengeance, but the sentences were carried out.

## Works by Gandhi

### Authorial and editorial caution

Gandhi wrote in Gujarati and English, dictated extensively, translated or supervised translations, and published serially. Titles often exist in several editions. Many familiar “books by Gandhi” are posthumous selections assembled by editors and should not be confused with books he personally structured.

### Principal genuine works

1. **The Vegetarian** essays and London Vegetarian Society writings, 1890–1891. Early journalism; later collected.
2. **An Appeal to Every Briton in South Africa** (1895). Political pamphlet.
3. **The Indian Franchise: An Appeal** (1895).
4. **The Grievances of the British Indians in South Africa**, commonly the **Green Pamphlet** (1896).
5. **Sarvodaya**, Gujarati paraphrase of John Ruskin’s *Unto This Last*, serialized in 1908; later translated back into English as *Unto This Last: A Paraphrase*.
6. **Hind Swaraj or Indian Home Rule** (*Hind Swaraj*), Gujarati, serialized December 1909; English translation by Gandhi, 1910.
7. **Neeti Dharma / Ethical Religion**, adapted from William Salter; serialized in Gujarati and later published in book form.
8. **A Guide to Health** (*Arogyani Chavi* and precursor health writings), developed from articles beginning in South Africa; revised editions culminated in *Key to Health*.
9. **Satyagraha in South Africa** (*Dakshin Africana Satyagrahano Itihas*), serialized in Gujarati in the 1920s; English translation by Valji Govindji Desai, 1928.
10. **The Story of My Experiments with Truth** (*Satyana Prayogo athva Atmakatha*), serialized in *Navajivan*, 1925–1929; English translation by Mahadev Desai, issued in volumes in 1927 and 1929. Covers life only through approximately 1921.
11. **Anasaktiyoga**, Gandhi’s Gujarati translation and interpretation of the Bhagavad Gita, published 1930; English versions include *The Gospel of Selfless Action* and *The Gita According to Gandhi*.
12. **From Yeravda Mandir / Ashram Observances in Action**, writings on ashram vows composed during imprisonment, first published in the early 1930s.
13. **Songs from Prison**, translations and reflections produced during imprisonment, 1934.
14. **Constructive Programme: Its Meaning and Place**, first edition 1941; revised 1945.
15. **Key to Health**, 1942.
16. **Delhi Diary**, covering Gandhi’s final months; published posthumously in 1948 from prayer speeches and records.
17. **Calcutta Diary**, posthumous compilation concerning the 1947 peace mission.
18. **Noakhali writings and diary materials**, principally preserved through collected speeches, correspondence, and associates’ diaries rather than one definitive author-prepared book.

### Newspapers and journals edited or directed

- *Indian Opinion*, 1903 onward.
- *Young India*, Gandhi’s major English weekly platform, 1919–1931.
- *Navajivan*, Gujarati; Hindi counterpart.
- *Harijan*, English, 1933–1948 with interruptions.
- *Harijanbandhu*, Gujarati.
- *Harijan Sevak*, Hindi.

Thousands of unsigned or editorial pieces require consultation of publication records and the *Collected Works* for attribution.

### Major speeches, statements, and manifestos

- 1906 Johannesburg pledge against Asian registration.
- 1922 “Great Trial” statement.
- 1930 letter to Viceroy Irwin before the Salt March.
- 1931 Federal Structure Committee speech at the Round Table Conference.
- 1942 Quit India speech, including “Do or Die.”
- Repeated All-India Congress Committee interventions.
- Prayer-meeting addresses, especially Noakhali, Calcutta, and Delhi, 1946–1948.
- Letters to Hitler, 1939 and 1940.
- Extensive correspondence with Tolstoy, Tagore, Nehru, Patel, Jinnah, Ambedkar, Andrews, Kallenbach, Mirabehn, Einstein, Roosevelt, British officials, and family members.

### Authoritative collected corpus

**The Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi**, Government of India, original standard edition:

- Volumes 1–90: principal chronological texts.
- Volumes 91–97: supplementary material.
- Volumes 98–99: cumulative indexes.
- Volume 100: prefaces and editorial material.

The project collected approximately 33,000 Gandhi letters and tens of thousands of articles, speeches, telegrams, interviews, and notes. A defective revised edition issued around 2001 was withdrawn; researchers should use the restored original standard edition.

### Important posthumous selections—not author-prepared books

- *All Men Are Brothers*, edited by Krishna Kripalani.
- *The Mind of Mahatma Gandhi*, compiled by R. K. Prabhu and U. R. Rao.
- *The Essential Gandhi*, edited by Louis Fischer.
- *Non-Violence in Peace and War*, compiled from writings.
- *Truth Is God*, selections.
- *My Religion*, selections.
- *Selected Letters* and numerous subject anthologies.
- *The Power of Nonviolent Resistance*, modern edited selection by Tridip Suhrud.

Quotations in such books should be checked against the original periodical or *Collected Works*.

### Major films

- *Mahatma: Life of Gandhi, 1869–1948* (1968), directed by Vithalbhai Jhaveri. Extensive documentary using newsreel footage, photographs, animation, and Gandhi’s words.
- *Nine Hours to Rama* (1963), directed by Mark Robson, based on Stanley Wolpert’s novel; fictionalized account centered on Godse. The novel and film encountered censorship in India.
- *Gandhi* (1982), directed by Richard Attenborough, with Ben Kingsley. Won eight Academy Awards, including Best Picture, Director, and Actor—not nine, as some summaries state. It established the dominant global screen image but compresses chronology and simplifies conflicts.
- *The Making of the Mahatma* (1996), directed by Shyam Benegal, based on Fatima Meer’s work; focuses on South Africa.
- *Hey Ram* (2000), directed by Kamal Haasan; fictional narrative about communal violence and a plot against Gandhi.
- *Dr. Babasaheb Ambedkar* (2000), directed by Jabbar Patel; includes the Gandhi-Ambedkar confrontation.
- *Gandhi, My Father* (2007), directed by Feroz Abbas Khan; centers on Harilal and the costs of Gandhi’s public vocation.
- *Maine Gandhi Ko Nahin Mara* (2005), directed by Jahnu Barua; uses Gandhi’s assassination as memory and guilt rather than direct biography.
- *Lage Raho Munna Bhai* (2006), directed by Rajkumar Hirani; popularized “Gandhigiri,” a comic modern adaptation of nonviolence.
- *Road to Sangam* (2009), directed by Amit Rai; fictional story involving Gandhi’s ashes.
- *Welcome Back Gandhi* (2012), directed by A. Balakrishnan; speculative return narrative.
- *Gandhi Godse – Ek Yudh* (2023), directed by Rajkumar Santoshi; counterfactual debate between Gandhi and Godse.

### Television and radio

- *Bharat Ek Khoj* episodes on the nationalist movement.
- *Lord Mountbatten: The Last Viceroy* (1986), with Gandhi portrayed by Sam Dastor.
- Numerous BBC, All India Radio, Doordarshan, and international documentary programs preserve interviews with associates and historians.

### Archival repositories

- Gandhi Heritage Portal: digitized *Collected Works*, key texts, journals, chronologies, photographs, and family trees.
- Sabarmati Ashram Preservation and Memorial Trust.
- National Gandhi Museum, New Delhi.
- Gandhi Smriti and Darshan Samiti, including the Birla House assassination site.
- Nehru Memorial Museum and Library/Prime Ministers’ Museum and Library.
- National Archives of India.
- British Library India Office Records.
- South African archival repositories.
- Mani Bhavan Gandhi Sangrahalaya.
- University and private collections holding Kallenbach, Polak, Andrews, Tagore, and Einstein correspondence.
- Newsreel archives preserving the Round Table Conference, wartime statements, final peace missions, funeral, and later commemorations.

No complete audio-visual record of Gandhi exists. Surviving speech recordings are comparatively few; many apparent “Gandhi quotations” in modern videos are narration or dramatic reconstruction.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Gandhi’s assassination immediately intensified his status as a martyr to interreligious peace. The Indian state adopted his image while pursuing centralized planning, industrialization, armed defense, and parliamentary government inconsistent with large portions of *Hind Swaraj*.

### Political influence

Documented or explicitly acknowledged influence appears in:

- Martin Luther King Jr. and the US civil-rights movement.
- Nelson Mandela and South African liberation thought, though the armed turn of the ANC differed from Gandhi.
- Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan’s Khudai Khidmatgar movement.
- César Chávez and farmworker organizing.
- James Lawson and nonviolent direct-action training.
- The Dalai Lama.
- Václav Havel and some East European dissident movements.
- Environmental, village-development, anti-nuclear, and peace movements.

Influence rarely meant exact imitation. Activists adapted satyagraha to distinct legal, racial, labor, and revolutionary settings.

### Indian political memory

Competing traditions appropriate him as:

- Congress nationalist founder.
- Hindu religious reformer.
- Secular interfaith icon.
- Village socialist or anarchist.
- Defender of private-property trusteeship.
- Anti-industrial ecological thinker.
- Conservative opponent of revolutionary and Dalit autonomy.
- Symbol of state ritual emptied of political challenge.

Hindu-nationalist narratives range from formal official reverence to celebration or mitigation of Godse. Dalit critiques foreground Ambedkar and the Poona Pact. African and Black-diaspora critiques foreground Gandhi’s early racial language. Feminist and sexuality scholarship has transformed interpretation of the ashram and celibacy experiments.

### Material commemoration

Gandhi’s face on currency, statues, museums, road names, postage stamps, educational syllabi, and United Nations observances created a global icon. The most reproduced visual attributes—round spectacles, shawl or loincloth, sandals, staff, and spinning wheel—were products of a deliberate political aesthetics of renunciation.

### Reinterpretation

The historiography has moved through overlapping phases:

1. Disciple memoir and hagiography.
2. Nationalist state commemoration.
3. Political and institutional biography.
4. Psychological interpretation.
5. Subaltern, Ambedkarite, feminist, African, and postcolonial critique.
6. Renewed archival biography integrating family, emotion, print networks, caste, race, ecology, and global reception.

No single phase has displaced the others.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the reviewed sources or a well-established documentary record are included.

- **Albert Einstein — documented fact.** Einstein and Gandhi exchanged correspondence beginning in 1931 through intermediaries. Gandhi wrote to Einstein from London on 18 October 1931. Einstein praised Gandhi’s nonviolent political method. They did not meet.

- **Annie Besant — documented fact.** Gandhi encountered Besant through Indian nationalist, Theosophical, educational, and Home Rule politics. They shared Congress platforms but differed over Gandhi’s mass non-cooperation strategy and political methods.

- **Benito Mussolini — documented fact.** Gandhi met Mussolini in Rome on 12 December 1931 after the Round Table Conference. Gandhi recorded the appointment tersely and later commented on Mussolini’s energy, physical-culture projects, and authoritarian environment.

- **Herbert George Wells — documented fact.** Gandhi corresponded publicly with H. G. Wells in the 1940s concerning declarations of human rights and duties. Gandhi argued that rights flowed from duties.

- **Jesus of Nazareth — documented intellectual influence.** Gandhi repeatedly identified the Sermon on the Mount, especially love of enemies and non-retaliation, as formative. This is influence through scripture, not a biographical meeting.

- **Jiddu Krishnamurti — reported fact.** Both moved in overlapping Theosophical and Indian reform networks, and published records report meetings and mutual awareness. The connection was not central to Gandhi’s politics and should not be inflated into a teacher-student relationship.

- **John Ruskin — documented intellectual influence.** Gandhi read *Unto This Last* in South Africa in 1904, credited it with transforming his life, translated/paraphrased it as *Sarvodaya*, and used its themes in founding Phoenix Settlement. They never met; Ruskin died in 1900.

- **Maria Montessori — documented fact.** Gandhi met Montessori in London in October 1931 and addressed the Montessori Training College. They discussed children, education, freedom, and peace.

- **Martin Buber — documented correspondence and dispute.** Buber publicly challenged Gandhi’s 1938 advice to European Jews and his position on Palestine. Their connection was intellectual and epistolary; no meeting is established here.

- **Nelson Rolihlahla Mandela — documented influence.** Mandela and other South African leaders discussed Gandhi’s South African campaigns as part of the genealogy of resistance. Mandela honored Gandhi’s influence while explaining why the ANC later adopted armed struggle. They could not have met: Mandela entered national political prominence after Gandhi left South Africa and was thirty when Gandhi died.

- **Rabindranath Tagore — documented friendship and debate.** They met, corresponded, and publicly debated nationalism, non-cooperation, education, spinning, reason, and religion from 1915 until Tagore’s death in 1941. Tagore popularized “Mahatma”; Gandhi called him “Gurudev.”

- **Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov (Lenin) — reported intellectual-political comparison, not a documented personal connection.** Gandhi and Lenin became rival symbols of revolutionary transformation in twentieth-century debate. No meeting or correspondence between them was found. Mere contemporaneity is not counted as a personal connection.

- **George Orwell — documented posthumous critical reception, not a personal relationship.** Orwell’s 1949 essay “Reflections on Gandhi” analyzed Gandhi’s sainthood, nonviolence, family renunciation, and political realism. Gandhi had died; no correspondence or meeting was found.

- **Abraham Lincoln — reported intellectual reference only.** Gandhi invoked Lincoln in discussions of freedom and political courage, but no personal connection was possible. The documentary evidence reviewed does not justify describing Lincoln as a principal formative influence.

- **Francis of Assisi — symbolic comparison, not demonstrated direct influence.** Later writers frequently compare Gandhi’s poverty and nonviolence with Franciscan practice. No strong evidence reviewed establishes Francis as a major explicitly acknowledged formative source.

No supported direct connection was found with the great majority of the 896-person roster. Shared nationality, era, geography, philosophical resemblance, or later comparison was not treated as a connection without documentary evidence.
