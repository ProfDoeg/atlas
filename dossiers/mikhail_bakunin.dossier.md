# Mikhail Aleksandrovich Bakunin: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Conventions

This dossier reconstructs the life of Mikhail Aleksandrovich Bakunin from published writings, correspondence, archival descriptions, contemporaneous records, documentary editions, and historical scholarship. Dates before Russia adopted the Gregorian calendar are sometimes given in both Old Style and New Style.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a primary document, archival record, contemporaneous official record, or strong agreement among specialist sources.
- **Reported fact:** stated in credible historical scholarship but not independently confirmed here from the original record.
- **Allegation:** a claim made by an identifiable person or institution without adequate proof.
- **Disputed attribution:** authorship or responsibility remains contested.
- **Rumor or myth:** a circulating story for which evidence is absent, contradictory, or demonstrably defective.
- **Interpretation:** a scholar’s explanation rather than a directly provable event.

## Family and Ancestry

### The Bakunin family

**Documented fact:** Bakunin belonged to an established but not princely Russian noble family. The family’s securely documented history reaches the seventeenth century.

**Family legend:** later genealogies claimed descent from the Hungarian-Transylvanian Báthory dynasty, through a supposed Zenislav Bakunin who entered the service of Ivan III in 1492. The documentary line is much later, beginning with Nikifor Bakunin in the seventeenth century. The Báthory origin should therefore be treated as noble-family mythology, not established ancestry.

Pryamukhino, established as a family seat by Mikhail Vasilyevich Bakunin, was both a working landed estate and an unusually cultivated household. At the time of Mikhail’s youth, the estate included hundreds of serfs; commonly repeated estimates place the number at approximately 500. That material setting—an educated family sustained by coerced peasant labor—formed an important contradiction in his development.

### Parents

**Father: Aleksandr Mikhailovich Bakunin** (1768–1854)

- Educated partly in Italy.
- Served in the Russian diplomatic service, including assignments in Florence and Naples.
- Studied philosophy and natural science and reportedly completed a philosophical dissertation.
- Returned to Russia after the French Revolution had radicalized European politics.
- Managed Pryamukhino, served as marshal of the local nobility, and attempted agricultural and educational improvements.
- Favored a Rousseau-influenced domestic education but remained a paternalist landowner and defender of the family’s social position.
- Became progressively blind.
- Married at approximately forty to a woman much younger than himself.

**Mother: Varvara Aleksandrovna Muravyeva** (1792–1864)

- Came from the prominent Muravyev family.
- Was related to Nikolai Nikolayevich Muravyev-Amursky, later governor-general of Eastern Siberia.
- Had eleven children between 1811 and 1824.
- Was less intellectually dominant in surviving family recollections than her husband but taught music and participated in the children’s education.

### Siblings

Sources differ in casual summaries between nine, ten, and eleven children because one or more children died very young. The fullest family reconstruction gives eleven births. The principal surviving siblings included:

- **Varvara Aleksandrovna Bakunina**
- **Lyubov Aleksandrovna Bakunina** (died young in 1838), poetically inclined and engaged to Nikolai Stankevich
- **Tatyana Aleksandrovna Bakunina**, emotionally and intellectually close to Mikhail; connected with Ivan Turgenev
- **Aleksandra Aleksandrovna Bakunina**
- **Nikolai Aleksandrovich Bakunin**
- **Pavel Aleksandrovich Bakunin**, later a philosopher and writer
- **Aleksandr Aleksandrovich Bakunin**
- **Aleksei Aleksandrovich Bakunin**
- A younger child, **Sofia**, who died in infancy
- At least one additional sibling whose inclusion varies with the genealogy used

The older sisters’ resistance to marriages selected for them became an early arena for Mikhail’s rebellion against patriarchal authority. He encouraged their intellectual independence, but his own interventions could also be controlling and emotionally overwhelming. His intimate family correspondence reveals intense attachments, possessiveness, moral exhortation, and an expectation that siblings subordinate conventional life to a shared spiritual vocation.

### Marriage

**Wife:** Antonina/Antonia Ksaveryevna Kwiatkowska, also Kwiatkowskaya, Kviatkovskaya, or Kwjatkowska (approximately 1840 or 1841–1887).

- Daughter of Ksawery Kwiatkowski, an impoverished Polish or Belarusian-Polish nobleman employed in Siberian gold-mining enterprises, and Julia Kwiatkowska.
- Bakunin met the family in Tomsk while teaching French to Antonina and her sister.
- The marriage took place in Tomsk on 5 October Old Style / 17 October New Style 1858. The parish extract described Antonina as seventeen.
- The age difference was about twenty-six years.
- Bakunin initially wrote that they were passionately attached and that Antonina shared his Slavic aspirations. Later evidence suggests that she had little sustained interest in his political work.
- His 1861 escape separated them. They reunited in Stockholm in April 1863.
- Their marriage continued legally until his death.

### Children, legal paternity, and biological paternity

Antonina bore three children during Bakunin’s lifetime:

- **Carlo/Karl Bakunin**, born in Geneva in 1868; later a mining engineer who emigrated to Argentina.
- **Sofia Bakunin** (1870–1956), later wife of surgeon Giuseppe Caccioppoli and mother of mathematician Renato Caccioppoli.
- **Maria “Marussia” Bakunin** (1873–1960), later a prominent chemist at Naples.

**Documented legal status:** the children bore Bakunin’s surname and were accepted by him as members of his household.

**Strongly supported biological attribution:** their biological father was the Neapolitan lawyer and revolutionary associate **Carlo Gambuzzi** (1837–1902), Antonina’s partner. Bakunin referred in correspondence to Gambuzzi as Antonina’s “effective husband.” Antonina married Gambuzzi in 1879, after Bakunin’s death.

This arrangement was not exposed through a legal scandal. Bakunin appears to have known of and accepted it, continued supporting Antonina and the children, and did not publicly contest their status. Modern descriptions calling all three Bakunin’s biological children are genealogically misleading; descriptions calling him wholly unrelated to them ignore his legal and social paternity. The surviving family documents are discussed in the [Bakunin Almanach material on Antonina](https://www.bakunin.de/sammelbaende/almanach_1.html) and the study of the [Bakunin papers in Amsterdam](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/359379039_Les_papiers_de_Michel_Bakounine_a_Amsterdam).

## Childhood and Early Life, 1814–1828

Bakunin grew up at Pryamukhino in a large, close-knit household. His father designed an educational environment inspired in part by Rousseau: languages, literature, history, philosophy, mathematics, science, and music were taught at home. The children staged plays, read poetry, and participated in intensive family discussions.

Bakunin later remembered this childhood as a protected idyll. It was nevertheless made possible by the labor of serfs. There is no evidence that the young Bakunin led a practical campaign to emancipate the family’s serfs. Awareness of the disparity between noble cultivation and peasant subjection developed gradually and later became central to his politics.

His early letters display:

- religious and romantic idealism;
- a desire for total emotional and intellectual communion;
- impatience with conventional careers and marriages;
- fascination with heroic self-sacrifice;
- a tendency to turn personal relationships into philosophical missions.

These traits remained visible after his conversion from metaphysical idealism to materialist anarchism.

## Military Education and Service, 1828–1835

### Artillery School

In 1828 Bakunin was sent to St. Petersburg to prepare for military training. He entered the Mikhailovsky Artillery School in 1829.

His education included mathematics, fortification, artillery science, drill, and military discipline. He disliked the hierarchical culture, performed unevenly, accumulated debts, and preferred metropolitan social life and private reading.

### Officer service

He was commissioned as a junior artillery officer in 1833. After poor performance and conflicts with military expectations, he was assigned to units in the western borderlands, including Minsk and Grodno, in territories recently shaken by the Polish uprising of 1830–1831.

The encounter with Russian imperial rule in Poland and Lithuania contributed to his later sympathy for Polish independence, though relations with Polish nationalists would remain difficult. He objected to Polish claims over Ukrainian, Belarusian, and Lithuanian territories and demanded a federation of free Slavic peoples rather than restoration of the Polish Commonwealth within its historic frontiers.

Bakunin resigned from the army in 1835, despite family pressure and the danger of leaving the conventional career assigned to a noble son.

## Education and Intellectual Formation, 1835–1842

### Moscow philosophical circles

Bakunin moved into Moscow intellectual life and became associated with:

- Nikolai Stankevich;
- Vissarion Belinsky;
- Aleksandr Herzen;
- Nikolai Ogarev;
- Vasily Botkin;
- Ivan Turgenev;
- the Slavophile and Westernizer circles that were beginning to divide Russian intellectual culture.

He never completed a regular university degree. His education was largely self-directed and conducted through discussion circles, private study, translation, and later attendance at lectures in Berlin.

### German idealism

His early progression was approximately:

1. **Kant and Schelling:** moral autonomy, inward freedom, and romantic reconciliation.
2. **Fichte:** vocation, action, and the self’s moral development.
3. **Hegel:** historical dialectic, reconciliation of reality and reason, and a totalizing conception of historical development.
4. **Young Hegelianism and Feuerbach:** transformation of dialectic into criticism of religion, state, and established society.

In 1836 he translated Fichte’s lectures on the vocation of the scholar into Russian. In 1838 he published a translation and extensive preface to Hegel’s Gymnasium Lectures. His Hegelianism at this stage was conservative: he treated reconciliation with reality as spiritual liberation.

### Family and interpersonal crises

Bakunin’s attempts to guide his sisters’ marriages and spiritual lives produced conflict with his father. His relationship with Belinsky also ruptured temporarily amid disputes involving the Bakunin sisters. The surviving episode is important because it complicates the later image of Bakunin as simply libertarian in personal conduct: in youth he could be domineering, absolutist, and intrusive.

### Berlin and Dresden

In June 1840, financially assisted by Herzen, Bakunin left for Berlin, nominally to prepare for a professorship in philosophy at Moscow University. He attended lectures, including those of Friedrich Schelling, but abandoned an academic career.

By 1842 he had moved toward the Young Hegelian left. In Dresden he collaborated with Arnold Ruge’s *Deutsche Jahrbücher*. Under the pseudonym Jules Elysard, he published “Die Reaction in Deutschland,” ending with the famous formulation usually translated as: “The passion for destruction is also a creative passion.” The exact standard English wording varies because the original speaks of the “Lust der Zerstörung.”

The article made him a recognizable revolutionary intellectual.

## Chronological Life History

## Switzerland, Brussels, and Paris, 1843–1847

In early 1843 Bakunin went to Zürich, where he met the communist tailor and organizer Wilhelm Weitling. Swiss police surveillance connected him with radical émigré networks. He also met German communists and developed a stronger interest in social rather than purely philosophical revolution.

From Switzerland he moved through Brussels to Paris in 1844. The Russian government ordered him to return. He refused.

**Documented consequence:** in December 1844 the Russian state stripped him of noble and civil rights, confiscated any property available to him, and sentenced him in absentia to exile and hard labor in Siberia if captured.

### Parisian associations

In Paris he encountered:

- Pierre-Joseph Proudhon, with whom he formed an important friendship and spent long nights discussing philosophy and political economy;
- Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels;
- George Sand;
- Polish émigrés;
- French socialists, communists, and republicans;
- German radical exiles.

Bakunin admired Marx’s learning and economic analysis but disliked his personality and what he perceived as doctrinaire and centralizing tendencies. Marx, in turn, recognized Bakunin’s energy but distrusted his political judgment, pan-Slavism, and conspiratorial methods.

Bakunin’s relationship with Proudhon was warmer. He later described Proudhon as revolutionary by instinct despite criticizing his idealism and weaknesses on organization.

### Polish speech and expulsion from France

On 29 November 1847 Bakunin addressed a Paris meeting commemorating the Polish uprising of 1830. He called for revolutionary reconciliation between Russians and Poles against tsarism. At the request of the Russian ambassador, the French government expelled him.

He moved to Brussels, where his relations with Marx’s communist circle remained uneasy.

## Revolutions of 1848

After the February Revolution, Bakunin returned to Paris and immersed himself in the revolutionary atmosphere. He sought funds and official assistance to travel east and promote insurrection among the Slavs.

The French republican government granted him money for the journey. A police official’s frequently quoted description called him a remarkable revolutionary on the first day but someone who should be shot on the second. The wording survives through memoir and biographical transmission rather than a universally cited original record and should be treated as a reported anecdote.

Bakunin traveled through Germany and Poland but failed to reach Russian territory. He attended the Slav Congress in Prague in June 1848.

### Prague

At Prague he advocated:

- destruction of the Austrian Empire;
- a democratic federation of Slavic peoples;
- social revolution alongside national liberation;
- cooperation rather than domination among Poles, Czechs, Slovaks, South Slavs, Ukrainians, Belarusians, and Russians.

Moderate Czech and Slav delegates rejected his revolutionary program. During the Prague Whitsun uprising, he participated in the barricade struggle. Austrian forces under Prince Windischgrätz suppressed the revolt.

Bakunin escaped through German territories. He continued attempting to construct a secret revolutionary network and drafted plans for coordinated Slav uprisings. In 1849 he published *Appeal to the Slavs*, attacking both imperial domination and bourgeois-nationalist compromise.

## Dresden Uprising, Arrest, and European Prisons, 1849–1851

Bakunin reached Dresden during the constitutional crisis of May 1849. He joined radical democrats opposing the Saxon monarchy.

### Role in the uprising

**Documented fact:** Bakunin participated actively in the uprising’s leadership and military deliberations. He advised on barricades, defense, communications, and concentrating revolutionary forces.

Later legend magnified some episodes—for example, that he proposed placing Raphael’s paintings from the Dresden gallery on the barricades because Prussian troops would not fire on them. The story appears repeatedly in memoir and biography, but its precise documentary foundation is weak. It functions as a durable illustration of the insurgent Bakunin’s theatrical imagination.

Richard Wagner, court conductor August Röckel, Samuel Tzschirner, and members of the provisional revolutionary government operated in the same Dresden insurgent network. A Saxon wanted notice and Bakunin’s interrogation identify Wagner among those in Bakunin’s immediate revolutionary circle. Bakunin and Wagner were therefore not merely coincidental residents of Dresden; their association during the rising is documented by official investigative material. See the [1851 Saxon notice](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Bekanntmachung%3A_21083%29_Bakunin).

The uprising collapsed. Bakunin, Röckel, and Otto Leonhard Heubner were arrested at Chemnitz on 10 May 1849.

### Saxon and Austrian proceedings

- Held and interrogated in Saxony.
- Tried for treason.
- Sentenced to death in January 1850.
- The Saxon king commuted the sentence to life imprisonment.
- Extradited to Austria in June 1850.
- Imprisoned in Prague and then Olmütz/Olomouc, often chained.
- Tried by Austrian authorities for his role in Prague.
- Again sentenced to death in May 1851.
- The sentence was commuted, and he was extradited to Russia.

These were formal convictions and sentences for revolutionary activity, not allegations.

## Russian Imprisonment, 1851–1857

Bakunin was confined without an ordinary Russian trial in the Peter and Paul Fortress in St. Petersburg. In 1854 he was transferred to the harsher Schlüsselburg Fortress.

### The Confession to Nicholas I

At Nicholas I’s command, Bakunin wrote a lengthy autobiographical *Confession* in 1851. It mixed deference, self-analysis, accounts of the revolutions, criticism of European politics, and appeals for mercy.

**Documented fact:** he adopted the posture of a repentant noble subject and asked for relief.

**Documented fact:** he did not supply the tsar with a useful roster of active conspirators. Nicholas I’s marginal comments show dissatisfaction with his failure to make a fuller practical confession.

**Bakunin’s later account:** he told Herzen that the text combined truth with fantasy and that he wrote as a prisoner seeking survival.

**Controversy after publication:** the document remained in police archives and was published in full only in 1921. Some anarchists and socialists regarded it as shameful capitulation; others emphasized the coercive circumstances, lack of denunciations, and continued revolutionary content. Victor Serge described the publication controversy while refusing both hagiographic suppression and simplistic condemnation. The [1851 text](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1851/confession.htm) and [Serge’s 1921 discussion](https://www.marxists.org/francais/serge/works/1921/12/bakounine.htm) permit direct comparison.

### Health deterioration

During approximately eight years of prison and fortress confinement, Bakunin suffered:

- scurvy;
- loss of most or all of his teeth;
- severe weight gain or metabolic deterioration associated with inactivity;
- chronic digestive and circulatory problems;
- psychological distress from solitary confinement;
- possible heart, liver, and kidney disease that worsened later.

He contemplated suicide and repeatedly petitioned for exile rather than continued fortress imprisonment.

## Siberian Exile, Marriage, Employment, and Escape, 1857–1861

After Alexander II succeeded Nicholas I, Bakunin’s family petitioned for clemency. In March 1857 he was sent to Siberian exile, initially to Tomsk.

### Tomsk

In Tomsk he met the Kwiatkowski family and married Antonina in October 1858.

### Irkutsk and Muravyev-Amursky

Through his maternal relative Nikolai Muravyev-Amursky, governor-general of Eastern Siberia, Bakunin received permission to move to Irkutsk and opportunities for employment.

His activities included:

- work connected to the Amur Development Agency;
- travel and commercial assignments;
- reported participation in schemes concerning Siberian trade and regional development;
- renewed political discussion among officials, exiles, merchants, and Polish deportees.

Bakunin briefly hoped Muravyev might lead a liberal or national movement against St. Petersburg. He praised the governor’s role in expanding Russia to the Amur and imagined an autonomous Siberian and pan-Slavic future. Muravyev, however, remained an imperial official rather than a revolutionary.

This episode later fed claims that Bakunin was a Russian nationalist or imperial agent. The documentary record instead shows a political misjudgment: he tried to interpret an expansionist governor as a possible vehicle for anti-bureaucratic transformation.

### Escape

In June 1861 Bakunin obtained authorization for a commercial journey down the Amur. At Nikolaevsk he boarded the American vessel *Vickery*, left Russian jurisdiction, and traveled through:

- the Amur region;
- De-Kastri;
- Hakodate;
- Yokohama;
- San Francisco, arriving in late September or early October 1861;
- Panama, crossing the isthmus;
- New York;
- Boston;
- Liverpool;
- London, arriving in December 1861.

His letter dated 3 October 1861 from San Francisco announced that he had escaped through the Amur, the Tatar coast, and Japan and expected to reach London by mid-December. The letter is available in [English translation](https://libcom.org/library/3-10-1861-bakunin). The journey is independently examined in the scholarly study [“Bakunin and the United States”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-social-history/article/bakunin-and-the-united-states/7F143C4E17C27A9D58CDA7D5EFA22060).

Popular retellings sometimes imply a furtive flight across the entire world under constant pursuit. In fact, the decisive illegality was leaving Russian exile; afterward he traveled openly enough to meet sympathizers, obtain funds, write letters, and use normal commercial transport.

## London, Herzen, and the Polish Insurrection, 1861–1863

In London Bakunin reunited with Herzen and Ogarev, publishers of *Kolokol* (*The Bell*). They received him publicly as a returned revolutionary martyr.

Bakunin contributed to their publishing circle but disagreed with Herzen’s gradualism. His texts included:

- “To Russian, Polish, and All Slav Friends”;
- “The People’s Cause: Romanov, Pugachev, or Pestel?”;
- appeals to Russian officers and youth.

In *Romanov, Pugachev, or Pestel?* he briefly left open the possibility that Alexander II could become a “people’s tsar” by calling a national assembly and granting land and liberty. If the emperor refused, the alternatives were popular rebellion symbolized by Pugachev or revolutionary republicanism symbolized by Pestel. This was a transitional position, not yet mature anarchism.

### Polish uprising

When the Polish January Uprising began in 1863, Bakunin tried to join and broaden it into a Russian and pan-Slavic social revolution.

He traveled to Hamburg, Copenhagen, and Stockholm. He became involved with an expedition led by Teofil Łapiński intended to land volunteers and arms on the Baltic coast. The operation was badly organized, diverted, and failed without producing the expected uprising.

Antonina joined him in Stockholm in April 1863. His Scandinavian stay brought publicity, lectures, contacts with radical democrats, and renewed financial dependence on supporters.

## Italy and the Formation of Revolutionary Anarchism, 1864–1867

Bakunin traveled through London, Brussels, Paris, Turin, Genoa, and Caprera before settling in Florence early in 1864.

### Garibaldi and Italian networks

He visited Giuseppe Garibaldi at Caprera on or about 19 January 1864. Garibaldi supplied introductions to Italian republican circles. Bakunin also corresponded with him and used Masonic connections shared with radical democrats. The relationship was real, although Bakunin increasingly rejected Garibaldi’s nationalism and political alliances.

### Freemasonry

**Documented or strongly reported fact:** Bakunin entered or reactivated Masonic affiliations in Italy and received high Scottish Rite recognition associated with Garibaldi. He attempted to radicalize lodges toward atheism, federalism, and social revolution.

The exact significance is disputed:

- Masonic and Italian sources report that Garibaldi conferred the 30th degree, Kadosh, at Caprera.
- Bakunin regarded lodges instrumentally, as existing transnational networks that might be converted into revolutionary organizations.
- He later concluded that established Freemasonry was too bourgeois, ritualized, and politically moderate.
- Claims that he was controlled by a hidden Masonic command or that anarchism was a Masonic creation are conspiracy narratives unsupported by evidence.

### Secret organizations

Between 1864 and 1867 Bakunin drafted programs and statutes for bodies variously called:

- the International Brotherhood;
- Revolutionary Brotherhood;
- International Revolutionary Association;
- the Secret Alliance;
- National Families or national revolutionary committees.

Their exact membership and institutional continuity are uncertain. Wolfgang Eckhardt and other archival scholars emphasize that multiple draft programs do not necessarily prove that every projected organization existed as described. Some were small networks bound by personal relationships; some may have existed chiefly on paper.

In Naples from 1865, Bakunin developed relationships with Carlo Gambuzzi, Giuseppe Fanelli, Saverio Friscia, Alberto Tucci, Giuseppe Caporusso, and other radicals. His circle helped transform sections of Italian republicanism toward socialism and anarchism.

### Mature ideological transition

By approximately 1866–1868 Bakunin had moved from revolutionary pan-Slavism to an explicitly socialist, federalist, atheist program:

- abolition of monarchy and centralized states;
- collective ownership of land and instruments of production;
- worker and peasant associations;
- federation from commune upward;
- equality of women;
- rejection of inherited property privileges;
- destruction of established churches;
- opposition to a transitional revolutionary state;
- international organization of workers.

His 1866 *Revolutionary Catechism*—not to be confused with Nechaev’s later *Catechism of a Revolutionary*—is a relatively systematic statement of this program.

## League of Peace and Freedom and the First International, 1867–1869

Bakunin joined the League of Peace and Freedom and attended its Geneva congress in 1867. He hoped to draw radical democrats—including figures associated with Victor Hugo and Garibaldi—toward socialism.

At the League’s Bern congress in September 1868, he proposed a socialist program. Defeated, he and his supporters withdrew.

### International Alliance of Socialist Democracy

On 25 September 1868 Bakunin and associates founded the International Alliance of Socialist Democracy. Its public program demanded atheism, abolition of classes, political and economic equality, and universal federation.

The Alliance applied to join the International Working Men’s Association as an international body. The General Council rejected the application because the International’s rules did not permit a second international organization within it. The Alliance announced its dissolution as an international body, and its sections entered the International separately.

### Central unresolved institutional question

**Documented fact:** Bakunin and his collaborators maintained confidential personal and organizational networks after the public Alliance’s formal dissolution.

**Disputed interpretation:** Marx, Engels, and allies described this as a disciplined secret organization covertly controlling International sections. Bakunin’s defenders describe a loose ideological network rather than a command hierarchy. Archival work indicates that Bakunin repeatedly drafted hierarchical-looking rules but that actual groups were smaller, less coherent, and more autonomous than either his plans or his opponents’ accusations implied.

Bakunin attended the Basel Congress of the International in September 1869. He supported collective ownership of land, abolition of inheritance, and strong federations of workers’ sections. His influence was greatest in the Jura, Italy, Spain, and among some French and Russian exiles.

## Nechaev Affair, 1869–1872

Sergei Nechaev arrived in Switzerland in 1869 claiming to represent a powerful Russian revolutionary organization. Bakunin and Ogarev accepted much of his story.

Bakunin helped him:

- obtain introductions and political credibility;
- use Herzen-related revolutionary funds;
- publish appeals and propaganda;
- represent himself as connected with a serious European revolutionary center.

Nechaev returned to Russia, created a tightly controlled organization, and arranged the murder of student Ivan Ivanov in November 1869 after Ivanov resisted his authority. Nechaev fled back to Western Europe.

### Bakunin’s responsibility

**Documented fact:** Bakunin promoted Nechaev before knowing the full facts, accepted false claims about Nechaev’s organization, and participated in propaganda that celebrated ruthless revolutionary commitment.

**Documented fact:** once he understood Nechaev’s manipulation, theft of correspondence, blackmail, and authoritarian methods, Bakunin broke with him in June 1870 and warned associates.

**Not established:** that Bakunin knew of or authorized Ivanov’s murder.

### *Catechism of a Revolutionary*

The notorious text beginning that the revolutionary is a “doomed man” has often been published under Bakunin’s name.

The attribution history is complex:

- Max Nettlau, E. H. Carr, Franco Venturi, Natalia Pirumova, and some former associates once attributed it to Bakunin or to joint collaboration.
- Some witnesses claimed to have seen a manuscript in Bakunin’s hand.
- A long Bakunin letter to Nechaev of 2–9 June 1870, discovered and published by Michael Confino in 1966, calls it “your Catechism” and criticizes its “abrek” or brigand morality.
- Linguistic and contextual evidence places it in the Russian student-revolutionary milieu more naturally than in Bakunin’s earlier catechisms.
- Confino concluded that decisive proof was still absent but that the strongest evidence favored Nechaev as conceiver and author.
- Some scholars retain the possibility that Bakunin discussed, edited, or influenced portions.

**Current evidentiary classification:** principally attributable to Nechaev; Bakunin’s co-authorship is disputed and unproved. It must not be confused with Bakunin’s genuine 1866 *Revolutionary Catechism*. See the [Confino documentary study](https://www.persee.fr/doc/cmr_0008-0160_1966_num_7_4_1686) and [Bakunin’s June 1870 letter](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-bakunin-to-nechayev-on-the-role-of-secret-revolutionary-societies).

### The Marx translation advance

Bakunin had contracted with publisher Sergei Polyakov to translate volume I of Marx’s *Capital* into Russian and received an advance customarily reported as 300 rubles. He began but abandoned the translation. Nechaev sent the publisher a threatening letter demanding that Bakunin be released from the obligation.

**Documented fact:** Bakunin accepted the advance and did not complete the work.

**Documented fact:** Nechaev sent an intimidation letter.

**Disputed question:** how much Bakunin knew beforehand. Bakunin denied authorizing the threat. The extant evidence does not establish that he commissioned it.

The affair was used at the Hague Congress as evidence against him. It involved breach of a publishing obligation and Nechaev’s coercion, but no court finding of fraud by Bakunin is known.

## Franco-Prussian War and Lyon, 1870

After France’s military collapse, Bakunin argued that only decentralized popular war and social revolution could defeat Prussia and prevent restoration of centralized authority.

He left Locarno on 9 September and reached Lyon around 15 September 1870. Working with local radicals, he helped prepare an insurrection.

On 28 September insurgents occupied the Hôtel de Ville and proclaimed revolutionary measures, including abolition of the state administrative machine. The attempt lacked adequate coordination and popular or military support. National Guards expelled the insurgents. A warrant was issued for Bakunin, who escaped to Marseille and returned by sea to Locarno in October.

**Result:** operational failure. It nonetheless supplied one of the earliest attempts to turn France’s wartime collapse into a communalist and federal social revolution.

Later Marxist polemics caricatured the episode as Bakunin abolishing the state by paper proclamation. Anarchist retellings sometimes exaggerated it as a near-success. The evidence supports neither extreme: it was a real armed political intervention, locally rooted but poorly prepared and rapidly defeated.

## Paris Commune and Conflict with Mazzini, 1871

Bakunin welcomed the Paris Commune as confirmation of communal federation and working-class self-government, while criticizing its hesitation and incomplete destruction of the old state.

After Giuseppe Mazzini condemned the Commune, Bakunin published a sustained attack on Mazzini’s religious nationalism and republican statism. This polemic helped accelerate the growth of anti-authoritarian socialism in Italy.

He worked on *The Knouto-Germanic Empire and the Social Revolution*. The section later published as *God and the State* belonged to this unfinished manuscript.

## Hague Congress and Split in the International, 1871–1873

The conflict concerned:

- whether workers should form political parties and seek state power;
- the authority of the General Council;
- federal autonomy of sections;
- Bakunin’s alleged secret Alliance;
- personal distrust and national antagonisms;
- the Nechaev-Polyakov letter;
- competing strategies after the Commune.

At the Hague Congress in September 1872, delegates voted to expel Bakunin and James Guillaume. The commission cited the continued existence of the Alliance and the Polyakov affair. The vote occurred in a congress whose delegate credentials and factional organization were disputed by both sides.

**Formal institutional finding:** Bakunin was expelled by vote of the Hague Congress.

**Not a judicial conviction:** the congress was a political association, not a court; its findings do not establish criminal fraud or conspiracy.

Anti-authoritarian delegates met at Saint-Imier on 15–16 September 1872 and rejected General Council centralization and compulsory political-party action. This initiated the anti-authoritarian or federalist International.

Marx and his allies then moved the General Council to New York. The centralized International declined rapidly; the federalist current survived in Switzerland, Italy, Spain, Belgium, and elsewhere.

## *Statism and Anarchy*, 1873

Published in Russian in Switzerland in 1873, *Государственность и анархия* (*Gosudarstvennost’ i anarkhiia*) was the only substantial book-length part of Bakunin’s projected theoretical works to appear during his lifetime.

It argued that:

- German state power posed a danger to European revolution;
- parliamentary socialism would reproduce domination;
- a “scientific” revolutionary elite could become a new ruling class;
- peasants and marginalized laboring populations were revolutionary actors;
- the Russian village commune contained solidaristic possibilities but also patriarchal, tsarist, and religious defects;
- revolution required an alliance of workers and peasants;
- freedom had to be constructed through federation rather than decreed from above.

The work also contains anti-German generalizations and antisemitic passages concerning Marx, Jewish financiers, journalism, and a supposed Marx-Rothschild connection. These are authentic passages, not hostile inventions.

## La Baronata, Finances, and Rupture with Cafiero, 1873–1874

Bakunin rarely had stable income. His financial support came at different times from:

- his noble family;
- Herzen;
- Ogarev and revolutionary funds;
- lecture or writing payments;
- followers and political associates;
- loans and advances;
- Carlo Cafiero.

Cafiero, an affluent Italian revolutionary, purchased and developed the property known as **La Baronata** at Minusio near Locarno as a refuge and revolutionary center. Construction and renovation expanded rapidly. Bakunin wanted space for his household, comrades, arms, and possible defense.

Expenses consumed a large part of Cafiero’s inheritance. Contemporaries disputed responsibility:

- Cafiero and some associates believed Bakunin had been extravagant, evasive, and insufficiently accountable.
- Bakunin maintained that Cafiero had approved the project and later reversed himself.
- Antonina was told that Bakunin had exploited Cafiero.
- The friendship deteriorated severely.
- Bakunin eventually returned the property rather than retaining it.

**Documented outcome:** the project failed financially and damaged Cafiero’s fortune.

**No established finding:** there was no lawsuit, conviction, or documentary proof that Bakunin stole Cafiero’s money.

The episode demonstrates Bakunin’s persistent inability to budget, his dependency on wealthier comrades, and the collision between communal revolutionary plans and personal household needs.

## Bologna Rising and Final Political Failure, 1874

Italian anarchists planned coordinated insurrections for summer 1874. Bakunin entered Bologna in disguise despite failing health.

The uprising collapsed before it properly began:

- police had penetrated or discovered preparations;
- participants failed to assemble in adequate numbers;
- weapons and communications were insufficient;
- arrests disrupted the plan.

Bakunin escaped disguised as a priest or elderly cleric and returned to Switzerland.

The costume is well attested in biographical tradition, though details have been embellished. The operation itself was an unambiguous failure.

Afterward he withdrew from active leadership, though not completely from correspondence or political thought.

## Later Life, Illness, and Death, 1874–1876

Bakunin lived principally in Lugano and the Swiss-Italian region, separated for periods from Antonina and the children, who had closer ties to Gambuzzi in Italy.

His final years included:

- estrangement and partial reconciliation with former comrades;
- recurring financial distress;
- disillusionment after failed insurrections;
- chronic illness;
- unfinished writing projects;
- continued correspondence with James Guillaume, Adhémar Schwitzguébel, Nikolai Zhukovsky, Elisée Reclus, and others.

His health problems included edema, shortness of breath, obesity, digestive illness, chronic pain, and probable cardiovascular and kidney disease. His prison illnesses had left permanent damage.

In June 1876 he traveled to Bern for medical treatment and entered the hospital or clinic associated with his friend and physician Adolf Vogt. He died at noon on 1 July 1876.

**Cause of death:** accounts commonly identify uremia or kidney failure, sometimes compounded by heart disease and long-term effects of imprisonment. No modern autopsy-quality diagnosis is available.

He was buried at Bremgarten Cemetery in Bern. His grave became a site of anarchist commemoration.

## Employment, Institutions, Organizations, and Projects

### Employment and income-producing activity

Bakunin’s conventional employment was intermittent:

- Imperial artillery officer, 1833–1835.
- Intended philosophy lecturer or professor, never appointed.
- Political writer, translator, journalist, and pamphleteer; earnings were irregular.
- Commercial or administrative work in Siberia connected with Amur development and trade.
- Contracted Russian translator of Marx’s *Capital*; project abandoned.
- No stable business, company ownership, patent, invention, or long-term salaried academic position is documented.

### Organizations and institutional relationships

- Stankevich philosophical circle.
- Young Hegelian and émigré radical networks.
- Slav Congress, Prague, 1848.
- Dresden revolutionary leadership, 1849.
- Herzen and Ogarev’s Free Russian Press/*Kolokol* circle.
- Italian Freemasonry and radical Masonic networks.
- International Brotherhood and related projected secret organizations.
- League of Peace and Freedom.
- International Alliance of Socialist Democracy.
- International Working Men’s Association.
- Geneva central section and Alliance section.
- Jura Federation.
- Anti-authoritarian International associated with Saint-Imier.
- Italian Internationalist and insurrectionary networks.

### Technologies, patents, and inventions

None documented. Bakunin wrote about industrial development, science, railways, military power, and agricultural organization but was not an inventor or patent holder.

## Political, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

### Phase I: Christian-romantic idealism

His early worldview joined Orthodox and romantic religious feeling to German idealism. Freedom meant spiritual self-realization and reconciliation with the universal.

### Phase II: Conservative Hegelianism

In the later 1830s he treated existing reality as an expression of rational development. This briefly supported accommodation rather than revolt.

### Phase III: Young Hegelian revolutionary democracy

Between 1840 and 1847 he inverted the conservative interpretation of dialectic. Negation became the active force of liberation. Religion and state were historical forms to be overcome.

### Phase IV: Revolutionary Slav federalism

From 1847 through the early 1860s he focused on destruction of the Russian, Austrian, Prussian, and Ottoman imperial structures and federation of liberated peoples. He opposed both Russian pan-Slavic domination and Polish restorationism, though his own rhetoric sometimes privileged a revolutionary mission for the Slavs.

### Phase V: Collectivist anarchism

By 1866–1868 his mature position combined:

- philosophical materialism;
- atheism;
- class struggle;
- collective ownership;
- federalism;
- revolutionary trade-union and communal organization;
- abolition of the state;
- opposition to parliamentary capture of state power;
- mass insurrection;
- a role for organized revolutionary minorities acting through persuasion and initiative.

### Liberty and sociality

Bakunin rejected the idea that freedom was simply isolation from others. He argued that freedom became real through recognition and cooperation among equals. Society was natural and indispensable; the state was a historically coercive form imposed on society.

### Authority

He distinguished:

- imposed, institutional authority, which he rejected;
- freely accepted expertise, which he considered unavoidable.

His often-quoted example was that he would consult a bootmaker about boots or an architect about a building but would not grant any expert permanent coercive power.

### Religion

Bakunin treated belief in divine sovereignty as the metaphysical model for earthly domination. *God and the State* reverses Voltaire’s maxim: if God existed, it would be necessary to abolish him. He nevertheless distinguished the social and emotional roots of religion from clerical institutions and did not claim that belief could be eliminated solely by decree.

### Class, workers, and peasants

Unlike socialist currents centered almost exclusively on industrial workers, Bakunin assigned revolutionary importance to:

- peasants;
- artisans;
- unemployed and precarious workers;
- colonized or oppressed peoples;
- socially marginalized strata.

He expected revolutionary initiative particularly from Italy, Spain, Russia, and the Slavic lands. Critics saw this as romanticization of social backwardness. Bakunin himself acknowledged reactionary elements in peasant life: patriarchal authority, religion, village exclusiveness, and tsarist mythology.

### Nationality and internationalism

He supported national liberation when it destroyed empires and enabled voluntary federation. He opposed formation of new centralized nation-states. His language nonetheless contained sweeping ethnic characterizations, especially concerning Germans, Russians, Poles, and Jews.

### Women and family

His formal programs demanded complete political, social, and economic equality for women and abolition of legally or religiously imposed marriage. This was advanced relative to most contemporary political programs.

His personal record was mixed:

- he defended his sisters against arranged marriages;
- accepted Antonina’s autonomy and her relationship with Gambuzzi;
- supported the children socially;
- but his youthful interventions in his sisters’ lives were emotionally authoritarian;
- revolutionary organizations around him remained overwhelmingly male.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Aleksandr Herzen

Friend, financial supporter, publisher, and recurrent opponent. Herzen financed Bakunin’s travel to Berlin and welcomed him after Siberia. They differed over nationalism, revolutionary timing, and Bakunin’s impatience. Their attachment survived serious quarrels.

### Nikolai Ogarev

Close associate in London and collaborator in Russian propaganda. Ogarev also accepted Nechaev’s claims and allowed revolutionary funds to pass to him.

### Nikolai Stankevich

Mentor and center of the philosophical circle that formed Bakunin intellectually. His engagement to Bakunin’s sister Lyubov strengthened the family connection.

### Vissarion Belinsky

Friend and philosophical associate; their relationship passed through intense conflict, partly over Bakunin’s family conduct.

### Ivan Turgenev

Friend from the Russian and Berlin circles. Turgenev visited Pryamukhino and became involved in the emotional world of the Bakunin sisters. The fictional Rudin has often been read partly through Bakunin-like traits, though it is not a one-to-one portrait.

### Pierre-Joseph Proudhon

Friend and major intellectual interlocutor. Bakunin absorbed Proudhon’s federalism and anti-statism while moving beyond his mutualist property theory toward collectivism.

### Karl Marx

Acquaintance from Paris; intermittent correspondent; organizer in the same International; eventual principal political rival. Bakunin respected Marx’s economic scholarship and began translating *Capital*. Their conflict concerned organization, political parties, state power, national questions, and personal factional struggle.

### Friedrich Engels

Met or associated with Bakunin in the 1840s and became one of his leading opponents in the International. Engels prepared or supported organizational accusations against the Alliance.

### Sergei Nechaev

Protégé and collaborator in 1869; later exposed as a manipulator and authoritarian conspirator. Their relationship damaged Bakunin’s reputation and supplied evidence in the Hague expulsion.

### James Guillaume

Swiss teacher, printer, historian, Jura organizer, and one of Bakunin’s closest late associates. Expelled with him at The Hague; later wrote an important biographical sketch.

### Adhémar Schwitzguébel

Jura watchmaker and labor organizer; important collaborator in the federalist International.

### Giuseppe Fanelli

Italian revolutionary dispatched by Bakunin to Spain in 1868. Fanelli’s meetings helped establish Internationalist and anarchist currents in Madrid and Barcelona despite linguistic difficulties.

### Carlo Cafiero

Initially an emissary of Marx and Engels in Italy, then a close Bakunin ally and financial patron. Their relationship collapsed over La Baronata and money.

### Carlo Gambuzzi

Neapolitan lawyer, political associate, Antonina’s partner, and biological father of the children in Bakunin’s household.

### Elisée and Elie Reclus

Geographers and federalist socialists associated with Bakunin’s networks and the anti-authoritarian current.

### Giuseppe Mazzini

Initially part of the common European revolutionary environment; later a major ideological enemy because of Mazzini’s nationalism, religiosity, and condemnation of the Commune.

### Giuseppe Garibaldi

Met Bakunin at Caprera; provided introductions; corresponded with him; shared radical and Masonic networks. Bakunin later criticized Garibaldi’s compromises with monarchy and nationalism.

### Nikolai Muravyev-Amursky

Maternal relative and governor-general who facilitated Bakunin’s movement and employment in Siberia. Bakunin briefly projected revolutionary possibilities onto him.

## Finances, Property, and Material Circumstances

Bakunin inherited social rank but never built an independent fortune. The state’s 1844 sentence deprived him of noble rights and claimable property. His later life was financially unstable.

Principal features:

- frequent personal debt;
- dependence on family remittances;
- dependence on Herzen and Ogarev;
- irregular payments for writing and translation;
- revolutionary fundraising;
- use of aliases and informal transfers necessitated by exile;
- unfinished paid translation of *Capital*;
- support from Cafiero;
- excessive costs at La Baronata;
- continuing obligations toward Antonina and the children.

No evidence found supports modern-style allegations of corporate fraud, offshore finance, money laundering, patent exploitation, or investment companies. Those categories are anachronistic to his actual material life.

## Controversies, Allegations, Crimes, and Evidentiary Findings

### Participation in political violence

**Documented fact:** Bakunin participated in armed uprisings at Prague, Dresden, Lyon, and Bologna-related preparations. He advocated social revolution, destruction of state institutions, confiscation of aristocratic property, and—under some conditions—violent insurrection.

He was formally convicted and sentenced to death by Saxon and Austrian courts for revolutionary treason. Both sentences were commuted.

No documentary evidence establishes that he personally killed anyone. His revolutionary plans did, however, anticipate civil conflict, coercive destruction of institutions, and punitive measures against counterrevolution.

### Secret “invisible dictatorship”

Bakunin wrote of an “invisible,” “collective,” or revolutionary dictatorship exercised by a coordinated minority without formal office.

**Critical interpretation:** Peter Marshall, Aileen Kelly, Marxist contemporaries, and others argue that an unaccountable secret elite contradicts anarchist freedom and could be more tyrannical than an open party government.

**Defensive interpretation:** Bakunin also insisted that the minority must possess no official power, impose no decrees, and influence events only through ideas, initiative, and organization among the people. Defenders interpret “dictatorship” as provocative nineteenth-century language for organized ideological influence.

**Documentary assessment:** the contradiction is real. Some draft statutes demand strict discipline and obedience; other writings expressly forbid seizure of governmental power. His actual networks were too loose and weak to implement either model fully. The issue cannot be resolved by quoting only one side of his manuscripts. Competing arguments and texts are collected in [Bakunin on Anarchy](https://www.theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-on-anarchy-en) and [David Adam’s study](https://libcom.org/article/marx-bakunin-and-question-authoritarianism-david-adam).

### Antisemitism

**Documented fact:** Bakunin used antisemitic stereotypes and slurs, particularly during conflicts with Marx, Moses Hess, Nikolai Utin, and German social democrats. He depicted Jews as an interconnected commercial, banking, journalistic, and political network and at times alleged coordination between Marxian socialists and Rothschild finance.

The material occurs in private letters and in published work, including *Statism and Anarchy*. It is therefore incorrect to claim that the evidence consists of a single forged or private sentence.

**Context, not exculpation:** similar tropes circulated among nineteenth-century radicals, including Proudhon and figures in socialist and nationalist movements. Bakunin’s anti-Jewish polemics often fused with anti-German rhetoric and personal factional conflict.

**Counter-evidence to broader claims:** his formal revolutionary programs demanded equality irrespective of nationality and religion. No evidence was found that he designed a legal program for persecution, expulsion, or civic disability of Jews. Spanish anarchist Anselmo Lorenzo reportedly found Bakunin’s appeal to Marx’s Jewish origin contrary to internationalist fraternity.

**Assessment:** antisemitic expression and conspiracy thinking are documented. Claims that his entire anarchist doctrine was a program of racial antisemitism overstate the evidence; claims that he was not antisemitic suppress authentic texts. One accessible compilation reproducing the passages and their publication context is [Zoe Baker’s documentary essay](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/zoe-baker-bakunin-was-a-racist).

### Anti-German and ethnic generalizations

Bakunin often treated “Germans” as disciplined, statist, bureaucratic, and susceptible to authoritarian organization. His hostility intensified after Prussia’s victory over France and during the struggle with Marx.

He also made essentializing observations about Slavs, Poles, Russians, Italians, and other peoples. These statements coexisted with advocacy of multinational federation and opposition to state-imposed nationality.

### Nechaev and revolutionary amorality

**Established:** Bakunin was politically and morally compromised by credulity toward Nechaev and willingness to associate with ruthless clandestine rhetoric.

**Not established:** responsibility for Ivanov’s murder or sole authorship of Nechaev’s *Catechism*.

**Established:** Bakunin eventually repudiated Nechaev’s blackmail, deception, centralized command, and manipulation of comrades.

### Russian-agent allegations

In 1848 the *Neue Rheinische Zeitung* repeated a rumor that George Sand possessed documents proving Bakunin was a Russian agent responsible for arrests of Polish revolutionaries. Bakunin demanded evidence. Sand denied possessing such documents and defended him.

The accusation resurfaced in later polemics, including statements by Francis Marx—not Karl Marx—and circulation by Wilhelm Liebknecht and others.

Karl Marx’s own 1853 published response distinguished the conservative journalist Francis Marx’s accusation from Karl Marx and observed that its logic would make every continental revolutionary a Russian agent. See [Marx’s 1853 statement](https://marxists.architexturez.net/archive/marx/works/1853/09/02.htm).

**Assessment:** rumor or political libel. No documentary evidence establishes Bakunin as a paid tsarist agent. His imprisonment, death sentences, loss of noble rights, Siberian exile, and illegal escape make the allegation implausible, though they do not alone logically disprove every conceivable contact.

### The Hague charges

**Formal political finding:** expelled from the International.

**Supported components:** secret networks did continue; the Polyakov contract was unfinished; Nechaev wrote a threatening letter.

**Disputed components:** whether a centrally governed Alliance existed exactly as alleged; whether Bakunin authorized Nechaev’s threat; whether the congress applied its rules impartially.

### La Baronata

**Supported:** financially disastrous; Cafiero spent heavily; Bakunin exercised poor judgment; relationships collapsed.

**Alleged by associates:** Bakunin exploited Cafiero’s generosity.

**Not established:** theft or criminal fraud.

### Treatment of women

Bakunin’s formal program supported women’s equality and free unions. Claims that he was consistently misogynistic require qualification. His youthful behavior toward his sisters could be controlling; his later acceptance of Antonina’s relationship was unusually non-possessive. No evidence found supports allegations of sexual violence or coercive sexual conduct.

### Psychological interpretations

Aileen Kelly and other biographers have interpreted Bakunin through romantic self-abnegation, sublimated sexuality, authoritarian longings, or a need to merge himself with a universal collective.

These are **scholarly interpretations**, not clinical diagnoses. No reliable basis exists for diagnosing him retrospectively with a modern psychiatric disorder. Prison despair and suicidal thoughts are documented situational crises.

### Dedicated recent adversarial-search finding

Searches pairing Bakunin’s full name with controversy, scandal, accusations, fraud, conspiracy, criticism, lawsuits, and related terms—including material indexed or published in 2024–2026—produced renewed debate about antisemitism, “invisible dictatorship,” the Nechaev attribution, and his status as an alleged Russian agent. They did not reveal new court records, financial crimes, sexual-abuse allegations, or newly authenticated intelligence files. Recent online claims that he was a tsarist operative largely recycle the disproved 1848–1850s rumor or infer agency from his aristocratic birth.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Propaganda

### “Father of anarchism”

**Story:** Bakunin invented anarchism.

**Origin and circulation:** late nineteenth- and twentieth-century anarchist and anti-anarchist histories often call him the “father of anarchism.”

**Evidence:** anti-state ideas and the term *anarchist* predated him; Proudhon publicly adopted the term before Bakunin. Bakunin’s distinct contribution was to connect collectivist anarchism with mass worker organization and the revolutionary International.

**Effect:** elevated him into a founder figure while obscuring Proudhon, Joseph Déjacque, women militants, mutualists, and collective labor movements.

### Giant of the barricades

Bakunin’s large physical stature, booming voice, appetite, chains, prison suffering, and repeated arrival at uprisings generated a heroic image: the wandering revolutionary giant whom no empire could contain.

The image rests on real events but was amplified by memoir, caricature, and anarchist commemoration.

### The Dresden art-gallery barricade

**Story:** Bakunin proposed placing Raphael’s masterpieces on barricades because cultured Prussian troops would not fire on them.

**Evidence:** repeated in biographical literature and attributed through recollections, but no definitive operational order or contemporaneous corroborating document has been identified here.

**Classification:** plausible anecdote or legend, not secure fact.

**Reputational effect:** portrays him either as brilliantly theatrical or recklessly indifferent to art.

### “The passion for destruction is a creative passion”

The sentence is genuine in substance, from his 1842 article, but frequently detached from its dialectical argument and rendered as a celebration of indiscriminate violence. In context, it referred to revolutionary negation of obsolete institutions. It still expresses approval of destructive revolutionary action; contextualization does not make it pacifist.

### Author of the terrorist *Catechism*

A durable publishing myth fused Bakunin’s genuine *Revolutionary Catechism* of 1866 with Nechaev’s *Catechism of a Revolutionary* of 1869. Cheap editions, propaganda, and older scholarship circulated the latter under Bakunin’s name.

The 1870 letter materially shifted scholarship toward Nechaev’s authorship, though limited Bakunin influence remains possible.

### Bakunin as Russian spy

Originated in émigré rumor and newspaper polemic in 1848, was denied by George Sand, and was recycled during later factional conflict. It has never been substantiated.

### Bakunin as prophetic opponent of Lenin or Stalin

Later anarchists credited him with predicting one-party communist dictatorship and a bureaucratic “red” ruling class.

**Evidence:** he did warn that revolutionary scholars using state power would rule over workers and reproduce domination.

**Limit:** he died before Bolshevism and cannot literally have predicted the specific Soviet institutional system in detail. The “prophecy” is a retrospective analogy grounded in genuine texts.

### Bakunin as a pure libertarian

Sympathetic mythology sometimes suppresses his secret-society drafts, disciplinary language, ethnic prejudices, political manipulation, and willingness to use violence.

### Bakunin as a proto-fascist or “anarcho-Nazbol”

Recent polemical internet discourse has extracted his antisemitic and pan-Slavic statements to characterize him as proto-fascist.

**Against:** his central program rejected nationalism, racial states, capitalism, clericalism, dictatorship, and state sovereignty.

**For the limited underlying claim:** authentic antisemitic conspiracy language and ethnic stereotypes make sanitized portrayals untenable.

**Assessment:** “proto-fascist” is an anachronistic ideological label; the prejudiced passages themselves are documented.

### Escape “around the world”

The phrase is geographically accurate—Siberia to Japan, North America, and London—but can create a myth of continuous clandestine pursuit. Most of the oceanic and American journey used ordinary commercial routes after the initial escape.

## Works by Bakunin

Bakunin was a prolific but radically unsystematic writer. Many projected books remained unfinished. Titles such as *God and the State*, *Marxism, Freedom and the State*, and *The Political Philosophy of Bakunin* are posthumous editorial constructions or excerpt collections rather than books he completed under those titles.

The most extensive modern source base is the documentary *Archives Bakounine*, edited by Arthur Lehning, and the digital/critical projects surveyed by the [Bakunin Portal](https://www.bakunin.de/schriften/index.html).

### Early philosophy and revolutionary democracy

- **1836 — Russian translation of Johann Gottlieb Fichte, *Einige Vorlesungen über die Bestimmung des Gelehrten*.** Bakunin’s translation introduced Fichte’s lectures on the scholar’s vocation to a Russian readership.
- **1838 — Preface and translation of Hegel’s Gymnasium Lectures.** Russian title commonly rendered as “Гегель. Речи гимназического директора.”
- **1842 — “Die Reaction in Deutschland. Ein Fragment von einem Franzosen.”** Published as Jules Elysard in *Deutsche Jahrbücher*.
- **1847 — Speech on the seventeenth anniversary of the Polish Revolution.** Delivered in Paris on 29 November.
- **1848 — “Aufruf an die Slaven” / “Appeal to the Slavs.”** Published in early 1849 from the 1848 revolutionary context.
- **1848–1849 — Draft programs for Slav federation and revolutionary organization.**
- **1851 — *Исповедь* / *Confession*.** Written under compulsion for Nicholas I; first fully published posthumously in 1921.

## Death, Burial, and Immediate Aftermath

Bakunin died without completing a systematic exposition of his anarchism. Antonina and the children settled in Italy within Gambuzzi’s household. She married Gambuzzi in 1879.

James Guillaume, Elisée Reclus, Carlo Cafiero, Max Nettlau, and later editors reconstructed Bakunin’s thought from unfinished manuscripts, pamphlets, letters, and organizational drafts. This editorial process created the familiar “books” through which he became known.

His grave at Bremgarten acquired symbolic importance. Memorial inscriptions have varied, and modern commemorations periodically renew debates about whether he should be remembered principally as a theorist of freedom, a revolutionary insurgent, Marx’s rival, or a compromised nineteenth-century radical.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Anarchism

Bakunin’s principal historical contribution was linking anti-state socialism to organized worker and peasant movements. His influence entered:

- the Jura Federation;
- Italian anarchism;
- Spanish Internationalism and later anarcho-syndicalism;
- Russian populism;
- French revolutionary syndicalism;
- Latin American anarchist labor movements;
- later debates over federalism, direct action, and revolutionary organization.

He did not found every organization later labeled “Bakuninist,” and many militants developed his ideas independently.

### Marxism and socialist historiography

Marxist histories often presented Bakunin as:

- politically adventurist;
- theoretically incoherent;
- representative of peasant or petty-bourgeois revolt;
- a conspiratorial opponent of disciplined working-class politics.

Anarchist histories countered by presenting Marx as the centralizer who destroyed the First International. Archival scholarship has made the division less binary: both camps engaged in factional organization, polemical distortion, credential disputes, and strategic maneuvering.

### Russia and the Soviet period

Russian revolutionaries read *Statism and Anarchy* and clandestine Bakunin texts. Populists drew on his faith in peasant revolt, while figures such as Peter Tkachev and Nechaev developed more centralized conspiratorial conclusions.

Soviet scholarship acknowledged his revolutionary role but classified anarchism as a petty-bourgeois deviation defeated by Marxism. Publication of the *Confession* also served polemical purposes, although serious Soviet historians contributed important archival work.

### Later libertarian interpretation

Twentieth-century anarchists emphasized:

- prediction of bureaucratic dictatorship;
- federalism;
- direct action;
- worker self-management;
- rejection of revolutionary government;
- recognition that political and economic domination reinforce each other.

### Reassessment of prejudice and organization

Recent scholarship and movement debate give greater attention to:

- antisemitic passages;
- anti-German stereotypes;
- tensions between women’s equality in theory and male-dominated practice;
- the “invisible dictatorship” language;
- romanticization of insurrection;
- failures of financial accountability;
- editorial misattribution of Nechaev’s *Catechism*.

This reassessment has not produced a single new consensus. It has made both devotional and dismissive portraits harder to sustain.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the consulted sources are included. Mere intellectual succession or occupation of the same century is excluded.

### Karl Marx — documented fact

- Met Bakunin in Paris in 1844 and interacted within émigré socialist circles.
- Met again in London in 1864; Marx sent Bakunin the International’s statutes and inaugural address.
- Corresponded intermittently.
- Bakunin began but did not finish a Russian translation of *Capital*.
- Became rival leaders or reference points within the First International.
- Marx and allies supported the Hague proceedings that expelled Bakunin in 1872.
- Bakunin respected Marx’s economic scholarship while attacking his political strategy and using antisemitic language against him.
- Marx criticized Bakunin’s pan-Slavism, secret organization, and political abstention.

The Marx-Bakunin correspondence includes Bakunin’s 1868 letter preserved in [MEGA Digital](https://megadigital.bbaw.de/briefe/detail.xql?id=M0000884&view=l).

### Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel — documented intellectual influence

Bakunin studied Hegel intensively in Moscow, translated Hegelian lectures into Russian in 1838, and passed from a conservative to a revolutionary reading of dialectic. He did not meet Hegel, who died in 1831.

### Johann Gottlieb Fichte — documented intellectual influence

Bakunin translated Fichte’s lectures on the vocation of the scholar into Russian in 1836. Fichte’s conception of moral vocation and action strongly influenced his early formation. There was no personal meeting.

### Immanuel Kant — documented intellectual influence

Bakunin read and discussed Kant during his early philosophical period. Kant’s treatment of autonomy formed part of the path through which he reached Fichte and Hegel. There was no personal meeting.

### Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling — documented teacher-auditor connection and influence

Bakunin read Schelling in Russia and attended lectures in Berlin. Schelling was therefore both a formative author and, in the limited lecture-hall sense, a teacher whom Bakunin heard personally.

### Jean-Jacques Rousseau — documented family and intellectual influence

Bakunin’s father used a Rousseau-influenced educational scheme at Pryamukhino. Bakunin later engaged the problems of natural freedom, society, and authority associated with Rousseau, while rejecting the sovereign-state implications he found in social-contract theory. No personal connection was possible.

### Francois Marie Charles Fourier — documented intellectual and movement connection

Bakunin encountered Fourierist socialism within French and international radical circles and included Fourier among significant socialist predecessors. The connection is intellectual rather than personal.

### Claude-Henri de Rouvroy, comte de Saint-Simon — documented intellectual influence

Saint-Simonian ideas formed part of the socialist environment Bakunin studied and criticized. No personal meeting occurred.

### Giuseppe Maria Garibaldi — documented fact

- Bakunin visited Garibaldi at Caprera around 19 January 1864.
- Garibaldi supplied introductions to Italian republican circles.
- They corresponded.
- Their relationship overlapped with radical Freemasonry; Italian sources report Garibaldi’s conferral of the Scottish Rite 30th degree on Bakunin.
- Bakunin later criticized Garibaldi’s compromises with monarchy, nationalism, and Mazzinian politics.

### Wilhelm Richard Wagner — documented fact

- Both participated in the Dresden revolutionary movement of May 1849.
- Saxon investigative material lists Wagner among Bakunin’s immediate revolutionary associates.
- Bakunin stated during interrogation that he had repeatedly met Wagner and other Dresden radicals.
- Their later political and artistic trajectories diverged; no evidence supports a continuing close friendship.

### Victor-Marie Hugo — reported organizational connection, no verified personal meeting

Hugo and Bakunin were both associated with the League of Peace and Freedom’s broad public constellation. The consulted evidence establishes shared institutional association but not a direct personal meeting or correspondence. This is therefore weaker than the Garibaldi or Wagner connections.

### Friedrich Nietzsche — no supported direct connection

Nietzsche and Bakunin were contemporaries for part of their lives, and later writers compare their treatments of destruction, morality, and power. No meeting, correspondence, or explicit Bakunin influence acknowledged by Nietzsche was established.

### Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov (Lenin) — posthumous intellectual opposition only

Lenin did not meet Bakunin; he was six years old when Bakunin died. Lenin’s Marxist political tradition rejected Bakuninist anarchism, and later anarchists used Bakunin’s warnings about revolutionary state power against Leninism. This is a posthumous interpretive connection, not a lifetime relationship.

### Lev Davidovich Bronstein (Trotsky) — posthumous polemical connection only

Trotsky did not meet Bakunin. Later Marxist controversies juxtaposed Bakunin’s anti-statism with Bolshevik strategy. No personal connection existed.

### Georges Sorel — reported intellectual reception

Sorel wrote in a later environment shaped by the conflict between Marxism, anarchism, and revolutionary syndicalism. Bakunin formed part of that inherited revolutionary tradition, but no direct personal connection existed.

### Albert Camus — documented posthumous intellectual discussion

Camus discussed Bakunin and revolutionary nihilism in *L’Homme révolté* (*The Rebel*, 1951), treating the relationship among revolt, destruction, Nechaev, and revolutionary ends. This is posthumous interpretation.

### Avram Noam Chomsky — documented posthumous intellectual reception

Chomsky has repeatedly identified with the libertarian-socialist and anarcho-syndicalist tradition in which Bakunin is a canonical predecessor. No personal connection was possible.

### Aleksandr Gelyevich Dugin — no supported direct or acknowledged connection established

Modern commentary sometimes places both men in discussions of Russia, empire, Slav identity, or anti-liberal thought. No reliable source consulted here established a meaningful direct intellectual lineage acknowledged by Dugin. Similar subject matter alone is insufficient.

### Remaining atlas roster

No documented meeting, correspondence, collaboration, rivalry, family tie, shared institution, or explicit acknowledged influence was established for the other listed atlas figures. Given the roster’s size, absence should not be interpreted as proof that no minor textual reference exists anywhere; it means no connection meeting the atlas’s documentary threshold emerged from the research.
