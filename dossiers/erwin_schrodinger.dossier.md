# Erwin Schrödinger: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Erwin Rudolf Josef Alexander Schrödinger (1887–1961)  
**Scope:** Documentary biographical dossier, with disputed claims and later mythology separately identified  
**Prepared:** 22 August 2026

## Basic Identifying Information

- **Full name:** Erwin Rudolf Josef Alexander Schrödinger; normally published as **Erwin Schrödinger**. English-language printing often substitutes **Schrodinger**. The spelling **Schroedinger** transliterates the umlaut.
- **Birth:** 12 August 1887, Vienna, Austria-Hungary.
- **Death:** 4 January 1961, Vienna, Austria, aged 73. Contemporary and institutional biographies identify tuberculosis as the cause.
- **Burial:** Alpbach, Tyrol, Austria, where he had long spent holidays; the grave bears the wave equation.
- **Nationality/citizenship:** Austrian; naturalized Irish in 1948 while retaining Austrian nationality. He resumed permanent residence in Austria in 1956.
- **Fields:** theoretical physics, statistical mechanics, quantum mechanics, electrodynamics, general relativity and unified-field theory, colorimetry, philosophy of science, philosophy of mind and theoretical biology.
- **Principal offices:** professor at Stuttgart, Breslau, Zürich, Berlin and Graz; Fellow of Magdalen College, Oxford; Director of the School of Theoretical Physics, Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies (DIAS); professor at the University of Vienna.
- **Best-known scientific contributions:** the nonrelativistic wave equation bearing his name; the wave-mechanical formulation of quantum theory; the Schrödinger picture; early work on quantum statistics and color theory; the 1935 cat thought experiment; the term *Verschränkung* (“entanglement”); and *What Is Life?* (1944).
- **Highest honor:** half of the 1933 Nobel Prize in Physics, shared in equal overall award with Paul A. M. Dirac, “for the discovery of new productive forms of atomic theory.”

The Nobel and Dictionary of Irish Biography notices are reliable compact anchors for dates and offices; Walter J. Moore’s *Schrödinger: Life and Thought* remains the fullest narrative biography, but its reconstructions of intimate life require explicit attribution because they draw on diaries, family recollections and pseudonymized subjects.

## Family and Ancestry

Schrödinger was the only child of **Rudolf Schrödinger** (1857–1919) and **Georgine Emilia Brenda “Emily” Bauer** (1867–1921). Rudolf inherited and managed a linoleum/oilcloth business and was also an accomplished amateur botanist, especially interested in plant taxonomy. Emily was the daughter of Vienna chemistry professor **Alexander Bauer** and an English mother from Leamington Spa. The household was materially comfortable, cultivated and bilingual; Erwin learned English and German early.

His parents came from different confessional backgrounds—Rudolf Catholic, Emily Lutheran. Erwin was baptized Catholic but was not formed into an observant adult believer. He later described himself as atheist while repeatedly using religious language and developing a metaphysics influenced by Schopenhauer, Spinoza and Indian monism.

On 6 April 1920 he married **Annemarie “Anny” Bertel** (3 December 1896–3 October 1965), the daughter of a Salzburg-area photographer/industrial family. The marriage produced no children and was never dissolved. Both spouses had other relationships; Anny’s long relationship with the mathematician **Hermann Weyl** is documented in the biographical literature. The marriage nevertheless remained an enduring domestic, practical and emotional partnership.

Schrödinger fathered at least three daughters outside the marriage according to Moore’s research:

- **Ruth Georgie Erica March** (born 1934), daughter of **Hildegunde “Hilde” March**, wife of physicist **Arthur March**. Schrödinger, Anny and Hilde at times maintained a conspicuous extended household. Ruth’s son, quantum physicist **Terry Rudolph**, is Schrödinger’s grandson.
- A daughter with the Dublin woman Moore calls **“Kate Nolan”**, a pseudonym used to protect identity.
- A daughter with Irish political activist **Sheila May**. The precise public record of these Dublin relationships is incomplete, and popular claims that both women were his “students” are not supported by Moore’s account.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1887–1906

Schrödinger grew up in Vienna in a bourgeois home at the end of the Habsburg era. Private tuition preceded entry into the **Akademisches Gymnasium** in 1898. He excelled across mathematics, physics, ancient languages and German literature and retained serious literary ambitions. Vienna’s combination of rigorous physics, philosophical culture and artistic modernism mattered to his formation, although later reminiscence sometimes turns this general environment into direct intellectual contact for which no evidence exists.

He spent holidays in the Austrian countryside and developed lifelong interests in hiking, theater and poetry. The broad education helps explain the unusual range of his mature writing: mathematical papers, lectures on Greek science, philosophical dialogues, autobiographical fragments and poetry.

## Education and Formation, 1906–1914

In 1906 Schrödinger entered the **University of Vienna**. His principal teachers included experimental physicist **Franz Serafin Exner** and theoretical physicist **Friedrich Hasenöhrl**, a student and successor of Ludwig Boltzmann. Boltzmann died shortly before Schrödinger began university, but Boltzmann’s statistical mechanics profoundly shaped the Viennese school and Schrödinger’s later work.

He received the doctorate in 1910 for **“Über die Leitung der Elektrizität auf der Oberfläche von Isolatoren an feuchter Luft”** (On the conduction of electricity on the surface of insulators in moist air), supervised within Hasenöhrl’s circle. He then performed military service and became Exner’s assistant in 1911. Work with **Fritz Kohlrausch** included experimental radiation studies. His early research covered atmospheric electricity and radioactivity, dielectric theory, Brownian motion, statistical methods and color perception. He made measurements at **Seeham** in 1913 and completed his habilitation in 1914.

These years undermine the retrospective image of Schrödinger as purely an abstract theorist. His early experimental training and later colorimetry joined measurements, physiology and mathematical representation.

## War Service and Immediate Postwar Years, 1914–1921

Mobilized in the First World War, Schrödinger served as an officer in Austro-Hungarian fortress artillery, with postings on the Italian front around Gorizia, Duino, Sistiana and Prosecco and later in Vienna. He continued theoretical reading and research when conditions permitted. He was not imprisoned, and no evidence indicates intelligence work or a war-crimes proceeding.

After the imperial collapse, Austrian inflation and the decline of the family business destroyed much of the family’s security. Rudolf died in 1919 and Emily in 1921. Schrödinger received the Austrian Academy’s **Haitinger Prize** in 1920 for earlier work. Academic mobility became both professional opportunity and financial necessity.

In 1920 he briefly became assistant to **Max Wien** at Jena, then extraordinary professor at Stuttgart. In 1921 he accepted a full professorship at Breslau, but remained only months before moving to the better-supported chair at the **University of Zürich**. His marriage to Anny began amid this rapid movement.

## Zürich and the Creation of Wave Mechanics, 1921–1927

Zürich supplied stability, colleagues and access to the international physics network. Schrödinger worked on statistical mechanics, atomic theory, color and quantum problems and interacted with **Hermann Weyl**, **Peter Debye** and others. He absorbed **Louis de Broglie’s** matter-wave thesis through the quantum-theory conversation associated with Einstein and Zürich colleagues.

During late 1925 and 1926 he found an equation whose stationary solutions reproduced the hydrogen spectrum. In four installments under the common title **“Quantisierung als Eigenwertproblem”** (Quantization as an Eigenvalue Problem), published in *Annalen der Physik* in 1926, he developed wave mechanics. Additional papers demonstrated relations to Heisenberg–Born–Jordan matrix mechanics and explored conservation, perturbation and time dependence.

The achievement did not arise in isolation. Its documented ingredients include Hamiltonian mechanics, de Broglie waves, Hilbert-space/eigenvalue mathematics familiar through Weyl and the existing quantum results of Planck, Einstein, Bohr, Sommerfeld, Heisenberg, Born, Jordan and Pauli. **Wolfgang Pauli** rapidly solved hydrogen using matrix mechanics and engaged Schrödinger critically; **Max Born** supplied the probabilistic interpretation of the wavefunction, which Schrödinger resisted in favor of a more continuous wave picture.

The priority story is sometimes simplified into “Schrödinger invented quantum mechanics.” More accurately, wave mechanics and matrix mechanics were independently developed formulations within a collective revolution; their mathematical equivalence was soon established. Schrödinger’s equation became the standard computational language for nonrelativistic quantum mechanics, while his preferred realist interpretation did not prevail.

The winter setting of the breakthrough became part of scientific folklore. Schrödinger spent Christmas/New Year 1925–26 at **Arosa** with an unidentified companion while Anny remained in Zürich. Moore associates an intense erotic episode with the creative period. The trip is documented; claims that a particular lover directly “inspired” the equation are biographical conjecture, not a scientific causal fact.

## Berlin, 1927–1933

In 1927 Schrödinger succeeded **Max Planck** in a prestigious chair at the Friedrich Wilhelm University of Berlin. He entered a community including Einstein, Planck and von Laue, lectured widely and received the **Matteucci Medal** (1927). His relationship with Einstein combined friendship, intellectual agreement on the incompleteness or strangeness of orthodox quantum interpretation, and later disagreement over unified-field announcements.

Schrödinger never reconciled himself to the Copenhagen insistence on discontinuous quantum jumps and complementary classical descriptions. His disagreements with **Niels Bohr** were substantive but should not be converted into personal enmity. During a 1926 Copenhagen visit, extended debate with Bohr reportedly exhausted the ill Schrödinger; retellings dramatize the episode, but the underlying philosophical conflict is well documented.

In 1933, after Hitler’s accession and the purge of Jewish and politically suspect academics, Schrödinger left Germany despite not being a direct racial-law target. He opposed Nazi antisemitism and did not want to function under the regime. **Frederick Lindemann** helped secure him a position at Oxford. He departed before receiving the Nobel announcement.

## Oxford, Nobel Prize and Graz, 1933–1938

Schrödinger became a Fellow of **Magdalen College, Oxford**. In December 1933 he and **Paul Dirac** received the Physics Nobel; because the 1932 award to Heisenberg was announced in the same cycle, the Stockholm ceremonies visually joined leading creators of quantum mechanics. Schrödinger’s Nobel lecture surveyed wave mechanics.

Oxford never became a comfortable permanent home. He disliked aspects of the tutorial system and academic culture; institutional discomfort with his domestic arrangement involving Anny and the pregnant Hilde March also mattered. Claims that Oxford formally expelled him for a ménage à trois overstate the evidence: he remained a fellow while seeking another post.

In 1934 he lectured at Princeton but declined an anticipated permanent offer. In 1935 he published the three-part **“Die gegenwärtige Situation in der Quantenmechanik”** (The Present Situation in Quantum Mechanics) in response to the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paper. There he coined **Verschränkung** for entanglement and introduced the sealed-box cat. The cat was designed to expose the apparent absurdity of applying a superposition without qualification from microscopic trigger to macroscopic animal; it was not a proposal to perform the experiment and not originally a cute illustration that a cat is literally both alive and dead.

In 1936 he accepted a chair at the **University of Graz**. After the March 1938 Anschluss, he published a conciliatory/recanting statement toward the Nazi regime in an attempt to retain his position. He later confessed shame to Einstein, explaining that he had wanted to remain free and had resorted to duplicity. The strategy failed: authorities dismissed him for “political unreliability.” This episode is documented evidence of tactical accommodation under threat, following his anti-Nazi departure from Berlin; it is neither evidence of Nazi political commitment nor a story of unwavering public resistance.

Schrödinger and Anny escaped through Italy, then reached Oxford and **Ghent**, where he accepted a temporary position associated with the Fondation Francqui. The approaching European war again made the position insecure.

## Dublin under de Valera, 1939–1956

Irish Taoiseach **Éamon de Valera**, trained in mathematics, wanted a national institute modeled partly on Princeton’s Institute for Advanced Study. Diplomatic and scholarly intermediaries connected him with Schrödinger. De Valera’s government arranged entry for Schrödinger, Anny and Hilde March with her daughter. Schrödinger reached Dublin in October 1939 and became the founding director of the **School of Theoretical Physics** in the **Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies**, legally created in 1940.

He lived principally in Clontarf, including at **Kincora Road**, and worked at the institute’s Merrion Square premises. Ireland’s neutrality offered security during the Second World War, though travel and international communication were constrained. He became an Irish citizen in 1948. “De Valera’s patronage” was real in the specific sense of personal recruitment, immigration assistance, legislation and state institutional support; Schrödinger was a salaried scientific director rather than a private court dependent.

His Dublin output was extensive: statistical thermodynamics, relativity, cosmology, unified-field projects, quantum foundations and public lectures. He trained and attracted researchers, although DIAS was not a laboratory or commercial enterprise. There are no significant patents, companies or equity investments associated with him. His economic life was that of a professor/director supported by salaries, lecture fees, royalties, prizes and later a pension, not an industrial physicist.

### *What Is Life?*

Public lectures delivered under Trinity College auspices in February 1943 became **What Is Life? The Physical Aspect of the Living Cell** (1944). Drawing especially on Max Delbrück’s mutation work and the chromosome theory of heredity, Schrödinger described the gene as an “aperiodic crystal” and asked how organisms maintain order. The book influenced **James Watson**, **Francis Crick**, **Maurice Wilkins** and other entrants into molecular biology.

The common myth that Schrödinger “discovered DNA” is false. DNA was known; Avery, MacLeod and McCarty’s transforming-principle paper appeared in 1944; the double helix came in 1953. Schrödinger supplied a provocative physical framing rather than the decisive experimental identification or structure. His phrase “negative entropy” was influential but technically controversial.

### Unified field theory

Schrödinger devoted much of the 1940s and 1950s to affine and other unified-field theories. In 1947 he announced a purported breakthrough at a Dublin press conference attended by de Valera. Einstein publicly objected that the claim was premature. The episode was a conspicuous failure: the program did not yield a successful theory of gravitation and electromagnetism and has a much smaller legacy than his quantum work.

### Irish relationships

Moore documents multiple affairs in Dublin and two daughters outside marriage there. The unconventional household was socially visible, but sources vary in naming women because Moore employed pseudonyms and families guarded privacy. There is no known criminal prosecution, civil suit or formal university investigation during Schrödinger’s lifetime concerning his sexual conduct.

## Return to Vienna, Illness and Death, 1956–1961

Austria repeatedly invited Schrödinger back after the war. He retired from DIAS in 1955 and returned permanently in 1956 to a specially created chair at the University of Vienna, after delays involving pension and employment arrangements. Austria awarded the first **Erwin Schrödinger Prize** to him in 1956. He continued lecturing and writing, including the Tarner Lectures published as *Mind and Matter*.

Recurrent tuberculosis had affected his health over many years. He spent time in Alpine sanatoria and at Alpbach. He died of tuberculosis in Vienna on 4 January 1961. Anny survived him until 1965.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

Schrödinger was not a party politician. His clearest political acts were leaving Nazi Germany in 1933, attempting an accommodation after the 1938 Anschluss, being dismissed anyway, and accepting refuge and public office in neutral Ireland. He was culturally Austrian, valued European intellectual exchange and made occasional speculative comments about Celtic affinities between Austria and Ireland.

Philosophically he rejected simple positivism and the orthodox quantum insistence that theory should stop at observations. **Schopenhauer** mediated his engagement with the **Upanishads** and **Advaita Vedānta**; Spinoza and ancient Greek natural philosophy were also important. He argued that plurality of individual minds is appearance and that consciousness is fundamentally one. This was philosophical monism, not evidence that quantum mechanics scientifically proves mysticism—a recurrent popular extrapolation.

He called himself an atheist in private writing, rejected conventional dogma, and nevertheless used “God,” religious imagery and mystical language. Classifying him simply as Hindu, Buddhist, Christian or occultist is therefore misleading. No evidence supports a substantive link to Robert Fludd, Athanasius Kircher or Pico della Mirandola beyond broad retrospective histories of cosmology/mysticism; all died centuries before him. Halley belongs to the ancestry of celestial mechanics but was not a direct Schrödinger source comparable to Boltzmann, de Broglie or Einstein.

## Important Relationships and Networks

- **Franz Exner and Friedrich Hasenöhrl:** teachers; connected him to Viennese experimental physics and Boltzmann’s theoretical tradition.
- **Albert Einstein:** intellectual precursor, correspondent and friend; encouraged attention to de Broglie; shared objections to orthodox quantum completeness; criticized Schrödinger’s 1947 unified-field publicity.
- **Louis de Broglie:** matter-wave hypothesis was a direct precursor of wave mechanics.
- **Hermann Weyl:** Zürich colleague, mathematical interlocutor and Anny’s lover.
- **Wolfgang Pauli:** brilliant, sometimes abrasive correspondent and critic; helped clarify equivalence and quantum problems.
- **Werner Heisenberg, Max Born and Pascual Jordan:** creators of matrix mechanics; rivals only in the limited sense of competing formalisms. Born’s probabilistic reading diverged from Schrödinger’s preferences.
- **Niels Bohr:** sustained interpretive antagonist and host for foundational discussion, not simply an enemy.
- **Paul Dirac:** co-recipient of the 1933 Nobel; parallel architect of quantum theory.
- **Max Planck:** predecessor in Berlin and senior patron of German theoretical physics.
- **John von Neumann:** formalized Hilbert-space quantum mechanics and measurement; part of the adjacent foundations network, but not a primary collaborator.
- **Arthur and Hilde March:** Arthur was a physicist and colleague; Hilde was Schrödinger’s long-term partner and mother of Ruth.
- **Éamon de Valera:** political patron and institutional founder who made the Dublin refuge possible.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Evidentiary Status

### Relationships with adolescents and younger women

This issue requires distinctions among what Schrödinger recorded, what Moore reconstructed, and later public labels.

**Documented through Moore’s use of diaries and interviews:** Schrödinger pursued numerous extramarital relationships; he began tutoring **Itha “Ithi” Junger** when she was about fourteen; Moore describes physical intimacy during tutoring, says intercourse began shortly after her seventeenth birthday, and records a pregnancy and abortion when she was about twenty. Moore characterized Schrödinger as having a “Lolita complex.” He also records Schrödinger’s infatuation in Ireland with **Barbara MacEntee**, then about twelve, and says another adult intervened, after which he desisted. Schrödinger’s private lists and diary language displayed attraction to very young girls.

**Later accusation:** A 2021 *Irish Times* investigation and ensuing commentary called him a serial abuser and, in the broad popular sense, a pedophile. Trinity College Dublin subsequently removed/changed commemorative naming associated with a lecture theater and lecture series. These are posthumous historical and institutional judgments, not criminal findings.

**Limits and disputes:** No trial, conviction or contemporaneous formal adjudication exists. The surviving evidence does not establish intercourse with a prepubescent child. “Pedophilia” has a narrower clinical meaning than its broad journalistic use, and diagnosis cannot be made retrospectively from newspaper reporting. At the same time, the absence of prosecution does not negate diary-based evidence of grooming-like conduct, sexual contact with an adolescent, or attraction to children. Recent historian **Klaus Taschwer** has argued that reporting sometimes conflated Moore’s pseudonyms, converted adult Irish partners into “students,” and overstated some claims. A 2026 scholarly study by Taschwer and colleagues revisits newly accessible sources; its abstract explicitly describes the earlier evidentiary basis as uncertain. The responsible conclusion is: substantial troubling conduct is documented; some viral formulations exceed what the sources securely establish.

### Nazi-era conduct

- **Established:** left Berlin voluntarily in 1933 because of the Nazi regime; issued an accommodationist statement after Anschluss in 1938; was dismissed as politically unreliable; fled Austria.
- **His explanation:** a frightened and duplicitous effort to preserve freedom and position, later regretted.
- **Not established:** Nazi Party membership, ideological Nazism or participation in persecution.

### Scientific overclaim

The 1947 Dublin announcement of a unified-field breakthrough was not vindicated. It produced a public correction/critique from Einstein. This was an episode of premature scientific publicity, not fraud: there is no evidence of fabricated data, financial deception or misconduct proceeding.

### Criminal, intelligence and financial allegations

No reliable evidence located in the consulted record connects Schrödinger to fraud, corruption, money laundering, espionage or intelligence service. His military service was conventional wartime service. No material lawsuits or patent disputes appear in the standard archival and biographical record.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The cat is both alive and dead”

1. **Story:** quantum mechanics declares a real cat simultaneously alive and dead.
2. **Origin:** Schrödinger’s 1935 *Naturwissenschaften* paper, responding to EPR.
3. **Circulation:** physicists, textbooks, popular science, cartoons and internet culture.
4. **Evidence:** the paper describes an entangled superposition if the formalism is extended to the whole apparatus. Schrödinger called the result ridiculous; it was a reductio/problem case, not an animal experiment.
5. **Effect:** it made Schrödinger the most recognizable quantum name outside physics while often reversing his critical intention.

### “Schrödinger invented quantum mechanics alone”

The legend compresses a network—Planck, Einstein, Bohr, de Broglie, Heisenberg, Born, Jordan, Pauli, Dirac and others—into one equation. His 1926 achievement was foundational and independent in formulation, but neither isolated nor the whole theory.

### “He discovered DNA”

This story grew from scientists’ testimony that *What Is Life?* inspired them. The book anticipated a stable information-bearing molecular structure but did not identify DNA as the genetic material or derive the double helix. Its reputational effect joined Schrödinger to molecular biology and sometimes displaced Delbrück, Avery–MacLeod–McCarty and experimental geneticists.

### “A lover inspired the equation”

Moore’s biography linked the Arosa interlude and Schrödinger’s erotic life to his creative intensity. Later popular accounts convert temporal coincidence into a muse narrative. No document demonstrates that sexual experience supplied the mathematical idea. The story persists because it fits Schrödinger’s own fusion of eros, unity and creativity.

### “A Hindu/quantum mystic proved universal consciousness”

Schrödinger seriously read Vedānta and argued for unity of mind. But the claim that his equation experimentally proves Eastern mysticism is a later synthesis. He distinguished scientific results from metaphysical interpretation more carefully than this legend suggests.

### “He fled the Nazis without compromise” / “he collaborated with them”

Each is a politically simplified half-story. The 1933 departure, the 1938 conciliatory text, dismissal and flight must be held together.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

Schrödinger’s equation is basic infrastructure in atomic, molecular, condensed-matter, nuclear and chemical physics. “Schrödinger” names pictures, operators, groups, functionals, cat states and scientific prizes. The cat became a global symbol of ambiguity and quantum strangeness, largely independent of its author’s intended criticism.

*What Is Life?* remains influential in histories of molecular biology and information, though historians increasingly restore the experimental work and refugee-science networks on which it drew. His color science has received renewed specialist study. His unified-field work is chiefly historical.

Commemoration includes the Austrian Academy’s Erwin Schrödinger Prize, university buildings/rooms, plaques, stamps, currency imagery and his equation on the Alpbach grave. The 2021–22 public controversy changed commemoration: Trinity College Dublin removed his name from a lecture theater/lecture series context after scrutiny of his relationships with girls and young women. This did not alter the attribution of scientific work but changed the institutional terms of honor.

His life now supports two simultaneous reputational histories: canonical architect of quantum mechanics and subject of source-critical investigation into sexual conduct, power and the selective silence of older scientific biography. Neither record cancels the other.

## Atlas Connections

### Albert Einstein

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Schrödinger and Einstein maintained an extensive scientific correspondence and personal intellectual relationship from the 1920s through the 1950s. Their exchanges addressed wave mechanics, the interpretation of quantum theory, the EPR problem and Schrödinger’s cat, and later unified-field theory. Surviving examples include Einstein’s letter of 31 May 1928 inviting Schrödinger to visit and criticizing the Bohr–Heisenberg interpretation, Schrödinger’s confessional letter from De Panne on 19 July 1939, and Einstein’s criticism of Schrödinger’s 1947 Dublin field-theory announcement. The correspondence is catalogued in the [University of Vienna Schrödinger archive](https://services.phaidra.univie.ac.at/api/object/o%3A2085354/download).

### Niels Bohr

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Schrödinger visited Bohr’s Copenhagen institute in autumn 1926 and debated the meaning of wave mechanics and quantum jumps with Bohr at length. Their subsequent correspondence continued the interpretive dispute: a surviving archival description records Schrödinger sending Einstein copies of a letter from Bohr and his answer on 30 May 1928. Both also participated in the October 1927 Fifth Solvay Conference in Brussels. See the [University of Vienna archive catalogue](https://services.phaidra.univie.ac.at/api/object/o%3A2085354/download) and the [Solvay Institutes’ conference history](https://solvayinstitutes.be/conference/30th-solvay-conference-on-physics/).

### Werner Heisenberg

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Schrödinger directly analyzed Heisenberg’s formulation in his 1926 paper “Über das Verhältnis der Heisenberg-Born-Jordanschen Quantenmechanik zu der meinen,” demonstrating the relationship between matrix mechanics and his own wave mechanics. The two physicists also attended the Fifth Solvay Conference in Brussels in October 1927. Schrödinger was therefore the later writer in this particular connection, not merely a scientist retrospectively associated with Heisenberg.

### Paul Adrien Maurice Dirac

- **[T5]** **Documented fact:** Schrödinger and Dirac met within the international quantum-physics community, including at the Fifth Solvay Conference in Brussels in October 1927. They were jointly honored in Stockholm in December 1933 when the 1933 Nobel Prize in Physics was divided between them for new forms of atomic theory. The meeting and shared award are documented by the [Solvay Institutes](https://solvayinstitutes.be/conference/30th-solvay-conference-on-physics/) and [Nobel Prize records](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1933/summary/).

### Wolfgang Pauli

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** Schrödinger and Pauli conducted a sustained, technically detailed correspondence beginning during the creation of wave mechanics. Pauli evaluated Schrödinger’s early papers, discussed hydrogen, spin, relativistic wave equations and quantum interpretation, and remained a forthright critic and interlocutor. A surviving example is Pauli’s letter to Schrödinger dated 4 October 1939 in Zürich; their correspondence is preserved in the Vienna and CERN collections. See [University of Vienna Phaidra](https://phaidra.univie.ac.at/detail/o%3A261222) and the [CERN Pauli archive](https://cds.cern.ch/record/83595?ln=en).

### John von Neumann

- **[T3]** **Documented fact:** A letter from von Neumann to Schrödinger dated 11 April 1936 survives in the Archive for the History of Quantum Physics. It establishes direct written contact, although the available catalogue entry alone does not justify describing the relationship as a close collaboration or friendship. See the [American Philosophical Society catalogue](https://as.amphilsoc.org/repositories/2/archival_objects/26769).

### Marie Skłodowska-Curie

- **[T4]** **Documented fact:** Curie and Schrödinger were both participants at the Fifth Solvay Conference on “Electrons and Photons,” held in Brussels from 24–29 October 1927, and appear in its official group photograph. This establishes shared conference milieu, but the located evidence does not by itself establish correspondence, friendship or a substantive personal exchange. The gathering is described by the [Solvay Institutes](https://solvayinstitutes.be/conference/30th-solvay-conference-on-physics/).

### William Rowan Hamilton

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Schrödinger explicitly developed wave mechanics through Hamiltonian mechanics and the optical–mechanical analogy created by Hamilton in the nineteenth century. Schrödinger’s notebooks and 1926 wave-mechanics papers use and discuss that inherited framework, making Schrödinger the later writer in the pair. Historical analysis identifies Hamilton’s analogy as a central heuristic in Schrödinger’s derivation of wave mechanics; see the study “[The classical roots of wave mechanics](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.shpsb.2009.06.007).”

### Plato

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Schrödinger discussed Plato as part of his historical examination of Greek natural philosophy, the conflict between reason and sensory evidence, and the intellectual ancestry of modern science in lectures delivered at University College Dublin in 1948 and published as *Nature and the Greeks* (1954). This is a later author’s engagement with an ancient figure, not evidence for any direct or esoteric transmission. See the [Cambridge University Press edition](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/nature-and-the-greeks-and-science-and-humanism/9781107431836).

### Aristotle

- **[T1-]** **Documented fact:** Aristotle appears within Schrödinger’s written treatment of ancient Greek accounts of nature and scientific explanation in *Nature and the Greeks*, based on his 1948 Dublin lectures. The supported crossing is textual and retrospective only: Schrödinger was the later writer assessing an earlier thinker. See the [Cambridge University Press edition](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/nature-and-the-greeks-and-science-and-humanism/9781107431836).
