# José de San Martín: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

This dossier reconstructs José de San Martín’s life from surviving civil, military, governmental, epistolary, and historiographical evidence. Dates before his public career are sometimes uncertain because no securely authenticated baptismal record is presently available.

Evidentiary labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by a surviving contemporary official document, authenticated correspondence, civil record, or similarly direct evidence.
- **Reported fact:** supported by contemporary testimony or responsible historical reconstruction but not independently established by a surviving primary record.
- **Disputed claim:** competing interpretations exist or the evidence is incomplete.
- **Allegation or theory:** a claim advanced by identifiable writers without sufficient evidence for acceptance as fact.
- **Rumor or myth:** folklore, patriotic legend, hostile mythology, or a story whose documentary basis is absent or demonstrably late.

---

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** José Francisco de San Martín y Matorras.

**Common name:** José de San Martín.

**Titles and offices:**

- Lieutenant colonel and later colonel in the United Provinces of the Río de la Plata.
- Founder and commander of the Regiment of Mounted Grenadiers.
- Commander of the Army of the North.
- Governor-intendant of Cuyo.
- General-in-chief of the Army of the Andes.
- Captain general of Chile.
- General-in-chief of the United Liberation Army of Chile.
- Generalissimo of Peru.
- Protector of the Liberty of Peru, 3 August 1821–20 September 1822.
- Brigadier general of the Argentine Confederation, the title recorded in his French death certificate.

**Honorifics and posthumous epithets:**

- *El Libertador*.
- *Libertador de Argentina, Chile y Perú*.
- *Padre de la Patria* in Argentine civic usage.
- *Fundador de la Libertad del Perú*, used in his lifetime and recorded in his death certificate.
- *El Santo de la Espada*, popularized by Ricardo Rojas’s twentieth-century biography; it is a hagiographic literary epithet, not a title San Martín used.

**Birth:** Conventionally 25 February 1778, Yapeyú, Guaraní Missions, Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata, Spanish Empire; now Yapeyú, Corrientes Province, Argentina.

**Date qualification:** No uncontested contemporary baptismal certificate has been produced. The date 25 February 1778 is supported retrospectively by family tradition, later records, and the precise age entered in the 1850 French civil death certificate. The latter calls him seventy-two years, five months, and twenty-three days old.

**Death:** 17 August 1850, approximately 3:00 p.m., 105 Grande Rue, Boulogne-sur-Mer, France.

**Burial history:**

- Initially placed in the crypt of the Basilica of Notre-Dame de l’Immaculée-Conception at Boulogne-sur-Mer.
- Later transferred to the Balcarce family vault at Brunoy.
- Repatriated to Buenos Aires in 1880 and entombed in a chapel of the Metropolitan Cathedral.
- His tomb’s position beside, rather than within the central liturgical body of, the cathedral has generated a persistent Masonic-exclusion legend; the architectural arrangement does not by itself prove such an exclusion.

## Family and Ancestry

### Legally and contemporaneously recognized parents

**Father:** Juan de San Martín Gómez, born in Cervatos de la Cueza, Palencia, Spain, a Spanish army officer and administrator. He served in the Río de la Plata and became lieutenant-governor of Yapeyú and its mission district.

**Mother:** Gregoria Matorras del Ser, often styled Gregoria Matorras, born in Paredes de Nava, Palencia, Spain.

San Martín consistently belonged publicly and legally to this family. His French death registration describes him as the son of Colonel Juan de San Martín, governor of Misiones, and Francisca/Gregoria Matorras; the record’s “Francisca” appears to be a registrar’s or informant’s naming error. His 1844 testament and family records do not identify alternative parents.

### Siblings

The five recognized children of Juan de San Martín and Gregoria Matorras were:

1. María Elena de San Martín.
2. Manuel Tadeo de San Martín.
3. Juan Fermín Rafael de San Martín.
4. Justo Rufino de San Martín.
5. José Francisco de San Martín, the youngest.

His brothers pursued Spanish military careers. This fact complicates later nationalist portrayals of the family as uniformly committed to Spanish-American independence: José changed political allegiance, while his brothers’ careers remained tied to the Spanish monarchy.

### Marriage

San Martín married **María de los Remedios de Escalada y de la Quintana** in Buenos Aires on 12 September 1812. He was thirty-four; she was fourteen. The marriage connected him to a wealthy and politically influential Buenos Aires merchant-patrician family. Her parents were Antonio José de Escalada and Tomasa de la Quintana.

The union provided San Martín with entry into elite Buenos Aires networks at a moment when he was a recently arrived officer whose twenty-two-year service in Spain made some revolutionaries suspicious of his loyalties.

Remedios spent much of the marriage separated from him by his campaigns. She supported patriotic fundraising and the Mendoza women’s contribution to the Army of the Andes. Her health deteriorated, probably from tuberculosis. She returned to Buenos Aires and died there on 3 August 1823, aged twenty-five. San Martín, then in Mendoza and subject to political danger if he returned, did not reach her before her death.

### Child

The marriage produced one recognized child:

**Mercedes Tomasa de San Martín y Escalada**, born in Mendoza on 24 August 1816; died at Brunoy, France, on 28 February 1875.

San Martín took Mercedes to Europe in 1824. She studied languages, music, drawing, painting, and household management at a Brussels boarding school. His surviving letters show that her upbringing governed many of his residence and financial decisions. In 1825 he composed the *Máximas para Merceditas*, twelve precepts emphasizing truthfulness, charity, respect for others’ beliefs, tenderness toward the poor, and humane treatment of servants.

Mercedes married the Argentine diplomat **Mariano Severo Balcarce** in Paris in 1832. Their daughters were María Mercedes and Josefa Dominga Balcarce. San Martín lived for long periods with the Balcarce household.

### Claimed relationships outside marriage

**Rosa Campusano:** Later Peruvian and Argentine accounts describe San Martín as having an intimate relationship in Lima with Rosa Campusano Cornejo, a Guayaquil-born patriot and salon figure. Campusano’s political activity and association with the independence movement are historical; the precise nature and duration of her relationship with San Martín rest mainly on recollection and later biographical tradition. It should be classified as a **reported but incompletely documented relationship**, not a proven secret marriage or stable partnership.

No other children have been securely established.

## The Parentage Controversy

### The conventional genealogy

**Documented legal identity:** José was treated throughout his life as the legitimate son of Juan de San Martín and Gregoria Matorras. His military files, marriage, testamentary identity, family correspondence, and French death registration are consistent with that status.

### Diego de Alvear–Rosa Guarú theory

**Allegation or theory:** Hugo Chumbita and José Ignacio García Hamilton publicized the hypothesis that San Martín was the biological son of naval officer Diego de Alvear y Ponce de León and an Indigenous Guaraní woman commonly called Rosa Guarú, and that Juan and Gregoria raised him as their son.

The theory draws chiefly upon:

- A memorandum attributed to María Joaquina de Alvear, a daughter of Carlos María de Alvear, calling San Martín a natural son of her grandfather Diego.
- Oral traditions in the Yapeyú region about an Indigenous woman, Rosa Guarú, caring for the child.
- Interpretations of San Martín’s complexion and physical descriptions.
- Claims that Diego de Alvear could have been in the mission region near the relevant time.

Evidence against treating it as established fact includes:

- The absence of a contemporary acknowledgment by Diego de Alvear, Rosa Guarú, Juan de San Martín, Gregoria Matorras, or José.
- Uncertainty over the date, provenance, and accuracy of María Joaquina’s statement.
- Chronological and travel disputes concerning Diego de Alvear’s presence.
- The consistent legal record identifying Juan and Gregoria as the parents.
- The failure to complete a publicly accepted comparative DNA test.

Courts became involved in disputes over opening family tombs for genetic testing, but no conclusive DNA finding has settled the matter. The proper status is **unresolved modern parentage theory**, historically important because it contests the Europeanized national iconography of San Martín, but not documentary fact.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1778–1789

Yapeyú had been a Jesuit mission before the expulsion of the Society of Jesus in 1767. When San Martín was born, it was a frontier administrative and military center populated largely by Guaraní communities. His earliest environment was therefore not the later urban and overwhelmingly European setting sometimes implied by patriotic iconography.

The family left Yapeyú during his early childhood. Accounts place them first in Buenos Aires and, by 1784 or 1785, in Spain after Juan de San Martín’s return from American service. They settled at different times in Cádiz and Málaga.

Claims that José studied in Buenos Aires at the school associated with Francisco Javier Argerich are possible but not securely documented. The frequently repeated assertion that he attended Madrid’s Real Seminario de Nobles is also disputed; his early formal schooling remains incompletely reconstructed.

---

## Education and Formation in Spain

San Martín’s most certain education was military and practical.

He entered the **Regiment of Murcia** as a cadet on 21 July 1789, aged eleven. His service record shows training in infantry drill, fortification, weapons, battlefield administration, and the professional culture of the late Bourbon army.

Formative influences included:

- The Bourbon Spanish army’s professional reforms.
- Combat against Muslim North African forces, revolutionary France, Britain, Portugal, and Napoleonic France.
- The political crisis produced by the abdications of Charles IV and Ferdinand VII.
- The Cádiz constitutional and Atlantic revolutionary milieu.
- Spanish Enlightenment political language.
- Secret patriotic networks formed by Spanish-American officers in London and Cádiz.
- British military experience observed during the Peninsular War.

He read French and had access to a substantial military and political library. His later donation of books to the National Library of Peru included works on warfare, history, geography, law, political thought, and the sciences.

No single person can be securely designated his principal teacher. His mentors were institutional and military: superior officers, experienced campaign commanders, and the transatlantic independence network.

---

## Spanish Military Career, 1789–1811

### North Africa and the War of the Pyrenees

San Martín’s service record places him in the defense of **Melilla** and under fire during attacks on **Oran** in 1791. He fought against Revolutionary France in the War of the Pyrenees, including operations in the eastern Pyrenees and Roussillon.

He became a second lieutenant in 1793. Subsequent promotions reflected long service rather than aristocratic patronage alone.

### Maritime and Mediterranean service

During war with Britain he served in or around Spanish naval operations. He was aboard the frigate *Santa Dorotea* when it was captured by HMS *Lion* in 1798. He survived captivity and returned to Spanish service.

He later campaigned in Portugal during the War of the Oranges and served at Cádiz and in southern Spain.

### Peninsular War

After Napoleon compelled the Spanish royal abdications and installed Joseph Bonaparte in 1808, San Martín joined the Spanish resistance. His service included:

- **Arjonilla**, where his small detachment attacked French cavalry and he received recognition.
- **Battle of Bailén**, 19 July 1808, a major Spanish victory over a Napoleonic army.
- Operations under the Central Junta and later Spanish authorities.
- **Battle of Albuera**, 16 May 1811, against Marshal Soult’s army.

He received decorations and rose to lieutenant colonel. His French death certificate and later patriotic narratives often compress this twenty-two-year Spanish career, but it was indispensable to his later effectiveness: he reached the Río de la Plata as one of the revolution’s few officers with sustained European combat and staff experience.

### Leaving Spanish service

In 1811 San Martín requested retirement or separation from the Spanish army, went to Portugal, and then traveled to London. The precise sequence and the assistance he received remain partly obscure.

**British-agent theory:** His London stop, contact with British or British-connected merchants, travel aboard a British vessel, and adoption of a strategic plan compatible with British commercial interests generated claims that he was a British intelligence agent.

- **Documented:** Britain favored opening Spanish-American markets; British merchants and naval officers assisted several independence movements; San Martín traveled from London on the British packet *George Canning*.
- **Not documented:** No employment file, salary record, oath, intelligence instruction, or British government command establishing San Martín as an agent has been found.
- **Assessment:** Political and logistical contact is established; formal British agency is an **unverified theory**.

---

## London, the Lautaro Network, and Return to America

In London, San Martín associated with Spanish-American revolutionaries including Carlos María de Alvear, José Matías Zapiola, Andrés Bello, and others connected to circles previously shaped by Francisco de Miranda.

They formed or joined the **Lodge of Rational Knights**, a secret political association committed to Spanish-American emancipation. San Martín later helped establish the **Lautaro Lodge** in Buenos Aires.

### Was San Martín a Freemason?

**Documented:** He participated in lodges or lodge-like secret societies employing initiation, grades, oaths, and organizational forms resembling Freemasonry.

**Disputed:** Whether these bodies were regular Masonic lodges subject to recognized Masonic authority. Bartolomé Mitre, himself a Freemason, described Lautaro as essentially a secret political society. Later Masonic organizations claimed San Martín as a brother and “great initiate.” Modern scholarship remains divided.

The safest formulation is that San Martín belonged to a **secret revolutionary lodge operating through Masonic forms**; formal regular Masonic affiliation is disputed.

San Martín, Alvear, Zapiola, Francisco Chilavert, Eduardo Kailitz and others sailed from Britain aboard the *George Canning* and reached Buenos Aires on 9 March 1812.

---

## Buenos Aires and the Mounted Grenadiers, 1812–1814

The revolutionary government accepted San Martín’s services but initially watched him closely because of his long Spanish career and lack of involvement in the May Revolution.

On 16 March 1812 the government authorized creation of the **Regiment of Mounted Grenadiers**. San Martín trained it in European cavalry discipline. Its institutional culture emphasized orderly conduct, unit honor, weapons proficiency, and penalties for behavior thought dishonorable.

He married Remedios de Escalada in September.

San Martín and the Lautaro Lodge participated in the political movement of 8 October 1812 that displaced the First Triumvirate and installed the Second Triumvirate. This was not a nonpolitical military career: from his first year in Buenos Aires he participated in organized pressure on governments.

### Combat of San Lorenzo

On 3 February 1813 San Martín’s grenadiers attacked a royalist landing party commanded by Antonio Zabala near the Convent of San Carlos at San Lorenzo.

His horse was killed or fell, trapping him. Grenadier **Juan Bautista Cabral** helped free him and was mortally wounded. Another soldier, **Juan Bautista Baigorria**, killed or drove off a royalist threatening San Martín.

The action was small—lasting minutes and involving a few hundred combatants—but strategically protected Paraná traffic and established the regiment’s reputation. It was San Martín’s only battle on what is now Argentine soil.

### Cabral’s last words

The familiar phrase “Muero contento; hemos batido al enemigo” is a **patriotic tradition**. Cabral’s rescue and death are well supported; the exact deathbed sentence appears through later accounts and cannot be authenticated verbatim.

---

## Command of the Army of the North, 1814

San Martín was ordered to reinforce and then replace Manuel Belgrano after the defeats at Vilcapugio and Ayohuma. They met around Yatasto in January 1814, although the traditional “Posta de Yatasto” setting and details have been debated.

Their correspondence shows mutual respect. San Martín reorganized forces near Tucumán and developed the strategic conclusion that repeated direct invasion of Upper Peru was unlikely to defeat the royalist center at Lima. Instead, he proposed:

1. Defending the northern frontier through local forces.
2. Building an army in Cuyo.
3. Crossing the Andes into Chile.
4. Securing Pacific naval power.
5. Attacking Peru by sea.

Ill health contributed to his withdrawal to Córdoba, but political maneuvering in Buenos Aires and his desire for command in Cuyo were also important.

---

## Governor-Intendant of Cuyo and the Army of the Andes, 1814–1817

San Martín became governor-intendant of Cuyo in September 1814, governing Mendoza, San Juan, and San Luis.

### Government and mobilization

He turned Cuyo into a military-production state:

- Conducted censuses and requisitions.
- Increased taxation and solicited compulsory and voluntary contributions.
- Organized workshops for arms, gunpowder, uniforms, saddles, and artillery.
- Used the skills of friar and engineer **Luis Beltrán**.
- Recruited free men, enslaved men promised freedom through service, rural laborers, exiles, Chilean refugees, and convicts.
- Confiscated or requisitioned livestock, food, metals, cloth, and transport.
- Conducted intelligence and misinformation operations known as the *guerra de zapa*.

The mobilization enjoyed substantial provincial participation but was not purely voluntary. Coercive recruitment, emergency taxation, requisition, surveillance, and restrictions on opponents were normal components of his wartime government.

### Political struggle

San Martín resisted an attempted replacement after the fall of Supreme Director Carlos María de Alvear in 1815. Cuyo’s cabildos supported his continuation. His power rested on local support, the army, lodge networks, and alliances in Buenos Aires.

He pressed Juan Martín de Pueyrredón and delegates to the Congress of Tucumán to secure independence and support the continental campaign. Argentina declared independence on 9 July 1816.

### Family life in Mendoza

Remedios joined him in Mendoza. Mercedes was born there in August 1816. The women of Mendoza contributed money and made a ceremonial flag for the Army of the Andes, though later art inflated a complex community mobilization into a genteel domestic tableau.

---

## Crossing of the Andes and Chilean Campaign, 1817–1818

Approximately 4,000 combat troops, with auxiliaries, artillery, horses, and pack animals, crossed the Andes in January–February 1817 by several routes. San Martín and Bernardo O’Higgins led the principal columns through Los Patos; Juan Gregorio de Las Heras used the Uspallata route. Smaller columns dispersed royalist defenses.

The crossing involved cold, altitude sickness, animal losses, supply shortages, and difficult coordination. It was an elaborate military operation, not a single mass column.

### Chacabuco

On 12 February 1817 the patriot army defeated Rafael Maroto’s royalists at Chacabuco and entered Santiago.

The Chilean authorities offered San Martín supreme political power. He declined; Bernardo O’Higgins became Supreme Director. San Martín remained commander of the combined military enterprise and exercised major political influence.

### The Carrera conflict

The Chilean independence movement was divided between supporters of O’Higgins and the Carrera family. José Miguel Carrera opposed the O’Higgins–San Martín ascendancy from exile.

Juan José and Luis Carrera were captured in the Río de la Plata and executed at Mendoza on 8 April 1818 after criminal proceedings connected to their attempted return and conspiracy.

- **Documented:** The executions occurred under a Cuyo administration politically aligned with San Martín and O’Higgins.
- **Disputed responsibility:** San Martín did not personally sit as trial judge and was then campaigning in Chile, but his political system regarded the Carreras as dangerous rivals. Historians disagree over whether he directed, approved, anticipated, or merely accepted the executions.
- **Not established:** Claims that San Martín personally ordered a predetermined judicial murder without process.

José Miguel Carrera was later captured and executed in Mendoza in 1821, while San Martín was in Peru. The broader conflict permanently divided Chilean and River Plate historical memory.

### Cancha Rayada

On 19 March 1818 Mariano Osorio’s royalists surprised the patriot army at Cancha Rayada. The patriot command lost control, units dispersed, and Santiago feared catastrophe. Las Heras preserved an organized division, permitting rapid reconstruction.

This was one of San Martín’s most serious battlefield failures.

### Maipú

On 5 April 1818 San Martín decisively defeated Osorio at Maipú. The victory secured central Chile and made a maritime expedition to Peru feasible.

The celebrated “Embrace of Maipú” between O’Higgins and San Martín rests on contemporary knowledge that they met after the victory, but the exact pose and dialogue belong substantially to later visual and patriotic representation.

---

## Naval Strategy and the Expedition to Peru, 1818–1820

Chile lacked sufficient naval power to attack Peru. O’Higgins’s government created a navy and employed the British naval officer **Thomas Cochrane**, whose relations with San Martín became increasingly hostile.

Cochrane favored aggressive naval action and accused San Martín of excessive delay, political ambition, and mishandling prize money. San Martín distrusted Cochrane’s independence, financial demands, and willingness to subordinate political strategy to naval operations.

The expedition was financed primarily by Chile under severe fiscal pressure, with contributions from the Río de la Plata and captured resources. The River Plate government ordered San Martín to return his army during the civil crisis of 1819–1820. He declined to abandon the continental campaign, resigned nominally, and obtained his officers’ reaffirmation of his command in the **Act of Rancagua**, 2 April 1820.

Interpretations differ:

- Sanmartinian historiography treats the act as preservation of a continental army from civil war.
- Critics characterize it as military autonomy from lawful civil authority.
- The record establishes both: San Martín refused political redeployment and relied on his army’s collective confirmation.

The expedition sailed from Valparaíso on 20 August 1820 and landed at Paracas/Pisco in September.

---

## Peru Campaign and Protectorate, 1820–1822

### Strategy

San Martín generally avoided a frontal assault on the large royalist army. He combined:

- Coastal movement and blockade.
- Negotiations with royalist commanders.
- Propaganda and recruitment.
- Encouragement of desertion.
- Expeditions into the interior.
- Economic pressure on Lima.
- Political offers of constitutional monarchy.

Royalist forces evacuated Lima in July 1821, retreating into the highlands. San Martín entered the capital.

### Independence

A Lima cabildo meeting signed an independence act on 15 July 1821. San Martín proclaimed Peruvian independence publicly on 28 July. The famous proclamation—“El Perú es desde este momento libre e independiente…”—is contemporary, although paintings of the ceremony are later reconstructions.

### Protector

On 3 August 1821 San Martín united supreme political and military command in himself under the title **Protector of the Liberty of Peru**. This was an executive dictatorship justified as temporary wartime government, not an elected presidency.

His government:

- Created ministries and administrative institutions.
- Established the Order of the Sun.
- Founded the National Library of Peru and donated books.
- Promoted a patriotic press, theater, symbols, anthem, flag, and civic ritual.
- Abolished the Indigenous tribute in the territory it controlled.
- Prohibited compulsory personal service without contract.
- Ordered that Indigenous inhabitants be called “Peruvians,” not “Indians” or “naturals.”
- Declared children born to enslaved mothers after 28 July 1821 free.

The slavery measure was **free-womb legislation**, not immediate abolition. Existing enslaved people remained enslaved unless freed through military service, manumission, or later law. Complete Peruvian abolition did not occur until 1854.

### Monarchical project

San Martín and his minister **Bernardo de Monteagudo** favored a constitutional monarchy, possibly headed by a European prince. Diplomatic missions explored candidates. Their arguments emphasized regional fragmentation, civil war, and the absence of stable republican institutions.

Republican critics regarded the project as elitist, secretive, and incompatible with independence. No prince accepted, and the plan lacked sufficient Peruvian support.

San Martín’s political thought combined:

- American independence.
- Strong executive authority.
- Constitutional limitations.
- Social hierarchy and gradual reform.
- Suspicion of immediate mass democracy.
- Opposition to military participation in domestic civil war—though his own career involved coups, secret societies, and concentrated wartime government.

### Monteagudo and repression

Monteagudo directed harsh policies against peninsular Spaniards, including expulsions, confiscations, restrictions on residence and trade, and political surveillance. Supporters described these as security measures against counterrevolution; opponents described them as persecution and expropriation.

San Martín cannot be separated from the administration he headed, although the degree to which he initiated individual measures remains case-specific. During his absence for Guayaquil, Peruvian opposition forced Monteagudo from power.

### Military limitations

The royalist army retained the highlands. The patriot defeat at Ica/La Macacona in April 1822 exposed the protectorate’s military weakness. Finances deteriorated; troops went unpaid; relations with Cochrane collapsed; and political opposition increased.

Cochrane seized money stored aboard San Martín’s ship at Ancón, claiming it as unpaid naval wages. This was a documented financial confrontation, not a proven personal-embezzlement case against San Martín.

## Guayaquil and Simón Bolívar, July 1822

San Martín met Simón Bolívar privately at Guayaquil on 26 and 27 July 1822. No mutually approved transcript exists.

A report by Bolívar’s secretary José Gabriel Pérez to Antonio José de Sucre, correspondence written shortly afterward, and later letters permit partial reconstruction. Subjects included:

- Military aid required to finish the war in Peru.
- Command of combined forces.
- The status of Guayaquil.
- Republican versus monarchical constitutional arrangements.
- San Martín’s willingness to serve under Bolívar.
- The future of the liberation war.

San Martín later wrote that he offered to serve under Bolívar and withdrew because Bolívar would not accept a divided or subordinate command arrangement. Bolívar’s correspondence presents San Martín as politically weakened and intending to retire.

### “Two suns cannot shine in the same sky”

This sentence, often treated as Bolívar’s explanation, is a **later literary or legendary formula**, not a securely authenticated quotation from either private meeting.

### Meaning of the conference

The claim that Bolívar forced San Martín out at Guayaquil is an interpretation, not a documented ultimatum. San Martín already faced military shortages, Peruvian opposition, Chilean instability, River Plate civil conflict, illness, and weakened authority. Guayaquil clarified that Bolívar would lead the final campaign.

---

## Resignation and Departure, 1822–1824

San Martín returned to Lima, installed the first Peruvian Constituent Congress, and resigned before it on 20 September 1822. He left Peru that night.

He traveled through Chile to Mendoza. Remedios died in Buenos Aires in August 1823. Political hostility associated with Bernardino Rivadavia’s circle, investigations of correspondence, and fear that he would be drawn into factional struggle delayed his return. He reached Buenos Aires late in 1823, recovered Mercedes, and sailed for Europe in February 1824.

Claims that he “fled” directly from Bolívar are too simple: approximately eighteen months separated Guayaquil from his European departure, during which he resigned publicly, returned across the continent, and dealt with his wife’s death and daughter’s custody.

---

## European Exile, 1824–1850

### London, France, and Brussels

San Martín arrived in Britain and then lived chiefly in Brussels from 1824 to 1830. Political suspicion complicated residence in Restoration France. Brussels was comparatively affordable and allowed Mercedes’s education.

He suffered financial uncertainty:

- Delays or defaults in salary and pension payments from Peru.
- Income problems involving small properties and rents.
- Poorly performing securities and investments.
- The expenses of Mercedes’s schooling and European life.

He was not destitute throughout exile, nor did he possess the kind of enormous fortune sometimes alleged. His position varied. Assistance, family resources, arrears, property income, and later his son-in-law’s household supported him.

### Attempted return in 1829

San Martín sailed to the Río de la Plata during war with Brazil and arrived off Buenos Aires in February 1829. Governor Manuel Dorrego had been overthrown and executed by Juan Lavalle; civil war was underway.

San Martín remained aboard or in the roads, went to Montevideo, and returned to Europe without re-entering Argentine political life. Lavalle’s camp reportedly offered him authority; he refused to become identified with a faction.

The episode became central to the myth of the disinterested soldier. It also reflected political calculation: any landing by a figure of his stature could be used by one side.

### France and Grand Bourg

After the Belgian Revolution of 1830 he moved to France. He resided in Paris and at a country property at **Grand Bourg**, near Évry. The purchase was facilitated by his improved finances and his association with the wealthy Spanish-American banker and former comrade **Alejandro Aguado, Marquis of Las Marismas**.

Aguado was a significant friend and financial adviser. Some accounts suggest San Martín benefited from Aguado’s investment management and inheritance arrangements; exact amounts are inconsistently reported. There is no credible evidence that San Martín secretly controlled a great commercial empire.

Visitors included Argentine, Chilean, and Peruvian diplomats, travelers, political figures, and young Americans seeking the retired general’s recollections.

### Rosas and foreign intervention

San Martín corresponded with Juan Manuel de Rosas and supported the Argentine Confederation against the French blockade and later Anglo-French intervention in the Río de la Plata.

In his testament he left his curved saber to Rosas “as proof of the satisfaction” he felt at Rosas’s defense of the republic against foreign pretensions. This is a documented political endorsement on external sovereignty.

It does not prove agreement with every aspect of Rosas’s domestic government. Liberal and anti-Rosas writers minimized the bequest; revisionist writers sometimes converted it into blanket ideological identity. San Martín’s surviving letters show strong approval of resistance to European intervention and distaste for civil war, while providing less evidence about internal repression.

### Boulogne-sur-Mer

Revolutionary unrest in Paris in 1848 and declining health encouraged the family to move temporarily to Boulogne-sur-Mer. They rented rooms at 105 Grande Rue from Adolphe Gérard.

San Martín died there on 17 August 1850. The civil declaration was made the next day by Chilean chargé d’affaires Francisco Javier Rosales and Gérard.

## Military Doctrine, Institutions, and Projects

### Regiment of Mounted Grenadiers

San Martín created the regiment in 1812 as a disciplined professional cavalry corps. It fought at San Lorenzo and in the campaigns of the Andes, Chile, Peru, Ecuador, and the final independence war. The modern Argentine regiment, reconstituted in 1903, serves as presidential guard and custodian of his saber.

### Army of the Andes

The Army of the Andes was simultaneously:

- A multinational military organization.
- A Cuyo state-building project.
- A labor, supply, intelligence, and manufacturing system.
- A coalition of River Plate soldiers, Chilean exiles, Black troops, enslaved recruits promised freedom, Indigenous and mestizo laborers, craftsmen, women fundraisers, clerics, muleteers, and foreign officers.

San Martín supplied overall strategy and command. The later “great man” narrative frequently obscured the institution’s collective basis.

### Continental strategy

The crossing-and-seaborne-invasion plan is commonly called San Martín’s “continental plan.” The strategic idea did not arise from a single surviving master document and may have been discussed by several revolutionaries. San Martín’s distinctive achievement was converting it into a sustained operational program.

### Intelligence and deception

The *guerra de zapa* used agents, false information, correspondence, sympathetic Chileans, guides, deserters, and route concealment. Manuel Rodríguez’s guerrilla and intelligence activity inside Chile aided the campaign, though his later conflict with O’Higgins and subsequent killing created a hostile tradition depicting the San Martín–O’Higgins system as responsible for eliminating rivals. Direct evidence that San Martín ordered Rodríguez’s 1818 killing is lacking.

### National Library of Peru

The library was decreed in 1821 and inaugurated in 1822. San Martín donated part of his own collection. Twenty-six surviving bibliographical units associated with that donation have been declared Peruvian cultural patrimony.

### Symbols and honors

Under his protectorate Peru adopted early versions of its flag, coat of arms, anthem, and Order of the Sun. Some were later altered or abolished, reflecting republican resistance to monarchical and aristocratic forms.

## Wealth, Property, and Financial Relationships

San Martín’s finances passed through distinct phases:

1. **Spanish officer:** regular but limited military income.
2. **Buenos Aires marriage:** connection to the prosperous Escalada family.
3. **Cuyo and campaigns:** official salary and military allowances; no reliable proof of large-scale personal enrichment.
4. **Peru:** salary, honors, and grants were voted or promised, but payments became irregular.
5. **European exile:** dependence on arrears, rent, securities, family resources, and financial advice.
6. **Aguado period:** improved circumstances enabled residence at Grand Bourg.
7. **Final years:** lived in a comfortable multigenerational household, not in palatial wealth.

His testament named Mercedes his universal heir. He attempted to prevent disputes and directed that his funeral occur without ostentation. He also asked that his heart be taken to Buenos Aires, a wish not literally carried out as a separate transfer.

There is no substantiated case of fraud, money laundering, secret corporate structures, or personal embezzlement. Financial controversy attaches principally to wartime requisitions, confiscated royalist and Spanish property, prize disputes with Cochrane, unpaid military funds, and politically distributed honors.

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Monarchy and republic

San Martín was not consistently a republican in the later democratic sense. He supported independence but doubted the immediate viability of decentralized representative government amid war and social fragmentation. In Peru he backed constitutional monarchy and a powerful executive.

His language also incorporated liberal concepts:

- National sovereignty.
- Constitutional government.
- Equality of civic designation.
- Freedom of the press within political limits.
- Gradual abolition.
- Rejection of hereditary colonial subordination.

### Social order

His reforms coexisted with hierarchy and coercion. He promoted free-womb emancipation while retaining existing slavery; proclaimed Indigenous citizenship while presiding over military requisitions and labor demands; and established representative institutions while exercising emergency personal authority.

### Religion

He was baptized and married within Catholic practice, used Catholic ceremonies in public government, invoked Providence, and received Catholic burial. Claims that he was anti-Catholic or secretly rejected Christianity are unsupported. His *Máximas* instructed Mercedes to respect other people’s religions, indicating toleration rather than documented irreligion.

### Enlightenment and transatlantic thought

His library and policies suggest familiarity with Enlightenment, constitutional, military, and historical literature. Claims that a single thinker—Rousseau, Napoleon, Washington, or British constitutionalism—determined his ideology exceed the evidence. His political outlook was eclectic and adapted to war.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Bernardo O’Higgins

Principal Chilean collaborator, political ally, and friend. They worked together from the post-Rancagua exile through Chacabuco, Maipú, and preparation of the Peru expedition. Their correspondence continued in exile. Their alliance was also a political faction opposed by Carrera supporters.

### Manuel Belgrano

Predecessor in command of the Army of the North. Their meeting and letters document professional respect and shared concern for independence. San Martín adopted a different strategic approach rather than treating Belgrano as a failed rival.

### Juan Martín de Pueyrredón

Supreme Director who supplied the Army of the Andes under severe financial constraints. San Martín’s letters pressed him relentlessly for men, animals, equipment, and money. Their relationship was essential to the crossing.

### Martín Miguel de Güemes

Güemes and his Salta-based forces defended the northern frontier while San Martín pursued the Chile–Peru strategy. Their cooperation was strategic and epistolary. San Martín supported Güemes’s defensive role, although Buenos Aires political relations with Güemes fluctuated.

### Carlos María de Alvear

Fellow Spanish-trained officer, companion on the *George Canning*, and fellow lodge member. Their early collaboration became rivalry. Alvear’s rise, fall, and attempts to displace San Martín shaped Cuyo politics. Later parentage mythology made them alleged half-brothers, but that relationship is unproven.

### Simón Bolívar

They corresponded, coordinated indirectly through the Quito campaign, and met only at Guayaquil in 1822. San Martín withdrew; Bolívar and Sucre completed the war in Peru. Later national historiographies alternately portrayed them as complementary liberators or irreconcilable rivals.

### Antonio José de Sucre

San Martín’s Peruvian government sent troops commanded by Andrés de Santa Cruz to assist Sucre’s Quito campaign. Sucre received José Gabriel Pérez’s contemporary report of the Guayaquil meeting. No extended personal partnership between San Martín and Sucre is documented.

### Thomas Cochrane

Essential naval collaborator and bitter rival. Their conflict concerned operational control, speed, money, prizes, and political authority.

### Bernardo de Monteagudo

Minister, ideological collaborator, propagandist, and principal administrator of the protectorate’s strongest security measures. His forced removal during San Martín’s absence showed the government’s political fragility.

### Alejandro Aguado

Spanish-American comrade turned Paris banker; close friend, adviser, and important figure in San Martín’s later financial security.

### Tomás Guido

Diplomat, confidant, strategist, and one of San Martín’s most important long-term correspondents. Guido’s 1816 memorandum supported the Chile–Peru strategy.

### Juan Manuel de Rosas

They did not form an operational partnership, but maintained political correspondence. San Martín publicly supported Rosas’s defense against European intervention and bequeathed him the saber.

### Enemies and rivals

These included royalist commanders José de la Serna, Joaquín de la Pezuela, Mariano Osorio, Rafael Maroto, and José Canterac; domestic opponents associated at various moments with Alvear, Carrera, Rivadavia, and Peruvian republican factions; and estranged collaborators such as Cochrane.

## Controversies, Political Violence, and Unresolved Questions

### Use of coups and secret organizations

San Martín participated in the lodge-backed overthrow of the First Triumvirate in October 1812. His later reputation as wholly removed from politics is therefore inaccurate. He avoided later civil wars but used organized military-political pressure when he considered revolutionary strategy endangered.

### Forced recruitment and slavery

Black and mixed-race soldiers formed a substantial component of his armies. Enslaved men could be acquired from owners or recruited in return for conditional freedom. The system opened routes to emancipation but also treated enslaved people as military manpower and compensated or negotiated with owners. Describing this simply as abolition sanitizes its coercive and gradual character.

### Confiscation and taxation

Cuyo and Peru were financed through extraordinary levies, requisition, seizure of royalist assets, and pressure on merchants. Documentation supports wartime state extraction. Claims of personal theft require separate evidence and have not been established.

### Carrera executions

San Martín’s structural and political responsibility remains debated. He benefited from removal of rivals, but direct proof that he secretly dictated the Mendoza judgments has not been conclusively produced.

### Manuel Rodríguez’s death

Rodríguez was killed at Til-Til in May 1818 while in military custody. O’Higgins’s government bears immediate political responsibility; the killing is widely considered extrajudicial. Claims that San Martín personally ordered it remain allegations.

### Exile of Spaniards from Peru

The protectorate imposed collective penalties and expulsions on peninsular Spaniards. These were documented coercive policies affecting property, livelihood, residence, and family life. Monteagudo was the most visible administrator, but San Martín was head of government.

### Constitutional monarchy

Republican opponents accused him of intending to crown himself or establish a foreign dynasty. His pursuit of a European constitutional monarch is documented; evidence that he planned to make himself king is absent.

### British puppet or agent

British assistance and commercial interests were real; formal intelligence control is unproven. The theory became prominent in nationalist and Hispanist polemics because the independence wars weakened Spanish monopoly and expanded British trade.

### Abandonment of Peru

Hostile accounts say San Martín deserted an unfinished war. Documentary context shows that he resigned amid military stalemate and political loss of authority and judged that Bolívar possessed the necessary forces. Whether withdrawal was prudent sacrifice, political defeat, or both is interpretation.

### Rosas

Liberal national historiography sometimes minimized San Martín’s favorable correspondence with Rosas; revisionist historiography amplified it. The saber bequest and praise for resistance to blockade are facts. A comprehensive endorsement of Rosas’s internal rule is not demonstrated.

## Health, Injuries, Medication, and Cause of Death

San Martín suffered recurrent, sometimes disabling illness. Retrospective diagnoses must remain tentative because no autopsy and few detailed medical notes survive.

Documented or repeatedly reported symptoms include:

- Wounds and traumatic injuries during military service.
- Rheumatic pain.
- Asthmatic or respiratory attacks.
- Recurrent severe abdominal or gastric pain.
- Hematemesis—vomiting blood—suggestive of peptic ulcer or another upper gastrointestinal disorder.
- Hemorrhoids and constipation.
- Insomnia and nervous exhaustion.
- A serious infectious episode described retrospectively as cholera or typhoid-like illness.
- Cataracts and severe visual decline.
- A carriage accident causing shoulder dislocation and erysipelas.
- Failed or only partly successful eye surgery by ophthalmologist Louis-Auguste Desmarres or the Sichel clinic, depending on the account.

He used laudanum or opium medicinally for pain. Some medical histories call this an addiction. The evidence establishes repeated therapeutic use and possible dependence; it is insufficient to reconstruct a modern substance-use diagnosis with certainty.

### Death

He became acutely ill after eating and died on 17 August 1850. Retrospective proposals include:

- Gastrointestinal hemorrhage from chronic ulcer.
- Cardiovascular collapse.
- Complications of pulmonary tuberculosis.
- A ruptured aneurysm or other cardiac event.

The civil death certificate records the event but no medical cause. Any precise diagnosis is therefore **retrospective and unconfirmed**.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “Father of three nations”

The phrase reflects his leading role in the independence of Argentina, Chile, and Peru, but compresses multinational wars into one biography. In Argentina he participated after independence had already begun; in Chile he worked with O’Higgins, Chilean forces, and River Plate troops; in Peru independence remained militarily incomplete when he left.

### The immaculate apolitical soldier

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century hagiography portrayed him as above politics. He nevertheless belonged to secret political associations, helped overthrow a government, governed Cuyo, influenced Chilean politics, exercised dictatorial authority in Peru, pursued monarchy, and intervened through correspondence in debates over foreign war.

### Cabral’s final words

The rescue at San Lorenzo is historical; the polished patriotic sentence is not securely contemporary.

### The embrace of Maipú

The post-battle meeting is credible and reported; the familiar visual arrangement and dialogue were canonized through later painting and civic ritual.

### Crossing the Andes on a white horse

San Martín used horses and mules, and illness reportedly required him at times to travel on a litter. The solitary white-horse image is artistic shorthand. The operation involved many columns and thousands of people.

### Secret son of Rosa Guarú

This modern identity narrative derives from oral tradition and an Alvear family statement. It is unresolved, not established genealogy.

### British spy

Contacts and convergent interests are documented; secret employment remains unverified.

### Regular Freemason and anti-Catholic burial

Lodge membership is documented in a broad political sense; canonical Masonic status is disputed. His Catholic marriage, ceremonial practice, and burial contradict claims of an openly anti-Catholic identity. The placement of his Buenos Aires tomb does not prove ecclesiastical rejection.

### “Two suns” at Guayaquil

A literary encapsulation, not an authenticated transcript.

### Selfless poverty

San Martín experienced real financial anxiety and delayed payments, but later lived comfortably through property, family, and financial networks. Neither the image of a destitute abandoned exile nor that of a hidden magnate is accurate.

### Voluntary eternal exile

He did choose withdrawal from factional politics, but the decision was shaped by threats, party hostility, family obligations, financial circumstances, European residence restrictions, illness, and the aborted 1829 return. “Voluntary exile” is only partly descriptive.

## Works by José de San Martín

San Martín did not publish a systematic memoir or treatise. His authentic corpus consists principally of official papers and correspondence.

### Contemporary documents and writings

- **Military service record (*Hoja de servicios*)**, compiled through his Spanish career; official document, not an authored book.
- **Correspondence with Manuel Belgrano**, 1814 and later.
- **Correspondence with Juan Martín de Pueyrredón**, particularly 1816–1819.
- **Correspondence with Bernardo O’Higgins**, extending into exile.
- **Correspondence with Tomás Guido**, one of the largest and most revealing bodies.
- **Correspondence with Simón Bolívar**, especially 1821–1822 and retrospective explanation of Guayaquil.
- **Correspondence with William Miller**, Toribio de Luzuriaga, José de la Riva Agüero, Torre Tagle, Ramón Castilla, and other military and political figures.
- **Proclamation to the Army of the Andes**, 1817; surviving in documentary editions.
- **Proclamations to the people of Chile**, 1817–1818.
- **Proclamations to Peruvians**, 1820–1822.
- **Proclamation of the Independence of Peru**, 28 July 1821; delivered by San Martín, with textual forms transmitted through official and contemporary publication.
- **Decree assuming the Protectorate**, 3 August 1821.
- **Free-womb decree**, 12 August 1821.
- **Decrees regarding Indigenous tribute, personal service, and civic nomenclature**, August 1821.
- **Decree establishing the National Library of Peru**, 28 August 1821.
- **Founding and regulatory decrees of the Order of the Sun**, 1821.
- **Addresses and messages to the Peruvian Constituent Congress**, 1822.
- **Resignation before the Peruvian Congress**, 20 September 1822.
- **Máximas para Merceditas**, Brussels, 1825; private paternal instructions, first published posthumously.
- **Letter explaining the Guayaquil interview**, written to William Miller in 1827; essential but retrospective.
- **Correspondence with Juan Manuel de Rosas**, especially 1838–1850.
- **Testamento**, Paris, 23 January 1844.

### Major posthumous documentary editions

- *Documentos del Archivo de San Martín*, 12 volumes, Buenos Aires, 1910–1911. Posthumous archival compilation.
- *San Martín: su correspondencia*, edited by Adolfo P. Carranza, 1910; later editions.
- *San Martín: su correspondencia, 1823–1850*. Posthumous selection.
- *Documentos para la historia del Libertador General San Martín*, Instituto Nacional Sanmartiniano and Museo Histórico Nacional, multiple volumes from 1953 onward. The most extensive official documentary project.
- *José de San Martín: epistolario selecto*, edited by Julio César Raffo de la Reta, 1952.
- *San Martín pintado por sí mismo*, compiled by Luis Alberto Sánchez, 1941. A posthumous compilation, not an autobiography.
- *Ideario de San Martín*, various editors and editions. Thematic extracts from correspondence; not a coherent work authored as such.
- *Correspondencia entre San Martín y Rosas, 1838–1850*, multiple editions. Edited correspondence.
- *Archivo del General San Martín*, Museo Mitre holdings. Archival corpus rather than a publication authored by San Martín.

Attributions of polished maxims, speeches, and quotations circulating online should be checked against the documentary volumes. Many are paraphrases, altered versions, or patriotic inventions.

### Documentary and television material

- Argentine public television and Canal Encuentro have produced numerous episodes on San Martín, the Army of the Andes, San Lorenzo, and the Peru campaign.
- The Instituto Nacional Sanmartiniano, Museo Histórico Nacional, Museo Mitre, Casa de Grand Bourg, and Casa San Martín in Boulogne maintain lectures, audiovisual exhibits, and digitized documentary presentations.

### Visual culture

Important canonical images include:

- José Gil de Castro’s life portraits.
- The 1824 Brussels portrait by François-Joseph Navez.
- Later paintings by Pedro Subercaseaux of Chacabuco, Maipú, and the Andes.
- Monuments by Louis-Joseph Daumas and numerous copies derived from the Buenos Aires equestrian statue.
- Banknotes, stamps, school murals, military emblems, and official portraits across Argentina, Chile, and Peru.

Later historical painting often depicts events with invented poses, uniforms, flags, and dialogue. It is evidence of memory and nation-building, not a literal visual record.

## Death, Testament, and Repatriation

San Martín’s 1844 testament:

- Named Mercedes as universal heir.
- Requested a simple funeral.
- Declared that he owed no money.
- Bequeathed his saber to Juan Manuel de Rosas.
- Asked that his heart rest in Buenos Aires.
- Referred to his wife with affection and prohibited unnecessary family dispute.

The death act was entered at Boulogne on 18 August 1850 by Francisco Xavier Rosales and Adolphe Gérard. It provides his place of birth, titles, parentage, widowhood, exact age, address, and time of death.

Argentina did not immediately repatriate him. Political instability and disagreement over national memory delayed the transfer until 1880, during Nicolás Avellaneda’s presidency. The repatriation helped consolidate a national pantheon after decades of civil war.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Nineteenth century

His reputation was initially divided by region and faction. Chilean Carrera supporters treated the San Martín–O’Higgins alliance with hostility; Peruvian republicans criticized the protectorate’s monarchism; Argentine liberals had to reconcile his Rosas correspondence with anti-Rosas national history.

Mitre’s biography transformed him into the strategic architect of “South American emancipation,” complementary to Bolívar but politically more restrained.

### Twentieth-century civic cult

Schools, plazas, provinces, departments, streets, railways, ships, military units, medals, and towns received his name. Argentina’s 1950 centennial commemoration under Juan Domingo Perón promoted a powerful state cult around unity, sovereignty, military virtue, and social independence.

Different traditions appropriated him:

- Liberals emphasized constitutional restraint and refusal of dictatorship.
- The military emphasized discipline and strategic genius.
- Revisionists emphasized his support for Rosas against foreign intervention.
- Peronists emphasized continental independence and economic sovereignty.
- Social historians recovered the Black, Indigenous, female, artisan, and coerced labor behind his armies.
- Mestizo-origin advocates reinterpreted him as a concealed Indigenous-descended national founder.
- Chilean and Peruvian historiography alternately celebrated his liberation role and criticized external control, monarchy, factional repression, or incomplete emancipation.

### Continuing institutional legacy

His remains lie in Buenos Aires Cathedral. His saber is held by the Museo Histórico Nacional and ceremonially guarded by the Mounted Grenadiers. Major archival collections remain in the Museo Mitre and official documentary editions.

San Martín’s enduring historical importance rests on his role in connecting three theaters of war—Río de la Plata, Chile, and Peru—through a single strategic project. The heroic narrative is inseparable from the larger coalition, coercive wartime states, foreign officers, enslaved and free Black soldiers, Indigenous and rural labor, political conspiracies, and contested postwar regimes that made the campaigns possible.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by documentary or responsible historical evidence are included. Mere contemporaneity has been excluded.

### Carlos María de Alvear

**Documented fact.** Fellow Spanish-trained American officer, member of the London/Buenos Aires revolutionary lodge network, and passenger with San Martín aboard the *George Canning* in 1812. They became political rivals by 1814–1815. A modern theory makes them biological half-brothers through Diego de Alvear, but this is an **unresolved allegation**, not an established family connection.

### Francisco de Miranda

**Reported/documented institutional influence.** San Martín did not participate in Miranda’s Venezuelan expeditions, but the London revolutionary circles and lodge structures he joined in 1811–1812 grew from networks associated with Miranda. A direct substantive meeting between the two has not been conclusively documented.

### Manuel Belgrano

**Documented fact.** San Martín replaced Belgrano as commander of the Army of the North in January 1814. They met in the Tucumán/Yatasto region and maintained respectful correspondence about military organization and independence.

### Juan Martín de Pueyrredón y O’Dogan

**Documented fact.** Supreme Director and principal governmental patron of the Army of the Andes, 1816–1819. Their correspondence documents negotiations over money, recruits, arms, animals, and political authorization.

### Bernardo O’Higgins Riquelme

**Documented fact.** Chilean collaborator, friend, and political-military ally from the Mendoza exile community through Chacabuco, Maipú, and the Peru expedition. They continued corresponding after both left power.

### Martín Miguel de Güemes

**Documented fact.** Strategic associate responsible for the northern defense of the United Provinces while San Martín prepared and executed the Andes campaign. Their coordination and correspondence belong principally to 1814–1821.

### Simón Bolívar

**Documented fact.** Correspondent, counterpart in the liberation war, and participant with San Martín in the private Guayaquil meetings of 26–27 July 1822. The meetings concerned Peru, military command, Guayaquil, and constitutional arrangements.

### Antonio José de Sucre

**Documented indirect collaboration and reported personal connection.** San Martín’s protectorate sent Peruvian–River Plate forces to cooperate with Sucre in Ecuador. Bolívar’s secretary sent Sucre the contemporary report of San Martín’s Guayaquil visit. Evidence for extensive direct personal dealings is limited.

### Carlos María de Alvear and Diego de Alvear theory

The atlas lists Carlos María de Alvear but not Diego de Alvear separately. The alleged biological tie making Carlos and José half-brothers is an **allegation or theory**, based mainly on a later Alvear family memorandum and Yapeyú oral tradition.

### Napoleon Bonaparte

**Documented opposing historical relationship, not a personal meeting.** San Martín fought armies of Napoleonic France in Spain, especially at Bailén and Albuera. No meeting with Napoleon is documented. Napoleon’s warfare formed part of San Martín’s professional military environment.

### María Remedios del Valle

**Reported institutional overlap; direct meeting unproven.** Remedios del Valle served with the Army of the North. San Martín later commanded that army, but presently consulted sources do not securely document a personal encounter. No connection is asserted beyond possible institutional overlap.

### Manuela Sáenz

No securely documented meeting or correspondence with San Martín was found. Her association with Bolívar and the Peruvian independence theater is insufficient by itself to establish contact.

### Simón Rodríguez

No documented personal meeting or correspondence with San Martín was found in the consulted material. His relationship to Bolívar does not establish a San Martín connection.

### George Washington

**Documented cultural comparison; personal connection impossible.** San Martín was repeatedly compared to Washington by nineteenth-century writers because he relinquished power. No direct influence explicitly acknowledged by San Martín was located in the consulted evidence.

### Alexander Baring and Sir Francis Baring

British finance affected Spanish-American independence, but no sufficiently direct personal, business, or correspondence link between San Martín and either atlas-listed Baring was established.

### Domingo Faustino Sarmiento

**Reported fact.** Sarmiento visited San Martín at Grand Bourg in 1846 and later published recollections of him. The meeting contributed to the construction of San Martín’s image among the generation that organized Argentina after Rosas.

### Juan Manuel de Rosas

**Documented fact.** Political correspondent during San Martín’s European exile. San Martín supported Rosas’s resistance to French and Anglo-French intervention and bequeathed him his campaign saber in 1844.

### János Czetz

No documented personal connection was established. Czetz later became an important organizer of the Argentine military but arrived in Argentina after San Martín’s death.

### Giuseppe Garibaldi

No documented meeting or correspondence was found. Both were connected to warfare in the Río de la Plata at different moments, but contemporaneity and regional overlap do not establish contact.

### Abraham Lincoln, Alexander von Humboldt, Thomas Jefferson, and other roster contemporaries

No supported personal, institutional, or epistolary connection was located. Their omission from the substantive entries is deliberate; absence of evidence was not converted into an inferred ideological relationship.
