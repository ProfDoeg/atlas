# Johannes Kepler: Research Dossier

**Subject:** Johannes Kepler (1571–1630)  
**Scope:** documentary biography, scientific and literary corpus, institutions and patronage, controversies, myth, reception, and research apparatus  
**Prepared:** 22 August 2026

> **Method note.** Dates are New Style only where the cited source or modern scholarship normalizes them; the Holy Roman Empire contained territories using different calendars. “Kepler’s laws” are later labels: Kepler did not publish a numbered set called “three laws.” Claims about witchcraft, murder, and occult practice are classified by evidentiary status. Monetary figures are nominal gulden/florins and cannot be converted responsibly into a single modern value.

## Family and Ancestry

### Paternal line

- Grandfather **Sebald Kepler** was a furrier and repeatedly mayor of Weil der Stadt. The family possessed local standing but was declining economically by Johannes’s birth.
- Father **Heinrich Kepler** (born c. 1547) was restless, frequently absent, and served as a mercenary, including in the Low Countries. Johannes later described him harshly in an astrological family sketch. Heinrich’s final fate is not documented; he disappeared from the record after the 1580s and was probably killed or died abroad. The common statement that he died fighting in the Netherlands is plausible, not proved.
- Kepler’s paternal grandmother **Katharina Müller** was identified in family records; later romantic accounts sometimes conflate the family’s social standing with hereditary nobility. Kepler was not an aristocratic landholder.

### Maternal line and the Guldenmann family

- Mother **Katharina Guldenmann Kepler** (c. 1546–1622) was the daughter of innkeeper **Melchior Guldenmann**. She was literate only minimally or not at all, practiced household medicine and herb knowledge, litigated energetically, and had an abrasive reputation in some testimony.
- Later biographies routinely call her an “herbalist” or “healer.” Those terms are reasonable but should not be inflated into evidence of a professional medical practice or occult office.
- An aunt, also named Katharina, had reportedly been burned as a witch. This family memory increased the danger when accusations began against Kepler’s mother.
- Johannes was one of seven children. The securely visible adult siblings were **Heinrich** (who suffered instability and at one point helped provoke suspicions against their mother), **Christoph** (a pewterer), and **Margaretha**, who married Georg Binder. Exact infant sibling lists vary among biographies because parish evidence is incomplete.

### First marriage: Barbara Müller

- **Barbara Müller** (1573–1611), daughter of prosperous miller Jobst Müller, was twice widowed before meeting Kepler and had a daughter, **Regina Lorenz**, whom Kepler treated as a stepdaughter.
- The marriage, negotiated with assistance from Protestant officials despite Jobst Müller’s initial objection to Kepler’s poverty, took place 27 April 1597.
- Children: **Heinrich** (1598–1598), **Susanna** (1599–1599), **Susanna** (1602–after 1630), **Friedrich** (1604–1611), and **Ludwig** (1607–1663). The first two died in infancy; Friedrich died during a smallpox outbreak. Susanna married Kepler’s assistant **Jacob Bartsch** in 1630. Ludwig became a physician and helped publish *Somnium*.
- Barbara suffered fever and seizures in 1611 and died 3 July 1611 (some older accounts give 1612 because of calendrical or secondary-source error).
- Her estate produced litigation and continuing financial complications. The marriage was strained by illness, bereavement, religious exile, and money; Kepler’s private remarks are sometimes unflattering, but there is no documentary basis for modern claims of abuse.

### Second marriage: Susanna Reuttinger

- After evaluating eleven possible spouses in a remarkable, self-mocking letter sometimes called the “marriage problem,” Kepler chose the fifth candidate, **Susanna Reuttinger** (c. 1588–1636), an orphan of modest status.
- They married at Linz on 30 October 1613. Kepler emphasized her affection, economy, diligence, and care for his children.
- Their children were **Margareta Regina** (died young), **Katharina** (died young), **Sebald** (died young), **Cordula** (born 1621), **Fridmar** (born 1623), and **Hildebert** (born 1625). The last three survived childhood. Spellings and precise dates differ in the literature.
- Kepler left a widow and minor children. His family’s posthumous efforts to print *Somnium* were partly financial.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1571–1584

- Kepler was born prematurely and described himself as physically weak. Smallpox at about age four scarred him and impaired his eyesight; he later reported multiple vision, weak hands, stomach and skin complaints, and other chronic ailments in autobiographical/astrological notes.
- The family moved to **Leonberg** in 1576. Heinrich intermittently kept an inn and pursued military ventures. Katharina at times accompanied him, leaving children with relatives.
- Johannes remembered the **Great Comet of 1577**, shown to him by his mother, and the **lunar eclipse of 1580**, shown by his father. These recollections are autobiographical and plausible, though recorded retrospectively.
- He attended German and Latin schools in Leonberg. Poverty and the Württemberg scholarship system shaped his career: the territorial church identified talented boys for clerical education, supplying institutional mobility that his family could not purchase.

## Education and Formation, 1584–1594

### Monastic schools

- **1584:** entered the lower Protestant seminary at Adelberg.
- **1586:** moved to the higher seminary at Maulbronn, living under rigorous monastic-school discipline. Records and later autobiographical material describe intellectual distinction, illness, loneliness, quarrels, and religious scruple.
- The schools trained future Lutheran clergy in Latin, dialectic, rhetoric, music, and theology. Kepler’s lifelong habit of reading nature as a divine geometric text formed inside, not outside, this confessional system.

### University of Tübingen

- Matriculated 1589; bachelor’s degree 1588/89 under the institution’s sequence, master’s degree in 1591; continued theological study.
- **Michael Mästlin (Maestlin)** taught him astronomy and privately explained Copernicus’s heliocentric system, while publicly teaching the standard curriculum. Mästlin became teacher, correspondent, critic, and intermediary with printers.
- Other instructors included theologians **Jacob Heerbrand**, **Matthias Hafenreffer**, and **Veit Müller**. Kepler learned orthodox Lutheran doctrine but resisted compulsory precision where Scripture, reason, and conscience seemed to him misaligned.
- He defended Copernican arguments in student disputation. A proposed lunar-perspective disputation, sometimes treated as the seed of *Somnium*, was reportedly blocked by Veit Müller. The exact continuity between that lost exercise and the later literary work cannot be reconstructed.
- He originally expected a pastoral career. In 1594 Württemberg authorities nominated him for a vacant mathematics teaching post at the Protestant school in Graz. He accepted reluctantly before completing the theology degree.

## Early Career: Graz, 1594–1600

### School and government service

- Arrived in **Graz**, Inner Austria, in April 1594 as mathematics teacher at the Lutheran Stiftsschule and provincial mathematician. His initial salary was **150 gulden**, below his predecessor’s 200; it rose to 200 after marriage, with 60 gulden moving allowance. Calendar work and occasional honoraria supplemented it.
- Enrollment in his mathematics classes was poor; at times he had almost no pupils. Officials retained him because calendars, astrology, land measurement, and mathematical advice were useful.
- His first calendar and prognostication gained notice after apparently successful predictions of severe cold and Ottoman incursions. Such annuals mixed astronomy, weather lore, medicine, agriculture, politics, and astrology. “Prediction came true” reports were selective and do not establish predictive validity.

### Cosmological breakthrough and *Mysterium cosmographicum*

- On 19 July 1595, while drawing a geometrical figure for a class, Kepler conceived a model connecting the six known planets and five Platonic solids. In the mature construction, nested regular solids separated the planetary spheres in the Copernican order.
- Published at Tübingen in 1596 as *Prodromus dissertationum cosmographicarum, continens mysterium cosmographicum*. It was the first substantial published defense of Copernican cosmology by an astronomer after Copernicus.
- The model was numerically imperfect and ultimately false. Its importance lies in Kepler’s insistence that planetary number, spacing, and motion should receive a causal, mathematical explanation rather than merely computational description.
- He sent copies to **Galileo Galilei**, **Tycho Brahe**, and other scholars. Galileo’s private reply cautiously acknowledged Copernican sympathy but did not become close collaboration. Tycho criticized Kepler’s numbers yet invited further exchange.

### Marriage, networks, and religious expulsion

- Married Barbara Müller in 1597. Her property improved prospects but remained entangled with her kin and prior marriages.
- Kepler corresponded with **Nicolaus Reimers Ursus**, Rudolf II’s mathematician and Tycho’s bitter priority rival. Ursus printed Kepler’s complimentary private letter; Tycho consequently distrusted Kepler, though the two later reconciled enough to work together.
- Archduke Ferdinand’s Counter-Reformation forced Protestant teachers and clergy from Graz in September 1598. Kepler briefly returned under a special exemption because officials needed his mathematical services, but refusal to convert made his position untenable. In 1600 he left permanently, losing salary, home, and institutional security.

## Chronological Life History

## Prague and Tycho Brahe, 1600–1601

- Tycho, recently established near Emperor Rudolf II’s Prague court, invited Kepler to collaborate. Kepler reached **Benátky nad Jizerou** in February 1600.
- The relationship was productive and adversarial. Tycho controlled observations accumulated over decades; Kepler needed them. Kepler wanted intellectual autonomy and reliable pay; Tycho wanted a mathematically formidable subordinate who would demonstrate the superiority of his geoheliocentric system. Disputes over data access, status, contracts, and authorship caused Kepler to leave briefly before returning.
- Tycho assigned him the orbit of **Mars**, whose eccentric motion resisted conventional models. Kepler later said that an eight-minute discrepancy in longitude forced a reform of astronomy. The phrase captures his methodological resolve, though the discovery process was longer and less linear.
- Kepler also stayed with imperial councillor **Johannes Matthäus Wackher von Wackenfels**, a friend and patron who encouraged discussion of lunar astronomy and natural philosophy.
- After Tycho’s sudden illness and death on 24 October 1601, Rudolf II appointed Kepler **Imperial Mathematician**. Kepler inherited official responsibility for the promised Rudolphine Tables, but legal ownership of Tycho’s observations remained disputed with Tycho’s heirs.

## Imperial Mathematician in Prague, 1601–1612

### Office, patronage, and finances

- Kepler’s nominal imperial salary was substantial (often given as 500 gulden annually), but the treasury paid irregularly. Arrears became a central fact of his life. He wrote petitions, sought alternate patrons, sold calendars, performed horoscopes, and dedicated books strategically.
- Duties included astronomical calculation, calendars, astrological advice, responses to celestial events, and prestige service for the court. Rudolf’s Prague assembled artists, instrument makers, alchemists, physicians, and scholars; Kepler participated in this heterogeneous court without being an alchemist or secret initiate.
- He cooperated with Tycho’s former assistant **Christian Longomontanus** only briefly and negotiated bitterly with Tycho’s family. The 1604 agreement gave him access to the observations for completing the tables while recognizing Brahe family interests.

### Optics and the nova

- *Astronomiae pars optica* (1604), usually called *Ad Vitellionem paralipomena*, explained that the eye’s lens forms an inverted image on the retina, analyzed pinhole images, refraction, apparent sizes, and atmospheric optics, and gave a correct geometrical account of vision beyond classical “visual rays.”
- The bright new star of October 1604—the supernova now SN 1604—produced *De stella nova in pede Serpentarii* (1606). Kepler observed it, compiled others’ observations, rejected simple atmospheric explanations, debated astrology, and used the event against an immutable Aristotelian heaven.

### *Astronomia nova* and the first two planetary laws

- Working through geometrical hypotheses, physical analogies, and Tycho’s Mars observations, Kepler abandoned circular planetary paths.
- *Astronomia nova* (Heidelberg, 1609; title page dated 1609) presented:
  1. the **ellipse law**—a planet follows an ellipse with the Sun at a focus;
  2. the **area law**—the line from Sun to planet sweeps equal areas in equal times.
- The logical and historical order is complicated: an approximate distance law and area calculation emerged during the Mars work; the polished “laws” were not set off as a modern theorem list. Kepler’s physics invoked a solar motive force, compared to magnetism after William Gilbert, diminishing with distance. He did not possess inertia or Newtonian gravitation and sometimes treated planetary souls as regulators.
- This was “celestial physics”: he attempted to explain actual causes, not merely save appearances. His calculations exposed failed hypotheses rather than hiding them.

### Galileo, telescope, and lunar satellites

- In 1610 Kepler immediately supported Galileo’s *Sidereus nuncius* in *Dissertatio cum Nuncio sidereo*, despite lacking a suitable telescope and despite Galileo’s limited reciprocation.
- After independent observations by others confirmed Jupiter’s four moons, Kepler published *Narratio de observatis a se quatuor Iovis satellitibus* (1610). He promoted the term **satelles** (“attendant”), ancestor of “satellite.”
- *Dioptrice* (1611) developed lens theory and described the two-convex-lens “Keplerian” telescope, which gives a larger field and permits micrometers but produces an inverted image. It was a theory/design, not a patented commercial invention.

### Political and family crisis

- Rudolf II’s authority collapsed amid conflict with his brother Matthias. Both sides sought Kepler’s astrological counsel; surviving advice is politically cautious and not evidence that Kepler directed state policy.
- In 1611 son Friedrich died of smallpox. Barbara became seriously ill and died in July. Kepler explored posts in Linz, Württemberg, and Padua; the reported Padua possibility followed Galileo’s departure, but no completed appointment resulted.
- Rudolf died in January 1612. Matthias retained Kepler’s imperial title while permitting removal to Linz.

## Linz, 1612–1626

### District mathematician and confessional outsider

- Kepler arrived in Linz as Upper Austrian provincial mathematician and teacher, while remaining imperial mathematician. He made calendars, taught, surveyed, advised on the calendar, and continued the Rudolphine Tables.
- Lutheran pastor **Daniel Hitzler** denied him communion because Kepler would not accept the Formula of Concord’s account of Christ’s bodily ubiquity and sacramental presence. Kepler appealed to Württemberg theologians and civil authorities, without regaining regular communion.
- “Excommunicated” is commonly used, but the documentary condition was prolonged exclusion from the Eucharist rather than a modern denominational expulsion. Catholics offered practical tolerance but Kepler refused conversion. His ideal was a concord of Christians broader than confessional enforcement.

### Chronology, calendar, geometry, and marriage

- *De vero anno quo aeternus Dei Filius humanam naturam in utero benedictae Virginis Mariae assumpsit* (German 1613; Latin 1614) argued from historical and astronomical evidence about the year of Christ’s birth.
- Summoned to Regensburg in 1613 for imperial deliberations over calendar reform, Kepler supported the astronomical merits of the Gregorian calendar while resisting adoption merely by papal authority.
- Married Susanna Reuttinger in October 1613.
- Wine-barrel gauging at the wedding prompted *Nova stereometria doliorum vinariorum* (1615), using decompositions and proto-integral methods to calculate volumes of solids of revolution. He also produced a practical German gauging manual.

### The prosecution of Katharina Kepler, 1615–1621

**Documented judicial process, not evidence of witchcraft:**

- In 1615 Leonberg neighbor **Ursula Reinbold** accused Katharina of causing chronic illness by a bitter drink. Other claims accumulated: harming animals and children, passing through locked doors, riding a calf backward, and transforming into a cat.
- The Keplers first pursued a defamation action. Württemberg officials converted the matter into a criminal witchcraft investigation. Local magistrate **Lukas Einhorn**, who had overseen other witch prosecutions, participated; political and neighborhood conflicts shaped the case.
- Johannes received notice late in 1615, wrote petitions, visited Württemberg repeatedly, hired legal help, and eventually constructed a detailed defense. His university law contacts and medical-natural reasoning exposed hearsay, contradictions, delayed testimony, and ordinary causes for alleged injuries.
- Katharina fled temporarily to Linz in 1617. She was seized in August 1620 and imprisoned at Güglingen for about fourteen months, chained under harsh conditions.
- In September 1621 she underwent **territio verbalis**: the instruments and consequences of torture were described/shown to induce confession. She refused to confess. Authorities released her in October 1621 after the prosecution failed.
- She died at Roßwälden on 13 April 1622. Statements that she was “saved from burning” summarize the real risk but should not be mistaken for a pronounced death sentence; she was never convicted or formally sentenced to execution.
- Ulinka Rublack’s archival reconstruction revises older portraits—derived partly from hostile depositions and Kepler’s own difficult remarks—of Katharina as a stereotypical witchlike crone.

### *Harmonices mundi* and the third law

- *Harmonices mundi libri V* (Linz, 1619) joined geometry, music theory, astrology, psychology, and astronomy in a theology of created proportion.
- Book V announced what became the **third law**: for planets, the squares of orbital periods are proportional to the cubes of mean distances from the Sun. Kepler recorded finding the relation on 15 May 1618 after a false start on 8 March.
- Planetary “melodies” derived from ratios of maximum and minimum angular speeds were mathematical analogies, not a claim that audible music travels through vacuum.
- Kepler attacked **Robert Fludd** in an appendix, contrasting measured, mathematical harmonies with Fludd’s emblematic/Hermetic monochord. Fludd replied in *Veritatis proscenium* (1621) and *Monochordon mundi* (1622); Kepler answered with *Apologia pro suo opere Harmonices Mundi* (1622). The dispute concerned method, images, mathematics, divine archetypes, and priority—not simply “scientist versus occultist.” Kepler himself accepted astrology, cosmic animation, and theological archetypes, but demanded quantitative demonstrations.

### Textbook, tables, logarithms, and war

- The three-part *Epitome astronomiae Copernicanae* appeared in installments (1618, 1620, 1621). Cast as questions and answers, it extended Keplerian elliptical astronomy to all planets and treated satellites. Catholic authorities placed it on the Index of Prohibited Books in 1619.
- After learning of John Napier’s logarithms, Kepler promoted and recalculated them in *Chilias logarithmorum* (1624), accelerating table computation.
- The Thirty Years’ War and Upper Austrian Peasants’ War destabilized Linz. During the 1626 siege/fire, the printing establishment intended for the Rudolphine Tables was destroyed, though the manuscript survived.
- Kepler secured permission to move printing to **Ulm**, leaving Linz in November 1626.

## Ulm, Prague, and the Rudolphine Tables, 1626–1628

- At Ulm, printer **Jonas Saur** and collaborators executed the technically demanding *Tabulae Rudolphinae* (1627). Kepler supervised typography, diagrams, paper, computation, and financing.
- The tables combined Tycho’s observational catalogue with Kepler’s orbital theory and logarithmic methods. The elaborate frontispiece staged a history of astronomy; Tycho and Copernicus appear within the “temple of Urania.”
- Kepler negotiated with Tycho’s heirs over credit and property and dedicated the work to Emperor Ferdinand II. The Tables were far more accurate than predecessors and predicted the 1631 transits of Mercury and Venus. Pierre Gassendi observed Mercury’s transit; the Venus transit was not securely observed in Europe.
- Kepler brought presentation copies to Prague and continued seeking payment of enormous imperial salary arrears—often reported near **12,000 gulden**, though totals varied with accounting, assignments, and accumulated claims.

## Sagan and Wallenstein, 1628–1630

- Imperial generalissimo **Albrecht von Wallenstein**, Duke of Friedland and Sagan, had long used astrologers and received a horoscope from Kepler (originally cast anonymously in 1608; later annotations were requested). Kepler’s readings described tendencies and dangerous periods, not a verified supernatural forecast.
- Wallenstein offered Kepler patronage and a printing base at **Sagan** (Żagań, now Poland). Kepler arrived with family in July 1628. He was isolated linguistically and institutionally; promised facilities and money came slowly.
- He established a press and printed ephemerides with his son-in-law/assistant Jacob Bartsch. Wallenstein’s dismissal in 1630 again imperiled the arrangement.
- Kepler was neither Wallenstein’s intelligence chief nor political strategist. Astrology at court carried political information and patronage implications, but no evidence makes Kepler an intelligence operative.

## Final Journey and Death, 1630

- On 8 October 1630 Kepler rode toward Regensburg, where the imperial diet and financial decision-makers offered a chance to pursue unpaid claims.
- He arrived ill after the long autumn journey, developed fever, and died 15 November. Contemporary medicine did not establish a precise diagnosis; later labels such as acute fever, pneumonia, or typhus are retrospective possibilities, not findings.
- His estate was modest and claims-heavy: books, manuscripts, instruments, household responsibilities, and large unpaid receivables rather than liquid wealth.
- He was buried in a Protestant cemetery. The Thirty Years’ War soon obliterated the site. A transmitted Latin epitaph says, in paraphrase: “I measured the heavens; now I measure the shadows of Earth. My mind was in heaven; my body’s shadow lies here.”

Kepler had no company in the modern sense and no documented equity investments or patents.

### Finances

- Persistent paradox: high nominal offices, low cash flow. Imperial arrears forced travel, petitions, loans/assignments, dedications, calendar work, and dependence on new patrons.
- Barbara Müller brought property expectations, but prior marriages and family claims produced legal disputes rather than simple wealth.
- Book publication could confer reputation yet impose direct costs. The Tables and Sagan ephemerides were capital-intensive personal projects.
- Kepler’s 1630 trip was financially motivated. The claim that he “died chasing his salary” is compressed but substantially grounded.

## Political, Religious, Intellectual, and Artistic Development

### Copernicanism and physical astronomy

Kepler moved from a geometrical Copernican-Platonic architecture to an empirically corrected celestial physics. He never abandoned the belief that God created through number, geometry, and archetype. Failed elements—the nested solids, quasi-magnetic force, solar rotation model—were engines of research as well as errors.

### Theology

- Scripture and nature were two divine books, but biblical language about the sky accommodated ordinary human perception rather than teaching technical astronomy.
- Christological and Eucharistic disagreement prevented full Lutheran conformity. Kepler sought interconfessional concord but was no religious indifferentist.
- He calculated biblical chronology and treated astronomy as priestly reading of creation. Descriptions of him as a secular modern scientist omit the explicit motive structure of his work.

### Astrology

- Documented practitioner: natal charts for himself, relatives, patrons, and Wallenstein; annual calendars; weather and political forecasts.
- Documented critic: rejected rigid zodiacal signs, many traditional houses, and charlatan prediction; emphasized angular configurations, physical celestial effects, and psychological dispositions.
- It is misleading either to erase his astrology or to reduce all astronomy to occultism. The categories overlapped in his world but were internally contested.

### Geometry, harmony, and art

- The Platonic solids, regular polygons, musical consonance, and divine archetypes linked aesthetics to causal explanation.
- He studied stellated polyhedra (two now called Kepler–Poinsot solids), sphere packing (the Kepler conjecture), tilings, and snowflake symmetry.
- His book diagrams and the Rudolphine frontispiece were argumentative images, not decoration alone.

### Political activity

- No party affiliation in a modern sense. He served Catholic Habsburg rulers without converting, accepted Protestant provincial offices, advised rulers astrologically, and advocated confessional restraint.
- Counter-Reformation expulsion, the Rudolf–Matthias succession crisis, Thirty Years’ War, Peasants’ War, and Wallenstein’s rise/fall repeatedly dictated his mobility.

### Requested corpus links

- **Robert Fludd:** direct published controversy, 1619–22.
- **Edmond Halley:** born 1656, after Kepler’s death. Halley’s cometary and Newtonian astronomy belongs to Kepler’s reception, not his personal network.
- **Athanasius Kircher:** born 1602; no securely documented important meeting or correspondence with Kepler. Kircher’s harmonic and cosmological encyclopedism is a later parallel/contrast.
- **Giovanni Pico della Mirandola:** died 1494. Kepler knew the Renaissance inheritance in which Pico’s attack on astrology was central, but no personal link was possible.
- **Galileo:** real correspondence and mutual Copernican interest, but a notably asymmetrical relationship.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, Crimes, and Financial Questions

### Access to Tycho’s data

**Documented dispute.** Kepler pressed for observations; Tycho restricted access; after Tycho’s death Kepler used the materials as imperial successor while heirs asserted ownership. Kepler’s publications credited Tycho extensively but also converted the data to a theory Tycho rejected. “Stole the data” is polemical shorthand for a genuine but legally and institutionally ambiguous succession conflict.

### Did Kepler murder Tycho Brahe?

**Modern allegation; unsupported by current forensic evidence.** Joshua and Anne-Lee Gilder’s *Heavenly Intrigue* (2004) proposed that Kepler poisoned Tycho with mercury to obtain his observations. Motive and opportunity were inferred; no contemporary indictment, confession, eyewitness evidence, or toxicologically demonstrated poisoning implicated Kepler. Analyses following Tycho’s 2010 exhumation reported mercury concentrations insufficient to cause death. Medical scholarship favors complications following acute urinary retention, though an exact diagnosis four centuries later remains uncertain. The allegation affected popular reputation because it turned a data dispute into a murder narrative.

### Astrology and political prediction

**Documented practice, disputed efficacy.** Kepler prepared horoscopes, including Wallenstein’s, and sometimes highlighted dangerous intervals. Claims that he exactly predicted Wallenstein’s 1634 assassination are retrospective selection and revision: Kepler died in 1630, and the surviving readings did not name that event with modern specificity.

### Mother’s witch trial

**Documented accusation and prosecution; no conviction.** The charges are evidence about neighbors, law, gender, medicine, and witch belief, not proof of supernatural acts. Katharina denied them, withstood threat of torture, and was released.

### Confessional discipline

**Documented ecclesiastical action.** Denial of communion followed doctrinal refusal; it was neither a criminal conviction nor persecution by the Roman Inquisition. The Catholic Index separately prohibited the *Epitome*.

### Priority and polemic

- Tycho–Ursus controversy: Kepler’s private praise of Ursus was weaponized; he later prepared a defense of Tycho (*Contra Ursum*, unpublished in his lifetime).
- Fludd dispute: mutual public criticism; no fraud finding.
- Galileo: Kepler eagerly supported telescopic claims, while priority for the telescope belonged to Dutch spectacle-makers and Galileo’s astronomical exploitation, not Kepler.

No evidence was found of money laundering, corporate fraud, criminal conviction, addiction, or an intelligence-service role. Those modern categories are largely inapposite to the surviving record.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

### “The last scientific astrologer / first astrophysicist”

1. **Story:** Kepler stands exactly on a boundary between magical antiquity and modern physics.
2. **Appearance/circulation:** popularized especially by Carl Sagan and histories of the Scientific Revolution in the twentieth century.
3. **Evidence:** he did combine astrology and theological harmony with quantitative physical astronomy.
4. **Limits:** the slogan imposes modern occupational borders and makes history look teleological.
5. **Effect:** it humanized him but also encouraged selective quotation—either suppressing astrology or treating it as embarrassment.

### The “eight minutes” conversion

1. **Story:** one eight-arcminute error instantly caused Kepler to discover ellipses.
2. **Source:** Kepler’s own retrospective methodological drama in *Astronomia nova*.
3. **Evidence:** Tycho’s Mars data genuinely resisted models by about eight minutes, beyond claimed observational error.
4. **Against literal version:** years of calculation, false starts, an “oval” orbit, area reasoning, and physical hypotheses intervened.
5. **Effect:** canonical parable of empirical honesty.

### Somnium caused Katharina’s witch trial

1. **Story:** a leaked manuscript portraying a witch mother directly caused the accusation.
2. **Source/circulation:** Kepler himself worried that manuscript copies circulated in Tübingen; later biographies and science-fiction histories strengthened the causal link.
3. **For:** Fiolxhilde resembles aspects of Katharina; copies circulated; witch rumors were real.
4. **Against:** the formal case arose from Reinbold’s poisoning accusation and older local conflicts; no trial record establishes *Somnium* as the initiating cause.
5. **Effect:** fused science fiction, autobiography, and witch prosecution into Kepler’s central modern legend.

### “First science-fiction novel”

1. **Story:** *Somnium* invented science fiction.
2. **Circulation:** twentieth-century writers including Arthur C. Clarke/Carl Sagan-era popular science and modern publishers.
3. **For:** it uses an imagined lunar voyage to reason rigorously about viewpoint, lunar days, and an Earth seen from the Moon.
4. **Against:** genre categories are retrospective; Lucian, Plutarch, *The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter*, and other voyage/fantasy traditions precede it; the work is part dream narrative, part technical treatise with notes dwarfing the fiction.
5. **Effect:** greatly expanded Kepler’s literary reputation.

### “Kepler proved God designed the solar system with Platonic solids”

**False as a scientific result; accurate as an early research program.** Observational discrepancies forced modifications, and the model does not describe the solar system. Kepler reissued it in 1621 with extensive notes rather than concealing failure.

### “Kepler was a modern rationalist who defeated superstition”

**Whiggish myth.** His defense of Katharina used evidentiary and naturalistic criticism, but he did not deny the theoretical possibility of witchcraft and practiced astrology. His rationality operated inside Christian natural philosophy.

### “Kepler the occult Rosicrucian”

**Unverified/usually associative.** Sacred geometry, world harmony, Rudolfine Prague, and his dispute with Rosicrucian defender Fludd encourage esoteric appropriations. No reliable evidence establishes Kepler as a Rosicrucian initiate. He explicitly criticized Fludd’s non-quantitative Hermetic claims.

### Grave and relic legends

The grave is lost. Modern monuments in Regensburg and Weil der Stadt are commemorations, not authenticated tombs or relic sites.

### Correspondence and documentary corpus

- Thousands of letters survive in the KGW letter volumes and manuscripts dispersed historically, especially in St. Petersburg collections. Correspondents include Mästlin, Galileo, Herwart, Fabricius, Brengger, Bernegger, theologians, rulers, printers, family, and officials.
- The **Early Modern Letters Online (EMLO)** Kepler catalogue supplies searchable metadata and is a research index, not a substitute for the critical texts.
- *Contra Ursum*, a defense of Tycho in the priority controversy, remained unpublished in Kepler’s lifetime and survives as a manuscript/critical-edition work.
- Astrological nativities, calendars, official memoranda, petitions, legal submissions for Katharina, poems, and autobiographical horoscope notes are genuine but often known only through collected editions.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Seventeenth century

- Reception was gradual. Galileo and Descartes did not adopt the elliptical astronomy. Mästlin resisted parts of Kepler’s physics. Ismaël Bullialdus and Seth Ward used modified elliptical systems.
- The Rudolphine Tables quickly demonstrated practical superiority. Gassendi’s observation of the 1631 Mercury transit was a conspicuous success.
- **Jeremiah Horrocks** applied Keplerian astronomy, corrected the Venus transit prediction, and observed the 1639 transit.
- **Newton** subsumed the three relations under universal gravitation and mechanics. In Newtonian form, Kepler’s third law receives mass corrections and applies to two-body systems; the “laws” became central physics rather than their original theological-harmonic setting.

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries

- Histories increasingly treated Kepler as a founder of exact astronomy. The numbering “three laws” stabilized pedagogically.
- Christian Frisch’s collected edition (1858–71) transformed access. Monuments, schools, streets, and observatories adopted his name.
- **Kepler’s equation**, **Keplerian orbit**, **Kepler telescope**, and polyhedral terminology consolidated.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

- Max Caspar’s biography and KGW made documentary scholarship possible; Koestler and Sagan created the popular image of tortured genius between mysticism and science.
- Historians later rejected a simple “mystic becomes scientist” progression, showing that theology, astrology, harmony, observation, and physics were mutually productive.
- Feminist and legal histories, especially Rublack’s, reconstructed Katharina as a historical actor and corrected inherited caricatures.
- The formal proof of the **Kepler conjecture** on sphere packing by Thomas Hales (computer-assisted proof announced 1998; Flyspeck formal verification completed 2014) confirmed a problem Kepler posed in 1611.
- NASA’s **Kepler Space Telescope** (launched 2009; mission ended 2018) discovered thousands of exoplanet candidates/confirmed worlds via transits. The naming connected his planetary laws and plural-world imagination to modern exoplanet science.
- Other namesakes include asteroid **1134 Kepler**, lunar and Martian craters, the supernova remnant of SN 1604, Johannes Kepler University Linz, and ESA’s ATV **Johannes Kepler**.

## Atlas Connections

### Archimedes of Syracuse

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kepler explicitly recast Archimedean measurement for German readers in *Ausszug auss der uralten Messe-Kunst Archimedis* (Linz, 1616), connecting Archimedes’ geometry to the practical gauging of vessels and wine barrels. The publication’s title and catalogue record directly name Archimedes. [LEO-BW bibliographic record](https://www.leo-bw.de/web/guest/detail/-/Detail/details/DOKUMENT/bsz_swb/092142672/Au%C3%9Fzug%20au%C3%9F%20der%20Uralten%20MesseKunst%20Archimedis%20Und%20deroselben%20newlich%20in%20Latein%20au%C3%9Fgangener%20Ergentzung-%20betreffend%20Rechnun)

### Aristotle

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kepler repeatedly interpreted, translated, and contested Aristotle in his astronomy and natural philosophy—especially Aristotelian celestial physics, cosmology, and accounts of light and vision. This was direct textual engagement, not merely membership in a broadly “anti-Aristotelian” generation. Modern scholarship specifically treats “Kepler as Reader and Translator of Aristotle.” [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kepler/)

### Plato

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kepler explicitly reused the geometrical cosmology of Plato’s *Timaeus*. The five regular solids structured *Mysterium cosmographicum* (1596), while Platonic archetype and harmony remained central in *Harmonices mundi* (1619). [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Renaissance natural philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/natphil-ren/)

### Jesus of Nazareth

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kepler wrote directly about the chronology of Jesus’ conception and birth in the German treatise of 1613 and Latin *De vero anno quo aeternus Dei Filius humanam naturam in utero benedictae Virginis Mariae assumpsit* (1614). He combined historical testimony, biblical chronology, and astronomical reconstruction, including the proposed relevance of planetary conjunctions. [Contemporary overview and title reference](https://www.lemonde.fr/les-decodeurs/article/2024/12/26/jesus-serait-ne-en-moins-7-avant-lui-meme-ou-pourquoi-nous-devrions-feter-l-annee-2032_5055156_4355771.html)

### Nicolaus Copernicus

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kepler was an intensive reader, defender, and reviser of Copernicus. *Mysterium cosmographicum* (1596) defended the Copernican ordering of the planets; *Astronomia nova* (1609) replaced Copernicus’ uniform circular mechanisms with physical astronomy and elliptical motion; and *Epitome astronomiae Copernicanae* (1618–21) systematically reconstructed Copernican astronomy on Keplerian foundations. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kepler/)

### Giovanni Pico della Mirandola

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** Kepler engaged Pico’s posthumous *Disputationes adversus astrologiam divinatricem* while defending a restricted, reformed astrology. He accepted the force of parts of Pico’s criticism but rejected Pico’s wholesale repudiation of celestial influence. The relationship is therefore a documented textual dispute across generations, not personal contact. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Pico](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pico-della-mirandola/); [scholarly discussion indexed in *Journal for the History of Astronomy*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002182860503600102)

### Giordano Bruno

- **[T1-] Documented fact.** After learning of Bruno’s cosmology through the Prague intellectual milieu, Kepler addressed and opposed the Nolan’s infinite or indefinitely populated universe, particularly in *De stella nova* (Prague, 1606). Kepler’s finite, Sun-centered cosmos and enormous but bounded stellar sphere were developed partly against Bruno’s plurality of solar systems. [Miguel A. Granada, “Kepler and Bruno on the Infinity of the Universe and of Solar Systems”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002182860803900403)

### Galileo Galilei

- **[T3] Documented fact.** Kepler sent Galileo *Mysterium cosmographicum*, and Galileo acknowledged it in a letter of 4 August 1597, privately admitting longstanding Copernican sympathy. Their written engagement resumed around Galileo’s telescopic discoveries: Kepler publicly supported *Sidereus nuncius* in *Dissertatio cum Nuncio sidereo* (Prague, 1610), circulated and interpreted Galileo’s letters and anagrams, and published the *Narratio* concerning Jupiter’s satellites. No personal meeting is securely documented. [Museo Galileo correspondence chronology](https://galileoteca.museogalileo.it/GTArchivi/QCrono?anno=1597&lang=it); [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kepler/)

### Robert Fludd

- **[T3] Documented fact.** Kepler initiated a published controversy by criticizing Fludd’s diagrammatic and Hermetic account of cosmic harmony in an appendix to *Harmonices mundi* (Linz, 1619). Fludd replied in *Veritatis proscenium* (1621); Kepler answered with *Apologia pro suo opere Harmonices Mundi* (1622); and Fludd responded again in *Monochordum mundi* (1622). Their connection was a reciprocal print discourse over proof, images, mathematics, archetypes, and harmony; no personal meeting is established. [Oxford study of Kepler’s English reception](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Aef01b5b9-e928-4596-aafe-b0950926f8ca/files/m504c149c3a32f89f35009c4875614158)

### Francis Bacon

- **[T3] Reported fact.** Bacon sent copies of his *Instauratio magna/Novum organum* to diplomat Henry Wotton in 1620, and Wotton promised to give one to Kepler. Wotton also reported to Bacon on the portable optical or surveying apparatus he had inspected during his documented 1620 visit to Kepler at Linz. This establishes mediated, one-sided written engagement, but the surviving evidence does not securely prove that Kepler received or answered Bacon’s book. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Francis Bacon](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/francis-bacon/)

### René Descartes

- **[T1+] Documented fact.** Descartes studied Kepler’s optics and explicitly acknowledged the debt in a letter to Marin Mersenne dated 31 March 1638, calling Kepler his first teacher in optics and judging him more knowledgeable than his predecessors. This was posthumous written assessment rather than correspondence with Kepler. [Archive for History of Exact Sciences](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00407-019-00236-w)

### Isaac Newton

- **[T1+] Documented fact.** Newton’s *Philosophiae naturalis principia mathematica* (London, 1687) mathematically reformulated and dynamically explained the orbital relations derived by Kepler. Newton showed how central-force dynamics and universal gravitation yield the area relation and elliptical orbits, while generalizing the period–distance relation to account for mass. This was foundational posthumous engagement with Kepler’s results, not a personal crossing. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Newton’s *Principia*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/newton-principia/); [NASA overview](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/orbits-and-keplers-laws/)

### Albert Einstein

- **[T1+] Documented fact.** Einstein published “Über Kepler” in the *Frankfurter Zeitung* on 9 November 1930 for the tricentenary of Kepler’s death, interpreting Kepler’s discovery process and relation between theory and observation. He later supplied the introduction to Carola Baumgardt’s *Johannes Kepler: Life and Letters* (New York, 1951). [Bibliographic listing of the 1930 article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_scientific_publications_by_Albert_Einstein); [Folger Shakespeare Library catalogue record for the 1951 book](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/72879)

### Wolfgang Pauli

- **[T1+] Documented fact.** Pauli made Kepler the central historical subject of “The Influence of Archetypal Ideas on the Scientific Theories of Kepler,” published in *Naturerklärung und Psyche* (Zurich, 1952; later translated in *The Interpretation of Nature and the Psyche*). He examined Kepler’s trinitarian cosmology and controversy with Fludd through concepts developed in dialogue with analytical psychology. [CERN Pauli Manuscript Collection](https://cds.cern.ch/record/96730); [Institute for Advanced Study archival typescript](https://albert.ias.edu/entities/archivalmaterial/21f46f60-39ec-4a7a-859d-21d7549665ef)
