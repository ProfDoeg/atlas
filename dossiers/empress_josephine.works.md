## Works by Empress Joséphine

### Epistolary obra

- **1782–1814:** *Correspondance*. An incomplete obra of more than five hundred surviving personal, familial, financial, and intercessory letters, assembled in the standard modern edition by Bernard Chevallier, Maurice Catinat, and Christophe Pincemaille. It is not a deliberately composed “book”: the corpus is dispersed, excludes much routine writing, and is irreparably incomplete because letters—including many addressed to Napoleon—were destroyed. ([Le Monde](https://www.lemonde.fr/archives/article/1997/01/17/livraisons_3740689_1819218.html); [Bibliographie d’histoire du droit](https://bibliographienumeriquedhistoiredudroit-ifg.univ-lorraine.fr/s/droit/item/122688))

### Gesamtkunstwerk and landscape projects

- **1799–1814:** *Malmaison*. Joséphine turned an acquired seventeenth-century country house into a Gesamtkunstwerk joining architecture, interiors, painting and sculpture galleries, gardens, scientific horticulture, animals, music, theatre, hospitality, and political sociability. Authorship is necessarily distributed: Percier, Fontaine, Berthault, gardeners, botanists, artisans, Napoleon, and many artists made its components, while Joséphine supplied the continuing project, selection, patronage, and use; much of the estate was subsequently altered, demolished, or dispersed. ([Musée national de Malmaison](https://musees-nationaux-malmaison.fr/chateau-malmaison/le-domaine); [Fondation Napoléon](https://www.napoleon.org/magazine/lieux/musee-national-du-chateau-de-malmaison))

- **1800–1814:** *The gardens and acclimatization collection at Malmaison*. This was a living, continually revised landscape-and-science project rather than a stable object: an English-style park, rose collection, nurseries, model farm, and hundreds of rare plants cultivated under Joséphine’s direction with professional gardeners and botanists. Its boundaries and numbers resist exact reconstruction because the living collections were dispersed and contemporary publications recorded only selections. ([Musée national de Malmaison](https://musees-nationaux-malmaison.fr/chateau-malmaison/actualite/un-si-beau-jardin); [Journal18](https://www.journal18.org/issue8/josephine-at-malmaison-acclimatizing-self-and-other-in-the-garden/))

- **c. 1803–1805:** *The great glasshouse at Malmaison*. A purpose-built architectural and horticultural apparatus for maintaining exotic plants and entertaining visitors, made at Joséphine’s behest; it belongs simultaneously to the garden, the scientific collection, and the social architecture of Malmaison. ([Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/359457); [Fondation Napoléon](https://www.napoleon.org/magazine/itineraires/rueil-malmaison/))

- **c. 1800–1814:** *The menagerie at Malmaison*. A living collection of exotic animals and a site of exchange with naturalists, not a “work” in the ordinary art-historical sense; calling it something Joséphine made must also acknowledge the animals’ agency, colonial acquisition networks, and the labor of keepers and scientists. ([Université de Neuchâtel](https://libra.unine.ch/entities/publication/2f7336e6-131c-4871-9c7d-9898f91ab86c); [Fondation Napoléon](https://www.napoleon.org/histoire-des-2-empires/biographies/beauharnais-josephine-de-1736-1814-imperatrice-des-francais))

- **1810–1814:** *Navarre as a botanical estate*. After receiving the Normandy domain following her divorce, Joséphine extended her cultivation and documentation project from Malmaison to Navarre. This was unfinished at her death and is best understood as a second site within the same horticultural obra, not as a securely bounded creation attributable to her alone. ([Musée national de Malmaison](https://musees-nationaux-malmaison.fr/chateau-malmaison/actualite/un-si-beau-jardin); [Journal18](https://www.journal18.org/issue8/josephine-at-malmaison-acclimatizing-self-and-other-in-the-garden/))

### Publication projects

- **1803–1805:** *Jardin de la Malmaison*. A twenty-part botanical publication project initiated and financially supported by Joséphine, with descriptions by Étienne-Pierre Ventenat and 120 plates after Pierre-Joseph Redouté. It is her commission and patronal project, but not her authored text or drawn work; the title documents only a selection from the living garden. ([Bibliothèque nationale de France](https://www.bnf.fr/fr/mediatheque/magnolia-jardin-de-la-malmaison-ventenat-redoute); [Musée national de Malmaison](https://musees-nationaux-malmaison.fr/chateau-malmaison/actualite/un-si-beau-jardin))

- **1812–1817:** *Description des plantes rares cultivées à Malmaison et à Navarre*. A continuation entrusted to Aimé Bonpland after Ventenat’s death, illustrated principally from Redouté’s watercolors and supported by Joséphine. It is an unfinished-at-her-death project whose publication continued posthumously to 1817; Bonpland and the illustrators, rather than Joséphine, are the textual and pictorial authors. ([Musée national de Malmaison](https://musees-nationaux-malmaison.fr/chateau-malmaison/actualite/un-si-beau-jardin); [Journal18](https://www.journal18.org/issue8/josephine-at-malmaison-acclimatizing-self-and-other-in-the-garden/))

### Sculpture commissions

- **1802–1812:** *Dancer with Her Hands on Her Hips (Erato)*. An original marble sculpture commissioned from Antonio Canova for Malmaison and presented at the Salon of 1812. Joséphine made the commission, not the statue; the long interval between commission, model, and marble makes it a collaborative patronal project rather than her individual artwork. ([Hugh Honour, “Canova’s Statue of a Dancer”](https://epe.lac-bac.gc.ca/100/205/301/ic/cdc/bulletin/num11/honour1.html?nodisclaimer=1); [Journal of the History of Collections](https://academic.oup.com/jhc/article-abstract/16/1/19/602829))

- **1808–1812:** *Paris*. An original full-length marble commissioned by Joséphine from Canova in December 1808, completed in 1812, and installed at Malmaison. Contemporary and later records distinguish this commissioned statue from the several related busts, workshop versions, and subsequent marbles. ([Art Institute of Chicago](https://www.artic.edu/files/699c4b19-d705-4b21-a2e7-962cefd2c4ba/AIC_MuseumStudies_15-1_UPDF.pdf); [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/211505))

- **1808–after 1814:** *Love Launching His Arrows and Taking Flight*. Joséphine commissioned François-Joseph Bosio to translate his Salon plaster into marble, helping establish the sculptor’s career. The surviving Malmaison record describes the commission but leaves the marble’s completion history less secure, so this is most safely classified as a patronal project rather than an unquestionably completed object in her lifetime. ([Musée national de Malmaison](https://musees-nationaux-malmaison.fr/chateau-malmaison/collection/objet/lamour-lancant-ses-traits-et-senvolant))

- **1812–1817:** *The Three Graces*. Joséphine commissioned Canova’s marble group on 11 June 1812, but died before it was finished; Canova completed it for her heirs. It is therefore both a named sculpture and Joséphine’s unrealized-in-her-lifetime project, while the pre-existing 1810 terracotta shows that she commissioned a subject already under development rather than originating its conception. ([Fondation Napoléon](https://www.napoleon.org/en/history-of-the-two-empires/objects/the-three-graces/); [Victoria and Albert Museum](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/the-three-graces))

### Collection as obra

- **c. 1799–1814:** *The Malmaison art collection*. Joséphine assembled and staged old-master paintings, contemporary French art, antiquities, sculpture, decorative arts, natural-history objects, and curiosities as a consciously arranged collection. It resists treatment as a list of things she “made”: most objects were purchased, received, or appropriated rather than commissioned, but their selection and disposition—especially the modern-art rooms and Grande Galerie—constituted a curatorial obra that was dispersed after her death. ([Carol Solomon Kiefer, ed., *The Empress Josephine: Art and Royal Identity*](https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/publication/The-empress-Josephine-art-royal-identity--22458b49c13d6f8fa3a6d70c7510875c); [Journal of the History of Collections](https://academic.oup.com/jhc/article-abstract/16/1/19/602829))
