# Al-Malik al-Kamil: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary note

This dossier concerns **al-Malik al-Kāmil Muḥammad I**, Ayyubid ruler of Egypt, not his later namesake al-Kāmil Muḥammad II of Mayyāfāriqīn.

The surviving record is asymmetrical. Medieval Arabic chronicles document al-Kamil’s wars, negotiations, court, public patronage, and death in considerable detail, but preserve little about his childhood, household life, appearance, emotions, or private wealth. Latin Christian sources concentrate on the Fifth and Sixth Crusades and on Francis of Assisi. Exact Gregorian equivalents sometimes differ because medieval authors used Islamic, regnal, or liturgical calendars and modern conversions vary by one or several days.

Evidence labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** supported by contemporary material evidence, official instruments, or multiple near-contemporary chronicles.
- **Reported fact:** stated by a medieval or modern source but not independently confirmed.
- **Allegation or interpretation:** an accusation, motive, or causal explanation advanced by a source.
- **Rumor or myth:** a legendary or substantially embellished narrative.
- **Unresolved:** evidence does not permit a firm conclusion.

## Basic Identifying Information

**Full name:** Abū al-Maʿālī al-Malik al-Kāmil Nāṣir al-Dīn Muḥammad ibn al-Malik al-ʿĀdil Abī Bakr Muḥammad ibn Ayyūb.

**Arabic:** أبو المعالي الملك الكامل ناصر الدين محمد بن الملك العادل أبي بكر محمد بن أيوب.

**Common forms:**

- al-Malik al-Kāmil
- al-Kāmil Muḥammad
- al-Kāmil I, to distinguish him from later Ayyubid rulers of the same regnal name
- Malik al-Kamil or Melek el-Kamel in older European literature
- “Sultan of Babylon” in some Latin crusading texts, where “Babylon” means Egypt or Old Cairo rather than Mesopotamian Babylon

**Kunya:** Abū al-Maʿālī; some sources also give Abū al-Muẓaffar.

**Regnal titles:**

- al-Malik al-Kāmil, “the Perfect/Complete King”
- Nāṣir al-Dīn, “Defender of the Religion”
- Sultan of Egypt, conventionally 1218–1238
- In a Meccan sermon: *sulṭān al-qiblatayn*, “sultan of the two directions of prayer”; *khādim al-ḥaramayn al-sharīfayn*, “servant of the two holy sanctuaries”; and *khalīl amīr al-muʾminīn*, “friend of the Commander of the Faithful.” These titles expressed universal and caliphal legitimacy more than a centralized territorial state. [TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/el-melikul-kamil-muhammed)

**Dynasty:** Ayyubid.

**Religion:** Sunni Islam. Contemporary and later biographical literature portrays him as attached to Sunni hadith scholarship, but no complete statement of his personal theology survives.

**Birth:**

- **Best-attested version:** 25 Rabīʿ I 576 AH, corresponding to **19 August 1180**, in Cairo.
- **Competing version:** 573 AH/1177.
- **Status:** unresolved at the level of absolute certainty; 19 August 1180 is the more precise traditional date. [TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/el-melikul-kamil-muhammed)

**Death:**

- **Arabic biographical tradition:** 21 Rajab 635 AH, commonly converted as **9 March 1238**, Damascus.
- **Frequently repeated Western date:** **6 March 1238**.
- **Status:** the Hijri date and Damascus location are well attested; the Gregorian conversion varies.
- **Burial:** initially in the Citadel of Damascus, reportedly moved later to the Kāmiliyya mausoleum. [TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/el-melikul-kamil-muhammed)

**Principal offices and territories:**

- Ayyubid prince and administrator in the Jazira from 1191
- Father’s deputy and practical governor in Egypt after 1200
- Sultan of Egypt, 1218–1238
- Overlord, with varying degrees of actual control, in Palestine, the Jazira, Yemen, Mecca, and parts of Syria and southeastern Anatolia
- Ruler of Damascus from December 1237 until his death

## Family and Ancestry

### Dynastic ancestry

Al-Kamil was a grandson of Ayyūb ibn Shādhī and a member of the ruling house founded by Ayyūb’s son Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn Yūsuf ibn Ayyūb—Saladin.

His father was **al-Malik al-ʿĀdil Sayf al-Dīn Abū Bakr**, called “Saphadin” in many Latin sources. Al-ʿAdil was Saladin’s brother, military associate, diplomat, and eventual successor as paramount Ayyubid sultan.

The medieval mainstream describes the family as originating among the **Rawādiyya Kurds** of the Dvin region. Later members of the dynasty advanced alternative genealogies, including Arab or Umayyad descent. These prestigious lineages are best classified as **dynastic claims**, not demonstrated genealogy. The family’s historically traceable rise began when Shādhī and his sons entered military service in Iraq and Syria, first around Tikrit and then under the Zengids. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ayyubids/)

### Parents

**Father:** al-ʿAdil I, died in 1218 during the Fifth Crusade crisis.

**Mother:** her personal name is not securely established in the sources consulted. A report associates her with construction or sponsorship of the dome over the tomb of Imam al-Shāfiʿī in Cairo; al-Kamil subsequently had her buried near that shrine. The monument’s inscription attributes work to al-Kamil in 608 AH/1211–12, before his independent sultanate, which has encouraged the interpretation that his mother financed or initiated it. This remains a **reported attribution**, not a settled identification of the patron. [TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/el-melikul-kamil-muhammed)

### Siblings

Al-ʿAdil distributed territories among numerous sons. The most politically important brothers in al-Kamil’s life were:

- **al-Malik al-Muʿaẓẓam ʿĪsā**, ruler of Damascus; essential ally during the Fifth Crusade, later al-Kamil’s rival.
- **al-Malik al-Ashraf Mūsā**, ruler in the Jazira and later Damascus; alternately ally and rival.
- **al-Malik al-Fāʾiz**, whom dissident emirs attempted to elevate over al-Kamil in 1218–19.
- **al-Malik al-Ṣāliḥ Ismāʿīl**, ruler of Bosra and briefly Damascus after al-Ashraf’s death.
- **al-Muẓaffar Ghāzī**, ruler of Mayyāfāriqīn.
- Other brothers governed components of the Ayyubid confederation.

These were not merely relatives holding ceremonial appanages. Their armies, courts, alliances, and territorial claims determined much of al-Kamil’s career.

### Marriage and Saladin connection

A report in the biographical tradition makes Saladin both al-Kamil’s paternal uncle and his **father-in-law**, implying that al-Kamil married a daughter of Saladin. The identity and chronology of this wife are not consistently preserved in accessible accounts. The relationship should be marked **reported fact**, plausible within Ayyubid dynastic marriage practice but poorly documented in the sources consulted.

### Known or reported spouses and concubines

Evidence is fragmentary:

- **Sawda bint al-Faqīh** is identified in later family narratives as the mother of al-ʿAdil II.
- **Ward al-Munā** appears in accounts connected with al-Ṣāliḥ Ayyūb’s household; her precise relationship to al-Kamil varies in derivative sources and should not be stated more firmly without manuscript-level verification.
- Other wives or concubines almost certainly existed, but a dependable complete list has not been reconstructed.

### Children

Important known sons include:

- **al-Malik al-Ṣāliḥ Najm al-Dīn Ayyūb** (c. 1205–1249), used as a dynastic governor and military commander in the Jazira; later sultan of Egypt, 1240–1249.
- **al-Malik al-ʿĀdil II Sayf al-Dīn Abū Bakr** (c. 1221–1248), designated for Egypt and immediate successor there in 1238; deposed by his brother in 1240.
- **al-Malik al-Masʿūd Yūsuf**, ruler in Yemen and associated with Ayyubid control of Mecca; died before his father, usually dated 1229.
- At least one daughter whose marriage guardian was a member of the influential Awlād al-Shaykh administrative family. The surviving reference shows the closeness of that household to al-Kamil but does not establish a complete list of daughters. [Comparative study of the Ayyubid–Mamluk transition](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/14765276.2023.2274342)

Al-Kamil’s division of power between al-ʿAdil II in Egypt and al-Ṣāliḥ Ayyūb in the eastern territories did not produce a stable settlement. Their subsequent conflict accelerated the fragmentation of his political system.

## Childhood and Early Life, 1180–1191

No continuous childhood narrative survives.

Al-Kamil was probably born in Cairo while his father was already a senior member of Saladin’s military-political establishment. His upbringing would have occurred inside an itinerant ruling household spanning Egypt, Syria, and upper Mesopotamia. Unlike an ordinary courtier’s son, he was formed for provincial command, revenue administration, dynastic negotiation, and war.

Later biographers attribute to him substantial literacy in Arabic poetry and hadith. This does not prove every detail of a formal curriculum, but it is consistent with the education of an elite Ayyubid prince.

No reliable evidence was located concerning childhood illnesses, accidents, intimate friendships, or psychological crises.

## Education and Formation

### Teachers and hadith transmission

The most specific record identifies:

- **Ibn Dihya al-Kalbī**—the Andalusi traveler, philologist, hadith scholar, and literary author—as the tutor appointed by al-Kamil’s father.
- **Ibn Barrī**
- **Abū ʿAbd Allāh Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī ibn Ṣadaqa al-Ḥarrānī**
- **ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAlī al-Khiraqī**
- **Abū al-Qāsim ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAbd al-Majīd Ibn al-Ṣafrāwī**, from whom al-Kamil reportedly heard and transmitted forty hadiths.

These relationships make al-Kamil a participant, not just a financial patron, in the culture of hadith audition and authorization. [TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/el-melikul-kamil-muhammed)

Ibn Dihya later received direction of al-Kamil’s Cairo hadith college. He also composed works in the sultan’s orbit, including material on Maghribi and Andalusi poets. [Study of Ibn Dihya](https://abu.edu.iq/index.php/research/articles/13670)

### Literary formation

Medieval biographical reports say al-Kamil:

- knew poetry well enough to test court poets;
- composed verse;
- enjoyed literary disputation;
- cultivated scholars, poets, and men of learning.

Only scattered verse or attributed lines appear to have survived. No secure critical corpus of his poetry was identified.

### Political formation

His strongest formative influences were probably:

- his father al-ʿAdil’s coalition-building and diplomatic practice;
- Saladin’s model of dynastic kingship;
- hands-on provincial command in the Jazira;
- the Ayyubid practice of allocating territories among relatives while recognizing a senior sultan;
- repeated negotiation with Latin Christian states and trading communities.

## Early Career, 1191–1218

### Jazira appointment, 1191

In 587 AH/1191, while Saladin was still alive, al-Kamil received responsibility for part of the Jazira. His youth—roughly eleven if the 1180 birth date is accepted—means this was initially an appanage or supervised appointment rather than autonomous personal rule.

### Mardin campaign, 1199

In 595 AH/1199, while al-ʿAdil moved against Saladin’s son al-Afḍal in Damascus, al-Kamil was left in command of Jaziran forces besieging Mardin. This is the first clearly described major military responsibility of his career.

### Deputy in Egypt, 1200–1218

Al-ʿAdil entered Cairo on 6 July 1200, displaced Saladin’s sons from paramount rule, and established himself as sultan of Egypt and Syria. Al-Kamil became his principal deputy and was entrusted with Egypt’s government. [TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/el-melikul-kamil-muhammed)

During this long deputyship, al-Kamil acquired experience in:

- Egyptian taxation and treasury administration;
- Nile transport and logistics;
- military recruitment;
- relations with Syrian Ayyubid princes;
- diplomacy with the Latin states;
- court patronage and religious construction.

It is therefore misleading to treat 1218 as the beginning of his political career. By then he had governed Egypt in his father’s name for approximately eighteen years.

### Imam al-Shāfiʿī mausoleum

An inscription dated 608 AH/1211–12 associates al-Kamil with construction of the large dome over Imam al-Shāfiʿī’s tomb in Cairo. Because he was not yet independent sultan, some accounts assign the actual patronage to his mother. Al-Kamil’s burial of his mother nearby supports a family connection but does not conclusively resolve the patronage question.

## Chronological Life History

## The Fifth Crusade and Accession, 1218–1221

### Crusader landing and Damietta defenses

A multinational crusading force assembled at Acre and moved against Egypt in 1218. The strategy was to seize Egypt, the Ayyubids’ richest territory, and use it to recover Jerusalem.

Al-Kamil marched north with the Egyptian army and established a camp at al-ʿĀdiliyya, south of Damietta. Crusaders captured the chain tower controlling navigation into the Damietta branch of the Nile. News of that loss reached al-ʿAdil in Syria shortly before his death on 31 August 1218.

### Succession crisis and Ibn al-Mashtūb’s conspiracy

Al-Kamil inherited the Egyptian sultanate amid invasion.

The Kurdish Hakkari emir **ʿImād al-Dīn Aḥmad ibn ʿAlī Ibn al-Mashtūb**, supported by other commanders, attempted to replace him with his brother al-Fāʾiz. Al-Kamil, fearing arrest, left the camp at night for Ashmūm Tannāḥ. The army dispersed, leaving equipment and supplies; crusaders exploited the abandoned position.

This was a documented political conspiracy in the chronicle tradition, not a later rumor. Al-Kamil reportedly contemplated flight toward Yemen, where his son held power. His brother al-Muʿaẓẓam arrived from Damascus, restored his position, and removed al-Fāʾiz and Ibn al-Mashtūb from the Egyptian theater. [Arabic historical account](https://dorar.net/history/event/2417)

### Fall of Damietta

After a prolonged siege marked by disease, hunger, attrition, and disagreement among defenders, the crusaders captured Damietta in November 1219. Some chronologies render the event in October because of differences between entry, final occupation, and calendar conversion.

Al-Kamil’s failure to relieve the city was the greatest territorial defeat of his reign, although it did not destroy the Egyptian field army.

### Peace offers

During the siege and afterward, al-Kamil offered extensive territorial concessions in exchange for evacuation of Egypt. The reported terms included Jerusalem and much of Saladin’s former conquests, while initially excluding the strategically important Transjordan fortresses of al-Karak and al-Shawbak.

Crusader leaders divided over acceptance. **John of Brienne** was more receptive; papal legate **Pelagius of Albano** rejected or obstructed the settlement and sought additional fortresses and a large indemnity, reported as 300,000 or 500,000 dinars depending on the source.

The offers are documented in both Christian and Muslim narrative traditions. Their precise successive terms remain disputed.

### Francis of Assisi, 1219

During a pause in fighting, Francis of Assisi crossed from the crusader camp and obtained an audience with al-Kamil, probably in August or September 1219.

The earliest evidence includes:

- a letter and later history by **Jacques de Vitry**, bishop of Acre, who was present in the crusading theater;
- the account associated with **Ernoul**;
- Thomas of Celano’s *Vita prima* of 1228–29;
- later Franciscan lives and hagiographies.

The basic audience is a **reported fact with strong near-contemporary attestation**. No known Arabic chronicle records it, and no transcript of the conversation survives. [Cambridge study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-review/article/abs/friar-and-the-sultan-francis-of-assisis-mission-to-egypt/9DDB3996FE485ECBBB10B61121706F40), [study of the source tradition](https://www.terresainte.net/?p=28671)

Claims that Francis proposed trial by fire, challenged Muslim scholars, nearly converted al-Kamil, or secretly secured special privileges for Franciscans arise in later hagiographic development. They cannot be treated as contemporary documentation.

### Crusader advance and defeat, 1221

In summer 1221 the crusaders advanced south toward Cairo despite John of Brienne’s objections. Al-Kamil and his brothers exploited the Nile flood, opened or controlled waterways, severed the invaders’ communications with Damietta, and trapped the army amid inundated ground.

The crusaders requested terms on 27 August 1221. They surrendered Damietta in exchange for evacuation, safe passage, release of prisoners, and a truce, commonly given as eight years.

Al-Kamil’s son al-Ṣāliḥ Ayyūb, reportedly about fifteen, was exchanged as a hostage while the transfer occurred. Damietta returned to Ayyubid control on or about 8 September 1221. [Arabic account of the surrender](https://dorar.net/history/event/2429)

Crusader narratives record that al-Kamil supplied food and treated defeated enemies with restraint. This is broadly plausible and appears in sources not naturally inclined to flatter him, although exact quantities and set-piece speeches should be treated cautiously.

## Consolidation, Patronage, and Family Rivalries, 1221–1227

The victory brought Egypt a period of comparative security and prosperity. It did not end Ayyubid competition.

### Kāmiliyya college

In 621 AH/1224, al-Kamil founded Cairo’s **Dār al-Ḥadīth al-Kāmiliyya**, an institution devoted particularly to hadith. He appointed Ibn Dihya to direct it and endowed it through waqf property.

The institution was important because specialized *dār al-ḥadīth* foundations were still relatively new in Egypt. Its foundation joined scholarly patronage to the public presentation of Sunni kingship.

### Break with al-Muʿaẓẓam

Al-Kamil’s alliance with al-Muʿaẓẓam deteriorated soon after the Fifth Crusade. In 1222 al-Kamil aligned with al-Ashraf against him. Al-Muʿaẓẓam sought counteralliances with Muẓaffar al-Dīn Gökböri, the Seljuk ruler ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Kayqubād, and rulers of Ahlat and Mardin.

The struggle involved:

- dynastic seniority;
- allocation of Jaziran and Syrian cities;
- relations with the Khwarazmians;
- control of Jerusalem and Transjordan;
- fear that one brother would reunify the Ayyubid domains at the others’ expense.

### Yemen and Mecca

Al-Kamil’s son al-Masʿūd extended or maintained Ayyubid power in Yemen and Mecca around 1223. A sermon in Mecca proclaimed al-Kamil’s titles as protector of both holy sanctuaries. Actual control depended on regional governors and was less uniform than the titles imply.

## Frederick II and the Sixth Crusade, 1226–1229

### Diplomatic opening

Fearing al-Muʿaẓẓam and wider eastern coalitions, al-Kamil approached **Frederick II**, Holy Roman emperor and king of Sicily, through the emir and diplomat **Fakhr al-Dīn ibn al-Shaykh**.

Frederick’s court had long-standing Arabic and Mediterranean connections. Embassies exchanged gifts, information, and scientific questions. Some later stories describe mathematical or philosophical problems sent between courts. Intellectual exchange is credible, but individual anecdotes require source-specific verification.

Al-Kamil’s apparent purposes were:

- to deter a major crusading invasion of Egypt;
- to gain a counterweight against al-Muʿaẓẓam;
- to protect Mediterranean commerce;
- to settle the Jerusalem question by negotiation rather than another Egyptian campaign.

Al-Muʿaẓẓam died in November 1227, reducing the immediate need for Frederick’s military assistance. But al-Kamil had made commitments, Frederick required a visible result from his crusade, and both continued negotiations.

### Frederick’s arrival

Frederick reached Acre in September 1228 while excommunicated by Pope Gregory IX. He had only limited military strength and faced hostility from parts of the Latin ecclesiastical establishment.

Al-Kamil was encamped in Palestine while negotiating both with Frederick and with al-Ashraf over the partition of al-Muʿaẓẓam’s lands, then held by the latter’s son al-Nāṣir Dāwūd.

### Treaty of Jaffa

The agreement is dated variously:

- **18 February 1229** in much modern scholarship;
- **24 February 1229 / 28 Rabīʿ I 626** in another conversion tradition;
- preliminary arrangements may have been concluded before the public settlement.

The treaty’s major provisions reportedly included:

- a truce of approximately ten years;
- transfer of Jerusalem, Bethlehem, Nazareth, and connecting corridors or specified surrounding places to Frankish control;
- Christian access to Jerusalem;
- Muslim possession or administration of the **Haram al-Sharif**, including the Dome of the Rock and al-Aqsa Mosque;
- continued Muslim religious access and worship;
- restrictions on rebuilding Jerusalem’s fortifications;
- retention by Muslims of various villages and strategic positions;
- recognition of other territorial arrangements around Jaffa, Sidon, Toron, and Montfort, with the exact extent differing among sources.

A Latin narrative of Frederick’s expedition confirms negotiations at Jaffa while incorrectly calling al-Kamil ruler of both Egypt and Damascus at that moment. [Medieval Sourcebook](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/source/1228frederick2.asp)

### Reaction

The settlement was controversial on both sides.

Among Muslims, chroniclers describe public grief and denunciation over the transfer of Jerusalem only forty-one years after Saladin’s conquest. Preacher **Sibṭ ibn al-Jawzī** reportedly delivered a denunciatory sermon in Damascus. Ibn Wāṣil transmitted a family eyewitness account of crying and protest in Jerusalem.

Among Latin Christians, Frederick’s excommunication, unilateral conduct, and disputes with the patriarch and military orders complicated reception. He crowned himself—or ceremonially placed the crown on his own head—in the Church of the Holy Sepulchre rather than receiving a normal ecclesiastical coronation.

### The muezzin story

A frequently retold narrative says Frederick noticed that the call to prayer was absent during his Jerusalem stay; al-Kamil had ordered it suspended as a courtesy, but Frederick said he had wanted to hear it.

**Evidentiary status:** reported anecdote in medieval narrative tradition. It is consistent with the treaty’s preservation of Muslim worship at the sanctuary, but the dialogue is not verifiable verbatim.

### Assessment of motive

Three interpretations recur:

1. **Strategic settlement:** al-Kamil relinquished a largely unfortified city while retaining the Haram and protecting Egypt, trade, and his Syrian campaign.
2. **Dynastic expediency:** he used a foreign Christian ruler in an internal Ayyubid struggle.
3. **Betrayal:** hostile Muslim memory casts the treaty as surrender of a sacred trust for personal rule.

The transfer itself and resulting outrage are documented facts. “Betrayal,” “friendship,” and “religious tolerance” are interpretations, not documentary descriptions of motive.

## Eastern Campaigns and Expansion, 1229–1237

### Conflict over Damascus

After al-Muʿaẓẓam’s death, al-Kamil and al-Ashraf pressured al-Nāṣir Dāwūd. Al-Ashraf eventually obtained Damascus; al-Nāṣir was compensated chiefly with al-Karak and adjacent territories. The arrangements subordinated the nephew but did not eliminate him as a political actor.

### Khwarazmian and Mongol pressures

The collapse of Khwarazmian power under Mongol assault sent armies, refugees, and rival commanders into northern Mesopotamia. Al-Kamil had to manage:

- Jalāl al-Dīn Khwarazmshah;
- Seljuk expansion under ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn Kayqubād;
- Artuqid rulers;
- Ayyubid relatives;
- early Mongol incursions;
- appeals from the Abbasid caliphate.

### Campaign of 1230–1232

Al-Kamil campaigned in the Jazira and southeastern Anatolia. He appointed al-Ṣāliḥ Ayyūb to command advance forces and operated around Harran.

On 7 October 1232 he began the siege of **Āmid**—modern Diyarbakır. The Artuqid ruler al-Masʿūd surrendered the city on 18 October. Al-Kamil then took **Ḥiṣn Kayfā**, modern Hasankeyf, in November 1232, ending that Artuqid branch’s independent rule. [TDV chronology](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/el-melikul-kamil-muhammed)

### Anatolian campaign of 1234

Leaving al-ʿAdil as deputy in Cairo, al-Kamil went to Damascus in May 1234 and entered Anatolia with al-Ashraf and a coalition reportedly comprising sixteen Ayyubid princes.

The campaign failed to produce its intended result. Seljuk defensive strategy, difficult supply conditions, and the princes’ fear that victory would make al-Kamil too powerful undermined the coalition. This was a significant strategic failure.

### Recovery of Edessa and Harran

In January 1236 al-Kamil recovered **Edessa/Urfa**; in February he recovered **Harran**. Captured Seljuk soldiers were sent to Egypt.

He placed eastern possessions under al-Ṣāliḥ Ayyūb. This gave the son an independent power base but also helped structure the succession conflict that followed al-Kamil’s death.

---

## Final Syrian Campaign and Death, 1237–1238

Al-Ashraf died on 27 August 1237. Their brother al-Ṣāliḥ Ismāʿīl took Damascus and continued resistance to al-Kamil’s ascendancy.

Al-Kamil marched into Syria and besieged Damascus. Hostile later narratives accuse him of cutting or constricting the city’s water supply; siege pressure on water is plausible and appears in polemical retellings, but its exact extent requires direct comparison of Ibn Wāṣil, Sibṭ ibn al-Jawzī, and later chronicles.

Damascus surrendered on **28 December 1237**. For the first time near the end of his life, al-Kamil directly controlled Egypt and Damascus.

The Abbasid caliph’s envoys then asked for assistance against Mongol danger. Al-Kamil sent approximately 3,000 troops under his nephew al-Nāṣir Dāwūd toward Baghdad.

He fell ill at Damascus and died in Rajab 635 AH, probably 9 March 1238. The nature of the illness and immediate medical cause are not preserved. There is no strong evidence for poisoning, assassination, addiction, or a chronic psychiatric condition.

He was buried the following day in Damascus Citadel and later transferred to the Kāmiliyya mausoleum.

## Government, Institutions, Technologies, Projects, and Financial Interests

## Political structure

Al-Kamil did not rule a modern unitary state. The Ayyubid order was a dynastic confederation of appanages. Governance depended on:

- personal allegiance to the senior sultan;
- family bargaining;
- military households;
- iqṭāʿ revenue assignments;
- control of cities and fortresses;
- chancery and fiscal officials;
- negotiated relationships with local elites.

His effective authority was greatest in Egypt. Claims over Yemen, Mecca, Syria, and the Jazira fluctuated.

## Ministers and administrative families

The most important minister associated with his reign was **Ṣafī al-Dīn ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAlī ibn Shukr**. Ibn Shukr’s administration strengthened the vizierate and treasury but attracted hostility over fiscal severity. The evidence for individual exactions and confiscations needs to be distinguished from the broader fact of centralized revenue collection.

The **Awlād al-Shaykh**—the family of Fakhr al-Dīn and other senior officials—served as diplomats, commanders, marriage intermediaries, and political brokers under al-Kamil and his sons.

## Treasury and taxation

Biographical accounts credit al-Kamil, like his father, with strengthening the treasury. No complete budget, personal balance sheet, or inventory of private property survives. “Wealth” therefore cannot be quantified reliably.

The economic basis of his power included:

- Nile agriculture and land tax;
- customs and maritime commerce;
- Alexandria and Red Sea trade;
- urban taxes;
- iqṭāʿ-based military support;
- minting authority;
- income assigned through religious endowments.

## Monetary reform of 1225

In Dhū al-Qaʿda 622 AH/November 1225, al-Kamil initiated monetary changes:

- renewed or expanded copper coinage;
- changes in silver dirham types;
- gradual substitution of naskh for Kufic inscriptions;
- continuation of gold dinars in Cairo and Alexandria.

Modern chemical analysis led numismatist Paul Balog to call the silver reform a “colossal fraud”: the supposedly improved round dirham contained approximately the same low silver proportion as the unpopular older “black” dirham. Bacharach and Anwar reproduce this finding but observe that the visual distinctions were not necessarily clear enough to demonstrate a deliberate scheme by the sultan personally to deceive users.

Accordingly:

- **Documented fact:** a monetary reform and new coin types occurred.
- **Documented modern finding:** tested coins did not show the advertised or presumed dramatic improvement in fineness.
- **Interpretation:** calling this al-Kamil’s personal fraud goes beyond the evidence unless the ruler’s knowledge and intent can be demonstrated. [IFAO numismatic study](https://www.ifao.egnet.net/anisl/046/02)

A surviving Cairo dinar dated 628 AH records al-Kamil as issuing authority and provides direct material evidence for his titulature and mint system. [Ashmolean Heberden Coin Room](https://hcr.ashmus.ox.ac.uk/coin/hcr13518)

## Trade policy

Al-Kamil maintained commercial relations with Frankish ports and Mediterranean merchants even amid conflict. The Treaty of Jaffa has been interpreted partly as an effort to preserve profitable trade and avoid renewed devastation.

No evidence was found for privately held companies, patents, inventions, or business corporations in the modern sense. Such categories are anachronistic for his political economy.

## Construction and religious foundations

Projects securely or plausibly connected with him include:

- Dār al-Ḥadīth al-Kāmiliyya in Cairo, founded 1224;
- endowments for its staff and students;
- the dome of Imam al-Shāfiʿī’s mausoleum, whether directly his project or his mother’s;
- the Kāmiliyya mausoleum associated with his later burial;
- repairs, fortifications, and infrastructure connected with military operations, though comprehensive attribution remains difficult.

---

## Political, Religious, and Intellectual Development

### Dynastic pragmatism

Al-Kamil’s policies consistently prioritized survival of Egypt and primacy within the Ayyubid house. He alternated war, hostage exchange, territorial bargain, marriage alliance, religious patronage, and diplomacy with Christian rulers.

### Sunni scholarship

His transmission of hadith, patronage of Ibn Dihya, and creation of the Kāmiliyya indicate active identification with Sunni scholarly culture. Later biographers describe him as adhering to the Prophetic sunna and associating with learned and virtuous people. This is conventional praise but is supported by institutional acts.

### Attitude toward Christians

Evidence supports diplomatic flexibility, protected negotiation, and willingness to converse with Christian envoys. It does not prove modern pluralism or religious relativism.

He fought crusading armies, offered territorial concessions, received Francis, negotiated with Frederick, and preserved Muslim control of the Haram while transferring much of Jerusalem. These actions belonged to medieval statecraft and cannot be reduced either to “tolerance” or “hostility.”

### Literature and science

Al-Kamil reportedly enjoyed poetry and scholarly questions. Frederick II’s embassy exchanges contributed to later stories of a ruler interested in mathematics, philosophy, and natural science. The general intellectual atmosphere is documented; particular question-and-answer tales are unevenly sourced.

### Sufi associations

A later story says al-Kamil admired the poetry of **Ibn al-Fāriḍ**, offered him a large gift, and proposed a shrine or tomb construction, which the poet declined. This is best labeled **reported devotional-literary tradition**. It may reflect genuine court appreciation but is not strong evidence of a close personal relationship.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Al-ʿAdil I

Father, sovereign, political mentor, and predecessor. Al-Kamil’s long deputyship under him supplied the foundation of his rule.

### Al-Muʿaẓẓam ʿĪsā

Brother, rescuer during the 1218 conspiracy, military partner against the Fifth Crusade, then major rival. Their changing relationship is central to al-Kamil’s turn toward Frederick II.

### Al-Ashraf Mūsā

Brother and repeated ally against al-Muʿaẓẓam and eastern opponents; later a rival when subordinate princes feared al-Kamil’s concentration of power.

### Al-Nāṣir Dāwūd

Nephew and ruler of Damascus after al-Muʿaẓẓam. Al-Kamil displaced him from Damascus but retained him within the dynastic system and later entrusted him with troops for Baghdad.

### Ibn al-Mashtūb

Senior emir and leader of the attempt to depose al-Kamil during the Fifth Crusade. His conspiracy exposed the sultan’s initial dependence on military magnates.

### John of Brienne

King of Jerusalem and crusader commander. He opposed the papal legate’s decision to reject al-Kamil’s territorial offers and later shared in negotiating surrender after the failed advance.

### Pelagius of Albano

Papal legate and principal advocate of continuing the Egyptian campaign. His rejection of settlement offers indirectly enabled al-Kamil’s eventual military victory.

### Frederick II

Diplomatic counterpart, temporary strategic partner, and treaty signatory. Their relationship was based on reciprocal need rather than demonstrated intimate friendship.

### Fakhr al-Dīn ibn al-Shaykh

Commander, courtier, and diplomat who mediated between al-Kamil and Frederick II. His family remained influential under al-Kamil’s sons.

### Francis of Assisi

Christian friar received during the Fifth Crusade. The meeting became far more culturally prominent after both men’s deaths than it was in contemporary Arabic political history.

### Ibn Dihya al-Kalbī

Tutor, scholar, author, and first head of the Kāmiliyya hadith institution.

### Abbasid caliphs

Al-Kamil sought recognition within the Sunni caliphal order and placed caliphal names on coins. Shortly before his death, Baghdad requested his aid against the Mongols.

## Controversies, Allegations, Investigations, and Financial Questions

No medieval equivalent of a surviving lawsuit, criminal indictment, or formal corruption trial against al-Kamil is known. Controversies arise principally from chronicles, sermons, political polemic, and modern national or religious interpretation.

## “Surrender” of Jerusalem

**Documented fact:** al-Kamil transferred Jerusalem and associated territories to Frederick II under the 1229 truce while reserving Muslim control and worship at the Haram.

**Documented reaction:** many Muslims condemned the agreement, mourned the transfer, and compared it unfavorably with Saladin’s 1187 conquest.

**Hostile characterization:** later writers call al-Kamil a traitor who sold or gave away Jerusalem for his throne. Modern Arabic polemics continue to describe the treaty as *khiyāna*, betrayal. [Example of modern critical reception](https://www.almasryalyoum.com/news/details/482154), [explicitly hostile account](https://islamstory.com/ar/artical/3407838/%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%83%D8%A7%D9%85%D9%84-%D9%88%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AA%D9%81%D8%B1%D9%8A%D8%B7-%D9%81%D9%8A-%D8%A8%D9%8A%D8%AA-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%85%D9%82%D8%AF%D8%B3)

**Complicating evidence:**

- Jerusalem’s walls had been dismantled or impaired, limiting its military defensibility.
- The Muslim sanctuary remained under Muslim authority.
- The truce protected Egypt from a renewed invasion.
- Al-Kamil faced rival Ayyubids, Seljuks, Khwarazmians, and emerging Mongol pressure.
- Al-Kamil and his brothers had already offered Jerusalem during the Fifth Crusade in exchange for Damietta.

These points explain strategic logic but do not negate contemporary outrage.

## Use of foreign powers in a family conflict

**Documented fact:** al-Kamil solicited Frederick’s involvement while threatened by al-Muʿaẓẓam and continued negotiations after that brother died.

**Interpretation:** critics describe this as importing a crusader sovereign into an Ayyubid civil war. Defenders describe it as containment of an expedition already being prepared. Both interpretations arise from the same diplomatic record.

## 1218–19 military collapse

**Documented fact:** al-Kamil abandoned his camp during Ibn al-Mashtūb’s conspiracy, the army dispersed, and crusaders gained an operational advantage.

**Competing interpretations:**

- necessary escape from imminent arrest;
- failure of leadership under pressure;
- evidence of the fragility of Ayyubid military loyalty.

The subsequent recovery with al-Muʿaẓẓam’s support prevents the episode from being described as permanent flight.

## Damietta peace concessions

Al-Kamil offered Jerusalem and extensive territory while the crusaders held Damietta. Critics treat the offer as premature capitulation. Militarily, Egypt’s loss would have threatened the Ayyubid regime’s main revenue base, and exchange of inland territory for evacuation had precedent in crusader diplomacy.

The crusaders’ rejection made the offer moot. It should nevertheless be included as a documented willingness to relinquish major conquests.

## Monetary “fraud”

**Documented scientific finding:** surviving silver issues associated with the 1225 reform did not contain the greatly improved silver content implied by the reform’s reputation.

**Scholarly accusation:** Paul Balog called it a “colossal fraud.”

**Unresolved responsibility:** no evidence consulted establishes whether al-Kamil personally designed a deceptive standard, relied on mint officials, or was associated retrospectively with a technically disappointing reform. [IFAO study](https://www.ifao.egnet.net/anisl/046/02)

## Fiscal severity

Al-Kamil and Ibn Shukr strengthened the treasury. Medieval criticism of viziers frequently includes allegations of burdensome taxation, confiscation, and enrichment. Without surviving case records, individual claims should remain **reported accusations**, not convictions.

## Siege of Damascus and water

Hostile retellings accuse al-Kamil of depriving Damascus of water in 1237 to force its submission.

- **Probable fact:** Damascus was subjected to siege pressure and surrendered.
- **Reported accusation:** manipulation or denial of water endangered inhabitants.
- **Unresolved:** scale, duration, direct orders, and casualties.

## Dynastic dispossession

Al-Kamil repeatedly redistributed relatives’ territories and displaced al-Nāṣir Dāwūd and al-Ṣāliḥ Ismāʿīl from Damascus. These were recognized features of Ayyubid politics but generated hostile depictions of ambition and familial betrayal.

## Adversarial search findings, including 2024–2026 material

Searches combining his name in English and Arabic with controversy, scandal, accusation, betrayal, conspiracy, criticism, fraud, and recent-year filters produced:

- continuing polemics over the 1229 transfer of Jerusalem;
- disputes about whether the Francis encounter represents interfaith dialogue or attempted conversion;
- the numismatic “fraud” characterization;
- accusations of using Frederick II against Muslim relatives;
- retellings of siege coercion at Damascus;
- no credible evidence of modern-style lawsuits, money laundering, secret banking structures, sexual scandal, intelligence-service connections, or undiscovered criminal proceedings;
- no new documentary scandal concerning this thirteenth-century al-Kamil in the most recent two years.

Some recent search results concerned **different rulers named al-Malik al-Kamil**, especially al-Kamil Muḥammad II of Mayyāfāriqīn or Southeast Asian rulers. They are not evidence about this subject.

A 2026 Italian study reiterates the abundance of Arabic evidence for al-Kamil’s relationship with Frederick but the lack of direct Arabic testimony for Francis’s visit. [L’Idomeneo article](https://ese-journals.unisalento.it/index.php/idomeneo/article/view/32855)

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, Propaganda, and Disputed Narratives

## Francis converts—or nearly converts—the sultan

**Story:** Francis preached so persuasively that al-Kamil nearly accepted Christianity but refrained from fear of his subjects.

**Appearance and circulation:** developed in Franciscan hagiographies written after the encounter, especially Bonaventure’s later thirteenth-century life.

**Evidence for:** al-Kamil received Francis and allowed him to return safely.

**Evidence against:** no Arabic conversion narrative, no observable change in al-Kamil’s Sunni patronage, coinage, titles, or policies, and no contemporary transcript.

**Status:** hagiographic legend.

**Reputational effect:** made al-Kamil the receptive Muslim ruler in Christian saintly biography, sometimes reducing him to a supporting character in Francis’s spiritual triumph.

## Trial by fire

**Story:** Francis challenged Muslim religious scholars to enter a fire with him as a test of faith; they withdrew.

**Source history:** prominent in Bonaventure and later images, including Giotto-associated Franciscan iconography.

**Evidence:** absent from the earliest concise reports and from Arabic chronicles.

**Status:** later hagiographic elaboration.

**Effect:** became one of the dominant visual representations of the meeting, shifting its meaning from protected diplomatic audience to miraculous religious contest.

## Al-Kamil gave Francis a horn or safe-conduct

Objects and privileges have been associated with the meeting, including an ivory horn preserved in Franciscan tradition and claims that al-Kamil granted Franciscans lasting access to the Holy Land.

**Status:** the audience and safe return are well supported; the provenance of specific relics and claims of foundational legal privileges are disputed or legendary.

## The meeting as modern “dialogue summit”

Modern interfaith literature often presents Francis and al-Kamil as equal partners conducting peaceful theological dialogue.

**Evidence:** they met peacefully during war, and al-Kamil did not execute or imprison Francis.

**Limitation:** the content of their conversation is unknown; early Christian sources frame Francis as missionary, and al-Kamil’s motives may have included curiosity, diplomacy, piety, or routine protection of an envoy.

**Status:** modern interpretive mythology built on a historical encounter.

## Personal friendship with Frederick II

**Story:** al-Kamil and Frederick were intimate friends united by philosophy and mutual admiration.

**Evidence:** embassies, gifts, negotiations, shared diplomatic interests, and apparently courteous communication.

**Against overstatement:** they did not need to meet face to face to negotiate; they represented competing polities and used each other strategically.

**Status:** diplomatic relationship documented; intimate friendship unproved.

## Frederick as a secret Muslim

Frederick’s Arabic interests, conflict with the papacy, conduct at Jerusalem, and favorable relations with al-Kamil generated claims that he was secretly Muslim or irreligious.

There is no adequate evidence that Frederick converted. Consequently, this narrative cannot be used to claim that al-Kamil knowingly transferred Jerusalem to a Muslim ally.

**Status:** hostile or romantic legend about Frederick, secondarily attached to al-Kamil.

## Saladin’s legacy betrayed

**Story:** al-Kamil reversed his uncle’s sacred achievement and erased the glory of 1187.

**Evidence:** he did transfer much of Jerusalem.

**Complication:** Muslim custody of the Haram continued; the city was militarily compromised; the treaty was temporary; and Saladin himself had negotiated territorial compromises.

**Status:** historically influential political-moral narrative, not a neutral description of all treaty provisions.

## The merciful victor of Damietta

Christian accounts celebrate al-Kamil as feeding starving crusaders and protecting defeated enemies.

**Evidence:** the broad pattern is supported by crusading sources and the surrender arrangement.

**Possible embellishment:** precise quantities, speeches, and saint-like characterization may reflect moralized narrative.

**Status:** documented core with literary amplification.

## Successes and Failures

### Significant successes

- Survived the 1218–19 military conspiracy.
- Preserved Egypt during the Fifth Crusade.
- Recovered Damietta and compelled the crusader army’s evacuation in 1221.
- Maintained Egypt’s fiscal and commercial centrality.
- Used diplomacy to prevent Frederick II’s expedition from becoming another invasion of Egypt.
- Extended authority into Āmid and Ḥiṣn Kayfā.
- Recovered Edessa and Harran.
- Captured Damascus in 1237.
- Founded a significant hadith institution.
- Established coin designs influential into the early Mamluk era. [IFAO study](https://www.ifao.egnet.net/anisl/046/02)

### Significant failures or costs

- Failed to prevent the loss of Damietta in 1219.
- Was temporarily driven from his camp by his own emirs.
- Offered sweeping territorial concessions under military pressure.
- Provoked enduring opposition through the Treaty of Jaffa.
- Failed in the 1234 Anatolian campaign.
- Did not create a stable succession.
- Could not convert Ayyubid family supremacy into durable centralized government.
- His eastern expansion exposed the family to wider Seljuk, Khwarazmian, and Mongol conflict.

---

## Illness, Injury, and Death

No securely documented major injury, disability, addiction, or psychological disorder is known.

Al-Kamil became ill in Damascus shortly after taking the city and while preparing assistance for Baghdad. The sources consulted do not identify the illness. Claims that he died from grief, divine punishment for Jerusalem, poisoning, or assassination lack dependable evidence.

He died at approximately fifty-seven or sixty, depending on the accepted birth year.

His immediate successor in Egypt was al-ʿAdil II. Al-Ṣāliḥ Ayyūb retained an eastern base and eventually overthrew his brother in 1240.

## Works by or Attributed to Al-Kamil

No substantial surviving book securely authored by al-Kamil has been identified.

### Poetry

Medieval biographers report that he composed poetry and possessed enough expertise to examine poets.

- **Authorship:** reported genuine authorship.
- **Survival:** scattered lines or quotations may survive in biographical compilations; no verified collected *Dīwān* was located.
- **Date:** compositions would fall before 1238.

### Hadith transmission

Al-Kamil reportedly heard and transmitted a collection of forty hadiths from Ibn al-Ṣafrāwī and possessed scholarly authorizations from several teachers.

- **Nature:** oral or manuscript transmission, not necessarily original authorship.
- **Survival:** no independent edition under his name was identified.

### Diplomatic letters and treaty communications

His chancery produced:

- messages to Frederick II;
- instructions and credentials for Fakhr al-Dīn;
- correspondence with Ayyubid princes;
- messages to crusader leaders during the Fifth Crusade;
- communications with the Abbasid court and eastern rulers.

These are official products issued in his name, probably drafted by secretaries. They should be classified as **chancery documents under his authority**, not necessarily autograph compositions.

### Treaty of Jaffa, 1229

The agreement with Frederick II survives principally through chronicle summaries and documentary traditions rather than a universally accepted complete bilingual original.

- **Authorship:** negotiated and authorized by al-Kamil and Frederick; drafted by officials.
- **Date:** February 1229.
- **Status:** genuine diplomatic act; exact wording reconstructed from differing sources.

### Coins and inscriptions

Coins bearing his name, titles, caliphal allegiance, mint, and date are official textual artifacts published under his authority.

Examples include gold dinars from Cairo and Alexandria and silver and copper issues after the 1225 reform. [Ashmolean coin record](https://hcr.ashmus.ox.ac.uk/coin/hcr13518), [Princeton Kāmilī dirham exhibit](https://dpul.princeton.edu/coins_geniza/feature/6-kamili-dirham-post-1225-ce)

### Building inscriptions and endowment documentation

Inscriptions associated with:

- Imam al-Shāfiʿī’s mausoleum;
- the Kāmiliyya hadith college;
- associated waqf foundations.

These are patronal and administrative texts, not personal literary works.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Immediate dynastic aftermath

Al-ʿAdil II succeeded in Egypt but lacked his father’s authority. Al-Ṣāliḥ Ayyūb challenged him, entered Cairo in 1240, and became sultan.

The resulting Ayyubid conflicts strengthened military slave households. Under al-Ṣāliḥ, the Bahriyya Mamluks became increasingly important. After al-Ṣāliḥ’s death in 1249, the Ayyubid sultanate in Egypt gave way to Mamluk rule. This outcome was not solely caused by al-Kamil’s succession plan, but his territorial division failed to prevent it.

### Muslim historical memory

His reputation divided early:

- biographers praised his intelligence, restraint, scholarship, fiscal care, and patronage;
- chroniclers admired the defeat of the Fifth Crusade;
- Jerusalem’s transfer caused profound condemnation;
- later writers contrasted him with Saladin, frequently to al-Kamil’s disadvantage.

Ibn Khallikān’s tradition praises him as a great ruler of good memory, devoted to scholars and the sunna, prudent and neither wasteful nor miserly. This is formal biographical praise but reflects a strong favorable strand in medieval memory.

### Christian memory

In crusade history, he appears as:

- the defender of Egypt;
- the ruler whose generous peace offers were rejected;
- the negotiator who recovered Damietta;
- Frederick II’s sophisticated diplomatic counterpart;
- the hospitable sultan who received Francis.

Franciscan tradition increasingly transformed him into the tolerant or nearly converted sultan. Modern interfaith movements further elevated him as a symbol of Muslim-Christian encounter.

### Diplomatic legacy

The Treaty of Jaffa became an exceptional example of a crusade whose principal objective was achieved through negotiation. It demonstrated that sovereignty, sacred access, urban control, and fortification rights could be divided rather than treated as indivisible.

### Institutional legacy

The Kāmiliyya contributed to the expansion of specialized hadith education in Egypt. His coinage helped establish visual forms retained by later Ayyubids and early Mamluks.

### Modern political reuse

Al-Kamil is invoked in several incompatible ways:

- as proof that negotiation can succeed where religious war fails;
- as a model of interfaith hospitality;
- as a ruler who subordinated Jerusalem to dynastic interests;
- as an example of elite diplomacy detached from popular religious feeling;
- as a counterpoint to Saladin;
- as a symbol in Catholic-Muslim dialogue.

These are later appropriations. None alone exhausts the medieval record.

## Chronology

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1177 or 19 Aug. 1180 | Born, probably Cairo; 1180 is the more precise traditional date. |
| 1191 | Receives nominal or supervised authority in the Jazira. |
| 1199 | Commands Jaziran forces around Mardin while al-ʿAdil moves against al-Afḍal. |
| 6 July 1200 | Al-ʿAdil enters Cairo; al-Kamil becomes principal deputy and governor in Egypt. |
| 1211–12 | Dome over Imam al-Shāfiʿī’s tomb constructed under al-Kamil’s or his mother’s patronage. |
| May 1218 | Fifth Crusade lands against Damietta; al-Kamil commands Egyptian defenses. |
| 31 Aug. 1218 | Al-ʿAdil dies; al-Kamil succeeds in Egypt. |
| 1218–19 | Ibn al-Mashtūb conspiracy; al-Kamil abandons camp temporarily; al-Muʿaẓẓam restores him. |
| Aug.–Sept. 1219 | Francis of Assisi meets al-Kamil near Damietta. |
| Nov. 1219 | Crusaders capture Damietta. |
| 1219–21 | Al-Kamil repeatedly offers territory, including Jerusalem, for evacuation of Egypt. |
| July–Aug. 1221 | Crusader advance trapped by Nile flood and Ayyubid forces. |
| 27 Aug. 1221 | Crusaders seek terms. |
| Sept. 1221 | Damietta restored to al-Kamil; truce and prisoner exchange. |
| 1222 | Al-Kamil aligns with al-Ashraf against al-Muʿaẓẓam. |
| 1223 | Ayyubid authority under al-Kamil’s son extends through Yemen and Mecca. |
| 1224 | Kāmiliyya hadith college founded in Cairo. |
| Nov. 1225 | Monetary reform begins. |
| 1226–27 | Diplomatic opening to Frederick II through Fakhr al-Dīn. |
| Nov. 1227 | Al-Muʿaẓẓam dies; al-Nāṣir Dāwūd succeeds in Damascus. |
| Sept. 1228 | Frederick II arrives at Acre; negotiations continue. |
| 18 or 24 Feb. 1229 | Treaty of Jaffa transfers Jerusalem and other places under a ten-year truce while reserving Muslim control of the Haram. |
| Mar. 1229 | Frederick performs coronation ceremony in Jerusalem. |
| 1229–30 | Al-Kamil and al-Ashraf redistribute Syrian territories at al-Nāṣir Dāwūd’s expense. |
| 7 Oct. 1232 | Al-Kamil begins siege of Āmid. |
| 18 Oct. 1232 | Āmid surrendered. |
| Nov. 1232 | Ḥiṣn Kayfā taken; local Artuqid branch extinguished. |
| 5 May 1234 | Leaves al-ʿAdil as deputy in Cairo and begins major northern campaign. |
| 1234 | Campaign against Seljuk power fails. |
| Jan. 1236 | Recovers Edessa/Urfa. |
| Feb. 1236 | Recovers Harran. |
| 27 Aug. 1237 | Al-Ashraf dies. |
| 28 Dec. 1237 | Al-Kamil takes Damascus from al-Ṣāliḥ Ismāʿīl. |
| Early 1238 | Sends forces under al-Nāṣir Dāwūd toward Baghdad in response to Mongol danger. |
| 6 or 9 Mar. 1238 | Dies from an unspecified illness in Damascus. |
| Following day | Buried in Damascus Citadel; later transferred to the Kāmiliyya mausoleum. |
| 1238–40 | Al-ʿAdil II rules Egypt. |
| 1240 | Al-Ṣāliḥ Ayyūb displaces al-ʿAdil II and becomes sultan. |
| Later 13th century | Franciscan hagiography elaborates the audience into the trial-by-fire and near-conversion narratives. |
| Modern era | Al-Kamil becomes simultaneously a symbol of interfaith dialogue and of contested territorial diplomacy. |
