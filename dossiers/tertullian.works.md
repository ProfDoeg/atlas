## Works by Tertullian

### Apologetic and public interventions

- **Before 197:** *Ad martyras*. A short consolatory exhortation addressed to imprisoned Christians awaiting trial or execution. Its precise date is uncertain, but the standard CCSL chronology places it before *Ad nationes* and the *Apologeticum*. [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm)
- **197:** *Ad nationes*. A two-book apologetic and polemical address to non-Christians, often understood as a preliminary treatment of material recast in the *Apologeticum*. [Cambridge History of Early Christian Literature](https://www.cristoraul.org/ENGLISH/READINGHALL/CHRISTIANITY/nice-reading/The-Cambridge-History-of-Early-Christian-Literature.pdf)
- **197:** *Apologeticum*. A formally organized forensic apology protesting the unlawful treatment of Christians and attacking Roman religious and social practices; it is a public intervention as much as a descriptive treatise. [Cambridge History of Early Christian Literature](https://www.cristoraul.org/ENGLISH/READINGHALL/CHRISTIANITY/nice-reading/The-Cambridge-History-of-Early-Christian-Literature.pdf)
- **c. 197–198:** *De testimonio animae*. A brief apologetic treatise that develops the appeal to the soul’s supposedly spontaneous testimony about God first made in the *Apologeticum*. [Cambridge History of Early Christian Literature](https://www.cristoraul.org/ENGLISH/READINGHALL/CHRISTIANITY/nice-reading/The-Cambridge-History-of-Early-Christian-Literature.pdf)
- **Probably after 198:** *Adversus Iudaeos*. A Christian anti-Jewish polemic rather than a dialogue. Its unity and completeness are disputed: the received ending has often been judged unfinished, derivative, revised, or interpolated. [Tertullian Project works inventory](https://tertullian.org/works.htm)
- **212:** *Ad Scapulam*. An open admonitory letter to Scapula, proconsul of Africa, occasioned by persecution; its rhetoric simultaneously warns the magistrate and claims concern for him. The date follows its reference to the solar eclipse of 14 August 212. [Cambridge History of Early Christian Literature](https://www.cristoraul.org/ENGLISH/READINGHALL/CHRISTIANITY/nice-reading/The-Cambridge-History-of-Early-Christian-Literature.pdf)

### Anti-heretical and doctrinal polemics

- **After 198, probably before 207:** *De praescriptione haereticorum*. A juridically shaped polemic arguing that heretics lack standing to claim or interpret the Christian scriptures; in this sense it behaves partly as a protocol for refusing debate. The separately transmitted *Adversus omnes haereses* is not its authentic conclusion. [Tertullian Project works inventory](https://tertullian.org/works.htm), [Pseudo-Tertullian survey](https://tertullian.org/works_spurious.htm)
- **After 198, probably before 207:** *Adversus Hermogenem*. A polemical treatise against Hermogenes’ teaching that matter was eternal and not created by God. [Tertullian Project works inventory](https://tertullian.org/works.htm)
- **c. 207–212, in successive states:** *Adversus Marcionem*. A five-book refutation of Marcion’s theology, scriptures, and separation of the creator from the God revealed by Christ. It is not a single stable act of composition: Tertullian says that the surviving form is his third edition, while the chronology and contents of the earlier editions remain disputed. [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm), [Cambridge History of Early Christian Literature](https://www.cristoraul.org/ENGLISH/READINGHALL/CHRISTIANITY/nice-reading/The-Cambridge-History-of-Early-Christian-Literature.pdf)
- **c. 208–212:** *Adversus Valentinianos*. A satirical exposition and refutation of Valentinian cosmology, dependent in part on earlier heresiological accounts. [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm)
- **c. 208–212:** *De carne Christi*. A doctrinal polemic defending the real birth and human flesh of Christ against docetic and related teachings. [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm)
- **c. 208–212:** *De resurrectione carnis* (also transmitted as *De resurrectione mortuorum*). A sustained defense of bodily resurrection, conceived as a companion to *De carne Christi*. The variation in title belongs to its transmission and should not be converted into two works. [Tertullian Project works inventory](https://tertullian.org/works.htm), [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm)
- **c. 208–212:** *De anima*. A large Christian philosophical treatise on the soul—its origin, embodiment, faculties, death, and posthumous condition—and a continuation of arguments begun against Hermogenes. [Tertullian Project works inventory](https://tertullian.org/works.htm)
- **c. 208–212:** *Scorpiace*. An “antidote” in treatise form against teachings that permitted Christians to evade martyrdom or deny the faith under persecution. [Tertullian Project works inventory](https://tertullian.org/works.htm)
- **After c. 213:** *Adversus Praxean*. A polemic against modalist teaching and against Praxeas’s opposition to the New Prophecy; it develops a technical account of Father, Son, and Spirit. [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm)
- **Possibly 218 or later:** *De pudicitia*. A rigorist polemic against an episcopal “edict” offering reconciliation after sexual sins. Identification of the issuing bishop with Callistus of Rome, and consequently the late dating, remains strongly disputed. [Cambridge History of Early Christian Literature](https://www.cristoraul.org/ENGLISH/READINGHALL/CHRISTIANITY/nice-reading/The-Cambridge-History-of-Early-Christian-Literature.pdf)

### Protocols, exhortations, and disciplinary arguments

- **After 198, probably before 207:** *De spectaculis*. A disciplinary treatise instructing Christians to abstain from public entertainments because of their religious origins and moral effects. [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm)
- **After 198, probably before 207:** *De baptismo*. A doctrinal and practical protocol concerning the meaning, administration, and reception of baptism, written partly against a female opponent of the rite. [Tertullian Project works inventory](https://tertullian.org/works.htm)
- **After 198, probably before 207:** *De oratione*. An exposition of the Lord’s Prayer followed by disciplinary instructions about Christian prayer; it is both commentary and protocol. [Tertullian Project works inventory](https://tertullian.org/works.htm)
- **After 198, probably before 207:** *De patientia*. A moral exhortation on patience whose author presents himself, paradoxically, as especially afflicted by impatience. [Tertullian Project works inventory](https://tertullian.org/works.htm)
- **After 198, probably before 207:** *De paenitentia*. A disciplinary treatise describing initial repentance and the post-baptismal penitential act later called *exomologesis*. [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm)
- **After 198, probably before 207:** *De cultu feminarum*. A two-book exhortation against elaborate female dress, cosmetics, and ornament. Its books have distinct transmitted titles and may reflect separate stages of composition, but the critical corpus conventionally treats them together. [Tertullian Project works inventory](https://tertullian.org/works.htm)
- **After 198, probably before 207:** *Ad uxorem*. A two-book testamentary exhortation to Tertullian’s wife, urging her not to remarry after his death—or, failing that, to marry only a Christian. [Tertullian Project works inventory](https://tertullian.org/works.htm)
- **Date uncertain, probably early third century:** *De exhortatione castitatis*. An address to a widower arguing against remarriage; it marks a more rigorist position than *Ad uxorem*. [Tertullian Project works inventory](https://tertullian.org/works.htm)
- **c. 208–212:** *De corona*. A defense of a Christian soldier who refused a ceremonial garland, expanded into an argument about tradition, military service, and idolatrous custom. [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm)
- **c. 208–212:** *De idololatria*. A casuistical guide to avoiding complicity with idolatry in occupations, commerce, education, festivals, and civic life; it functions strongly as a protocol. [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm)
- **After c. 213:** *De fuga in persecutione*. A disciplinary argument that Christians must not flee persecution or purchase immunity from it. [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm)
- **After c. 213:** *De virginibus velandis*. A prescriptive treatise arguing that unmarried Christian women past puberty must be veiled; it presents itself as the Latin restatement of an earlier treatment whose language is not wholly certain. [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm), [lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **After c. 213:** *De monogamia*. A rigorist polemic that treats every second marriage, even after widowhood, as incompatible with Christian monogamy. [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm)
- **After c. 213:** *De ieiunio adversus psychicos*. A polemic defending the New Prophecy’s stricter fasting practices against Christians whom Tertullian labels “psychics.” It is simultaneously a disciplinary manifesto and an attack on rival practice. [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm)

### Literary self-presentation

- **c. 208–211:** *De pallio*. A virtuoso oration defending the author’s exchange of the Roman toga for the Greek philosopher’s cloak. It resists the usual apologetic, doctrinal, and disciplinary categories: the performance of a new intellectual identity is itself much of the made thing. [Cambridge History of Early Christian Literature](https://www.cristoraul.org/ENGLISH/READINGHALL/CHRISTIANITY/nice-reading/The-Cambridge-History-of-Early-Christian-Literature.pdf), [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm)

### Lost works securely or substantially attested

- **Youth; otherwise undated:** *Ad amicum philosophum* / *De virginitate*. A lost treatment of marriage’s burdens and virginity, known chiefly through Jerome; the two titles may designate the same work, but Jerome’s wording also permits more than one treatise. A possible fragment and possible unacknowledged use by Jerome have been proposed. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **After 204:** *De ecstasi*. A lost seven-book defense of Montanist prophecy and ecstatic inspiration, known through Jerome and later controversy. Whether it was composed in Latin, Greek, or in versions in both languages remains uncertain. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **After 204:** *De spe fidelium*. A lost eschatological treatise advancing a millennial expectation and interpreting prophecies of Judaea’s restoration in relation to Christ and the Church; it is attested by Tertullian, Jerome, manuscript evidence, and later references, with a possible fragment surviving. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **After 204:** *De paradiso*. A lost treatise on Paradise and the condition of souls after death, cited by Tertullian himself and listed in the *Codex Agobardinus*; a possible fragment survives. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **c. 208–212:** *De censu animae adversus Hermogenem*. A lost polemic on the soul’s origin, explicitly recalled in *De anima*, opposing Hermogenes’ claim that the soul derived from matter. A possible fragment has been identified. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **c. 208–212:** *De fato*. A lost Christian treatment of fate, necessity, fortune, divine and demonic agency, and free will. *De anima* announces it, while a later citation indicates that the projected work was actually completed. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **Before *De carne Christi*:** *Adversus Apelleiacos* [conventional title]. A lost short polemic against followers of Apelles, mentioned by Tertullian himself; its original title is unknown, so the modern label describes rather than securely names it. Two possible fragments survive. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **Undated:** *De Aaron vestibus*. A lost exegetical treatise on Aaron’s priestly garments, known to Jerome only from an ancient index of Tertullian’s writings. Its existence is therefore bibliographically attested rather than textually recoverable. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **Undated:** *De animae submissione*. Nothing survives beyond its title in the contents list of the damaged ninth-century *Codex Agobardinus*. It resists fuller classification because neither its subject in context nor its relationship to Tertullian’s other writings can now be established. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **Undated:** *De carne et anima*. Another otherwise unknown work listed in the lost portion of the *Codex Agobardinus*. The title is evidence for a made textual object, but not enough to reconstruct its genre, argument, or relation to *De anima* and *De resurrectione carnis*. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **Undated:** *De superstitione saeculi*. A lost work known from the *Codex Agobardinus* contents list. A proposal that *De execrandis gentium diis* preserves excerpts from it is possible but not established. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm), [Pseudo-Tertullian survey](https://tertullian.org/works_spurious.htm)

### Lost Greek versions and parallel manifestations

- **Soon after 197; translator unknown:** *Apologeticum* in Greek. Eusebius knew and quoted a Greek translation of Tertullian’s Latin apology, but the evidence identifies it as a translation and does not establish that Tertullian made it; it is therefore a lost manifestation of his work rather than securely another work by him. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **Before the surviving Latin version; date uncertain:** *De baptismo* in Greek. Tertullian’s Latin treatise refers to a fuller discussion written in Greek. Because that text is lost, it cannot be determined whether it was a first version, a distinct work, or a substantially parallel manifestation. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **Before c. 208–212:** *De spectaculis* in Greek. Tertullian refers to an earlier Greek treatment of spectacles; its relationship to the surviving Latin work is unknown, so it should not simply be counted as a translation or duplicate. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **Before the surviving Latin version; date uncertain:** *De virginibus velandis* in an earlier language-version. The Latin text says that Tertullian will present his case “also” in Latin, normally taken to imply an earlier Greek work, although the exact linguistic and textual relationship remains uncertain. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)

### Uncertain works and resisted attributions

- **Undated:** *De circumcisione*. Jerome’s wording may imply a work by Tertullian, but it may instead refer to a work by Novatian; no secure Tertullian text or unambiguous title survives. It cannot safely be counted as something Tertullian made. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **Undated:** *De mundis atque immundis animalibus*. A work on clean and unclean animals has been inferred from Jerome, but his syntax does not securely assign it to Tertullian. Its existence as a Tertullianic obra is therefore doubtful. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **Undated:** *De Trinitate*. Jerome says that Novatian’s *De Trinitate* epitomized a work of Tertullian, but this may mean *Adversus Praxean* rather than a separate lost treatise with that title. Counting it independently would manufacture a work from an ambiguous reference. [Tertullian Project lost-works inventory](https://www.tertullian.org/works_lost.htm)
- **c. 202–203:** *Passio sanctarum Perpetuae et Felicitatis*. This composite martyr narrative has sometimes been associated with Tertullian as editor or author of its framing material, but the attribution remains uncertain and it is normally edited separately. The embedded prison narratives also resist reduction to a single-author “work,” since they claim Perpetua and Saturus as first-person makers. [Tertullian Project chronology](https://tertullian.org/chronology.htm), [Pseudo-Tertullian survey](https://tertullian.org/works_spurious.htm)
- **Date disputed, perhaps represented by later excerpts:** *De execrandis gentium diis*. The surviving fragment was traditionally regarded as a later, probably sixth-century apologetic text falsely attributed to Tertullian; Marie Turcan and Anne-Marie Verkerk-Turcan proposed that it preserves excerpts from his lost *De superstitione saeculi*. It is consequently neither securely his work nor securely independent of one. [Pseudo-Tertullian survey](https://tertullian.org/works_spurious.htm)
- **Early third century, anonymous:** *Adversus omnes haereses*. A catalogue of heresies transmitted as an appendix to *De praescriptione haereticorum* and retained among Tertullianic spuria in CCSL. Modern scholarship does not regard Tertullian as its author; it may depend on a lost Greek heresiological source associated with Hippolytus. [Pseudo-Tertullian survey](https://tertullian.org/works_spurious.htm)
- **Late antique, exact date disputed:** *Carmen adversus Marcionitas*. A Latin anti-Marcionite poem transmitted under Tertullian’s name but excluded from his authentic corpus. Its verse form and later authorship make it a separate anonymous creation, not a poetic work made by Tertullian. [Pseudo-Tertullian survey](https://tertullian.org/works_spurious.htm)
- **Fourth century or later:** *Genesis*, *De Sodoma*, *De Iona propheta* / *De Nineva*, *Ad senatorem*, and *De iudicio Domini*. These poems entered parts of the Tertullianic printed or manuscript tradition, but modern scholarship assigns them to other late-antique poets or to Pseudo-Cyprianic collections. They are grouped here only to record and reject the historical attribution, not as works Tertullian made. [Pseudo-Tertullian survey](https://tertullian.org/works_spurious.htm)
- **Third century, by Novatian:** *De Trinitate* and *De cibis Iudaicis*. Both circulated in some Tertullianic manuscript or early printed contexts, but they are works of Novatian and should not be included in Tertullian’s oeuvre. [Pseudo-Tertullian survey](https://tertullian.org/works_spurious.htm)
