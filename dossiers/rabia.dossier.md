# Rabia al-Adawiyya: Research Dossier

## Scope and Evidentiary Method

Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya is among the most famous women in Islamic religious history and among the least securely documented. No contemporary biography survives. No manuscript, inscription, artifact, legal record, or text securely written by her has been identified. The earliest extant references were written several decades after her presumed death; the first sustained life narratives appeared roughly four centuries later.

This dossier therefore uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact:** supported by relatively early, independent, or otherwise strong evidence.
- **Reported fact:** stated in a transmitted early or medieval source but not independently verifiable.
- **Probable reconstruction:** an inference accepted by critical scholarship as the best explanation of the evidence.
- **Disputed claim:** competing sources or serious chronological or attributional problems exist.
- **Hagiographic tradition:** a religiously instructive story not independently documented.
- **Legend or myth:** a narrative with demonstrable literary development, anachronism, supernatural content, or very late attestation.
- **Modern fictionalization:** a modern literary, theatrical, cinematic, or ideological construction.
- **Unresolved:** the available evidence does not permit a confident conclusion.

The principal modern critical authority used here is Rkia Elaroui Cornell’s 2013 doctoral dissertation, revised and published as *Rabiʿa From Narrative to Myth* in 2019. Cornell identified previously neglected ninth-century material and systematically compared the development of Rābiʿa’s image in Arabic, Persian, Sufi, literary, and modern popular sources.

## Basic Identifying Information

### Names and titles

Forms found in the literature include:

- رابعة العدوية — **Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya**
- رابعة القيسية — **Rābiʿa al-Qaysiyya**
- رابعة البصرية — **Rābiʿa al-Baṣriyya**, “Rābiʿa of Basra”
- رابعة العتكية — **Rābiʿa al-ʿAtakiyya**, referring to a client relationship with Āl ʿAtīk
- رابعة بنت إسماعيل — **Rābiʿa bint Ismāʿīl**, a later-attested patronymic
- أم الخير — **Umm al-Khayr**, “Mother of Goodness,” a later kunya
- أم عمرو — **Umm ʿAmr**, found in some sources but probably produced by confusion with another Rābiʿa
- رابعة المسمعية — **Rābiʿa al-Musmiʿiyya**, interpreted by Cornell as “the woman who must be heard,” in Ibn Abī Ṭāhir Ṭayfūr
- “Rābiʿa of Basra”
- “Rābiʿa the Mystic”
- “The Bride of Asceticism”
- “The Martyr of Divine Love”
- “The Sufi Lover”
- “Mother/Sovereign of the Lovers”
- “First among Sufis,” a modern title rather than a contemporary designation

“Rābiʿa” literally means “fourth.” The widespread assertion that she received the name because she was her parents’ fourth daughter belongs to her later life story and is not established by early evidence.

### Identity and affiliation

**Documented or strongly supported:** An ascetic woman named Rābiʿa, identified as al-Qaysiyya or al-ʿAdawiyya, lived in or near Basra in eighth-century Iraq and acquired a local reputation for asceticism, eloquence, ethical instruction, and devotion to God.

The nisbas *al-Qaysiyya* and *al-ʿAdawiyya* most probably connect her to the Arab tribal structure of Qays and its ʿAdī branch. Al-Sulamī later calls her a *mawlāt Āl ʿAtīk*, a female client of the family or clan of ʿAtīk. A *mawlā* could be a freed slave, a descendant of freed people, a non-Arab client, or another dependent attached to an Arab lineage. It does not by itself prove that Rābiʿa personally had been enslaved.

### Dates

- **Traditional birth dates:** 95 AH/approximately 714 CE; 99–100 AH/717–719 CE.
- **Traditional death dates:** 135 AH/752–753 CE; 180 AH/796–797 CE; or 185 AH/801 CE.
- **Most commonly accepted modern lifespan:** approximately 714/717–801 CE.
- **Critical finding:** neither birth nor death date is securely established.

The date 135 AH is difficult to reconcile with traditions placing her among people active in the later eighth century, especially Sufyān al-Thawrī, who resided in Basra in 775–778. The later dates 180 or 185 AH fit those associations better, although the associations themselves remain transmitted rather than externally documented.

### Birthplace and principal residence

- **Probable birthplace:** Basra, Iraq.
- **Principal known setting:** Basra and its environs, including ascetic communities on the city’s margins.
- **Unverified journeys:** pilgrimage to Mecca and travel to Jerusalem appear in later traditions.
- **Probable death place:** Basra or its vicinity.
- **Competing burial claims:** Basra/al-Zubayr; Jerusalem; Cairo; Damascus; Afghanistan; and other local shrines.

## Historical Setting

Rābiʿa’s presumed life crossed the late Umayyad and early Abbasid periods. Basra was then a major port, garrison city, commercial center, and arena of intense religious and intellectual activity. Its communities included Qurʾān readers, transmitters of prophetic reports, jurists, grammarians, theologians, renunciants, itinerant preachers, and circles of women ascetics.

Later writers routinely call Rābiʿa a “Sufi.” That is legitimate as a retrospective classification, but **it is not securely documented that she called herself a Sufi**, belonged to an organized Sufi order, or occupied the later institutional office of shaykha. Organized Sufi orders did not yet exist. Early evidence better supports descriptions such as ascetic, renunciant, devotee, moral teacher, or woman of pious wisdom.

Cornell distinguishes several stages in her remembered identity:

1. Rābiʿa the eloquent teacher.
2. Rābiʿa the ascetic.
3. Rābiʿa the lover of God.
4. Rābiʿa the paradigmatic Sufi saint.
5. Rābiʿa the modern feminist, existentialist, literary, musical, and cinematic icon.

## Family and Ancestry

### Parents

**Disputed claim:** Later sources name her father Ismāʿīl. No early source securely establishes this. Cornell traces the patronymic’s consolidation to medieval authors, including Ibn al-Jawzī and Ibn Khallikān, in a textual environment where several women named Rābiʿa were being conflated.

Her mother’s name is unknown.

### Poverty narrative

In Farīd al-Dīn ʿAṭṭār’s *Tadhkirat al-awliyāʾ*, written approximately four centuries after Rābiʿa’s presumed death:

- her parents are extremely poor;
- there is no oil to light a lamp and no cloth in which to wrap the newborn;
- her father refuses to ask other people for help;
- the Prophet Muḥammad appears to him in a dream;
- the Prophet declares that the child will become a noble woman who will intercede for 70,000 members of his community;
- the father is directed to seek money from the governor of Basra.

This is **hagiographic tradition**, not contemporary family evidence. Its structure announces the saint’s election at birth and resembles saintly-birth narratives elsewhere.

### Siblings

The later master narrative says she was the fourth of four daughters. The sisters are separated during a famine after their parents’ deaths. Their names and later lives are not preserved. This is **legendary or unresolved**.

### Ancestry and ethnic status

Some modern biographies describe her as an Arab of the Qays tribe; others call her a non-Arab client or freed slave. The evidence permits no firm conclusion.

Cornell argues that early uses of *al-Qaysiyya* and *al-ʿAdawiyya* may point toward an Arab clan affiliation, while the later designation *mawlāt Āl ʿAtīk* permits several social possibilities. The familiar statement that she was born enslaved or was certainly non-Arab goes beyond the evidence.

### Marriage, partners, and children

**Probable reconstruction:** Rābiʿa remained voluntarily celibate.

The strongest relevant early testimony occurs in Ibn Abī Ṭāhir Ṭayfūr’s ninth-century *Balāghāt al-nisāʾ*. When questioned about marriage, Rābiʿa is represented as answering that she is wholly preoccupied with God and cannot divert herself to a husband.

Later sources attach proposals to:

- ʿAbd al-Wāḥid ibn Zayd;
- Muḥammad ibn Sulaymān al-Hāshimī, an Abbasid governor or notable;
- Sufyān al-Thawrī;
- al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī.

The al-Ḥasan proposal is chronologically implausible. Other proposal narratives are poorly attested and often serve as literary demonstrations of her rejection of wealth, sexuality, or worldly dependence.

Claims that she was married, widowed, or had a son named ʿAmr probably arise from confusion involving her kunya and other women named Rābiʿa—especially:

- Rābiʿa al-Azdiyya of Basra;
- Rābiʿa bint Ismāʿīl of Damascus, wife of Aḥmad ibn Abī al-Ḥawārī;
- other ascetic women sharing the common name.

No securely documented spouse, partner, or child is known.

## Childhood and Early Life

No historically secure account of Rābiʿa’s childhood survives.

### The canonical later narrative

The story best known from ʿAṭṭār presents the following sequence:

1. Rābiʿa is born into extreme poverty in Basra.
2. Her father receives a prophetic dream predicting her sanctity.
3. Both parents die while she is young.
4. Famine strikes Basra.
5. Rābiʿa and her sisters are separated.
6. A criminal abducts or captures her.
7. She is sold for six dirhams into slavery.
8. Her owner imposes hard labor.
9. While fleeing or moving through the city, she falls and breaks or dislocates her hand.
10. She prays that suffering matters only if God remains pleased with her.
11. Her owner sees her praying at night with a supernatural lamp or light suspended above her.
12. Recognizing her sanctity, he frees her.
13. She withdraws to the desert and devotes herself wholly to God.

Every part of this continuous childhood-and-slavery narrative is **late hagiography**. It cannot be treated as a documented biography.

### Was she enslaved?

**Unresolved and often overstated.**

Evidence sometimes offered:

- al-Sulamī calls her a client of Āl ʿAtīk;
- later accounts make her an abducted and sold orphan;
- her theology is expressed through “servitude to God.”

Counterpoints:

- *mawlā* does not necessarily mean that the person personally experienced slavery;
- no early source clearly states that she was captured and sold;
- Cornell suggests that theological language about making oneself God’s servant may have been literalized into a narrative of human enslavement;
- later stories commonly turn social marginality into a dramatic conversion narrative.

The most defensible statement is that she may have belonged to a client family and may possibly have experienced slavery, but the celebrated enslavement story is unverified.

### Singer, musician, dancer, courtesan, or prostitute

Some Arabic modern biographies and Egyptian films depict Rābiʿa as a singer, flute player, entertainer, dancer, courtesan, or participant in a dissolute urban milieu before repentance.

**Evidence assessment:** no early source establishes such a career. Cornell associates this development with later conflations and modern narrative needs. In classical society, an enslaved singing woman (*qayna*) was a familiar literary type. Modern films used a “fallen woman converted into saint” plot that supplied spectacle, music, romance, and moral transformation.

Assertions that the historical Rābiʿa practiced prostitution or lived by sexual entertainment are therefore **modern fictionalization or unsupported allegation**, not documented fact.

## Education and Formation

### Formal education

No school, curriculum, literacy history, or documentary teacher list survives. Claims that she memorized the Qurʾān as a child, mastered hadith, or received systematic instruction are plausible within the later saintly portrait but not independently established.

### Basran women’s ascetic tradition

Cornell places Rābiʿa within a pre-existing tradition of female asceticism around Basra. Important antecedents included:

- Muʿādha al-ʿAdawiyya, died about 702;
- Ghufayra al-ʿĀbida;
- Ḥayyūna of al-Ubulla;
- Maryam of Basra;
- other women catalogued much later by al-Sulamī.

This means Rābiʿa did not create female Muslim asceticism ex nihilo.

### Ḥayyūna of al-Ubulla

The eleventh-century writer al-Ḥasan ibn Muḥammad al-Nīsābūrī identifies Ḥayyūna as Rābiʿa’s teacher. The report is late and not independently corroborated, but it is chronologically possible. Cornell treats Ḥayyūna as a possible formative influence because traditions attributed to her combine criticism of performative piety with an emphasis on love and undistracted devotion.

**Label:** reported teacher; unresolved.

### Muʿādha al-ʿAdawiyya

Al-Sulamī describes Muʿādha as a contemporary associate of Rābiʿa. This is chronologically impossible or highly improbable because Muʿādha died around 702, before the usual dates assigned to Rābiʿa’s birth. The meaningful connection is institutional and genealogical: Muʿādha represented an older women’s ascetic tradition associated with the same broad clan and city.

### Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī

The most famous stories make al-Ḥasan Rābiʿa’s teacher, companion, admirer, pupil, or would-be husband.

**Chronological problem:** al-Ḥasan died in 110 AH/728 CE. If Rābiʿa was born around 714–717, she would have been approximately eleven to fourteen years old. No early evidence records a meeting.

Cornell concludes that the mature theological dialogues between them are myths. They first achieve major literary prominence in ʿAṭṭār’s thirteenth-century Persian hagiography. The pairing elevated Rābiʿa by bringing her into dialogue with Basra’s most famous earlier ascetic, while the stories frequently make her surpass him in spiritual insight.

### Mālik ibn Dīnār

Mālik ibn Dīnār, a Basran ascetic and transmitter associated with al-Ḥasan’s tradition, is a more chronologically plausible indirect influence than al-Ḥasan himself. Cornell suggests that teachings transmitted through Mālik’s milieu may help explain later narrative links between Rābiʿa and al-Ḥasan.

Direct meetings between Mālik and Rābiʿa in ʿAṭṭār are not secure and can be chronologically strained.

### Sufyān al-Thawrī

Al-Sulamī preserves reports in which the jurist and traditionist Sufyān al-Thawrī visits Rābiʿa, seeks her teaching, and calls her *al-muʾaddiba*, the educator or cultivator of character.

Sufyān lived in hiding in Basra during approximately 775–778. A meeting is therefore possible only within that short interval. The relevant traditions pass through transmitters such as Jaʿfar ibn Sulaymān al-Ḍubaʿī and Shaybān ibn Farrūkh.

**Assessment:** plausible reported association, supported by mutually compatible transmission strands but lacking independent contemporary confirmation.

## Chronological Life History

## Approximately 714–719: Birth in or near Basra

The usual dates are reconstructed backward from a traditional death in 185 AH/801 CE at approximately eighty years of age. No contemporary birth record exists.

Later tradition names her father Ismāʿīl, calls her the fourth daughter, and supplies a prophetic dream. These details are hagiographic.

## Childhood and adolescence: unknown

No secure information survives. Poverty, orphanhood, famine, kidnapping, sale into slavery, a broken arm, supernatural illumination, and manumission all belong to the much later complete vita.

Some elements are historically possible: Basra suffered political and economic instability, slavery was common, and famine could separate families. Historical plausibility, however, is not evidence that the particular narrative occurred.

## Early adulthood: entry into ascetic life

Rābiʿa appears to have joined or emerged from Basra’s culture of renunciation and women’s devotional practice. Whether she lived in a cave, desert retreat, hut, or modest urban house is unresolved.

Later traditions emphasize:

- fasting;
- night prayer;
- Qurʾān recitation;
- weeping;
- remembrance of death;
- refusal of unnecessary possessions;
- refusal to solicit help;
- rejection of marriage;
- independence from patrons;
- distrust of displays of piety;
- concentration on God rather than reward.

Early material more often portrays a disciplined ethical ascetic than the extreme miracle-working recluse of later accounts.

## Approximately 750s–770s: growth of a teaching reputation

The earliest recoverable image is not of a passive recluse but of an eloquent counselor. Al-Jāḥiẓ later includes her among women known for speech, wisdom, and religious eloquence. Ibn Abī Ṭāhir Ṭayfūr similarly preserves her as a woman of judgment and memorable utterance.

Traditions place male scholars and ascetics in her presence and show her correcting them. The historical details of individual scenes are uncertain, but their recurrence across relatively early literary traditions supports a reputation for authoritative speech.

## 775–778: possible association with Sufyān al-Thawrī

During Sufyān’s final years in Basra, he could have visited Rābiʿa. Al-Sulamī’s transmitted reports include exchanges in which:

- Sufyān asks how a servant can draw near to God;
- Rābiʿa answers that nearness begins when the servant knows that nothing in this world or the next is loved apart from God;
- she criticizes his request for worldly “safety,” saying that safety from the world consists in leaving what is in it;
- he asks to be taken to “the teacher” because he finds no solace when absent from her.

These reports fit the earliest image of Rābiʿa as an aphoristic ethical teacher. They remain reported traditions rather than contemporaneous transcripts.

## Later life: household, students, and visitors

Later accounts depict her living in extreme simplicity. Her possessions are variously said to include:

- a broken water jar;
- a reed mat;
- a brick used as a pillow;
- a shroud suspended in her house;
- little or no money.

The inventories are hagiographic symbols of renunciation and cannot be audited as property records.

### Maryam of Basra

Al-Sulamī calls Maryam a companion who survived Rābiʿa and spoke about divine love. The chain reportedly runs through Aḥmad ibn Abī al-Ḥawārī, whose wife was another Rābiʿa. Confusion is possible.

**Label:** reported companionship; weakly attested.

### ʿAbda bint Abī Shawwāl

Ibn al-Jawzī identifies ʿAbda as Rābiʿa’s maid or servant late in life and attributes deathbed and posthumous dream accounts to her. Al-Sulamī does not mention her, and Ibn al-Jawzī gives no usable chain for several stories.

**Label:** possible attendant; existence and relationship unresolved.

## Approximately 796–801: death

Later sources portray her as elderly, physically weak, and bent from austerity. No reliable diagnosis or documented terminal illness exists.

Deathbed traditions say she asked visitors to leave so that the messengers of God might enter. Another story has her recite or hear Qurʾān 89:27–30, the address to the “soul at peace.” These are conventional saintly-death narratives.

No evidence establishes addiction, psychiatric crisis, accident, homicide, imprisonment, or execution.

### Competing death dates

- **135 AH/752–753:** stated in some late chronologies; difficult to reconcile with associations in the later eighth century.
- **180 AH/796–797:** adopted by al-Dhahabī and others.
- **185 AH/801:** given by Ibn Khallikān and widely accepted by Margaret Smith, Louis Massignon, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Badawī, and later reference works.

The safest formulation is: **died in the late eighth or opening years of the ninth century, conventionally 185 AH/801 CE.**

## Residences, Travel, and Geographical Movements

### Basra

Basra is the only location consistently supported by early evidence. The city and its nearby ascetic settlements form the historically plausible center of her life.

### Al-Ubulla and Basran hinterland

A link to al-Ubulla, a port near Basra, arises through the reported teacher Ḥayyūna. This is a possible regional connection, not a documented residence.

### Desert retreat

ʿAṭṭār says she went into the desert after manumission. Early ascetics did inhabit retreats around Basra, so the setting is plausible. No exact site or duration can be established.

### Mecca and pilgrimage

Later stories repeatedly place Rābiʿa on pilgrimage:

- she crawls or travels slowly toward Mecca;
- the Kaʿba comes to meet her;
- Ibrāhīm ibn Adham spends fourteen years crossing the desert only to find that the Kaʿba has gone to welcome Rābiʿa;
- she rebukes attention to the “house” when the Lord of the house is the true object.

These narratives are **legendary**. They articulate inward devotion over external display. They are not usable itineraries.

### Jerusalem

No early evidence documents a journey to Jerusalem. Ibn Khallikān’s claim that she was buried on the Mount of Olives probably reflects confusion with Rābiʿa bint Ismāʿīl of Damascus or another local holy woman.

### Other purported residences or graves

Cairo, Damascus, Afghanistan, and Erzurum have hosted tombs or traditions attributed to Rābiʿa. These reflect the portability of saintly memory, not demonstrated travel.

## Employment, Property, Institutions, and Finances

No documentary evidence survives for employment, salary, inheritance, business ownership, land, investments, debts, taxes, or property transactions.

Reported or legendary occupations include:

- enslaved domestic laborer;
- singer or flute player;
- entertainer or courtesan;
- ascetic teacher;
- religious counselor.

Only the last accords consistently with the early reputational evidence.

Later stories emphasize refusal of wealth:

- she rejects marriage to a rich governor;
- she refuses gifts or servants;
- she declines money from admirers;
- she tells rulers that worldly desire produces anxiety;
- she refuses to ask created beings for what belongs to God.

These traditions construct financial independence as spiritual independence. They do not prove that she possessed no property or never accepted support.

No company, patent, technology, invention, investment, or commercial organization is associated with her.

---

## Political Activity

No secure evidence connects Rābiʿa to a political office, faction, rebellion, court appointment, or state prosecution.

Her probable associate Sufyān al-Thawrī was in conflict with Abbasid authority, but this does not establish Rābiʿa’s participation in his politics.

The story of an Abbasid notable or governor proposing marriage to her turns her refusal into an implicit critique of wealth and power. Its documentary basis is weak.

Later authors have interpreted ascetic refusal as social or political protest, but the surviving sayings do not constitute a political program.

## Religious, Philosophical, and Intellectual Development

## Asceticism

Rābiʿa inherited a developed Basran environment of *zuhd*—renunciation—and devotional discipline. Cornell argues that she came to personify “essential asceticism”: renunciation arising from undivided devotion rather than as a transaction for heavenly reward, a performance of extraordinary suffering, or a political protest against wealth.

Reported themes include:

- sincerity;
- exclusive reliance on God;
- constant recollection;
- self-examination;
- distrust of spiritual display;
- indifference to worldly praise;
- celibacy as vocation;
- detachment from both material reward and social recognition.

## Love of God

Rābiʿa became the paradigmatic voice of *maḥabba*, divine love. It is inaccurate to say that no Muslim before her spoke of loving God: Qurʾānic, prophetic, and earlier ascetic traditions already used the language of love. Her importance lies in the later concentration of this theology around her remembered persona.

The earliest extant reference, in al-Muḥāsibī’s *al-Qaṣd wa-l-rujūʿ ilā Allāh*, associates her with exclusive love of God. Abū Ṭālib al-Makkī’s tenth-century *Qūt al-qulūb* then gives her a major role in a developed theology of love, satisfaction with God, and inward knowledge.

## Love without fear of hell or desire for paradise

The best-known doctrine attributed to her holds that God should be worshipped for God alone, not from fear of punishment or hope of reward.

Several forms circulate:

- a prayer asking that if she worships from fear of hell, she be burned in hell, and if for paradise, be excluded from paradise;
- the torch-and-water story, in which she intends to burn paradise and extinguish hell so that God may be loved without ulterior motive;
- sayings in which concern for either this world or the next is treated as a distraction.

These formulations appear in different sources and at different dates. The underlying theme is consistent with early portrayals of exclusive devotion; the most theatrical versions are later legends.

The doctrine did not amount to a documented denial that paradise or hell existed. Claims that she rejected Qurʾānic eschatology are later interpretive extrapolations.

## “Two Loves”

The poem conventionally called the “Poem of the Two Loves” begins, in paraphrase, “I love You with two loves: a passionate love and a love of which You are worthy.”

Abū Ṭālib al-Makkī is the earliest surviving author explicitly to attribute it to Rābiʿa and cites several earlier transmitters. A near-contemporary version in another source is attributed only to “one of the Sufis.”

**Assessment:** ancient attribution and possibly connected to her circle, but authorship remains disputed. It cannot be presented as a surviving autograph poem.

## Was she the founder of Sufism?

No. Ascetic and mystical currents predated her. The term “Sufi” had not yet acquired all its later institutional and doctrinal meanings. She became a foundational emblem retrospectively.

## Was she a philosopher?

Her attributed aphorisms contain ethical and theological thought, but no systematic treatise survives. Modern descriptions of her as a philosopher are retrospective.

## Gender and religious authority

Early literary authors used her as an example of authoritative female eloquence. Later Sufi writers repeatedly presented celebrated men as seeking her instruction. This did not erase the patriarchal environment of medieval authorship, but it preserved a model in which a woman’s perceived spiritual attainment authorized her to correct jurists, preachers, rulers, and male ascetics.

## Important Relationships and Networks

### Sufyān al-Thawrī

- **Nature:** reputed visitor, student, interlocutor, and recipient of ethical correction.
- **Date/place:** possibly Basra, 775–778.
- **Evidence:** al-Sulamī’s transmitted reports through Jaʿfar ibn Sulaymān and Shaybān ibn Farrūkh.
- **Assessment:** plausible reported association, not independently confirmed.

### Jaʿfar ibn Sulaymān al-Ḍubaʿī

- **Nature:** important transmitter of reports about Rābiʿa and Sufyān.
- **Evidence:** chains preserved by al-Sulamī.
- **Assessment:** transmission relationship; whether every reported scene is historical remains unresolved.

### Shuʿba ibn al-Ḥajjāj

- **Nature:** reputed visitor in Sufyān’s company.
- **Assessment:** chronologically possible transmitted association without external corroboration.

### Riyāḥ al-Qaysī

- **Nature:** reputed fellow seeker or visitor; later reports place him with Rābiʿa in ascetic settings.
- **Assessment:** reported association.

### Ṣāliḥ al-Murrī

- **Nature:** preacher criticized by Rābiʿa for talking excessively about the world.
- **Reported saying:** whoever loves something speaks of it often.
- **Assessment:** chronologically plausible report, though the exact exchange is unverified.

### ʿAbd al-Wāḥid ibn Zayd

- **Nature:** ascetic and alleged suitor.
- **Assessment:** the proposal story is hagiographic or disputed; confusion with Rābiʿa al-Azdiyya is possible.

### Ḥayyūna of al-Ubulla

- **Nature:** reported teacher and precursor in love-centered asceticism.
- **Assessment:** late but possible tradition.

### Maryam of Basra

- **Nature:** reported companion and fellow teacher of divine love.
- **Assessment:** weakly attested and vulnerable to transmission confusion.

### ʿAbda bint Abī Shawwāl

- **Nature:** purported household attendant during Rābiʿa’s final years.
- **Assessment:** unresolved; known primarily from Ibn al-Jawzī’s late accounts.

### Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī

- **Nature in legend:** teacher, disciple, friend, interlocutor, or suitor.
- **Historical assessment:** sustained adult association is chronologically untenable.

### Ibrāhīm ibn Adham

- **Nature in legend:** ascetic counterpart on pilgrimage.
- **Historical assessment:** no documented meeting; the Kaʿba story is hagiographic.

### Later “relationships”

Abū Ṭālib al-Makkī, al-Sulamī, al-Qushayrī, al-Ghazālī, Ibn al-Jawzī, ʿAṭṭār, Ibn ʿArabī, Jāmī, and later Sufi writers were not her personal associates. They created, transmitted, systematized, or reinterpreted her posthumous image.

## Controversies, Allegations, and Critical Questions

No lawsuit, investigation, indictment, conviction, financial fraud, corruption case, espionage connection, violent organization, or documented criminal proceeding is associated with Rābiʿa.

The relevant controversies are historiographical and theological.

### Historical existence

**Question:** Was Rābiʿa a wholly literary invention?

**Evidence for a historical individual:**

- al-Muḥāsibī, al-Burjulānī traditions, al-Jāḥiẓ, and Ibn Abī Ṭāhir Ṭayfūr refer to her within roughly a century of her presumed life;
- the references occur in both Sufi and non-Sufi literary contexts;
- Basran al-Jāḥiẓ treats her reputation as already intelligible to his readers.

**Assessment:** a historical Basran ascetic named Rābiʿa probably existed. The familiar biography is nevertheless a composite construction.

### Conflation of several Rābiʿas

This is the central documentary problem. Reports about at least three women were merged:

1. Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya/al-Qaysiyya of Basra.
2. Rābiʿa al-Azdiyya of Basra.
3. Rābiʿa bint Ismāʿīl of Damascus, wife of Aḥmad ibn Abī al-Ḥawārī.

Consequences include uncertainty about:

- her father’s name;
- marriage;
- widowhood;
- household behavior;
- travels;
- burial in Jerusalem;
- poetry;
- sayings transmitted by Aḥmad ibn Abī al-Ḥawārī.

### Claim that she was a prostitute or dissolute entertainer

**Status:** unsupported allegation and modern fictionalization.

The claim became prominent through twentieth-century books and Egyptian films. No early evidence documents prostitution, sexual misconduct, or a career as a dancer. The singing-girl motif may derive from literary conventions or conflation.

### Claim that she was a heretic or denied paradise and hell

Some anti-Sufi criticism treats statements about worship without desire for paradise as rejection of scripture.

**Evidence:** the attributed statements criticize self-interested worship; they do not necessarily deny eschatological realities. Al-Dhahabī, while recording her piety, warned against extremist or “incarnationist” interpreters who might falsely attach their doctrines to her. This is evidence of later attributional conflict, not of a formal heresy proceeding against Rābiʿa.

No contemporary charge, trial, punishment, or excommunication is known.

### Extreme asceticism and bodily harm

Later accounts describe sleeplessness, prolonged fasting, constant weeping, emaciation, and physical weakness. They may preserve practices associated with early ascetics, but hagiography characteristically intensifies them. No clinical evidence exists.

### Authorship inflation

A substantial body of Arabic and translated verse circulates under her name. Cornell’s conclusion is that no written work is conclusively linked to her. Treating all modern “Rābiʿa poems” as authentic eighth-century compositions is methodologically unsound.

### Modern political confusion around the name

Searches for recent “Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya controversy” are dominated by Cairo’s Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya Mosque, the 2013 sit-in and mass killing near it, and the later four-finger R4BIA symbol. These events concern a place named for the saint, not actions by the historical woman. They should not be imported into her personal biography.

### Results of the dedicated adversarial search

Searches in English and Arabic combining her name with controversy, scandal, allegation, lawsuit, fraud, conspiracy, criticism, prostitution, heresy, and accusations, including material from 2024–2026, produced no evidence of:

- criminal conduct attributable to the historical Rābiʿa;
- financial impropriety;
- political violence;
- litigation;
- abuse;
- a state or ecclesiastical prosecution during her lifetime.

The substantive negative or contested material concerns false attribution, conflation, claims of an immoral youth, suspicions about Sufi theology, shrine authenticity, and modern fictional depictions. Recent web material largely repeats old traditions without new primary evidence.

## Myths, Legends, Rumors, and Disputed Narratives

## The prophetic dream at her birth

**Story:** Her father dreams of Muḥammad, who predicts that Rābiʿa will intercede for 70,000 people and directs him to obtain money from Basra’s ruler.

**Appearance and circulation:** prominent in ʿAṭṭār’s *Tadhkirat al-awliyāʾ* and later popular biographies.

**Evidence:** no early corroboration.

**Function and effect:** establishes predestined sanctity, prophetic endorsement, and spiritual power before adulthood.

**Label:** hagiographic legend.

## Abduction and sale for six dirhams

**Story:** Orphaned during famine, she is abducted and sold cheaply.

**Evidence:** absent from the earliest sources; social-client terminology may have encouraged later literalization.

**Effect:** makes liberation from human bondage the prelude to total servitude to God.

**Label:** late hagiographic tradition; possible historical background but unverified event.

## The broken hand and divine assurance

**Story:** Rābiʿa falls while escaping, breaks her hand, and asks only whether God remains pleased with her; a voice reassures her.

**Evidence:** late hagiography.

**Effect:** exemplifies satisfaction with suffering and disregard for bodily pain.

**Label:** legend.

## The lamp suspended without a chain

**Story:** Her owner sees a supernatural lamp over her head while she prays and frees her.

**Evidence:** miracle story in the later vita.

**Effect:** converts private sanctity into visible proof recognized by worldly authority.

**Label:** miracle legend.

## The Kaʿba comes to Rābiʿa

**Story:** Ibrāhīm ibn Adham reaches Mecca after years of pious travel but cannot see the Kaʿba because it has gone to meet Rābiʿa.

**Evidence:** literary hagiography, not itinerary.

**Effect:** places inward realization above spectacular male ascetic effort and establishes her superiority.

**Label:** myth.

## The torch and water

**Story:** Rābiʿa runs through Basra carrying fire and water, declaring that she will burn paradise and extinguish hell so that God will be worshipped for God alone.

**Dating:** the motif is later than the earliest notices and circulates in multiple versions.

**Possible analogues:** comparable stories occur in Christian and other ascetic traditions, though direct borrowing is not established.

**Effect:** became the most portable symbol of disinterested divine love.

**Label:** didactic legend expressing a theology plausibly associated with her reputation.

## Satan and love of God

**Story:** Asked whether she hates Satan, she replies that love of God leaves her no time to hate him.

**Evidence:** later attribution.

**Effect:** presents exclusive attention as transcending even hostility toward evil.

**Label:** attributed aphorism; authenticity unresolved.

## Refusal to pray for relief from illness

**Story:** Visitors urge her to ask God to remove pain; she refuses because the illness is God’s will or gift.

**Evidence:** hagiographic reports.

**Effect:** illustrates *riḍā*, satisfaction with divine decree.

**Label:** reported tradition.

## Marriage proposals

**Story:** Famous ascetics, rulers, or al-Ḥasan seek her hand; she rejects all because her heart belongs to God.

**Evidence:** individual versions are late, and some involve anachronisms or conflated women.

**Effect:** makes celibacy a public assertion of religious vocation and independence.

**Label:** partly grounded in early evidence of celibacy; proposal scenes disputed or legendary.

## Meetings with al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī

**Story:** Rābiʿa repeatedly corrects al-Ḥasan in prayer, love, trust, miracles, and knowledge.

**Evidence against:** he died when she was probably a child. ʿAṭṭār introduces many accounts with “it is related,” signaling inherited story.

**Effect:** made her the spiritual superior of one of Islam’s most famous male ascetics.

**Label:** anachronistic myth.

## A sinful youth followed by conversion

**Story:** After manumission she becomes a singer or courtesan, lives amid revelry, then undergoes a dramatic repentance.

**Appearance:** especially important in modern Arabic biographies, radio drama, and the 1962 and 1963 Egyptian films.

**Evidence:** no early corroboration.

**Effect:** supplied modern drama with music, romance, sexual danger, repentance, and transformation; it has often been mistaken for biography.

**Label:** modern fictionalization.

## Posthumous green garments

**Story:** ʿAbda sees Rābiʿa in a dream wearing green heavenly clothing; her earthly shroud has been raised to heaven.

**Evidence:** first clearly preserved by Ibn al-Jawzī centuries later and without a usable early chain.

**Effect:** confirms divine acceptance and saintly status after death.

**Label:** posthumous dream legend.

## Death, Burial, and Shrines

### Basra/al-Zubayr

Cornell judges the most likely grave tradition to be in the cemetery near the tomb attributed to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī in al-Zubayr, the area associated with old Basra. Local tradition also places other early ascetics there.

**Assessment:** most historically plausible, not archaeologically proven.

### Mount of Olives, Jerusalem

Ibn Khallikān states that her grave was visited east of Jerusalem on the hill called al-Ṭūr. The cave shrine has also been identified with:

- the Jewish prophetess Huldah;
- Saint Pelagia in Christian tradition;
- Rābiʿa in Muslim tradition.

Architectural features are later than Rābiʿa’s lifetime. Yāqūt and later critics distinguished the Basran saint from another Rābiʿa connected with Syria.

**Assessment:** important cult site and shared sacred geography; attribution to Rābiʿa of Basra is probably mistaken.

### Cairo and other tombs

Cairo, Damascus, Afghanistan, and Erzurum traditions demonstrate the wide diffusion of her cult. None provides strong evidence for her bodily burial.

## Works by or Attributed to Rābiʿa

## Secure works

**None known.**

No book, letter, diary, signed poem, dictated treatise, manuscript, sermon transcript, or documentary correspondence can be securely attributed to her.

## Early attributed sayings

Collections preserve aphorisms concerning:

- exclusive love of God;
- sincerity;
- reliance on God;
- renunciation;
- celibacy;
- remembrance;
- spiritual knowledge;
- criticism of worldliness;
- satisfaction with divine decree.

These are transmitted sayings, not writings. Their reliability varies by source and chain.

## “Poem of the Two Loves”

Arabic title commonly supplied: أحبك حبين (*Uḥibbuka ḥubbayn*), “I love You with two loves.”

- **Earliest explicit attribution:** Abū Ṭālib al-Makkī, *Qūt al-qulūb*, late tenth century.
- **Competing attribution:** an early variant names no author and says only that one of the Sufis recited it.
- **Status:** disputed attribution; possibly old and related to her remembered teaching, not securely authored by her.

## “My Beloved is Always with Me”

Often circulated from a poem beginning, in translation, “My cup and my wine are the companion of my heart.”

- **Status:** attribution uncertain; versions and translations vary.

## “O my joy, my desire, my refuge”

A devotional poem frequently published under her name.

- **Status:** posthumously attributed; no autograph or contemporaneous witness.

## Prayer of hell and paradise

Often rendered: if I worship You from fear of hell, burn me in hell; if for paradise, exclude me; if for You alone, do not withhold Your beauty.

- **Status:** famous attributed prayer; exact wording and provenance vary.
- **Distinction:** not a securely authored written composition.

## Modern collections published under her name

Numerous books titled *The Sayings of Rābiʿa*, *Doorkeeper of the Heart*, *Rābiʿa: Selected Poems*, and similar titles are editorial anthologies assembled from medieval sources and modern translations. They must not be described as books written by Rābiʿa.

## Premodern Sources Preserving Her Image

Listed approximately by authorial date:

1. Al-Ḥārith al-Muḥāsibī, *al-Qaṣd wa-l-rujūʿ ilā Allāh* — earliest extant identified reference.
2. Muḥammad ibn al-Ḥusayn al-Burjulānī, *Kitāb al-ruhbān* — lost; citations survive in later works.
3. Al-Jāḥiẓ, *Kitāb al-ḥayawān* and *al-Bayān wa-l-tabyīn* — early non-Sufi references emphasizing eloquence and ascetic reputation.
4. Aḥmad ibn Abī Ṭāhir Ṭayfūr, *Balāghāt al-nisāʾ* — early presentation of Rābiʿa as a woman of judgment and celibate devotion.
5. Abū Naṣr al-Sarrāj, *Kitāb al-Lumaʿ*.
6. Abū Ṭālib al-Makkī, *Qūt al-qulūb* — decisive for “Rābiʿa the Lover.”
7. Al-Kalābādhī, *al-Taʿarruf li-madhhab ahl al-taṣawwuf* and attributed related material.
8. Al-Sulamī, *Dhikr al-niswa al-mutaʿabbidāt al-ṣūfiyyāt* — first surviving Sufi work devoted to women; preserves chains of transmission.
9. Abū Nuʿaym al-Iṣfahānī, *Ḥilyat al-awliyāʾ* — references in surrounding biographies; the status of a purported separate complete notice has been debated.
10. Al-Qushayrī, *al-Risāla al-Qushayriyya* — incorporates her sayings into the stages and vocabulary of Sufism.
11. Al-Ghazālī, *Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn* — uses her as an authoritative exemplar of love and sincerity.
12. Ibn al-Jawzī, *Ṣifat al-ṣafwa* — important extended Arabic life, including material attributed to ʿAbda.
13. Farīd al-Dīn ʿAṭṭār, *Tadhkirat al-awliyāʾ* — the most influential complete Persian vita.
14. Ibn Khallikān, *Wafayāt al-aʿyān* — influential for names, dates, and the Jerusalem grave claim.
15. Al-Dhahabī, *Siyar aʿlām al-nubalāʾ* — records piety and transmitted sayings while warning against doctrinal misuse.
16. Jāmī, *Nafaḥāt al-uns* — integrates Rābiʿa into Persian Sufi sacred history.
17. ʿAbd al-Raʾūf al-Munāwī, *al-Kawākib al-durriyya* — late synthesis that attempts, not always successfully, to distinguish different Rābiʿas.

## Films, Radio, Television, Recordings, and Archival Material

### Radio

- **ʿĀzifat al-nāy** (“The Flute Player”), Egyptian radio drama, 1955. Written by Saniyya Qurraʿa; associated with Samīḥa Ayyūb and songs performed by Umm Kulthūm. Its success encouraged later screen adaptations.
- Saniyya Qurraʿa, *ʿArūs al-zuhd: Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya* (“The Bride of Asceticism”), 1960. A half-historical, half-fictional book containing the outline used in the later film.

### Cinema

- **Shahīdat al-ḥubb al-ilāhī / ʿAdhrāʾ al-Baṣra** (“Martyr of Divine Love” / “Virgin of Basra”), 1962, directed by ʿAbbās Kāmil. Depicts an unhappy or dissolute early life followed by repentance. This is dramatic fiction, not documentary reconstruction.
- **Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya**, 1963, directed by Niyāzī Muṣṭafā, starring Nabīla ʿUbayd, Farīd Shawqī, and ʿImād Ḥamdī. Screenplay associated with Saniyya Qurraʿa. Songs performed by Umm Kulthūm became culturally influential.
- The 1963 film’s sequence of poverty, entertainment, sin, romance, repentance, and sainthood became more influential in popular memory than the medieval source distinctions.

### Music

Umm Kulthūm recorded a set of songs associated with the 1963 film, including devotional texts attributed to or inspired by Rābiʿa. Their modern lyric and musical production must be distinguished from authentic eighth-century composition.

### Television

- **Rābiʿa taʿūd** (“Rābiʿa Returns”), Egyptian television miniseries, 1996, written by Yusrī al-Jundī and directed by Ṣafwat al-Qushayrī. Samīḥa Ayyūb appears as Rābiʿa. The drama concerns another woman, Badr, guided by appearances of the saint from beyond the grave.
- Various Arabic religious and historical programs have retold her life, commonly reproducing the ʿAṭṭār–Badawī–Qurraʿa composite rather than conducting source criticism.

### Archival and digital material

- Digitized Arabic editions of al-Sulamī’s *Dhikr al-niswa*.
- Digitized manuscripts and editions of Abū Nuʿaym’s *Ḥilyat al-awliyāʾ*.
- Public scans of al-Jāḥiẓ’s *al-Bayān wa-l-tabyīn*.
- Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam’s complete repository copy of Cornell’s 2013 dissertation.

## Posthumous Reputation and Legacy

### Medieval Islamic tradition

By the tenth and eleventh centuries, Rābiʿa had become a central illustration of:

- love of God;
- satisfaction with divine will;
- pure intention;
- renunciation without transaction;
- female spiritual authority;
- celibate vocation;
- the superiority of inward realization to outward display.

Al-Makkī was especially important in constructing “Rābiʿa the Lover.” Al-Sulamī made her the opening paradigm of female Sufi servitude and teaching. Al-Qushayrī and al-Ghazālī integrated her sayings into increasingly systematic Sufi doctrine.

### Persian hagiography

ʿAṭṭār’s *Tadhkirat al-awliyāʾ* supplied the narrative architecture that most later readers recognize as her “life.” It placed her among male saints and explicitly defended her inclusion: when a woman becomes a “man” on the path of God, ʿAṭṭār argued, she is no longer to be judged by ordinary gender categories.

His chapter produced enduring scenes:

- orphanhood and slavery;
- miraculous manumission;
- the Kaʿba’s movement;
- superiority to al-Ḥasan;
- rejection of marriage;
- pure love beyond heaven and hell;
- saintly death and posthumous visions.

### Influence on later Sufism

Rābiʿa became an exemplary predecessor for discourses of love in writers such as al-Ghazālī, ʿAṭṭār, Ibn ʿArabī, Jāmī, and later Persian, Arabic, Turkish, South Asian, African, and Southeast Asian devotional traditions.

Influence is often literary rather than a traceable chain of doctrine. Later Sufis cited a remembered Rābiʿa whose voice had already been edited by generations of transmitters.

### Southeast Asian reception

Malay *Hikayat Rabiʿah* traditions adapted her trials and sanctity to local genres. These versions demonstrate that her narrative became mobile across languages and could serve polemical, devotional, and gendered purposes far from Basra.

### Modern Arab interpretations

- Badawī interpreted her through existential struggle and divine love.
- Al-Sakkakini emphasized female autonomy.
- Radio, cinema, music, and television turned her into a mass-cultural figure.
- The sinner-to-saint story, although historically weak, became dominant because it suited dramatic form.

### Modern global reception

She is frequently represented as:

- Islam’s first woman Sufi;
- founder of divine-love mysticism;
- a Muslim equivalent of a Christian nun;
- a feminist rejecting marriage and male authority;
- a universal mystic beyond institutional religion;
- a poet of unconditional love.

Each description captures part of her later symbolic use but can misstate the eighth-century evidence.

### Shrines and cult

The competing tombs illustrate how cult memory can detach from biography. The Jerusalem cave is especially important as a shrine shared or contested among Jewish, Christian, and Muslim identifications. Its historical importance lies in the practice of sacred remembrance even if it is not Rābiʿa’s grave.

### Namesakes and political afterlife

Mosques, schools, streets, institutions, and women have been named after her. Cairo’s Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya Mosque gave its name to the site of the 2013 sit-in and killing. The R4BIA hand symbol subsequently became internationally recognizable. This political symbolism is a namesake afterlife, not evidence about her beliefs or actions.

### Historiographical reinterpretation

Modern scholarship has moved through three broad phases:

1. acceptance and harmonization of hagiographic narratives;
2. comparative study of Rābiʿa as a mystic, often using Christian analogies;
3. source-critical reconstruction of how different “Rābiʿas” and narrative tropes were combined.

Cornell’s major contribution is not to declare the saint unreal but to distinguish a probable historical ascetic from the evolving master narrative.

## Atlas Connections

Only connections supported by the sources are included. Mere contemporaneity or membership in the broad Sufi tradition is excluded.

### Aishah bint Abi Bakr

- **Nature:** posthumous institutional and devotional lineage, not a personal meeting. Cornell traces the Basran women’s ascetic tradition through Muʿādha al-ʿAdawiyya, a companion and transmitter associated with ʿĀʾisha. Rābiʿa later became the best-known representative of that regional tradition.
- **Dates/place:** ʿĀʾisha died in 678; Rābiʿa was probably born decades later. Basra is the connective setting in the reconstructed tradition.
- **Label:** probable intellectual-institutional lineage; no direct contact.

### Muhammad ibn Abdullah

- **Nature:** no historical meeting was possible. In ʿAṭṭār’s birth legend, Muḥammad appears in Rābiʿa’s father’s dream, predicts her sanctity, and directs him to seek aid.
- **Dates/place:** dream placed at her birth in Basra, approximately 714–719; story written centuries later.
- **Label:** myth/hagiographic vision.

### Ibrahim ibn Adham

- **Nature:** paired with Rābiʿa in the pilgrimage legend where the Kaʿba leaves Mecca to welcome her after Ibrāhīm’s prolonged ascetic journey.
- **Dates/place:** legendary pilgrimage route and Mecca.
- **Evidence:** later hagiography; no documented meeting.
- **Label:** myth.

### Abu al-Qasim Abd al-Karim ibn Hawazin al-Qushayri

- **Nature:** posthumous transmitter and systematizer. Al-Qushayrī’s *Risāla* cites sayings attributed to Rābiʿa in discussions of Sufi states and stations.
- **Dates/place:** eleventh-century Nishapur/Islamic scholarly world, roughly two and a half centuries after her death.
- **Label:** documented posthumous textual reception.

### Farid al-Din Attar

- **Nature:** author of the most influential complete Persian hagiographic life of Rābiʿa in *Tadhkirat al-awliyāʾ*.
- **Dates/place:** late twelfth or early thirteenth century, Nishapur/Persian literary world.
- **Label:** documented posthumous literary construction.

### Ibn Arabi

- **Nature:** posthumous Sufi reception. Ibn ʿArabī refers to Rābiʿa among spiritually authoritative women and participates in the later tradition in which she exemplifies divine love and sainthood.
- **Dates/place:** twelfth–thirteenth centuries, western and eastern Islamic worlds.
- **Label:** documented posthumous influence/reception; no personal contact.

### Abu Yazid Tayfur ibn Isa al-Bistami

- **Nature:** both appear as exemplary early mystics in later Sufi compendia and modern histories. No source consulted documents a meeting, correspondence, teacher-student bond, or explicit influence between them.
- **Label:** no supported personal connection; shared later canon only.

### Abu al-Qasim al-Junayd ibn Muhammad al-Baghdadi

- **Nature:** both were incorporated into later Sufi genealogies and manuals. Al-Junayd was born after or near the end of Rābiʿa’s life; no supported direct relationship was found.
- **Label:** no supported personal connection; shared later canon only.

### Mansur al-Hallaj

- **Nature:** later histories place both in the genealogy of Islamic love mysticism, but no documented direct line of influence or contact exists.
- **Label:** posthumous comparative association only.

### Jalal al-Din Rumi and Shams al-Din Muhammad of Tabriz

- **Nature:** Rābiʿa belongs to the inherited Persian Sufi world in which Rūmī and Shams wrote and taught, but the sources consulted did not establish a specific acknowledged personal or textual dependence sufficient for an Atlas connection.
- **Label:** no specific documented connection found.

### Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani, Khwaja Mu'in al-Din Hasan Chishti, Nizam ad-Din Auliya, and Muhammad Baha al-Din Naqshband Bukhari

- **Nature:** later Sufi traditions venerated Rābiʿa, but no specific meeting, correspondence, family relationship, or explicit documentary line involving these individuals was established in the consulted material.
- **Label:** no supported direct connection found.

### Overall Atlas finding

The roster contains numerous later Sufi figures, but Rābiʿa’s sparse eighth-century record supports no securely documented personal crossing with them. The strongest Atlas connections are posthumous: ʿAṭṭār’s construction of her vita, al-Qushayrī’s doctrinal transmission, Ibn ʿArabī’s reception, the legendary pairing with Ibrāhīm ibn Adham, and the indirect Basran women’s ascetic lineage associated with ʿĀʾisha.
