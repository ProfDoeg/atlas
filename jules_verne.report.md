# Jules Verne (1828-1905): research report
*2026-08-09. Prepared for the Atlas of Journeys. Subject slug: `jules_verne`.*

Legend: **[A]** = attested, source named · **[R]** = reconstruction, tradition, or an
attribution that could not be verified against a primary text. Contradictions between
sources are flagged where they occur and are **not** adjudicated.

## Method and the shape of the evidence

This life is unusually well documented and unusually badly transcribed. English Wikipedia's
"Jules Verne" supplies a dense year-by-year chronology, right about most addresses and
decorations, wrong in at least four places checked here about years its own sibling articles
contradict; where it and a specialist source disagreed, both readings are recorded. Four
bodies of work carry the weight: Ian Thompson (*Jules Verne's Scotland*, and the Verniana
paper with Philippe Valetoux on Gibraltar), the Verniana / DePauw translation scholarship, the
Musée Jules Verne de Nantes, and the French Wikipedia articles on the boats, plays, and
houses, consistently better sourced than the English biography.

**A systematic defect:** almost every coordinate in circulation for this life is off by 400 m
to 3.5 km. Every pin was checked against Nominatim or a French Wikipedia infobox.

## I. Nantes, 1828-1846

**[A]** Jules Gabriel Verne is born 8 February 1828 at No. 4 rue Olivier-de-Clisson on the Île
Feydeau, an artificial island in the Loire at Nantes, in his maternal grandmother's house
(Wikipedia, "Jules Verne"; Musée Jules Verne de Nantes). Shipowners and notaries on the
mother's side, a provincial lawyer on the father's.

**[A]** The removals inside the city are documented address by address: No. 2 quai Jean-Bart
in 1829, the year his brother Paul is born; boarding school at 5 place du Bouffay in 1834;
the École Saint-Stanislas and a vacation house at 29 rue des Réformés in Chantenay from 1836;
No. 6 rue Jean-Jacques-Rousseau in 1840; the Petit Séminaire de Saint-Donatien as a lay
student in 1842, the year his sister Marie is born; the Lycée Royal, now the Lycée
Georges-Clemenceau, in 1844 (all Wikipedia, "Jules Verne").

**Correction flagged.** The geography material treats "Petit Séminaire / Lycée Royal de
Nantes" as one site at 47.221,-1.556. They are two institutions, and the Lycée
Georges-Clemenceau (rue Malherbe) is at 47.2190,-1.5456, 800 m off. The journey pins the
Lycée only, and says so.

**[R]** The Coralie story: that at eleven the boy signed on as cabin boy aboard a three-master
bound for the Indies, meaning to bring his cousin Caroline a coral necklace, and that his
father caught him at Paimboeuf before the ship cleared the estuary. It comes from **Marguerite
Allotte de la Fuÿe**, Verne's *grand-niece*, in her 1928 biography, and modern scholarship
treats it as exaggerated or invented. One research lens attributed it to "his niece"; the
error is recorded rather than silently fixed, since the same lens dates the story to 1839.

**[A]** Baccalauréat taken at Rennes, 29 July 1846, graded *Assez Bien* (Wikipedia, "Jules
Verne").

## II. Paris, 1847-1857

**[A]** Sent to Paris to read law at his father's insistence, 1847; returns July 1848
(Wikipedia; Britannica summary).

**Correction flagged.** The chronology lens has him "witnessing the upheaval of the French
Revolution of 1848." He did not: the February overthrow of Louis-Philippe and the June Days
(23-26 June) were finished before he arrived in July. He saw the aftermath and the December
election of Louis-Napoléon, and the journey says so.

**[A]** Meets Alexandre Dumas *père* and befriends Dumas *fils*, 1849. *Les Pailles rompues*
staged at the Théâtre Historique, Boulevard du Temple, 12 June 1850. Licence en droit, January
1851. First stories in the *Musée des familles* under Pitre-Chevalier: "Les Premiers navires
de la marine mexicaine" (July 1851), "Un voyage en ballon" (August 1851), "Martin Paz" and
"Les Châteaux en Californie" (1852). Secretary at the Théâtre Lyrique and founder of the
bachelors' club Onze-sans-femme, 1852. (All Wikipedia, "Jules Verne".)

**Correction flagged.** "Maître Zacharius" ran in the *Musée des familles* April-May 1854, but
"Un hivernage dans les glaces" appeared March-April **1855** (fr.wikipedia; *Musée des
familles*, 2e série, livraisons 21-22, 1855).

**[A]** May 1856: best man at the Amiens wedding of Auguste Lelarge, where he meets the widow
Honorine Anne Hébée Morel (née de Viane). They marry 10 January 1857 in Paris; about the same
time he takes a stockbroker's position at the Bourse with the associate Fernand Eggly, arranged
through his wife's family. **Gap:** no source reached names the church or town hall of the
ceremony. Date and city are attested; the room is not.

## III. Britain and Scotland, 1859

**Contradiction, resolved in favour of the specialist source but recorded in full.** English
Wikipedia's "Jules Verne" dates the voyage with the composer Aristide Hignard to **July
1858**. Its own article on *Backwards to Britain* dates it to late July-September **1859**,
and Ian Thompson's *Jules Verne's Scotland* gives dates: Bordeaux 2 August 1859, the SS
*Hamburg* landing at Liverpool 25 August, Edinburgh by train late on 26 August. The journey
uses 1859 and names the disagreement in `date_confidence`. The 1858 reading is what most
English summaries repeat and is not defensible against Thompson.

**[A]** Bordeaux, free passage arranged by Hignard's shipowning brother; Liverpool; Edinburgh;
Glasgow, Loch Lomond, the Trossachs; London on the way home.

**Tag corrected.** The London leg was carried as [R], known only from an itinerary summary. It
is [A]: London closes Verne's own *Voyage à reculons en Angleterre et en Écosse* (London
Bridge, St Paul's, Westminster Abbey, a Shakespeare production at the Princess's Theatre), and
Thompson treats it as part of the real 1859 route.

## IV. Scandinavia, 1861

**[A]** The itinerary, from the diary reconstruction at norwegianjulesverne.wordpress.com:
Paris 2 July, Hamburg and Lübeck, Ystad 5 July, Stockholm 7-14 July, Gothenburg 17 July, the
paddle steamer *Viken* across the Skagerrak, Stavern 18 July, Christiania 19 July, Telemark
23-30 July with the Rjukan waterfall and the climb of Gaustatoppen.

**A flagged contradiction that dissolves on inspection.** Both research lenses reported a
conflict between "Stockholm first" and "Gothenburg-Stavern first" accounts and left it open.
There is none: Stockholm (7-14 July) came *before* the Gothenburg crossing (17 July). The
Stockholm entry was also carried as [R]; it is [A].

**[A]** Verne leaves Hignard in Denmark and races home, missing the birth of his son Michel in
Paris on 3 August 1861.

## V. Hetzel and the Voyages Extraordinaires, 1862-1872

**[A]** Introduced through Alfred de Bréhat to Pierre-Jules Hetzel, 1862. *Cinq semaines en
ballon* published 31 January 1863. *Voyage au centre de la Terre*, 1864. *De la Terre à la
Lune*, 1865. The quarrel with Hetzel over Captain Nemo's past, 1869, settled by leaving Nemo
deliberately unexplained.

**Correction flagged.** The 1862-63 contract is often given a clause tying Verne to the
*Magasin d'Éducation et de Récréation*. Anachronistic: the *Magasin* was not founded until
March 1864. The first agreement was three volumes a year at a flat fee; the *Magasin* tie
dates from 1864, the twenty-year contract from 1871.

**Correction flagged, two events welded to one date.** "9 April 1870: *Vingt mille lieues sous
les mers* published; Verne made a Knight of the Legion of Honour." Only the decoration belongs
to that date. The novel was serialised 20 March 1869 to 20 June 1870, book edition 1870,
illustrated Hetzel edition November 1871.

**Correction flagged.** *Le Tour du monde en quatre-vingts jours* ran in *Le Temps* in 1872 and
the d'Ennery stage version of 1874 was the financial triumph. But the *Michel Strogoff* entry
conflates novel and play: 1876 is the **novel**; the play premiered at the Théâtre du Châtelet
**17 November 1880**, 386 performances to 13 November 1881 (fr.wikipedia, "Michel Strogoff
(pièce de théâtre)").

**Correction flagged.** The *Saint-Michel I* was not bought in 1867: the hull, a bourcet-malet
fishing boat of about 9 m at Le Crotoy, was completed 29 May 1868 and the deed signed 3 June
1868 (fr.wikipedia, "Saint-Michel I"). Nor does Le Crotoy begin in 1856, as the geography lens
has it: a rented villa in **August 1865**, rue Lefèvre from 1866, the villa "La Solitude" from
spring 1868, departure for Amiens February 1871, summers only until about 1875.

## VI. The Great Eastern, 1867

**[A]** With his brother Paul, Verne sails from Liverpool aboard Brunel's *Great Eastern* for
New York, arriving 9 April 1867 after a fourteen-day crossing, and goes by rail to Niagara.
The crossing supplies *Une Ville flottante*. (Maison de Jules Verne archive; julesverne.ca,
Andrew Nash; Adrien Marx on the landing.) **Contradiction, unresolved:** sources give the
sailing date as both **16** and **26 March 1867**, and nothing reached settles it. The journey
uses 1867-03-16 and says in `date_confidence` that 26 March is equally attested.

## VII. The cruises of the Saint-Michel III, 1878-1884

**[A]** June 1878: weather forces the yacht into the Ría de Vigo instead of Lisbon; the party,
including Paul Verne and the publisher's son, waits four days (Puerto de Vigo). Then Lisbon,
Cádiz, Tangier, Gibraltar. The *Gibraltar Chronicle* logs the steam yacht "Saint Michel, J.
Verne owner aboard" arriving four hours from Tangier and clearing for Málaga, Tetuán, Oran and
Algiers (Thompson and Valetoux, *Verniana* vol. 1). Algiers is the far point; a second
Gibraltar call is documented for 1884. The Cádiz call is **[R]**: a Spanish popular account
(VigoE) carries it, the *Chronicle* log does not.

**[A]** 1881: the Baltic cruise published as *De Rotterdam à Copenhague*, written up by Paul
Verne for *L'Union bretonne*. Rotterdam 5 June, Wilhelmshaven, the Eider Canal, Kiel 17-18
June, Copenhagen in the second half of June, departure 27 June, the cruise running 3 June to
17 July 1881 (kiel-maritim.de).

**Correction flagged.** The geography lens dates Copenhagen to "4 June 1881 (approx)." That is
impossible: the yacht only reached Rotterdam, the voyage's named starting point, on 5 June.
The Saint-Malo return of 3 July is likewise [R] and sits awkwardly against the 17 July end
date; it is carried as an unresolved edge in the journey's Kiel and Copenhagen entries.

## VIII. Amiens, the shooting, and the last years

**[A]** Verne settles in Amiens in 1871 and lives there until his death: 2 rue Charles-Dubois
from 1882 to 1900 (fr.wikipedia, "Maison de Jules Verne"), then the smaller house at 44
boulevard Longueville, now boulevard Jules-Verne. Elected to the municipal council in 1888, a
seat held some fifteen years; Officer of the Legion of Honour 19 July 1892; dies 24 March 1905,
aged 77, of complications of chronic diabetes with a stroke; buried at the Cimetière de la
Madeleine (Wikipedia; Encyclopedia.com).

**[A]** 9 March 1886: coming home in the evening, Verne is shot twice by his 26-year-old
nephew Gaston. The first shot misses; the second lodges in his left leg and leaves him
permanently lame. The press keeps it quiet; Gaston is committed to an asylum and never
released (Wikipedia; Biography.com).

**Correction flagged.** Hetzel died 17 March 1886, eight days after the shooting. Verne's
mother Sophie died in **1887**, not 1886. The observation that the late novels darken after
this pair of deaths stands; the year of the mother's death does not.

## IX. Afterlife

**[A]** The grave first carries a plain cement slab reading "Jules Verne 1905." In 1907 the
family commissions **Albert Roze**, who had known Verne since the 1890s, to carve *Vers
l'immortalité et l'éternelle jeunesse*: a shrouded man breaking through his own tombstone and
reaching skyward, the face taken from the death mask (Wikipedia, "Jules Verne's tomb").

**[A] with a correction.** Georges Bareau's monument is inaugurated in the Jardin des Plantes
at Nantes on 29 May 1910, but the **bronze bust was requisitioned and melted down in 1942**
under Vichy and replaced in 1945 by a stone bust by Jean Mazuet: what stands today is not the
1910 bronze. The circulating description "the figure sits atop a globe with scenes from his
novels carved into the base" is supported by no source reached and is dropped.

**[A]** The Musée Jules Verne opens at 3 rue de l'Hermitage, Butte Sainte-Anne, Nantes, on 8
April 1978, the 150th anniversary of the birth, on the initiative of Jean Bruneau and Luce
Courville. The Maison de Jules Verne at 2 rue Charles-Dubois, Amiens, preserves the 1882-1900
residence.

**[A]** The English translations. From Lewis Mercier's 1873 *Twenty Thousand Leagues*, the
standard nineteenth-century English versions cut a fifth to two-fifths of the French, blunt or
delete scientific matter, and remove passages critical of Britain and the United States. These
are the editions sold for decades in both countries, and they are credited with fixing Verne's
Anglophone reputation as a writer of boys' adventures (Verniana / DePauw). Walter James Miller
led the counter-argument from the 1960s, naming the problem "two Jules Vernes." Michel Verne
rewrote several posthumous novels under his father's name; manuscripts surfacing from the
1970s support restored editions, among them the Nebraska *Meteor Hunt* (najvs.org; File 770).

**Correction flagged.** *L'Invasion de la mer* was **not** posthumous: it ran in the *Magasin*
during 1905 and is the last novel published in his lifetime. Only *Le Phare du bout du monde*
belongs to the Michel-overseen posthumous sequence.

**Contradiction, unresolved.** *Paris au XXe siècle*, refused by Hetzel around 1863, appeared
in French in 1994 and English in 1996. Some accounts describe a 1989 rediscovery by the
great-grandson Jean Verne opening a family safe; at least one holds that the location had been
known for years and the discovery was promotional. Both recorded; neither preferred.

**[A]** Neil Armstrong opened the final Apollo 11 telecast, 23 July 1969, with "A hundred years
ago, Jules Verne wrote a book about a voyage to the moon," adding "His spaceship, Columbia,
took off from Florida and landed in the Pacific Ocean." In the novel the **Columbiad is the
cannon**, not the vehicle, so the repeated parallel "a projectile named Columbiad" is wrong;
the three-man crew, Florida launch, and ocean splashdown hold. Verne's fictional launch site
is "Tampa Town."

## Quotations: what survived checking

- **[A]** "Science, my boy, is composed of errors..." *Journey to the Centre of the Earth*,
  trans. William Butcher (Oxford World's Classics), p. 146.
- **[A]** "I have done with society entirely..." *Twenty Thousand Leagues*, ch. X, verified by
  direct fetch of Project Gutenberg #164. Nemo speaking, not Verne.
- **[A]** "We may brave human laws, but we cannot resist natural ones," Part II ch. XV.
  **Replaces** the circulating "One can defy human laws, but cannot resist natural laws,"
  which todayinsci.com labels as its own webmaster's rendering and which returns zero hits in
  Open Library full-text search.
- **[A]** "The sole preoccupation of this learned society..." *From the Earth to the Moon*,
  trans. Walter James Miller (1978).
- **[A]** "I have always made a point in my romances of basing my so-called inventions upon a
  groundwork of actual fact..." Gordon Jones, "Jules Verne at Home," *Temple Bar*, June 1904,
  vol. 129, p. 670. Verne in his own house, a year before his death.
- **[A] with a translation flag.** "The sea doesn't belong to tyrants..." is one of several
  renderings of the Nemo speech at Part 1 ch. 10; Mercier Lewis has "The sea does not belong to
  despots." Flagged, not harmonised.
- **[A] with a translation flag.** The aluminium passage ("as incorrodible as gold...") belongs
  to the **Edward Roth** translation, not the standard text (Project Gutenberg #83). Not used.
- **[R]** "The great regret of my life is that I have never taken any place in French
  literature" (Sherard, *McClure's*, January 1894) and "I make use of physics. He invents"
  (Sherard, *T.P.'s Weekly*, 9 October 1903). **Neither could be verified**: jv.gilead.org.il
  fails TLS certificate validation and web.archive.org fetches are blocked here.
- **Excluded as debunked:** "Quoi que j'invente, quoi que je fasse, je serai toujours
  au-dessous de la vérité," from Félix Duquesnel's necrology, later presented by Allotte de la
  Fuÿe as a letter to Charles Lemire. Noted so it is not taken for verified elsewhere.

## Honest gaps

1. The church or mairie of the 10 January 1857 marriage is unknown to every source reached.
2. The exact March 1867 sailing date of the *Great Eastern* from Liverpool (16 vs 26).
3. Whether the Cádiz call of June 1878 happened at all; it rests on one popular account.
4. The Saint-Malo return date of 3 July 1881 against a cruise that runs to 17 July.
5. Both Sherard interviews, the two most quoted Verne self-assessments in English, are
   unverified against their primary texts for the access reasons named above.
6. Verne never went south of New York, never saw Argentina, inland Africa, India, or the
   Pacific: the geography of the *Voyages extraordinaires* is read, not walked. A fact about
   the life rather than a gap in the research.
