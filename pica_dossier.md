# Pica — a belief-and-myth dossier
**Madonna Pica, mother of Francis of Assisi, wife of Pietro di Bernardone; in later tradition "Pica de Bourlemont"**
*2026-07-12. Register: belief and myth mapping, not fact adjudication. We take on faith the connection between Pica and her contemporary Bourlémont family of the Vosges; the ledger below pins each belief to its earliest attestation and carriers.*

Method note: quotes from Celano verified against the full text of the Ferrers Howell translation (1908, archive.org: `livesofsfranciso00thom`); the Legend of the Three Companions against the 1899 Latin critical edition (Civezza–Domenichelli, archive.org: `laleggendadisan00marcgoog`) and Salter's 1902 English (`legendsaintfran00saltgoog`); Bartolomeo da Pisa against the Latin of Analecta Franciscana IV (`analectafrancisc04coll`); Sabatier against the full English text (Gutenberg 18787); FA:ED (Armstrong/Hellmann/Short, *Francis of Assisi: Early Documents*) pages via Wayback captures of franciscantradition.org. Everything not verified against a text is graded as such.

## LAYER 1 — THE EARLIEST SOURCES

**Celano, Vita Prima (1C, 1228): the mother is unnamed, and gets exactly one act.** 1C 1 is hostile to both parents: "From the earliest years of his life his parents reared him to arrogance in accordance with the vanity of the age" (*a parentibus secundum saeculi vanitatem nutritus est insolenter*, FA:ED I, 182). Her one act is 1C 13, under the chapter title "How his mother released him, and how he stripped himself before the Bishop of Assisi": "his mother, who had been left alone at home with him, disapproved of what her husband had done... she yearned over him with maternal compassion, and she loosed his chains and let him go free... Meantime his father came back, and, not finding his son, heaped sin on sin and turned round to upbraid his wife" (Howell pp. 14–15; FA:ED I, 190–93; the FA:ED marginal cue is 1 Kings 3:26, the true mother before Solomon). No name, no Elizabeth, no prophecy, no naming-of-John anywhere in 1C.

**Celano, Vita Secunda / Remembrance (2C, 1245–47): the typological mother arrives — still unnamed.** 2C 3, chapter heading "How he was first called John and afterwards Francis. How his mother prophesied of him...": "was called by his mother John when, being born again by water and the Holy Ghost... The conduct of this woman (*Quae mulier*)... was in some sort privileged to resemble the holy Elizabeth, both as regards the giving of a name to her son, and as regards the prophetic spirit. For while her neighbours were wondering at Francis' noble bearing... she would say (as though prompted by a Divine oracle): 'How think you that this son of mine will turn out? Be assured that he will become a son of God by his merits'" (Howell pp. 145–46; FA:ED II, 241–42). Note two things believers later smooth over: 2C credits the name *Francis* to "Divine Providence," not to Pietro; and 2C has **no** mother-frees-him scene — its chains (2C 4–5) are the Perugia war prison. The freeing lives only in 1C 13 and L3C 18. The FA:ED editors' note at 2C 3: she is "presented in the classical role of a saint's mother, e.g. the mothers of Saints Bernard and Dominic."

**Legend of the Three Companions (L3C, c. 1241–46): the naming legend — mother still unnamed in the critical text.** L3C 2 (Latin verified in the 1899 edition): "*Franciscus, de civitate Assisii oriundus... Ioannes prius vocatus est a matre; a patre vero tunc redeunte a Francia, in cuius absentia natus erat, Franciscus est postmodum nominatus*" — "first named John by his mother; then, when his father returned from France, in whose absence he had been born, he was afterward named Francis." The prophecy is here placed in the mother's own mouth: "*Quid de filio meo putatis? Adhuc erit filius Dei per gratiam.*" Also L3C 9 ("his mother loved him more than the other children") and L3C 18 (the freeing, reworked from 1C). No "Pica" in the critical Latin — verified by grep of the whole 1899 text and against FA:ED II, 68.

**Where the NAME "Pica" first appears — two independent doors:**

1. *The archival door (earliest dates, if Fortini's documents are accepted).* Assisi notarial acts identify Francis's brother by **matronymic**: "Angelus Pice," and his son "Johannectus olim Angeli domine Pice." Carrier chain: Antonio Cristofani, *Storie d'Assisi* I, 78ff (1866–75) → Sabatier, *Vie de S. François* (1893/94), note: "appellations which might be cited in favor of the noble origin of Pica" (verified verbatim in the English edition) → Fortini, *Nova Vita di San Francesco* (1959; family chart II, 100) → Augustine Thompson OP, *Francis of Assisi: A New Biography* (Cornell, 2012), ch. 1: "She was dead by 1211, at which date her son Angelo 'remade' her by naming his own son 'Picone,' a male version of her name... Angelo himself never appeared in any record as Angelo di Pietro, but always as Angelo di Pica... Records suggest that the successful family business was itself founded on Pica's dowry." On this reading the name is a real 12th-century Assisi woman's name, in legal records decades before any legend uses it.
2. *The hagiographic door.* Earliest occurrence: **Liber exemplorum Fratrum Minorum §116 (c. 1280–1310)** — an Assisi neighborhood memory (Brother Nicholas of Assisi, whose family house adjoined the Bernardone house): "*Tunc domina Pica mater: Afferte, inquit, puerum ut videat*" — "Then Lady Pica, the mother of Saint Francis, said: 'Take the baby out so he can see it'" (ed. Oliger, *Antonianum* 2 [1927] 262–63; FA:ED III, 800). The FA:ED footnote there, verbatim: "**It should be noted that this is the only early source that mentions the name of Francis's mother, Lady Pica**," adding that a version of the episode "is contained in some older editions of the L3C 2, which were based on Vatican Latin codex 7739, where it is certainly an interpolation." That interpolated L3C recension — "a very honest mother, Pica by name, who was another Elizabeth" + the pilgrim — was printed in 1831 (Goretti Publications says "Codex Bibliothecae Vaticanae n. 7359, printed at Pisa in 1831"; Sabatier cites the same printing as "3 Soc., Edition of Pesaro, 1831"; FA:ED says Vat. lat. 7739 — the codex number is reported inconsistently across carriers; noted as-is). Salter's actual 1902 Temple Classics translation has no Pica ("was at first named John by his mother" — verified); the Pica lines on the popular gorettipub.org text are the editor's own insertions from the 1831 Latin.
3. *Negatives (verified).* Bernard of Besse, *Book of the Praises* (1277–83): "a very upright mother. Like another Elizabeth..." — **unnamed** (FA:ED III, 33). Chronicle of the 24 Generals (c. 1370s): no "Pica" in Muscat's full English translation, and zero hits in Gallica's full-text search of Analecta Franciscana III (OCR caveat). Then the name is normalized in **Arnald of Sarrant, Kinship of Saint Francis 3b (1365)**: "Francis... had a brother called Angelo and a mother named Pica" (FA:ED III, 680), and **Bartolomeo da Pisa, De Conformitate (1385–99)**: "*mater ipsius beati Francisci, domina Pica vocata*" (verified verbatim, Analecta Franciscana IV).

**What "Pica" means.** Latin/Italian *pica* = magpie (also an Italian regional name for the bird alongside *gazza*). Interpretive carriers: Giuseppe Abate (mid-20th c., via Raphael Brown, *The Roots of St. Francis*, 1982, App. C): a nickname, "possibly because of her hair and skin colour," or a marker that "her family came from Picardy" — with Brown's verdict, "No satisfactory evidence exists in support of these theories." Thompson treats it as her operative legal name regardless of origin. The circulating "her real name was Giovanna/Johanna" (Porziuncola Project; Basilica magazine "Madonna Giovanna Pica"; Geni) has **no pre-20th-century or scholarly source found** — it reads as a back-formation from the son's baptismal name. Honest gap.

## LAYER 2 — THE LEGENDS ATTACHED TO HER

**The pilgrim at the door** (Simeon typology; distinct from and older than the stable). First: Liber exemplorum §116 (c. 1280–1310), above — pilgrim insists on seeing the newborn; Pica orders him shown; "Two babies were born the same day on this street... one will be one of the best men in the world, the other one of the worst." Chain: → Vat. lat. 7739 interpolation into L3C 2 → Arnald of Sarrant, *Kinship* 7b (1365), Simeon made explicit ("As we read that Christ was carried by Simeon in his arms...") → **Bartolomeo da Pisa** (1385–99) upgrades pilgrim to angel: "*beatus Franciscus declaratus fuit per angelum in specie peregrini suae matri*... he embraced and blessed him and, it is said, pressed the sign of the cross on his right shoulder... and the pilgrim immediately vanished" (AF IV, verified) → the bad twin gets a name, "Azolino de Navata," credited to Francesco di Bartolo (†before 1334, lost) via Mariano da Firenze (16th c.), per Fortini (secondhand grade). Spin-off: the angel-pilgrim reappears as godfather at the San Rufino font (Chalippe 1727: "A stranger presented himself as his godfather"), with the knee-print stone still shown under a grate in the cathedral (Italian church pages date the tale 15th c.).

**The stable birth** (the "stalletta," now the Oratorio di San Francesco Piccolino by the Chiesa Nuova): Pica in hard labor is told by a pilgrim she will deliver only in a stable, on straw, like Mary. Fortini (*Nova Vita* II, 21–23, verbatim Italian via Noel Muscat, "The Birth of Saint Francis of Assisi," franciscanstudies.com): the legend "cannot be earlier than the second half of the trecento"; its first literary witness is the **anonymous Brussels codex** (14th c. per Van de Gheyn/Fierens; 15th per Van Ortroy). The **stone may be older than the parchment**: the Gothic doorway inscription "*Hoc oratorium fuit bovis et asini stabulum / in quo natus est sanctus Franciscus mundi speculum*" is dated **1316–1354 by Giuseppe Abate**. The oratory was made a chapel in the late 13th c. by **Piccardo, Francis's nephew (Angelo's son)**. Striking negatives: the story is absent from Arnald's *Kinship* and from De Conformitate — "it seems that Bartolomeo did not know about it, or that it was not yet in circulation" (Muscat). Later carriers: Wadding's *Annales* (per Chalippe) → **Candide Chalippe OFM, 1727** (full pilgrim-and-stable narrative, Gutenberg 6367) → Catholic Encyclopedia 1913 verdict ("dates from the fifteenth century only... desire... to make his life resemble that of Christ") → modern Italian devotional press as plain fact (Famiglia Cristiana). Fortini rejected both Chiesa Nuova and the stalletta as the family house, placing it by San Nicolò/San Paolo off the Piazza del Comune from Assisi archival research (Moak trans., 88–91).

**The naming — Giovanni to Francesco.** Earliest: L3C 2 (father renames on return from France; verified Latin above). Rival early reading: 2C 3 (name given by Divine Providence; John from the mother). Bonaventure, *Minor Legend* I,1 harmonizes: "he held on to the name his father gave him, but did not abandon the meaning of the name given by his mother." The "Francesco = the Frenchman *because of the mother*" reading is a distinct, late layer: carriers include Antonio Tarallo in the Basilica's own magazine ("in onore del popolo gallico **delle origini materne**," sanfrancescopatronoditalia.it, "Donna Pica, la madre di Francesco") and en/fr Wikipedia; the father-side reading is carried by the Catholic Encyclopedia (Paschal Robinson, 1909: "through fondness it would seem for France, whither business had led him at the time of his son's birth") and Felice Autieri (same Basilica magazine). Chesterton (1923, ch. 2) carries the mother-named-John version without ever naming her.

**The prophecy.** L3C 2 (in her mouth) and 2C 3 ("You will see that he shall merit to become a son of God") — both verified above; De Conformitate carries the variant reading "*Meritorum gratia dei filium ipsum noveritis affuturum*" (Sabatier's note 17 prefers it); Sabatier's own text famously softens it to "if it pleases God, he will become a good Christian."

**The freeing from chains.** 1C 13 (1228) is the origin; L3C 18 repeats it nearly verbatim ("moved by her deep feeling for him, she removed the chains, and let him go free... he turned on his wife in abuse, heaping sin upon sin," FA:ED II, 79). Not in 2C. The place-legend: the grated "carcere di San Francesco" shown inside the Chiesa Nuova (built 1615); and the chain became her iconographic attribute — the 1984 bronze by Roberto Joppolo on the Chiesa Nuova parvis shows "Monna Pica" holding a chain while Pietro holds cloth (lafune.eu).

**The Elizabeth typology.** First: 2C 3 (1247), "with some of the virtue of Saint Elizabeth... both in the name she gave her son and in her prophetic spirit." Then Bernard of Besse (1277–83): "Like another Elizabeth, at the sacred font she called him John" (still unnamed). Full bloom in De Conformitate (verified Latin): "*domina Pica vocata... velut altera Elisabeth, spiritu irradiata divino*," followed by a point-by-point Francis/Baptist conformity list — and Bartolomeo also makes her a **second Mary**: as Mary offered Jesus to the Magi, Pica offered Francis into the pilgrim's arms (AF IV, 108–9), with angels rejoicing as at Bethlehem. The Vat. 7739 L3C interpolation carries "another Elizabeth" into the popular L3C.

**Caught in passing:** L3C 9 (extra loaves for the poor tolerated "because his mother loved him more than the other children"); L3C 16's anonymous household accomplice who fed Francis in the cave, folded into Pica by later devotional retellings; no early source or verified retelling places her at the bishop's-square renunciation (films cast her prominently — Valentina Cortese in Zeffirelli 1972 — but no staging of her in that scene could be verified). Honest gaps.

## LAYER 3 — THE FRENCH-ORIGIN TRADITIONS

**Baseline the tradition grew on:** the 13th-century texts give her no origin at all; the Assisi acts always style her *Domina* (Sabatier: "She was no doubt of noble stock, for official documents always give her the title Domina" — verified). Nobility-without-France: Ottavio, bishop of Assisi, *Lumi serafici di Portiuncula* (Venice 1701, from a Lucca MS he copied in 1689): Pietro "obtained the hand of a young girl of noble family, named Pica" — no country (via Chérancé and Jørgensen).

**The 'de Bourlemont' attribution — the spine.** Root carrier: **Claude Frassen OFM (1620–1711), *La Règle du Tiers-Ordre de la Pénitence*, Paris, 1st ed. 1666** (reprints to the "eighth and last edition," 1752): "La France a la gloire de lui avoir donné naissance, puisqu'elle est issue de l'illustre maison des Bourlemont, ainsi qu'il paraît par un ancien manuscrit conservé dans les archives de cette très noble famille" — verified as quoted verbatim in Chérancé's 1892 footnote (archive.org: `saintfranoisdass00cher`); Frassen's own pages not directly opened (Google Books, no access) — gap flagged. Companion relic-claim: a **marriage contract** of Pietro and Pica "in the archives of Provence," which Frassen (per Chérancé's 1881 footnote, "publié en 1703") "affirme l'avoir vu"; reinforced by Niccola Papini Tartagni (1751–1834); the Gubbio museum page's dry verdict: "Molti ne parlano, ma nessuno lo ha davvero visto" — many speak of it, no one has ever shown it (umbriametu.it).

Chain of carriers: Frassen 1666 → [**silence** in Chalippe 1728 ("His mother, whose name was Pica" — no France), Chavin de Malan 1841 ("Pierre Bernardone Moriconi et sa femme Picca" — nothing French), Ozanam 1852 (the book that made Francis a troubadour never names her)] → **Léopold de Chérancé 1879/1881/1892**, who grafts *Provence* onto Frassen's Lorraine-sounding name: "Sa mère, Pica, de la noble famille des Bourlemont en Provence" — while his own 1892 footnote points the other way: "On rencontre le nom des Bourlemont, dès le XIIIe siècle, dans les archives des **Vosges** et de la Côte-d'Or. Voir les Annales Franciscaines d'octobre 1890" (that 1890 article not located — gap) → **Johannes Jørgensen 1907 (Fr. tr. 1909/11)**, the romance: Pietro met her "dans la belle Provence, riche en légendes... au cours de l'un de ses voyages commerciaux," Francis's "ardent sang provençal" → **Marion A. Habig OFM, *The Franciscan Book of Saints*, 1959**: "Pica was descended from a noble French family of the Bourlemonts" — the devotional canonization (roman-catholic-saints.com; read aloud yearly as "Franciscan Saint of the Day," airmaria.com) → **Wikipedia** ("Pica di Bourlemont... a noblewoman originally from Provence"; fr.wiki adds "Dona Joanna Pica de Bourlémont" and seven children) → the genealogy web (Geni: "Donna Pica Petronella Bernardone (Bourlemont) 1160–1230" and elsewhere "†1211"; WikiTree's only source is Wikipedia; Wikidata Q110856621 has zero references). The given names Jeanne/Giovanna and Petronella, the dates, and the birth-at-the-castle are all database accretions with no located upstream carrier.

**Picardy variant** ("Pica = la Picarde"): no pre-20th-century carrier found. Verified carriers: Giuseppe Abate (via Raphael Brown 1982); Noel Muscat OFM (2003 formation text: "una ragazza dalla Piccardia, conosciuta appunto con il soprannome di Pica"); the anonymous web stratum ("her actual name was Giovanna... nickname... because she was from Picardy").

**Provence without Bourlemont:** Catholic Encyclopedia 1909 (Paschal Robinson): "Of his mother, Pica, little is known, but she is said to have belonged to a noble family of Provence." Provençal-regionalist carriers add detail (provence7.com: second marriage 1180 after widowhood); a Beaucaire parish page claimed the Bourlemont "originally from Tarascon" (now offline, HTTP 410 — low confidence).

**Italian repatriation counter-variants** (keeping the name, reclaiming the woman): "Bourlemont" as a French garbling of **Belmonte Piceno** (Giovanni Rocchi) or **Monte Burella** near Nocera Umbra (Arduino & Gentili), within Gianfranco Baleani's "Francia Picena" thesis (2015–16; larucola.org, 4 Oct 2023).

**The skeptical bench, as one more belief-position:** Stanislao Melchiorri 1856 (searched French records, nothing); Michele Faloci Pulignani 1885 (refused Frassen absent the documents); Léon Le Monnier 1889 (genealogical objection: the Bourlémont male line and its Anglure succession don't fit — relayed by *Il Mattino* 1926/larucola; originals not inspected); Sabatier 1893/94 (verified: "There is nothing impossible in her having been of Provençal origin, but there is nothing to indicate it in any document worthy of credence"); Omer Englebert 1947 ("nul n'ayant jamais vu le fameux manuscrit... entièrement fantaisiste"); the Omnibus of Sources 1973 ("this cannot be confirmed"); Francesca Gambino, *ZrP* 139 (2023) (the whole French frame "generally overestimated" — paywalled, wording unverified). Wadding's *Annales* (1625): probe of several archive.org volumes found no Pica passage, but tome I could not be positively identified — recorded as unresolved, not as a negative.

## LAYER 4 — LATER LIFE, DEATH, CULT

**Children.** The mocking brother ("sell me a penny's worth of your sweat" — Francis answers, in French, that he will sell it dearer to his Lord) is **unnamed** in 2C 12 and L3C 23. The name **Angelo** comes from the archival door: "Angelus Pice" in Assisi acts (Cristofani → Sabatier → Fortini, chart at *Nova Vita* II, 100); his sons Piccardo (the stalletta chapel-maker, alive 1281, Third Order) and Giovanni/Giovannetto ("Johannectus olim Angeli domine Pice"). Wadding (per the Omnibus note): Angelo's descendants "were reduced to begging in the streets of Assisi." Modern speculation makes Angelo Pica's son by a previous marriage (Porziuncola Project; a stage play casts him as stepbrother) — no early evidence, recorded as modern accretion. Fr.wikipedia's "seven children" is referenceless.

**Death and burial.** Two irreconcilable beliefs, both carried today: (a) scholarly-archival: **dead by 1211**, when Angelo names his son Picone "remaking" his deceased mother (Thompson 2012, from Fortini's documents); (b) devotional: she **outlived Pietro**, "donned the penitential garb of the Third Order, and lived a secluded life devoted to piety" (Habig 1959, copied across the devotional web). **No burial tradition exists anywhere** — not Chiesa Nuova, not San Nicolò; even the sites that give her a feast are silent on a grave. Verified gap.

**Veneration.** She is "Blessed Pica" **by custom, not decree**: the Franciscan menologium (per Habig: she "not only gave bodily birth to St Francis, but by her prayers and the example of her virtues gave him as a brightly shining light to Holy Church"), observed **April 14** in Franciscan circles (SSJ-TOSF congregational posts; AirMaria's "Franciscan Saint of the Day"); "has never been formally beatified" (Habig's own concession). Verified negatives: no entry at santiebeati.it, absent from the Roman Martyrology, no church or confraternity found.

**Iconography.** Bronze pair flanking the Chiesa Nuova (built 1615 over the reputed paternal house): **Roberto Joppolo, 1984** — Pica holds the chain, Pietro the cloth. Inside: the "carcere" cell. Paintings: **Benozzo Gozzoli, Montefalco, 1450–52** — first scene of the cycle combines the stall-birth (ox and ass), the pilgrim before Monna Pica, and the homage of the simple man; **Cesare Sermei, 1646–47**, Lower Basilica of Assisi entrance transept: "L'Angelo annuncia a Madonna Pica la nascita del figlio Francesco." She is absent from the Giotto cycle in the Upper Church. The knee-print stone of the angel-pilgrim at the San Rufino font is her adjacent relic-legend.

**Fortini** (*Nova Vita*, 1959; Moak trans. 1981): the stable legend is late-trecento at the earliest (Anonimo Brussellese); the real house stood between San Nicolò and San Paolo; the family documents (Angelo di Pica etc.) anchor Pica's historicity; the foundational modern study he built on is **Giuseppe Abate, "Storia e leggenda intorno alla nascita di S. Francesco d'Assisi" (1948–49)**. The Frassen/Bourlemont claim is not Fortini's; Italian carriers attribute the Provence-Bourlemont line to Frassen and the contract story to Papini (umbriametu.it).

**Modern treatments.** Films: Valentina Cortese as Pica (Zeffirelli, *Brother Sun Sister Moon*, 1972); Andréa Ferréol as the unnamed mother (Cavani, *Francesco*, 1989); Erika Blanc as Pica (*Francesco* miniseries, 2002); no Pica character in *L'ami* (2016). No novel titled for her was found (gap); a stage play (*Il Poverello*, Doug Goheen) features her; the Basilica's magazine runs recurring model-mother pieces (Alberzoni; Tarallo; "Da Madonna Pica a Madonna Povertà").

## LAYER 5 — THE BOURLÉMONT ↔ DOMRÉMY ↔ JOAN RESONANCE

**The castle and family (verified baseline).** Château de Bourlémont, Frebécourt (Vosges), above the Meuse ~5 km from Neufchâteau, facing Domrémy country; built by the bishops of Toul "sans doute entre 1100 et 1130"; held from 1157 by the **Brixey** family, whose branch took the name; first documented mention of the line 1184 — i.e., the only documented 12th-century Bourlémonts, squarely in Pica's supposed lifetime, are Lorraine's (fr.wikipedia "Château de Bourlémont"; Guy Lecomte 2014; the standard monograph is **Georges Poull, *Le château et les seigneurs de Bourlémont: pages d'histoire au pays de Jeanne d'Arc*, 2 vols., 1962/64** — the subtitle itself binds Bourlémont to Joan's country). Male line extinct **1412** with Pierre de Bourlémont — the year of Joan's birth; barony to the d'Anglure.

**Bourlémont in Joan's own mouth (trial-verified).** Condemnation trial, third public session, 24 Feb 1431 (Murray trans.): the Fairy Tree — "It is a beautiful tree, a beech... **It belongs to the Seigneur Pierre de Bourlement, Knight**" (famous-trials.com). Estivet's indictment calls it "l'arbre charmine faee de Bourlemont." The 1456 rehabilitation witnesses recall **Béatrice, dame de Bourlémont** picnicking under the tree with the village children — testimony used to clear Joan of maleficium; Jacques d'Arc leased the Bourlémont "château de l'Isle" in 1420 (jeannedomremy.fr).

**The explicit Pica↔Joan splice — found, and it is small and recent.** It lives in one French Johannic web ecosystem, **jeannedomremy.fr** (pages signed "THEVENIN"), c. 2016–2020: (a) the Isabelle Romée page: "Isabelette Romée est également parente de Saint François d'Assise... sa mère Dona Joanna Pica de Bourlémont," adding that the Bourlémonts "sont sans doute restés attachés au souvenir de Saint-François" through Isabelle's Franciscan Third Order piety, and deriving "Romée" from an October 1376 pilgrimage — the 150th anniversary of Francis's death; (b) the Domrémy page repeats it; (c) J.-C. Le Brigand, "Étude de l'anneau de Jeanne" (April 2016), strongest form: Joan was raised by Isabelle de Vouthon, "une **descendante de la famille maternelle de François d'Assise** — la famille de Bourlémont, propriétaire du château tout là-haut dans la forêt, où notre Jehanne a grandi." A Guy de Rambaud fandom wiki repeats it (fetch blocked). And the genealogy web has already inscribed the seam: Geneanet's index carries an individual recorded as "**de BRIXEY de BOURLEMONT Jehanne Pica**" — Pica grafted by name onto the Lorraine line of the Fairy Tree. Verified negatives: no Franciscan, Italian, or English-language carrier of the splice; the castle's own website mentions neither saint; the Italian Bourlemont-origin debate never mentions Joan. The pivot that makes the splice inevitable for literalists is Le Monnier's old objection itself: no Provençal Bourlemont family existed in Pica's era — "the family originated in Lorraine" (larucola.org) — so anyone taking "Pica de Bourlémont" on faith is pulled north, to Domrémy.

## LAYER 6 — THE TALLER STRATUM (this corpus as the newest carrier)

The belief did not stop accreting in 2020. The newest carrier is this house: the essays repo (numbered corpus 171–220 at root + `instagram/` extractions), whose distillation plan (`instagram/_seeds_54.md`) reserves a whole section — **V. La saga Bourlémont — Francisco**, seeds 24–28, plus seed 21 (*arbol_de_hadas_de_bourlemont*) and seed 44 (*sotah_49b_perro_del_sol*) — for the Pica histories. Mapped like every other stratum: what is believed, where it is written, and where it grafts onto the attested record.

**The cast the corpus adds:**

- **Pierre Bourlémont, the troubadour cousin** — as a boy travels with his father to the Caucasus on a trade-and-diplomacy mission; at the court of Ganja, Nizami takes a liking to the young Frenchman and tells him the Shahnameh — Rostam and Sohrab, Bijan's cipher, Zal's serenade, the Azhdaha dragons whose skin-cloaks cure the wounds of the heart (`instagram/2023-01-23-a-nizami-le-gusto-pierre-bourlemont.md`). Home in Provence he gifts his cousin **Giovanna** the Persian poems and coins her nickname **Pica** (`2023-01-14-la-familia-bourlemont-de-domremy-y.md`); he sings her the Khamsa's **five sufferings of love** — unreturned, impossible, forbidden, impotent, and the "quinta herida," the open heart, each answered by a practice: profession, pilgrimage, fasting, alms, prayer (`2023-01-14-pierre-cantaba-para-pica-llenando-los.md`). Seed 25 (*pierre_bourlemont_trovador*) binds this to Capellanus' *De Amore* and the sub-rosa erotics of secrecy (`2023-02-17-…de.md`, `2023-02-17-la-frase-latina-sub-rosa-que.md`).
- **Pica = Giovanna of the Bourlémont cloth branch** — the family holds Domrémy, the Vosges estate, and a Provençal branch in the cloth trade; she marries the Italian cloth merchant Pietro di Bernardone, bears her son **in 1181** while Pietro is trading in France, names him Giovanni for the Baptist, educates him in music, lyric song, the voice of nature, Latin and Provençal; Pietro renames him **Frances** in honor of *her* French culture, the troubadour poetry, the Provence cloth, and the Bourlémont family (`2023-01-14-la-familia-bourlemont-de-domremy-y.md`). Her consolation speech after the renunciation makes the name-braid explicit: **"Yo soy Giovanna y tú eres Giovanni. Somos John, Joan, Jeanne y Juana… Siempre eres mi hijo y un Bourlémont"** (`2023-01-22-cuando-el-pobre-giovanni-de-pica.md`).
- **The presepe inversion** — the corpus's boldest addition: it was **Pica who staged the first presepe**, 42/43 years before Greccio, emptying the ground floor of the house into a manger after a vision, so that Francis was *born into a performance* of Bethlehem (`2023-07-10-francisco-de-asis-es-reconocido-por.md` + `2023-07-10-antonin-artaud-performance-franciscana.md`, which run the line forward through Attisani's "Franciscan performance" to Artaud's 1922 *Poem of Saint Francis of Assisi*; seed 28, *performance_franciscana*). This takes the stalletta legend (Layer 2) and reverses its polarity: not a humiliation forced by a pilgrim's word but a deliberate installation by the troubadour mother — and Gozzoli's Montefalco fresco (1450–52), which already fuses stall-birth + pilgrim + homage in one panel, is its ready-made icon.
- **Joffrey Bourlémont, Pierre's son** — born amid the Children's-Crusade tide (Stephen of Cloyes), at **Greccio, Christmas 1223, aged 12**, holding his father's hand before his frail cousin at the first nativity (`2023-01-25-pierre-viajo-con-su-unico-hijo.md`); at 18 present for the **Treaty of 1229**; in Jerusalem with the friars of the Provincia de Tierra Santa, who read the **Vía Dolorosa's fifth station** (Simon of Cyrene) and the founder's five wounds as a Khamsa made flesh (`2023-01-25-cuando-joffrey-fue-a-jerusalen-en.md`, `2023-01-25-nuestro-hermano-francisco-estaba-ayunando-en.md` — the La Verna seraph narrative, with Joffrey rumored to have worn the Virgin's dress at the Bethlehem nativity); fights under **Thibault de Champagne**; returns 1244 when Jerusalem falls; and in grief **builds the well and the tower at the beech tree** on the road to Vaucouleurs, telling the dancing children of the Holy Land and the dragons of the forest — "un portal de magia celta… un espíritu adolescente que algún día salvaría a Francia" (`2022-12-22-joffrey-bourlemont-nacio-en-medio-de.md`; seed 24, *joffrey_y_lois*).
- **Lois de Conte** — born January 6, "200 years after Joffrey," in Neufchâteau; scribe of the Children of the Tree; Joan's birthday-twin (`2022-12-26-lois-de-conte-nacio-el-6.md`). This grafts **Mark Twain's narrator** (Sieur Louis de Conte, *Personal Recollections of Joan of Arc*, 1896 — whose fairy-tree song the corpus renders in Spanish, `2023-01-05-arbol-de-hadas-de-bourlemont-como.md`) into the Bourlémont line — a carrier adopting a carrier.
- **The five-knot braid** — corpus essay **207_hebraic_knots** (and its instagram sibling `2024-04-20-el-cinturon-de-san-francisco-es.md`) states the whole theology in one sentence: the Franciscan cord's knot "contains five loops and is topologically equivalent to the quipu five-knot. It represents the five wounds of Christ that Saint Francis sustained at La Verna and the five forms of divine suffering described in Nizami's Khamsa, **the same forms of passion Pierre Bourlémont communicated to Pica, the mother of Saint Francis**" — Khamsa → five sufferings → five wounds → cord → **quipu**, which is to say: the Pica myth is load-bearing for this whole inscription project. Seed 44 (`2023-05-22-katrin-vates-matiascarbone-dogecoin-bourlemont-sotah.md`) closes that loop onto the ledger itself: La Verna bordado, "Francisco de Italia, un descendiente de la familia francesa Bourlémont," Sotah 49b's dog-faced generation, the sun-dog coin.
- **The fairy-tree / Joan face** — corpus essay **218_atavismo_y_los_ninos_perdidos** ("la comunidad de niños que bailaban alrededor del Árbol de las Hadas de Bourlemont, del cual emergió La Pucelle") and `2024-09-27-1-en-el-juicio-de-rehabilitacion.md`, which quotes the 1455/56 rehabilitation testimony directly: the godmother Jeanne, widow of Thiescelin, told Joan a romance in which "**el Señor Pierre de Bourlément y una dama llamada Fée se reunieron bajo este árbol**." The Baptist typology that opens the whole tradition (Elizabeth, Layer 1) recurs at the far end of the corpus in the Reims/Visitation/Salomé constellation (`2025-01-28-reims-fue-el-sitio-donde-san.md`).

**Graft points — where the saga touches the attested record (see bourlemont_roster.md):**

1. **Joffrey ↔ Joffroy.** The corpus's Joffrey (born ~1211 by the age-18-in-1229 arithmetic) lands almost exactly on Poull's reconstructed **Joffroy de Bourlémont, c. 1210–†1268** — the one lord of the first house with a charter in his own voice (28 May 1248, giving Mureau abbey the *arages* of **Domremy and Greux** — issued right where the saga has him home from Jerusalem, consoling himself at the tree), **sénéchal de Navarre of Thibaut IV** (= the saga's "Thibault de Champagne," verbatim), returned from the Holy Land, rebuilder of the castle. The strongest single dovetail in the whole structure.
2. **Pierre ↔ Pierre II ↔ the Pierre of the romance.** The saga's Pierre (father of Joffrey) sits where the roster's **Pierre II (c. 1170–†c. 1226, father of Joffroy)** sits; his boyhood at Ganja falls inside **Nizami's actual floruit** (c. 1141–1209, resident in Ganja his whole life — the atlas already holds nizami.journey.json); and the 1456 testimony's romance-Pierre under the tree with the lady Fée gives the name a third, court-recorded face (the roster's Pierre V + Béatrix picnicking at the tree is the fourth).
3. **Pica = Giovanna.** Layer 1 found the circulating "her real name was Giovanna" claim **sourceless** — a back-formation from the son's baptismal name. The corpus doesn't source it; it *supplies the mechanism the belief was missing* (Pierre coining the magpie-nickname for his cousin), and its Giovanna→Giovanni→Jeanne→Juana braid rhymes with the oldest attested stratum, the Elizabeth/Baptist naming of 2C 3 (1247).
4. **Lois de Conte ↔ 1412.** "200 years after Joffrey" puts Lois's birth at ~1411–12 — Twain's own date for his narrator (Jan 6, 1410, Neufchâteau), Joan's birth year, and the **extinction year of the Bourlémont male line** (Pierre V, buried with the Franciscans of Neufchâteau). Three calendars close on the same seam.

**Internal variants (ledger-grade, not corrections):** the presepe-inversion pair disagrees with itself — "43 años antes" (Spanish) vs "42 years earlier" (English); with the corpus's own birth-year 1181 and Greccio 1223, the arithmetic favors 42. The saga attributes the inversion to "Bonaventure, *Leyenda Mayor*" — a mythic attribution (Bonaventure's actual LM has no presepe at the birth); it functions exactly as Frassen's unseen manuscript does in Layer 3, and is recorded the same way. The corpus consistently writes "Frances/Bernadone" where the ledger-stratum writes "Francesco/Bernardone."

**Atlas hooks** (where the saga can dovetail into journey files): saint_francis.journey.json already holds the canonical pins the saga touches — Greccio 1223, La Verna 1224, Rome 1210, Damietta 1219; nizami.journey.json holds Ganja for Pierre's boyhood; joan_of_arc.journey.json holds Domrémy and the tree for Joffrey's well-and-tower and Lois. If the saga is ever encoded as journeys (pierre_bourlemont, joffrey_bourlemont), the atlas convention applies: byte-identical sibling pins at every shared stop, and the Fairy Tree pin shared three ways (joan_of_arc, joffrey, 218's bordado).

**Shape of the whole:** three 13th-century texts build her in three acts — unchaining (1228), naming-and-prophecy with Elizabeth (1247), the father's French renaming (1246) — all without a name. The name arrives twice, independently: in Assisi ledgers as a matronymic, and in a neighbor's fireside tale c. 1300. The 14th–15th-century conformity machine makes her Elizabeth-plus-Mary at a second Bethlehem; a Parisian friar in 1666 gives her a French castle on the strength of a manuscript nobody has ever seen; a 19th-century Breton Capuchin moves the castle to Provence while his own footnote points to the Vosges; and since the only real 12th-century Bourlémonts are the lords of Joan of Arc's Fairy Tree, taking "Pica de Bourlémont" on faith pulls the line north, to Domrémy. The taller corpus is the newest carrier of that pull, and the one that closes it: Pierre carries the Khamsa west from Ganja, Pica knots it into her son's cord, Joffrey carries it back east and home to the beech tree, and the five-knot lands in the quipu — the belief inscribing itself into the ledger that maps it.
